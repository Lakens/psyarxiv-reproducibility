Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Abstract

Mental health problems represent a major global challenge, with access to appropriate care for children and adolescents remaining severely limited. Digital mental health interventions (DMHIs) can offer scalable, accessible support beyond traditional clinical settings. Despite a constantly growing number of youths DMHIs, the evidence base is constrained, due to a lack of replication and follow-up studies, scarce side-effect examination, and heterogeneous designs and characteristics. This is particularly concerning regarding young people as a vulnerable population. Knowledge of intervention processes is important to advance the field of DMHIs, while this topic is under researched in both DMHIs and traditional interventions for youth. Reaching full potential of DMHIs for youth requires an integrated research and development approach. We consulted experts from multiple disciplines, including youths psychiatrists, psychologists, psychotherapists, computer scientists and ethicists specializing in digital mental health, to integrate the current state of knowledge into a conceptual framework. The conceptual framework "From Lab to App (and Back)" emphasizes continuous exchange between DMHI research and development, with participatory collaboration, embedded ethics, and science communication relevant in all stages. We outline that DMHIs are particularly well suited to generate insight into mechanisms of change and discuss key factors in DMHIs for youth. Furthermore, we address the need for more unified conceptualizations and standardized reporting and illustrate a working intervention process model for youths DMHIs to induce an overarching research agenda.

Overall, the framework provides a structured pathway toward scientifically rigorous and clinically meaningful digital innovation in youth mental health research and practice.

Approximately half of all mental disorders begin during adolescence [1, 2], highlighting a great need for evidence-based mental health interventions for youth. However, even in high income countries, a significant proportion of affected youth receive no mental health support, with fewer than 50% accessing services [3] and up to 61% reporting difficulties obtaining treatment [4]. Barriers for access to mental health interventions include individual barriers (e.g., self-stigma), social barriers (e.g., limited knowledge in family's about mental health and services) and structural barriers (e.g., financial constraints, long waiting lists, institutional stigma) [5] [6] [7] [8]. Digital mental health interventions (DMHIs) appear promising to oppose some of the barriers -they are considered easy to access and cost-effective, might enhance acceptance of seeking support [9], and might enhance ecological validity of treatments. For instance, virtual reality can be used to simulate realistic scenarios [10], mobile apps can transfer therapy into daily life [11], and gamification can enhance motivation and compliance [e.g.12].

These advantages, along with daily use of technologies, have driven the development of numerous DMHIs for youth. There are preliminary promising findings on positive effects of DMHIs on youth well-being and user satisfaction across various mental health conditions [13, 9, [14] [15] [16]. However, there is an alarming gap between the availability of DMHIs in app stores and their evidence [17, 18]. On the one hand, DMHIs have high potential to help youth who are significantly underserved. On the other hand, there is a clear need to advance research on DMHIs for youth, with special attention to the vulnerabilities of minors.

Resolving this gap is challenging because DMHIs form a heterogeneous group of interventions that are technology-based or -supported. The most common forms for youth are apps followed by telemedicine [19]. Here, the primary recipient can vary (e.g., parental trainings vs. childcentered approaches). While DMHIs following a caregiver-focused approach show promising effects, e.g., [20, 21], less is known about interventions with the child as the primary recipient.

DMHIs also differ in design and targets. With a variety of possible implementations, the challenge is to identify the best fit between DMHI characteristics and a specific use case. For example, a DMHI targeting school anxiety within a school setting should consider device accessibility due to smartphone bans at schools and peer or teacher presence during use. In contrast, an intervention for specific phobia in a mental health center may involve virtual reality and therapist involvement. When evaluating DMHIs, this heterogeneity leads to high variations in study designs, measurements, insufficient reporting of clients' characteristics, as well as a deficient description and non-uniform taxonomy of DMHIs, especially regarding the role of human support [16].

One critical prerequisite for examining the effects of different interventions is a common language. Pineda and colleagues propose a useful taxonomy based on technology and human contact: (1) provider-administered, (2) provider-administered with blended digital adjuncts, (3) self-help with human support, and (4) fully automated self-help (see [22]). Figure 1 summarizes key aspects of this taxonomy and places them alongside use case characteristics. To date, both provider-administered (e.g., videoconference-delivered [23]) and self-help DMHIs (e.g., internet-based cognitive behavioral therapy with human support [13], gaming intervention with human support [24]) have demonstrated efficacy. There is some initial evidence for higher efficacy with more client-provider interaction [25]. Interestingly, conversational AI agents, which provide synchronous interaction in fully automated self-help DMHIs, show medium-tolarge effects in adolescents and young adults with depressive symptoms [26]. With a few exceptions [e.g.27], studies designed to provide evidence on the effectiveness of a specific DMHI, like randomized controlled trials (RCTs), are mostly found for non-commercial DMHIs [18]. DMHIs widely available in the app stores, however, have a documented lack of evidence [17]. Existing studies are mostly limited to short time periods, specific programs, settings, and target groups. Evidence for long-term effects is scarce and mixed [28, 9]. A recent review on mental health apps reported that final assessments in RCTs occurred, on average, less than three months post-randomization and highlighted a substantial lack of replication studies [18]. This might result from a trend to develop novel DMHIs, rather than refining, disseminating, and comprehensive evaluating existing ones [18]. Adverse emotional and behavioral effects, as well as dropout, remain largely understudied, particularly in youth [13, 18, 29].

A Conceptual Framework for Evidence-Based DMHIs: "From Lab to App (and Back)"

We propose a conceptual framework advancing the field through a "from lab to app (and back)" approach, namely the continuous synergistic exchange between research and development, aiming to enhance evidence and to maximize stakeholder acceptance (see Figure 2). The framework emphasizes a continuous, synergistic exchange between DMHI research and development to enhance user acceptance and strengthen the empirical evidence base. DMHI development and evaluation operate as collaborative processes, supported by science communication, embedded ethics, and participatory collaboration, which enhance each stage as well as the exchange between them. Knowledge, innovations, and publicly available evidence generated through this process may be transferable to traditional intervention settings, extending impact beyond the digital context to support child and adolescent mental health more broadly.

In this context, DMHIs can be seen as a dynamic system in which multiple components interact to support a child's transition from a present state toward a desired future state [30]. At its core are the structural (e.g., visual design, cover story) and functional components ("features", e.g., exposure to a prospective self by touching the screen to connect with a future-self avatar) of the specific DMHI [31]. While both types of components indirectly influence intervention progress, for instance, via user engagement [32, 33], the features are conceptualized as potential active ingredients that can drive change more directly. The process is characterized by mechanisms of change, mediators, and moderators. Mechanisms of change are internal processes that translate an intervention into actual improvements. Mediators are empirically testable variables that explain how or why change occurs, whereas moderators specify for whom and under which conditions the intervention is effective [30]. Surrounding these core processes are social factors, such as the digital working alliance [34, 35], caregiver involvement [36, 37], and broader human support, which can be shaped by specifically designed DMHI components, and might strengthen or weaken an intervention's impact. For example, a child's commitment and accountability may be shaped by the involvement of caregivers [37]. The therapeutic process is embedded within a broader context of environmental influences, extra-therapeutic factors, and social networks, including supportive or stigmatizing norms. Together, these interconnected elements illustrate how change emerges from the interplay between designed intervention components, individual psychological processes, interpersonal dynamics, and the broader environment. For a visualization of the described process model, see Figure 3. This model was developed based on a previously proposed model for depression-focused DMHIs [30] and requires adaptation in future studies. For instance, commitment and supportive accountability as well as user engagement are considered critical for DMHIs, yet knowledge remains limited. Indeed, recent accounts of digital working alliance indicate that applicationinduced accountability might not only be important in DMHIs with human support [30, 38], but also in self-help DMHIs, however, evidence is scarce [39, 40].

For the investigation of mechanisms of change in DMHIs, it is key to examine the intervention process in addition to the intervention outcome. This approach has rarely been adopted for DMHI research in children and adolescents. Even in classical face-to-face settings, our understanding of mechanisms of change in child and adolescent psychotherapy remains limited [30, 41]. Strategies to address this include closely replicating face-to-face interventions digitally or adapting adult DMHIs for younger populations. These strategies risk missing the unique opportunities digital interventions offer for youth. Indeed, the early stage of DMHIs offers a unique chance to establish a collaborative, structured research agenda that identifies key factors and mechanisms in youth-focused and traditional interventions, guiding future development [30]. To uncover mechanisms of change, study designs must go beyond simple pre-post comparisons. The literature recommends longitudinal studies with at least three measurement points to meaningfully assess mediators [30]. Intervention component studies examine intervention processes by isolating specific effects through varying individual components while keeping others constant. This approach is preferably conducted as an RCT to provide robust effect size estimates [30]. We argue that DMHIs offer strong potential for research on mechanisms of change in child and adolescent therapeutic processes. Due to their location-independent nature, they can reach larger sample sizes in more diverse samples.

Further, unlike face-to-face interventions, which struggle with standardization due to high interactivity, DMHIs provide a major advantage: their content is typically standardized and easily tracked [30]. We recommend using transdiagnostic scales to enable pooled analyses of raw data across diverse study populations. These scales can be applied across sites and modeled with study-and site-specific covariates in multilevel approaches [42], creating opportunities for systematic research beyond individual studies. A particularly important consideration in child and adolescent DMHIs is the developmentally sensitive implementation of components, as the youth's developmental stage and prior experiences act as a key moderator of intervention effects. While some components may not meaningfully contribute to the intervention process and can be considered inactive, others actively support change and are referred to as active ingredients or features. Functional components are intentionally designed to serve as such active ingredients. These include specific therapeutic techniques grounded in established theoretical frameworks (e.g., cognitive restructuring, exposure) as well as common factors relevant across therapeutic approaches and disorders (e.g., problem activation, motivation clarification). DMHI components may further influence cognitive and affective mediators, thereby promoting change. In addition, human support and caregiver involvement represent important influencing factors in youth DMHIs. These factors may operate as contextual influences or can also be explicitly supported or facilitated as part of the DMHI itself through dedicated features (e.g., guidance, feedback, caregiver modules), thereby shaping commitment, engagement, and intervention outcomes. Moreover, the working alliance is an important factor, which functions as both an active ingredient and a mediatorestablished through DMHI components and potentially human support, it influences engagement as well as mechanisms of change. The model conceptualizes the therapeutic process as embedded within a broader context that encompasses extra-therapeutic and environmental factors, such as stressors, individual life circumstances, social networks, and normative influences.

Key Process Determinants in Youth DMHIs

Cognitive and Affective Mediators. Cognitive mediators (e.g., self-efficacy, inhibitory control) represent the largest group of mediators that have been assessed for DMHIs.

Additionally, affective mediators (e.g., emotion regulation) so far show the highest proportion of significant associations with DMHI outcomes, albeit this is based on a limited number of studies [43, 44]. With a few exceptions (e.g., [45]), the evidence is largely derived from young adults and older adolescents. This is problematic, as cognitive and affective skills mature significantly during childhood and adolescence (e.g., [46, 47]), leaving the role of these mediators in DMHIs for younger populations insufficiently understood.

For instance, grounded in social cognitive theory [48], self-efficacy is expected to be a relevant factor in DMHIs. Particularly in self-help formats, which rely heavily on users' autonomous engagement, confidence in one's ability to use the application and apply its content independently is crucial for the intervention process [49]. This is supported by studies examining mainly adults using self-help apps targeting different mental health issues, showing mediating effects of self-efficacy (see [49]). While informant-report measures can be used to assess observable mediators, such as usage of emotion regulation strategies [45, 50], specific cognition about oneself, such as general self-efficacy perception, requires self-report measures. Some self-report questionnaires are available for adolescents and older children (e.g., [51, 52]); however, they remain scarce for younger children, hindering the investigation of these mediators in younger age groups. Thus, the adaptation of existing questionnaires for children is necessary. For instance, a self-report measure of control beliefs in preschool and elementary school children can be administered via an interview with short items in simplified language and a yes-no response format [53, 54]. Nevertheless, the developmental appropriateness of affective and cognitive constructs should be considered when developing or adapting questionnaires for younger age groups, as some (e.g., attributional style) are discussed to stabilize only after age 10 [55].

It has further been suggested that cognitive tasks, potentially in a gamified manner that deliver performance-based measures for cognitive functions like inhibitory control [56], working memory [57], or decision-making [58] are promising to assess mediators [59]. One advantage of gamified tasks is that they could be intuitive, requiring little instruction and might enhance motivation in children in contrast to often tedious and monotonous self-report questionnaires or traditional experimental tasks ([see 60]). However, while psychometric properties of experimental tasks can often be low [61], there is first evidence that these may be largely acceptable for gamified tasks even with limited trial counts [62]. The stop-signal task (measuring inhibitory control) has even been adapted to toddler populations, with acceptableto-good psychometric properties [63]. Regarding affective processes, existing negative affective priming tasks, which capture unique attentional processes and performance patterns of psychiatric populations for affective stimuli [64, 65] could be gamified and adapted to younger populations to allow for a performance-based assessment of affective mediators.

(Gamified) performance-based measures of cognitive and affective mediators represent promising but still underutilized means [66] of elucidating potential mechanisms of change in DMHIs for children, especially if self-report measures cannot easily be administered.

Digital Working Alliance.

An important influential factor in traditional psychotherapy is the working alliance [67, 68, 35, 34, [69] [70] [71] [72]. Alliance refers to the collaborative client-professional or client-application relationship [73, 39, 40]. It is commonly conceptualized by agreement on goals, collaboration on tasks, and an emotional bond [74], while some approaches highlight the relationship quality and the patient's active role [75].

Intuitively, digital working alliance might differ significantly from traditional interventions. DMHIs might lack some of the emotional responsiveness and nuanced understanding that face-toface interventions offer. However, there is novel experimental evidence in adults that AIgenerated empathetic responses are perceived as more compassionate and responsive than human generated responses, and that conversational AI can establish closeness better than human interaction partners (interestingly, humans still prefer to receive empathy from humans than from AI agents) [76] [77] [78]. However, DMHIs may feel judgement-free, offering around-theclock availability and a sense of control, especially for those hesitant to seek traditional therapy.

There is evidence that digital alliance can be established successfully in guided [79, 80], but also in fully automated self-help DMHIs [81] in youth, and might reduce risk of drop-out [82].

To date, empirical work on digital working alliance in youth is scarce and diverse in scope and methods, and complicated by the fact that our understanding about mechanisms of working alliance in classical child psychotherapy remains scarce [34, 71, 83, 84]. Evidence on digital working alliance is mixed and suggests that it may vary as a function of clinical foci and age [85, 86]. Factors that are proposed to contribute to digital working alliance are collaboration, praise, and a shared sense of teamwork [80]. Design features like surface credibility and dialogue support were found to enhance adolescents' engagement, adherence, and satisfaction [87, 33]. Qualitative studies suggest that DMHIs are perceived as a "safe" and "open space" [88], and personification with a digital agent [81] contributes to the digital working alliance in youth DMHIs. Beyond these preliminary observations, the extent to which specific features contribute to digital working alliance in youth is unknown and may vary across DMHI approaches [68]. Moreover, studying digital working alliance in youth remains challenging due to inconsistent measurements and the lack of validated tools, particularly for self-help DMHIs targeting children. Last, we must not only consider possibilities to enhance digital alliance, but also keep in mind potential risks: there is evidence that excessive attachment and parasocial relationships, for example, to conversational AI, may lead to social withdrawal and loneliness, emotional dependence, or unrealistic expectations of the AI's capabilities [89]. The observation of humans falling in love with or entering romantic relationships with chatbots might be at the extreme end but should be considered when designing ethically responsible DMHIs for developing individuals [90].

Developmentally Sensitive Design.

Research indicates that children and adolescents interact with technology differently than adults, showing distinct cognitive, emotional, and motor patterns (e.g., impulsive responses, variable attention spans) that influence engagement and may cause unintended effects (e.g., frustration, mental overload) that diminish effectiveness of DMHIs [91, 92]. As one example, therapeutically promising use of virtual companions in DMHIs (e.g., for social buffering [93]) requires careful design, particularly regarding how digital characters are introduced and appear, as subtle influences like transforming behavior based on digital self-representation must be considered [94]. For instance, adolescents and young adults showed greater empathy toward positively portrayed digital characters in a game and adjusted their behavior in a friendlier or more aggressive way, as well as their judgments of neutral faces, depending on their avatar's similarity to a superhero or villain [95].

Social Support System.

Youth, their families, and professionals are embedded within social structures and contexts that shape how they feel, think, and behave throughout the intervention process. For instance, in a qualitative study exploring motivational aspects related to completing unpleasant DMHI tasks, children frequently reported being motivated by a desire to follow their caregivers' instructions or to avoid their disapproval, whereas some adolescents named peer comparisons or general social expectations as motivating factors [96]. Indeed, caregiver support and engagement are recognized as highly relevant in classical youth interventions, although the extent and nature of active and responsive contributions to the intervention by the caregiver vary widely [37]. Evidence on the specific effects of caregiver involvement in youth DMHI is mixed [36, 97, 98]. Most studies are heterogeneous in terms of age ranges and the assessment and definition of caregivers' involvement (e.g., dichotomous parent involvement yes/no; focus solitary on parent-led homework) and lack standardization in reporting study characteristics [37]. Importantly, whether and how caregiver involvement is beneficial might vary as a function of clients' age: Systematic caregiver involvement through specific DMHI features (e.g., specific modules on how to support therapeutic exercises) may be fruitful for younger children due to their limited experience in handling technology, as well as due to less mature cognitive, linguistic, and self-regulation abilities. However, the same feature might be counterproductive for adolescents, who often perceive independent engagement as a key advantage of DMHIs [99]. In addition to the youth's perspective, the caregiver's perspective should be considered and examined in future studies, as the caregiver's' attitudes towards an intervention can have an influence on the intervention process and can be modified, e.g., by brief online-modules [100]. Some parents report feeling uncertain about their role in youth-centered DMHIs [99], thus it may be beneficial to offer caregivers insights into their child's DMHI use via the application. For example, through an educational module providing guidance on how to support their child in the intervention process while respecting their autonomy (e.g., allowing them to choose when and how they engage with an app, respecting digital privacy).

Risks and Barriers

Drop-out rates in youth psychotherapy are high -around 60% [101] [102] [103] -and DMHI tools generally show low uptake [104]. This indicates substantial challenges for DMHI acceptance in young populations. Further, underaged patient groups are especially vulnerable [105] [106] [107].

DMHIs use technologies that may support the promotion of health (e.g., by ameliorating symptoms), while causing potential aversive effects at the same time (e.g., overly strong emotional dependence on a digital agent) e.g., [108] [109] [110]. Thus, careful consideration of (unintended) risks is key in DMHI development for children and adolescents, to avoid diminishing the enriching potential of DMHIs through undesirable outcomes.

Persuasive Design. Youth are particularly vulnerable to persuasive design and gamification elements [111] [112] [113]. For example, the use of rewards, progress bars, or avatars for motivation enhancement in mental health apps for children might pose the risk of emotional overstimulation, cognitive overload, or externalized behavioral control [114, 111]. Technology assessments must provide early risk assessments, such as habituation or shifts in self-regulation, to ensure safe and sustainable integration of gamified DMHIs in youth psychotherapy [115]. Suitable instruments to assess these effects across age groups are scarce, underscoring the need for tailored formative and summative methods [116].

Complementing these efforts, virtual simulations and digital testbeds provide controlled environments to standardize and compare evaluations, enhancing methodological rigor [117, 118]. Integrating insights from these methods, systematic, longitudinal and interdisciplinary technology assessment can enhance therapeutic outcomes such as improved adherence and reduced dropout [119, 120]. Developing systematic, target-group-specific guidelines should be a future aim to ensure instruments and applications are child-appropriate and meet their unique needs [121, 115].

Stigma.

Stigma can impede both initial help-seeking and sustained engagement with any therapeutic intervention [122] [123] [124] [125]. DMHIs offer both opportunities and challenges in this regard. They can contribute to stigma reduction through improved access, enhanced privacy, increased flexibility, user-driven engagement, and greater mental health awareness and literacy [126] [127] [128] [129] [130]. However, DMHIs technical solutions, such as gamification elements intended to boost engagement, also risk inadvertently exacerbating stigma through unintended exposure of personal data and data tracking, as well as through increased visibility of therapeutic activities to others (e.g., frequent app use, notifications) and through potentially heightened disorder presentation resulting from reinforced illness identity (e.g., excessive engagement with disorder-related content) [131] [132] [133]. Yet, we have a limited empirical understanding of how digital environments shape stigma processes in youth populations. In addition, cultural, gender, and age-related factors shape how stigma is perceived and experienced, including in digitally mediated settings [105, 134, 135]. A key step toward addressing these gaps is the development and validation of psychometrically sound instruments to assess stigma in youth, yet such tools remain scarce and largely tailored to adults or specific contexts, limiting their applicability to digital settings.

Data Protection.

Thus, it is necessary, as in other interventions, to comply with child-specific data protection laws (e.g., GDPR-K in the European Union, COPPA in the US). Consent processes need to be designed in a transparent way for both guardians and minors.

Particularly for younger individuals, this requires an adaptation of language and presentation mode. [136, 137] Overcoming Barriers Embedded Ethics. The interdisciplinary nature of DMHI-research and implementation requires institutionalized ethical guidance, since existing ethical principles of one specific field (e.g. medicine) alone cannot guarantee ethical technologies nowadays [138]. Given the novelty and developmental dynamics of DMHIs, consequences are difficult to foresee and tend to materialize only fully once the technology has been fully implemented. Accordingly, anticipation of ethical issues often remains highly speculative and dependent on an in-depth knowledge of new tech developments. To address these challenges, we advocate for embedded ethics in DMHIs for children and adolescents. Embedded ethics refers to the theory and practice of integrating ethical considerations into entire research and implementation processes by having ethicists, researchers, and developers working together to identify and address ethical issues via an iterative and continuous process [139] [140] [141]. The main goal is to promote the advancement of DMHIs that are ethically and socially responsible. This requires interdisciplinary offers for practical guidance and regular interdisciplinary communication channels.

Participation and Communication.

Participatory approaches, involving children, caregivers, and clinicians, are increasingly integrated into DMHI frameworks and promising to overcome barriers [31, [142] [143] [144]. However, stakeholder involvement often remains limited to early phases of DMHI development [145], and often exclude children [145, 146]. Although participatory work with young children is demanding, age-appropriate co-design methods exist [147, 148], including gamified formats [149, 150]. Power imbalances among stakeholders remain a challenge; participatory formats like structured "turning tables" [151] offer starting points but need adaptation for younger users [152]. Overall, participatory design should follow established guidelines, be iterative, minimize participant burden [153, 146], and be formally evaluated [154]. There is discussion that youth involvement can foster empowerment, improve intervention relevance, and success, however, empirical evidence on the effects of co-design is lacking to date (for review [155, 145]). Public communication is crucial for the translational impact of DMHIs. Following models of knowledge translation and implementation science, communication should be embedded throughout the entire development lifecycle rather than treated as a final dissemination step [156, 157]. Audience-centered and dialogic communication strategies might mitigate stigma, strengthen digital health literacy, and build trust among diverse stakeholders, including youth, families, clinicians, educators, and policymakers [158, 159]. Leveraging digital infrastructures, such as podcasts, interactive hubs, and social media, can facilitate accessible bidirectional exchange [160, 161]. This can ultimately improve equitable access and maximize the societal impact of evidence-based DMHIs for children and adolescents.

Conclusion

DMHIs have the potential to significantly improve the landscape of therapeutic support for youth mental health. They hold potential in advancing our knowledge about active ingredients and mechanisms of change in child and adolescent psychotherapy. However, evidence-based tech development is still in its infancy. Our proposed framework "From Lab to App (and Back)" leverages synergies between research and DMHI development: that includes not only continuous interdisciplinary exchange between the different fields but also the integration of embedded ethics, participatory collaboration, and science communication across fields and work stages. Intervention effects and processes should be investigated strategically to enhance knowledge on intervention processes in general and build an open evidence-based pool of design feature concepts facilitating therapeutic success. This requires investigation, with respect to both the effects and directions of effects, and the examination of influential factors in a causal manner (e.g., multiple assessment time points, experimental studies).

Next steps include establishing reporting standards, refining classical concepts (e.g., working alliance) for youth-and DMHI-specific contexts, and adapting assessment tools for younger populations (e.g., for cognitive and affective mediators). This presents a tremendous opportunity for our understanding of mechanisms of change in child and adolescent psychotherapy in general, through high-frequency data collection, trackable content and usage, and standardized, modular structures that support experimental designs. Their variability allows study of engagement and user experience, while their location-independent nature facilitates larger samples for complex analyses. Finally, stakeholder involvement in DMHI development can help surface emerging research priorities. These advanfces could foster a research ecosystem in which digital mental health interventions are rigorously validated, developmentally attuned, and broadly accessible-ultimately contributing to evidence-based support for young people with mental health conditions.
