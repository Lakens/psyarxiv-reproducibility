Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

Full text

Abstract

Youth psychopathology is a pressing global challenge. High comorbidity rates and moderate psychotherapy effects highlight the need for transdiagnostic approaches, which target processes cutting across diagnostic categories to better understand the etiology and maintenance of mental disorders. Ecological momentary assessment (EMA) enables to examine transdiagnostic processes in (almost) real-time in the natural environment of children and adolescents and therefore offers the opportunity to capture the micro-level, within-person dynamics through which psychopathology unfolds in everyday life. However, transdiagnostic research in children and adolescents so far has seldomly incorporated EMA and EMA studies in youth have rarely adopted a transdiagnostic focus. Although systematic reviews show that EMA is feasible in children and adolescents and can capture fluctuations, contexts, social interactions, and treatment-related change related to psychopathology, transdiagnostic EMA applications in youth remain limited. This methodological perspective paper provides an overview of the advantages of integrating EMA into the methodological repertoire of transdiagnostic research and practice with children and adolescents. We further provide recommendations on how to realize a transdiagnostic perspective using EMA with children and adolescents, summarize recent developments in this field, and outline key steps for the development and evaluation of a transdiagnostic EMA tool for youth.

Introduction

Mental disorders in children and adolescents (hereafter: "youth") present a global societal challenge (McGorry et al., 2024). Most mental disorders have their onset between 12 and 25 years of age (Uhlhaas et al., 2023) with a substantial chronification into adulthood (Caspi et al., 2020). In addition, mental disorders frequently co-occur in youth (Merikangas et al., 2009). Importantly, youth with comorbid disorders or comorbid subclinical symptoms show greater symptom severity, functional impairment, and treatment resistance compared to those who have isolated mental disorders (Balázs et al., 2013; Melton et al., 2016).

Psychotherapy is the gold standard for the treatment of mental disorders in youth, showing, on average, small to moderate effect sizes (Weisz et al., 2017; Wuthrich et al., 2023). Notably, despite a marked increase in the quantity and quality of psychotherapeutic treatment options across the past decades, the efficacy rate of youth psychotherapy has not increased (Weisz et al., 2019), emphasizing the need for innovative approaches. Mental health research and treatment have traditionally been organized around disorder-specific diagnostic categories. The aforementioned high rates of comorbidity, substantial heterogeneity within diagnostic categories, only moderate treatment effects, and evidence for common underlying processes across disorders have motivated a shift toward transdiagnostic approaches (Dalgeish et al., 2020). In the past two decades, research has flourished on transdiagnostic conceptualizations of psychopathology (Eaton et al., 2023; Rief et al., 2023), transdiagnostic risk factors and processes explaining the co-occurrence of youth mental health problems (Lynch et al., 2021; Wade et al., 2025), and transdiagnostic treatments (Schaeuffele et al., 2021; Temkin et al., 2018). These lines of research aim at promoting our understanding on the nature and treatment of psychopathology, which is particularly relevant to children and adolescents, for whom mental disorders are embedded in ongoing developmental processes (Russel & Gajos, 2020).

The integration of ecological momentary assessment (EMA) methods into transdiagnostic research offers one promising methodological approach to advance our understanding on the dynamic micro-processes through which psychopathology emerges and fluctuates in daily life (Wichers, 2014; Vize & Wright, 2024). In this methodological perspective paper, we first provide a brief introduction to transdiagnostic approaches and the application of EMA in youth psychopathology. We then discuss how integrating EMA into transdiagnostic research with youth may advance transdiagnostic conceptualization of psychopathology, increase our understanding of transdiagnostic processes, and inform the development and optimization of transdiagnostic treatments. We summarize recent developments in transdiagnostic EMA research and outline important steps to realize the potential of a transdiagnostic EMA perspective in research and practice with youth.

Rethinking youth psychopathology

Transdiagnostic conceptualizations

The most common transdiagnostic conceptualizations to psychopathology comprise the network approach to psychopathology (Borsboom, 2017), the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP; Kotov et al., 2017), the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC; Insel et al., 2010), and the Clinical Staging Model (McGorry et al., 2006). A detailed description of each of these approaches and corresponding empirical research lies beyond the scope of this paper, but several recent reviews provide systematic comparisons and discussions of their conceptual foundations, empirical support, and implications for clinical practice, mostly for adults (Eaton et al., 2023; Rief et al., 2023). In brief, the network theory and related theories (e.g. dynamic complex systems theory; Borsboom, 2017; Robinaugh et al., 2019) conceptualize mental disorders as maladaptive patterns arising from causally interacting symptoms (or more broadly, biopsychosocial and environmental variables), rather than seeing symptoms as indicators of a common latent cause. HiTOP is based on a data-driven approach and defines symptom clusters organized within broader hierarchical dimensions (Kotov et al., 2017). RDoC is a framework for conceptualizing mental disorders across core domains of neurobehavioral functioning considering various aspects of environment and units of analysis (e.g., genetics, physiology, self-report; Insel et al., 2010), and finally, Clinical Staging approaches combine a categorical and dimensional approach including different stages of disease progression (e.g., from asymptomatic/at risk to treatment resistance), with specific implications for the allocation of prevention and prognosis of mental disorders (Cosci & Fava, 2013; McGorry et al., 2006).

While the four approaches have developed rather independently from one another and differ in their conceptual foundations, they coincide on the basic premise that traditional categorical conceptualizations of mental disorders as formulated in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) and the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) do not fully capture the complexity of mental disorders. These approaches also offer different accounts of the emergence and co-occurrence of mental disorders, while underscoring the importance of examining transdiagnostic (vs. disorder-specific) processes (Eaton et al., 2023; Rief et al., 2023).

This shared emphasis provides a strong rationale for methodological approaches capable of capturing transdiagnostic processes as they unfold over time. However, much of the empirical research underlying transdiagnostic conceptualizations of psychopathology has relied on cross-sectional data and between-person differences in adults (Eaton et al., 2023), providing limited insight into the dynamic nature of psychopathology within youth over time (Vize & Wright, 2024). Moreover, although an increasing body of research has applied these approaches to youth populations (e.g., Capon et al., 2022; Forbes et al., 2025; Freichel et al., 2024; Häfeli et al., 2026; Hankin et al., 2026; Iorfino et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2026), further research is needed to examine whether and how the different transdiagnostic conceptualizations apply across developmental stages and individuals' developmental trajectories (Tackett & Hallquist, 2022; Shah et al., 2020).

Transdiagnostic processes

It is useful to distinguish transdiagnostic factors that characterize differences between individuals and those that characterize differences within individuals over time. The former includes environmental factors (e.g., early life adversity: Wade et al., 2022; socioeconomic status: Peverill et al., 2021), biological (e.g., genetic risk; Riglin et al., 2020), and psychological factors (e.g., temperament: Rettew et al., 2005; executive functioning: Yang et al., 2022) that can explain why some individuals are more vulnerable than others. These factors are rather stable over time (i.e., trait-like) and often referred to as transdiagnostic risk factors in the literature as they may increase the risk for the development or exacerbation of (youth) psychopathology (see Lynch et al., 2021 for review). These can be distinguished from transdiagnostic factors that lie within individuals and can fluctuate or change with time.

Such within-person factors may include experiences and behaviors (and on a broader level 'symptoms', but see Guðmundsdóttir et al., 2026) that are shared across disorders (e.g., insomnia, difficulties concentrating, irritability; Forbes et al., 2023). A transdiagnostic process, here, refers to a temporal interaction of experiences, behaviors, biological, or socioenvironmental factors that increases the risk for the development or contributes to the maintenance of several (classes of) mental disorders, rather than being specific to a single diagnostic category (Dalgleish et al., 2020; Mansell et al., 2008). Transdiagnostic processes unfold across multiple timescales and may shape psychopathology from immediate reactions to long-term developmental trajectories (Nolen-Hoeksema & Watkins, 2011). In this context, Ehrenreich-May and Chu (2014) emphasized that a key aim of transdiagnostic research should be to identify the smallest number of key mechanisms (i.e., shared causal transdiagnostic processes; see also Harvey et al., 2011) with the most explanatory power that qualify as modifiable targets for clinical intervention, while still more research is needed to address this aim (Dalgleish et al., 2020; Sauer-Zavala et al., 2017).

In one of the earliest seminal works on transdiagnostic processes, Harvey et al. (2004) proposed 13 cognitive and behavioral transdiagnostic processes across the five domains attention (e.g., selective attention), memory (e.g., recurrent memory), reasoning (e.g., emotional reasoning), thought (e.g., recurrent thinking), and behavior (e.g., experiential avoidance). For youth in particular, previous literature described five (Ehrenreich-May & Chu, 2014) up to 38 relevant psychological processes (Wade et al., 2025). Other suggested psychobiological processes relevant across mental disorders include stress responsivity (e.g., Conway et al., 2016; Turner et al., 2020), interoception (e.g. Mayer-Benarous et al., 2025; Murphy et al., 2017), and reward processing (e.g. Baskin-Sommers & Foti, 2015). One very frequently empirically examined transdiagnostic process is emotion regulation: it has been shown that difficulties in emotion regulation are present across a wide range of mental disorders in youth (Kraft et al., 2023) and adults (Clamor et al., 2026). Moreover, initial evidence from prospective longitudinal studies also supports the notion that difficulties in emotion regulation may play a causal role in the development of internalizing psychopathology (Klein et al., 2022; Murray et al., 2025) and improvements in emotion regulation co-occur with improvements in internalizing psychopathology during treatment (Daros et al., 2021; Easdale-Cheele et al., 2024). Nonetheless, more prospective and experimental studies are needed to unravel the potential causal role of emotion regulation (Cludius et al., 2020) and other suggested transdiagnostic processes across mental disorders (Lynch et al., 2025).

Importantly, a comprehensive overview and theoretical framework comprising the shared or distinct relations, the interplay, and the hierarchical organization of proposed transdiagnostic processes is currently lacking (Schäuffele et al., 2021), highlighting the need for a systematic review to synthesize the existing evidence. In addition, methodological aspects limit our current understanding of transdiagnostic processes. First, the majority of present research is based on instruments that ask youth and/or their caregivers to recall behaviors, cognitions, and feelings within a specified time frame (e.g., traditional questionnaires; Russell & Gajos, 2020). Self-reports based on such retrospective methods can be affected by selective memory recall and thus over-or underestimation for certain events or experiences, so-called recall bias (Bone et al., 2021; Pacheco-Romero et al., 2025). Hence, despite the valuable insights provided by retrospective self-or proxy reports, these methods rely on remembered experiences or beliefs about oneself, rather than lived "in-the-moment" experiences or behaviors and therefore lack ecological validity (Scharbert et al., 2025). Second, the existing evidence on transdiagnostic "processes" across multiple forms of psychopathology is mostly based on aggregated data of between-person differences in individuals' typical tendencies or perceived difficulties, which cannot be generalized to temporal within-person associations (Hamaker, 2012). For example, adolescents who generally report greater use of behavioral avoidance may also report higher levels of anxiety and depression compared to others, but this between-person association does not imply that adolescents experience greater anxiety and depression at moments of or following increased behavioral avoidance (i.e., within-person association); indeed, associations may differ substantially in magnitude or even direction across levels of analysis (Fisher et al., 2018).

Consequently, appropriate study designs and assessment methods need to be applied to elucidate how proposed transdiagnostic processes such as emotion regulation, stress responsivity, interoception, and reward processing (among others) unfold dynamically within individuals and across mental disorders. Longitudinal research spanning weeks or months can capture longer-term processes and their associations with psychopathology, whereas examining subjective experiences and behaviors over periods of minutes and hours allows their short-term dynamics to be studied in everyday life. This micro-level perspective may help identify the smallest "building blocks" through which psychopathology emerges and fluctuates (Wichers et al., 2014).

Transdiagnostic treatments

A shift from disorder-specific treatments, which target symptoms and mechanisms characteristic of individual mental disorders (e.g., exposure-based CBT for social anxiety disorder) to transdiagnostic treatments is currently underway, emphasizing commonalities and shared processes rather than differences between disorders (Schäuffele et al., 2021; Temkin et al., 2018). Transdiagnostic treatments aim to treat multiple disorders or mental health problems using a common set of techniques or interventions (Schäuffele et al., 2021).

While most of these treatments were developed for adults, some have been adapted or developed specifically for youth. One of the earliest explicit transdiagnostic interventions for youth was the extension of the Unified Protocol for the Treatment of Emotional Disorders (UP, Barlow et al., 2004) to youth which aims to target transdiagnostic dimensions such as emotion awareness, cognitive reappraisal, tolerance and regulation of (negative) emotions through cognitive behavioral techniques (Ehrenreich et al., 2009; Ehrenreich-May et al., 2018). It is assumed that transdiagnostic treatments can more flexibly and efficiently address multiple clinical problems and have more robust, generalizable outcomes by targeting core processes (Ehrenreich-May & Chu, 2014). There has been mounting evidence for the efficacy of the UP in Children and Adolescents (UP-A/C) for improving symptoms of anxiety and depression, although more research is needed to substantiate the proposed advantages over disorder-specific treatments and to empirically corroborate the proposed processes of change underlying symptom improvement, such as changes in emotion regulation (Carlucci et al., 2021; Schäpermeier et al., 2025). In other transdiagnostic approaches, rather than emphasizing the identification of underlying processes common to several mental disorders, the focus lies on common elements interventions that are highly overlapping across evidence-based psychological treatments. For instance, the Modular Approach to Therapy for Children (MATCH, Chorpita & Weisz, 2009), aims to select evidence-based treatment modules for individual patients based on clinical judgement, case formulation, patientcentered data assessments, or a combination thereof (Schäuffele et al., 2021).

Despite these advances, and although transdiagnostic interventions are often based on assumptions about common underlying processes, process-based research examining whether the proposed processes actually account for treatment effects remains scarce (Schäpermeyer et al., 2025). Information capturing the expression and variability of experiences and behaviors in everyday life could be used to evaluate whether changes in targeted processes potentially account for subsequent improvements or deteriorations across psychotherapy. Moreover, such information could also be a valuable additional source to help clinician's identify individualized treatment targets and to inform the selection or sequencing of treatment modules (Li et al., 2024).

Ecological momentary assessment (EMA) and transdiagnostic research in youth

Despite promising developments in transdiagnostic research across the past decades, important challenges remain, including the dynamic expression of psychopathology across time and developmental stages in youth, accounting for individual differences in symptom expression, understanding how transdiagnostic processes unfold dynamically and within individuals in everyday life, and identifying which of these represent meaningful and changeable treatment targets (Vize & Wright, 2023; Dalgleish et al., 2020). We argue that EMA offers a valuable complementary approach to addressing these challenges.

What is EMA and what needs to be considered with youth?

EMAfoot_0 involves the repeated acquisition of data in everyday life typically via software applications on mobile devices. EMA therefore minimizes recall bias, maximizes ecological validity, and allows the study of the occurrence, fluctuations, and dynamic interactions of emotions, thoughts, and behaviors in the natural environment and across everyday situations (Shiffman et al., 2008; Trull & Ebner-Priemer, 2013).

As detailed in previous recommendations (e.g. Kaurin et al., 2023; Löchner et al., 2026; Mehl et al., 2014; Myin-Germeys & Kuppens, 2022) and as can be seen in Figure 1 (adapted from Kaurin et al., 2023), the duration of the EMA period (i.e., number of days), the sampling schedule (number and timing of prompts per day), and the content (i.e., constructs of interest, number of items per prompt) need to be carefully planned. The sampling schedule should be determined by the (expected) temporal characteristics of the phenomena and processes of interest (e.g., their frequency of occurrence, rate of change, and temporal relations). Systematic reviews have summarized the existing studies using EMA with youth with and without mental disorders and show that this method has been implemented successfully and safely (Baltasar-Tello et al., 2018; Heron et al., 2017; Thunnissen et al., 2022; van Roekel et al., 2019; Wen et al., 2017). Several aspects need to be considered when implementing EMA, particularly in youth populations. First, protocols should minimize participant burden and facilitate compliance.

Therefore, prompts should be brief and fast to respond (Eisele et al., 2022) and sampling schedules should be compatible with adolescents' daily routines (e.g., awakening, bedtime, school hours). Incentives such as monetary compensation, gamification, or feedback on one's own data may support participants' motivation and compliance ( Bülow et al., 2025; van Roekel et al., 2019). Moreover, passive sensing through smartphones or wearable devices offers a potential means of complementing self-reported data with behavioral or physiological information collected automatically in a low-burden manner (Leijse et al., 2026). Hardware and software choices require careful consideration of privacy and safety.

EMA software and study procedures should comply with applicable data protection regulations (e.g., General Data Protection Regulation in the European Union) and include age-appropriate, legally compliant procedures for obtaining informed consent and assent (Levesque, 2016). In addition, potential reactivity to repeated assessments (i.e., changes in responding resulting from the assessments themselves) should be monitored, as it may threaten internal validity (Gieles et al., 2025). In clinical populations, repeated assessment also raises safety considerations, particularly when EMA assesses potentially acute risks such as suicidality. Although perceived burden and negative emotional reactions to repeated assessments of suicidal ideation seems to be low in general, a minority of individuals has reported increased negative feelings or suicidal thoughts in previous studies (Kivelä et al., 2022; Lühr et al., 2026). Therefore, safety protocols should be installed that may include clearly defined safety measures for responding to concerning reports, such as provision of emergency contacts or real-time alerts to researchers or clinicians (Kivelä et al., 2022). Next, developmental differences represent a further important aspect. Youth differ substantially in cognitive abilities, reading comprehension, and capacity to report on internal experiences, making age-appropriate measurement particularly important (Wolley et al., 2004).

Developing developmentally appropriate items requires careful pre-testing and evaluation with the target population (Wolley et al., 2004; see also Zurbriggen et al., 2026 regarding EMA in youth with intellectual disabilities). For younger participants or constructs which benefit from a multi-informant perspective (e.g., parent-child interactions), caregiver reports via EMA may provide a valuable complementary perspective (e.g., Slaughter et al., 2019; Gadassi-Pollack et al., 2026). From a methodological perspective, EMA measures of specific psychological constructs often comprise single or several few items and this brevity, together with the dynamic nature inherent to the constructs and their assessment in real life presents unique challenges to the psychometric evaluation of reliability and validity (Löchner et al., 2026). While a previous review of EMA studies in psychopathology research pointed out that most included studies did not provide information on the psychometric properties of their items (Trull & Ebner-Priemer, 2020), the field has made important methodological advances in recent years. These include recommendations for the development and evaluation of EMA items (Eisele et al., 2025; Siepe et al., 2025), broader guidance on evaluating the reliability and validity of EMA measures in mental health research (Löchner et al., 2026; Wright & Zimmermann, 2019), and general reporting standards (Trull & Ebner-Priemer 2020; Dao et al., 2021). In parallel, efforts such as the ESM Item Repository (https://esmitemrepositoryinfo.com/; Kirtley et al., 2020) provide openly accessible resources for identifying suitable EMA items for both adults and youth. Recent studies have begun to systematically evaluate the reliability and validity of youth-specific EMA measures, including momentary anxiety (Byrne et al., 2023), irritability (Hansen et al., 2025), rumination (Webb et al., 2025), and well-being (Buzzi et al., 2025; Kral et al., 2026). Together, these studies suggest an emerging shift toward more systematic attention to the measurement quality and item standardization of EMA research. Finally, EMA data can be analysed at different levels depending on the research questions. When data from multiple individuals are collected, associations within individuals, between individuals, and whether within-person associations differ systematically between individuals can be examined. Such analyses require statistical techniques that account for the nested structure of EMA data (e.g., observations nested within individuals), and interested readers are referred to Bolger and Laurenceau (2013) for further guidance (see also Revol et al., 2024 for data preprocessing). Furthermore, EMA in individual patients can be used to identify person-specific ('idiographic') patterns of experiences and behaviors with a high temporal resolution and thereby inform clinical conceptualization and practice (Fisher et al., 2016; Mink et al., 2025; Piccirillo et al., 2026; Piccirillo et al., 2019). Since research has not yet established a consensus on how individual treatment targets can be most reliably identified (e.g., Bastiaansen et al., 2020), further methodological work is needed to address this topic.

Importantly, although EMA can characterize temporal dynamics, observational intensive longitudinal data are neither necessary nor sufficient to establish causal effects; rather causal inference requires a clearly specified causal question and a study design that allows causal effects to be distinguished from alternative explanations (Rohrer & Murayama, 2023). Randomized controlled trials and laboratory experiments can provide such evidence by experimentally manipulating the factor(s) of interest, while integrating EMA can reveal whether and how relevant findings on transdiagnostic processes generalize to within-person associations in naturalistic contexts (Crawford et al., 2022; Miller-Chagnon et al., 2024). In addition, experimental manipulation can be embedded directly within intensive longitudinal designs. Such 'microrandomized trials' can provide tests of within-person causal effects (Klasnja et al., 2015; Schmiedek & Neubauer, 2020; Neubauer et al., 2025).

The contribution of EMA for transdiagnostic approaches

Overall, EMA offers a unique opportunity to complement established transdiagnostic approaches by capturing the micro-level, within-person dynamics through which psychopathology unfolds in everyday life (Wichers, 2014). Combining traditional assessment methods in transdiagnostic research (e.g., questionnaires, clinical interviews, experimental tasks, biological assessments) with EMA can capture complementary facets of phenomena across levels of analysis and help link transdiagnostic processes identified in laboratory or longer-term longitudinal research to their temporal and contextual expression in everyday life. In youth, this may be particularly valuable because ongoing developmental changes may contribute to greater variability in emotions and cognitions (Bailen et al., 2018; Reitsema et al., 2021). For instance, for network approaches, EMA provides a high temporal resolution of psychopathology-relevant experiences and behaviors such as negative affect, irritability, rumination, avoidance, and social withdrawal, and allows to examine whether these dynamically influence one another (e.g., Roefs et al., 2022; Wichers, 2014). For HiTOP, EMA can facilitate investigation of how lower-level symptoms and processes fluctuate in daily life and how these dynamics vary across youths (Vize & Wright, 2024). For RDoC, EMA may capture the real-world expression of domains (e.g., negative/positive valence systems, social processes, arousal systems), particularly when combined with behavioral or physiological measures (Naim et al., 2021; Raugh et al., 2019). EMA may help clarify whether proposed transdiagnostic processes are consistently involved across mental disorders and whether their role varies across individuals, developmental stages, and contexts. This is particularly relevant to developmental psychopathology, where the same underlying processes may contribute to different outcomes across development and where psychopathology can follow heterogeneous trajectories (Nolen-Hoeksema & Watkins, 2011; Tackett & Hallquist, 2022). Combined with longer-term longitudinal designs, measurementburst approaches (i.e., repeated periods of EMA embedded within longer-term follow-ups), can link fine-grained within-person dynamics to longer-term intraindividual change (Chiang & Lam, 2020; Sliwinski, 2008). Such designs may therefore provide a promising methodological approach for studying transdiagnostic processes as they unfold across multiple timescales which is potentially relevant to disease progression and extension in clinical staging models (Haas et al., 2025; Sawrikar et al., 2022; Shah et al., 2020). Finally, EMA can benefit transdiagnostic treatments by facilitating the selection of individualized and contextually sensitive interventions and by monitoring processes of change in daily life (Fisher et al., 2016; Mink et al., 2025), although future research will need to establish how EMA-derived information can be most effectively integrated into treatment decision-making.

In addition, transdiagnostic treatments may benefit from including ecological momentary interventions (EMIs; Myin-Germeys et al., 2016). Such interventions can provide digitally delivered 'micro-interventions' in youth's daily life and even use information about individuals' momentary states or contexts to deliver support at potentially relevant moments ('just-in-time' EMIs; Guan et al., 2026; Nahum-Shani et al., 2018). Recent studies with adults have begun to examine transdiagnostic just-in-time EMIs using microrandomized trials, yielding promising initial evidence while highlighting the need for further research (Fiß et al., 2026; Laure et al., 2025).

Transdiagnostic EMA in youth: does it happen already?

Previous EMA research into youth psychopathology has some shortcomings that prevent a transdiagnostic perspective on youth psychopathology.

First, the majority of EMA studies focused on a narrow range of constructs. Second, previous EMA studies have focused mostly on youth within specific diagnostic categories including mood disorders, anxiety disorders, eating disorders, and ADHD (see Thunnissen et al., 2022). Although comorbidity has been reported in several of these studies, observed phenomena from daily life were not examined or discussed as potentially relevant in relation to comorbidity. In contrast, recent EMA studies in samples of late adolescents and young adults from the general population found dynamics of negative affect (e.g., variability and persistence of emotions) (Zhu et al., 2024), repetitive negative thinking (i.e., recurrent thinking process focused on negative content) (Funk et al., 2025), and stress responsivity (Speyer et al., 2023; Vaessen et al., 2017) as correlates or predictors for a range of (co-occurring) psychopathology, including depressive, anxiety, aggression, and ADHD symptoms. It remains to be examined whether these findings generalize to younger age groups with full-blown (comorbid) mental disorders.

Third, from an analytical perspective, many prior EMA studies in youth psychopathology have primarily adopted a descriptive focus in that they merely investigated temporal fluctuations or frequencies of occurrences of symptoms without systematically linking symptoms to contextual antecedents or consequences (Thunnissen et al., 2022). A purely descriptive focus overlooks EMA's unique potential in explaining psychopathology as it unfolds in daily life (Vize & Wright, 2024). Specifically, although the analysis of EMA data per se does not provide causal information, it can nonetheless be informative with regard to potential precursors and consequences of symptoms and thereby provide initial evidence for the functional role of transdiagnostic processes in youth psychopathology (Russell & Gajos, 2020; Vize & Wright, 2024). For instance, an interpersonal conflict (context) may be followed by momentarily increased negative affect, which, in turn, could increase the likelihood for non-suicidal self-injury in some adolescents and higher depressive mood in others (outcomes). Person-level factors (e.g. internalizing symptomatology, personality facets) could potentially explain such interindividual differences in the within-person processes.

Statistical techniques are available to model such complex interrelations across the momentand person-level (e.g., multilevel regression, multilevel structural equation modelling ; Bolger & Laurenceau, 2013; Briganti et al., 2024; Sadikaj et al., 2021). Yet, to date, only few studies have started to unravel such transdiagnostic processes using EMA and appropriate statistical techniques in youth with (comorbid) mental health problems (Thunnissen et al., 2022; see f.ex. Byrd et al., 2025; Tseng et al., 2023 for exceptions).

Fourth, EMA with youth has been applied mostly in research contexts, with very little translation into clinical practice (Kooiman et al., 2023). Yet integrating EMA into routine assessment could bridge the science-practice gap by providing individualized, contextsensitive insights that inform treatment planning (Hofmann & Hayes, 2024). A transdiagnostic EMA approach is particularly valuable in this regard, as it enables clinicians to monitor experiences and behaviors together with their situational embeddings across diagnostic boundaries, thereby avoiding the need to design separate EMA protocols for each disorder and providing a more efficient and clinically relevant picture of patients' difficulties.

This process-based information can highlight within-person processes that maintain comorbid mental problems, guide treatment selection, and help tailor interventions to the patient's needs (Hofmann & Hayes, 2024).

There is a lack of comprehensive EMA tools that capture key transdiagnostic experiences and behaviors in the daily lives of youth with psychopathology. Most existing studies are descriptive, overlooking EMA's unique potential to reveal how psychopathology unfolds in real time. A transdiagnostic EMA approach can monitor processes across diagnostic boundaries, and we argue that its adoption is a crucial next step for improving understanding and treatment of youth psychopathology.

Initiating transdiagnostic EMA: Current developments

Several large-scale projects have developed and evaluated transdiagnostic EMA tools, although the vast majority of research has focused on adults. For example, building on the network theory of psychopathology (Borsboom, 2017) Other projects have applied a daily diary approach (i.e., assessments once per day typically asking participants to retrospectively report on their experiences over the course of the day). For instance, Wright et al. (2025) developed and examined a HiTOP-informed transdiagnostic EMA battery spanning 27 facets (across 81 items) of adult psychopathology (see also Zimmermann et al., 2019). Similarly, Zheng and Zheng (2025) applied a daily diary study in adolescents (aged 12 -17 years) and young adults (aged 17 -29 years) from the general population, finding that a general p-factor was stable across individuals, while specific symptom dimensions showed limited reliability over time. EMA items in this study were adapted from the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (Goodman, 1997) with additional items capturing inattention/hyperactivity, panic disorder symptoms, daily stressors, neuroticism, emotion regulation (difficulties), and impulsivity. In another study in young adults (aged 21 -24 years), Schreuder et al. (2020) demonstrated the feasibility and validity of a 58item daily diary tool. Included items were derived from studies or existing (retrospective) transdiagnostic questionnaires that covered multiple diagnostic categories and were expected to vary from day to day (Schreuder et al., 2020).

Finally, several researchers directly integrated EMA into clinical practice to support case conceptualization, mostly in adults (Bos et al., 2022; Hall et al., 2025; Klipstein et al., 2023; Reininghaus et al., 2024; Scholten et al., 2024). In one previous feasibility study with 13-to 21 year olds in remission of anxiety or depression, Kooiman et al. (2023) included individual EMA-derived networks of affect (e.g., anxious, sad, angry, positive affect) and behaviors (e.g., behavioral avoidance, activity investment) together with data from diagnostic interviews and questionnaires into a multi-modal data-driven personalization approach. The different data sources provided complementary information for treatment selection and a basis for shared decision-making. Yet, efficacy comparisons of EMA-derived personalization to the treatment of youth is outstanding. Although most authors did not explicitly position their work as transdiagnostic research, they assessed constructs relevant across mental disorders, including mood/affect, social interactions, sleep, and emotion regulation, and some included customizable items tailored to patient needs (e.g., Bos et al., 2022; Scholten et al., 2024), highlighting the potential of EMA to combine standardized assessment with personalization.

Taken together, the above-mentioned studies illustrate the growing interest in applying EMA to capture experiences and behaviors across mental health problems in daily life and to inform personalized assessment and treatment recommendations. However, there is currently a focus on (young) adult populations. Existing measurements for adults cannot be simply applied to youth populations due to developmental differences in cognitive, emotional, and social functioning, as well as variations in symptom presentation and expression across age groups (Steinberg, 2005). Based on current recommendations for applying EMA in mental health research (Löchner et al., 2026) and for integrating transdiagnostic and EMA practices specifically (Vize & Wright, 2024), we here outline key steps for developing, evaluating, and implementing a transdiagnostic EMA tool for youth.

Development of a transdiagnostic EMA tool for youth

A transdiagnostic EMA tool for youth should be able to assess theoretically and empirically supported experiences and behaviors that fluctuate from moment-to-moment or day-to-day and are relevant across youth mental disorders. Even in the absence of conclusive evidence regarding causal roles, initial item sets can be derived from the literature and refined through expert consensus. To ensure content validity, comprehensiveness, comprehensibility, and therapeutic relevance, input from stakeholders, including youth with psychopathology and clinical professionals, is essential (Bülow et al., 2025; Kassam-Adams et al., 2015).

Feasibility and acceptability should be evaluated, optimally using a combination of quantitative and qualitative data to ensure greater depth of information (Aschbrenner et al., 2022), while monitoring potential negative effects and reactivity. Psychometric properties (i.e., item performance, reliability, validity) and measurement characteristics across age, gender, socioeconomic status, and clinical presentations should be examined (Löchner et al., 2026; Siepe et al., 2025; Wright & Zimmermann, 2019; see also 'What is EMA and what needs to be considered with youth?').

Identification of perceived benefits and barriers to implementation in research and clinical practice

Identifying benefits and barriers to integrating EMA into research and practice routines from the perspectives of youth, their caregivers, and clinicians will facilitate practical uptake and guide implementation. As outlined earlier, EMA with youth requires careful planning regarding assessment duration, sampling schedules, and content with the aim at maximizing compliance and minimizing participant burden (see Figure 1). Moreover, the potential benefits for youth in applying the EMA tool, such as fostering greater self-insight (Bülow et al., 2025) and for clinicians regarding case conceptualization and treatment planning should be evaluated (e.g., Hall et al., 2025; Scholten et al., 2022). This may include visualization and feedback of EMA data collected, which might serve psychoeducational purposes and facilitate shared decision making between youth, caregivers, and clinicians (Bosch et al., 2024; Hall et al., 2024). By incorporating stakeholder perspectives regarding these considerations, EMA tools can be optimized for feasibility, acceptability, and clinical utility, supporting their effective integration into routine practice (Vize & Wright, 2024).

Building an evidence base

An important next step is to establish and empirically examine the added value of the transdiagnostic EMA tool beyond established routine assessments and questionnaires traditionally applied in clinical research and practice (Vize & Wright, 2024). From a research perspective, this may include recruiting youth presenting with a broad range of (comorbid) internalizing and externalizing psychopathology, applying a multimethod approach, combining complementary assessment methods, including EMA, and systematically examining research questions such as (1) how transdiagnostic conceptualizations of psychopathology and previously identified transdiagnostic processes are reflected across different assessment methods, (2) how these processes operate at the micro-level and across developmental stages, and (3) how these processes change in response to transdiagnostic treatments. Of note, EMA studies are resource-intensive for both researchers and young participants.

Adhering to established reporting guidelines (e.g., Dao et al., 2021) and implementing open science practices, such as preregistration (Kirtley et al., 2021) and sharing anonymized data and analysis code, can not only increase replicability and reproducibility of findings, but facilitate the reuse and synthesis of EMA data across studies (Siepe et al., 2026). This can maximize the scientific value of EMA data and promote cumulative knowledge (Chiang & Lam, 2020).

We are currently implementing these steps in an ongoing project to develop, evaluate, and refine a transdiagnostic EMA tool for youth psychopathology in daily life (Feneberg et al., 2025.; https://osf.io/86rgb/). In line with the above-mentioned recommendations, this project aims to deliver a youth-tailored transdiagnostic EMA tool that captures dynamic transdiagnostic processes in daily life and advances both transdiagnostic research and clinical practice in youth psychopathology.

Conclusion

Overall, research on transdiagnostic frameworks, processes, and treatments in youth with mental disorders would greatly benefit from incorporating a dynamic and developmentally sensitive within-person perspective. Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA) provides a valuable methodological tool for realizing this perspective. However, EMA research in youth has largely neglected a transdiagnostic perspective. We therefore outlined which aspects need to be considered when implementing EMA into transdiagnostic research alongside current developments on transdiagnostic EMA tools. We provided recommendations on steps that should be taken to advance a transdiagnostic EMA perspective in youth and highlighted potential research questions concerning how transdiagnostic processes are reflected across assessment methods, unfold at the micro-level and across development, and change in response to transdiagnostic treatments. To conclude, integrating EMA within transdiagnostic research and clinical practice holds considerable promise for advancing our understanding of within-person processes that are shared across or differ between youth mental disorders, while supporting the development of more personalized transdiagnostic treatments (Hofmann & Hayes, 2024; Sauer-Zavala et al., 2017).
