Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Autistic people who are racially minoritised, are from areas of high economic deprivation, have co-occurring learning disabilities, or are non-or minimally-speaking are frequently excluded from autism research. This leaves critical gaps in evidence for service development. We addressed this by developing and evaluating "Participatory Creative Systems Mapping (PCSM)" -an emerging practice that combines Community-Based Participatory Research, Participatory Systems Mapping, and creative methods. The novelty arises from combining these approaches to systemically reduce barriers to underserved autistic communities' participation and engagement in research.

We partnered with disability-focused and community organisations, and an autistic steering group to develop and deliver accessible workshops. These workshops incorporated persona development, user journey mapping, storyboarding, and live artist visualisation to translate underserved autistic peoples' experiences of primary healthcare into system-level insights.

Thirty-one participants (including autistic adults and parents of autistic people) took part across four individual workshops. The participant cohort included representation from Somali and Black African communities (35.5%), areas of high neighbourhood deprivation, and reported a variety of methods for how they usually communicate. Autistic people with learning disabilities were represented, but this was not consistently self-reported by participants. Evaluation indicated high satisfaction, with all respondents who provided feedback about sharing their ideas and experiences reporting that they felt able to do so in the workshops. As proof of concept for PCSM as a method, we successfully produced a causal loop diagram reflected on by the steering group -the "Misalignment-Invalidation Cycle", illustrating how communication barriers and clinician invalidation generate reinforcing loops of misunderstanding and disengagement from healthcare.

Why was this program developed?

Autistic people are more likely to have health problems. It can be hard for autistic people to get good healthcare. Many autistic people are not included in research, for instance, autistic people from diverse backgrounds, particularly those from racial minority communities, deprived areas, and people with learning disabilities or communication differences. This program was developed to help these groups take part in healthcare research.

We created a new program that combines community-based work with creative activities to explore healthcare experiences. We designed this to be accessible for people with different communication styles, language backgrounds and support needs. Working with Sheffield Voices (a self-advocacy group for autistic and learning-disabled people) and a steering group of six autistic adults from diverse backgrounds, we designed a workshop. The workshop was delivered three times in partnership with different local community groups, and a fourth time with the steering group. Participants created an autistic character and used storyboards to follow the character's journey from feeling unwell to seeing a doctor. An artist drew these as "loops", which we used to discuss how problems (like a doctor not understanding autism) connect and feed into other issues.

We evaluated three main things. First, to check whether we reached autistic people usually left out of research, we asked participants to tell us about their backgrounds by writing about or drawing themselves. Second, participants told us if they felt heard during the workshops by placing feedback tokens into postboxes. Third, to show that participants could map their healthcare experiences, we combined the artists' drawings from across the workshops into a larger, more diverse group of autistic people to see if these problems are the same across the country.

This program provides a clear guide for including diverse autistic communities in healthcare research. By sharing findings with healthcare professionals and policymakers, we can recommend ways to improve healthcare for autistic people.

We successfully reached participants who are usually left out of research; 31 autistic adults or their parents from diverse backgrounds took part, with many from Somali and Black African communities and areas of high deprivation. Participants also reported a range of different ways they usually communicate. Participants felt heard; all those who gave feedback reported being able to share their ideas and experiences in the workshop. The program could map autistic healthcare experiences; the steering group's "loop" showed how communication differences can lead to doctors dismissing their worries, which makes an appointment feel like a waste of time, causing them anxiety and not wanting to go in the future.

The project was limited to South Yorkshire, so findings may not be the same elsewhere.

Because we relied on workshop participants to provide their own demographic information, we may not have captured how many had a learning disability. Participatory Systems

Mapping (PSM) brings together stakeholders with varied knowledge and perspectives to create a visual representation of a complex system, exploring and documenting perceived causal relations between its elements. 39 When co-led with autistic people, this participatory approach prioritises lived experience, aligning with authentic co-production. 40, 41 Crucially, this approach illuminates heterogeneity across autistic people by showing how individual differences and intersecting marginalisations interact with system-level factors to influence diverse healthcare experiences. 25 By collaboratively mapping these complexities, stakeholders can identify "leverage points" where interventions may create far-reaching positive impacts that benefit the widest range of needs. Establishing community priorities before interventions are developed or scaled is a critical first step in science. 42, 43 While PSM is well-established in other fields such as design, 44 systems perspectives remain underutilised in autism research. 32, 45 This gap exists despite excellent examples of autistic co-production in healthcare, 46, 47 and increasing calls for system-level approaches to address health inequalities. 45, 48 However, the accessibility of PSM cannot be assumed.

Mapping complex systems is cognitively demanding, can privilege certain communication styles, and presents significant accessibility challenges for those with communication differences, co-occurring learning disability, or for whom the dominant language of research or service delivery is not their first. Combined with pre-existing distrust of services among marginalised groups 49 , these barriers around PSM may deter autistic people from engaging at all. As a result, the potential of PSM to capture the lived complexity of underserved autistic people's healthcare experiences has remained largely unrealised. Existing approaches within autism research address either participation or systems-level complexity, but not both simultaneously. This is the gap the present study directly addresses through our Participatory Creative Systems Mapping (PCSM) approach. We argue that bringing PSM together with creative methods and a Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR) framework does not merely add accessibility as a supplementary feature, but fundamentally transforms what PSM can do and who can do it. Creative methods, specifically storyboarding and user journey mapping, have been used successfully with autistic people, 50, 51 and in healthcare intervention development. 52 These methods offer flexible, multimodal pathways for sharing experiences and facilitating understanding, thereby challenging traditional assumptions about communication and participation in research. 53, 54 Embedded within a co-production structure that attends to capacity, biography, and access needs, they create the conditions for underserved autistic people to engage meaningfully with systems thinking. No single component of this practice is novel in isolation. The innovation lies in their deliberate combination, designed from the outset to reduce barriers to participation as a structural feature of the method.

The overarching aim of this emerging practices paper is to outline the development and provide a methodological evaluation of PCSM as a transferable approach for engaging underserved autistic people in system-level inquiry. We address this by: 1.

Assessing whether underserved autistic communities (specifically autistic adults and parents of autistic people), as defined by the National Institute for Health Research (NIHR) INCLUDE guidance, 55 could be successfully reached and engaged; 2. Evaluating whether underserved autistic communities felt able to meaningfully contribute their expertise; and 3. Demonstrating how the healthcare experiences of underserved autistic communities can be translated into systems-level insights.

Workshop Methodology

The overall goal of the workshops was to translate the healthcare experiences of underserved autistic communities into systems-level insights by producing Causal Loop Diagrams (CLD) with each group. CLDs are a core tool in systems thinking that go beyond listing problems to articulating why those problems persist by mapping the causal relationships and feedback dynamics that maintain or worsen a system's current state. 38, 39 Unlike linear representations (e.g., A → B → C), CLDs capture circular causality, in which a change in one part of the system triggers a chain of effects that ultimately feeds back to influence where it began. 64, 65 These feedback loops are classified as either reinforcing, where change compounds in the same direction (for example, inaccessible healthcare environments mean that autistic patients are less likely to attend, leading to a lower likelihood of disclosure and recording of needs, therefore fewer adaptations, and continued inaccessibility) or balancing, where forces resist or stabilise change to maintain the status quo (e.g., offering longer appointments to improve accessibility, but this increases backlog and wait times, thus leading to less available appointments, reverting the system to its original state). See Figure 2 for a visualisation of these examples. Feedback loops therefore help explain not only the structure of a system but also why particular patterns of experience are sustained over time.

[INSERT FIGURE 2] By making causal relationships and feedback dynamics visible and discussable, CLDs serve as powerful analytical and design tools. They allow researchers, practitioners, and community members alike to identify leverage points: specific areas within the system where a targeted intervention is most likely to produce meaningful, lasting benefits. This avoids shifting problems elsewhere or generating unintended consequences. 38, 39 For example, in the balancing feedback loop, this might involve shifting performance metrics from "volume of patients seen" to "quality of patient outcomes".

To generate these CLDs, we used our PCSM approach to gather perspectives from four distinct groups, with three workshops held in community settings and one with the steering group, producing multiple views of the same system. Each workshop had at least four facilitators (3 researchers or academics and 1 co-lead from Sheffield Voices), as well as support from the community group organisers. We generally aimed to recruit 12 participants per workshop, in line with recommendations, 66 but group size was flexible based on interest and the size of community groups. Workshops were structured into two distinct activity phases of approximately one hour, separated by a one-hour structured lunch break. Creative methods were integrated throughout, as described below. Part 1. User Journey Mapping and Storyboarding

A foundational requirement in systems research with underserved communities is creating conditions where people can contribute meaningfully to abstract, analytical tasks.

Existing approaches to participatory systems mapping typically begin with an open discussion of problems and barriers. This format is highly likely to exclude people who find unstructured verbal participation difficult, or who carry a history of trauma and marginalisation from sharing their experiences with services and not being believed, helped, or taken seriously. Our approach addressed this via user journey mapping, which involves systematically tracing a person's experience through a service from start to finish. 68 This method is established in design and service improvement contexts but has rarely been integrated into participatory systems research, and to our knowledge, has not previously been combined with systems mapping in autism research. Its novelty here lies not in the technique itself but in its function: user-journey mapping served as a structured, lowthreshold bridge between lived experience and systems-level analysis, translating what participants know into a form which could be directly mapped onto causal relationships between system components.

During the first hour of the workshop, participants used this approach to trace an autistic personas experience of primary healthcare. Personas are fictional composite characters created to represent a particular group's interests and needs. 69 This approach allowed participants to discuss how they and others in their community interact with healthcare systems, encouraging them to think beyond their own experience to those who are similar. It also provided a mechanism to share these insights without the need to focus on or disclose sensitive or difficult personal experiences. 13, 17, 48 Participants developed the persona collaboratively at the start of the session, from an outline provided by the research team (name, age, gender, sexuality, neurotype, ethnicity, where they live, and values). They added details informed by their lived experience and those of their community, guided by starter sentence prompts (e.g., "… is important to them"). See Supplemental Figure 1 for an example of a developed persona. The persona was then presented with a non-urgent healthrelated scenario describing an itchy rash that has not gone away. We deliberately selected this as a relatable and routine health issue to keep the focus on primary care and avoid potential distress from discussing a more severe or traumatic medical event. This was endorsed by the steering group.

User journey mapping was conducted with the persona via a storyboarding activity, providing a creative visual and narrative element to the sequence of events (see Supplemental Figure 2). 52, 69 The storyboard was presented as a matrix, with rows representing the chronological healthcare journey across five distinct steps: 1.

Realising something is wrong; 2. Booking an appointment; 3. Travelling to the surgery; 4. Waiting for the appointment at the surgery; and 5. Seeing and talking to the doctor.

Columns of the storyboard template were aligned with the COM-B framework for behaviour change, 70 which posits that behaviour is generated by the interaction of Capability (psychological and physical capacity), Opportunity (external factors making behaviour possible or prompting it), and Motivation (brain processes that energise and direct behaviour). This framework was chosen because navigating a healthcare system involves a complex sequence of healthcare-seeking behaviours, and COM-B ensured we captured the full range of factors driving those actions. Crucially, COM-B was not used to suggest modifications or interventions to behaviours, but served as a taxonomy to untangle individual-level factors (Capability and Motivation) from systemic barriers (Opportunity). For each of the five healthcare journey steps, we operationalised the COM-B components into plain language, prompting participants to identify: • Goals: What are they (i.e., the persona) trying to achieve?

• Barriers: What made it difficult for them?

• Facilitators: What helped them?

• Emotions: How did they feel?

Participants used various media to populate the storyboard, including sticky notes, stickers, and drawings, alongside verbal contributions which were scribed by the researchers. The research team actively engaged in the workshops, exploring and clarifying the intended meaning of all storyboard additions with participants. Two designated researchers took notes during these interactions in a shared online document, separating out the healthcare journey steps and COM-B components. These notes digitally captured all media added and discussed, forming the raw data used to map causal relationships.

Following the storyboarding, participants voted on which step of the healthcare journey they wanted to explore in more detail by placing stickers directly onto the storyboard. Given the limited time of the workshop, the subsequent activity would focus exclusively on this prioritised stage where participants explored the causal relationships between components for that specific part of the system.

Part 2. Mapping Causal Relationships

The central methodological challenge of the workshops was bridging the gap between accessible creative participation and the technical demands of constructing a CLD.

Building a systems map from scratch is cognitively demanding, time-consuming, and typically requires prior familiarity with systems thinking. 71 To make rigorous CLD construction feasible within a single workshop session, without shifting the cognitive burden onto participants, we used a Large Language Model (LLM) and a live artist in distinct, sequential roles. Neither replaced participant input: both served as tools for translating what participants had already contributed into a form the group could interrogate, challenge, and refine.

i. LLM -Organising participant data into provisional feedback loops

During the one-hour structured break, the research team collated the digital notes from the storyboarding activity, which captured participants' contributions as described above, into a structured text prompt. The prompt instructed the LLM to reorganise these contributions into example feedback loops and plain-language summaries. We processed this using Google Gemini, 72 specifically because the University of Sheffield's institutional data governance requirements ensured that no information entered would be stored or used to train the model. Crucially, the LLM introduced no new content and acted purely as an administrative tool to rapidly structure what participants had already produced into a provisional framework, showing how their identified experiences might connect causally within the system. We explicitly framed and communicated this process as a starting point for discussion, not a finished or authoritative representation of participants' experiences. The full prompt and an example output are available in Supplemental Text 2 and 3. The steering group and community partners reviewed and endorsed integrating the LLM into the workshop structure, but emphasised that it was critical to explain to participants how the LLM would be used and how their data would be handled. Particular attention was given to the risk of reproducing ableist or deficit-based framings. 73 To mitigate this, the prompt imposed explicit boundaries on the LLM's output, including instructions to use only factors participants provided during the workshop and not to introduce or infer additional ones.

ii. Live Artist -Making causal relationships visible and discussable

Following this (also in the structured break), a live artist, experienced in community workshop facilitation and present throughout the workshop, translated the LLM's example feedback loops into hand-drawn illustrations. Research-artist collaborations are wellevidenced as a way to increase the accessibility, understandability, and communicative power of complex information. 74 Here, the artist's illustrations served a specific function: making causal relationships between system components tangible and engaging for a group that had not been asked to have any prior knowledge of systems thinking. For example, the artist depicted CLDs as a visual narrative that participants could immediately read and respond to (see Supplemental Figure 3 for an example). These illustrations were shared with participants immediately following the lunch break, functioning as dynamic discussion tools rather than finished outputs.

Part 3. Member Reflection

In the final part of the workshop, participants were invited to interrogate, challenge, and refine the illustrated feedback loops. Researchers verbally walked the group through each loop, step by step, referring to the visualisations created by the live-artist. Here, we reframe traditional qualitative concepts of "member checking" and "validation" (which position researchers as verifying the accuracy of data or analysis against participant experience) as "member reflection" to privilege ongoing, participant-led dialogue. 75 Participants assessed whether the suggested causal relationships were accurate, and identified anything that was missing, overstated, or wrongly connected. Some participants contributed verbally, while others used gestures or added stickers to the artwork. Engaging in this collective member reflection facilitated a shared understanding and ensured the emerging CLD was grounded in participants' lived realities, 76 rather than the LLM's. This stage also provided a natural entry point for participants to begin identifying external drivers which might interrupt an unhelpful cycle in the loop, serving as a precursor to formally establishing leverage points. This meant the final output was not only credible but also oriented towards potential entry points for intervention. 38

Accessibility and Inclusion in Practice

Conducting participatory research with underserved autistic people across diverse communities presents a distinct set of accessibility and inclusion challenges that standard research protocols are not designed to address. These fall into three broad areas: 1) the cognitive and emotional demands of participation; 2) communication and interaction differences; and 3) the structural and cultural barriers that shape who feels able to engage in the first place. The design of the workshops was shaped throughout by these challenges, informed by established recommendations for including autistic people in research. 40,77

1) Cognitive and emotional demands

Participatory research that asks people to reflect on difficult healthcare experiences across multiple sessions risks compounding emotional burden, particularly for those whose history with services has involved repeatedly presenting their needs and not being believed, helped, or taken seriously. We therefore designed all activities to be completed within a single three-hour session, minimising the demands placed on participants.

Containing the work within one session also carried both methodological and emotional benefits. Participants could reflect on how their contributions had been translated into systems-level insights while still in the room, reducing uncertainty about whether their experiences were understood and represented accurately, and ensuring oversight of the mapping remained with them. This also enabled in-room reflections on the example feedback loops, with the creative outputs still visible and the session's discussions fresh in mind. Real-time LLM synthesis was key to making this achievable within a single session.

When applied thoughtfully, LLMs can help mitigate time constraints while reducing cognitive burden and communication barriers for neurodivergent and other historically marginalised groups. 78, 79 While the session was structured around two hours of core activities and a one-hour break for food and refreshments, additional unstructured breaks were actively encouraged throughout. Most participants remained engaged for the full duration, consistent with evidence that three hours represents a recommended upper limit for workshops of this kind. 66

2) Communication and interaction differences

Autistic participants may experience barriers to participation emerging from unclear verbal communication, unstructured interaction, and sensory environments. To address this, facilitators adopted inclusive communication styles throughout, using clear, literal language, and allowing for extended processing time with regular summaries of activities. Contribution was actively invited while the right to pass or opt out was explicitly validated. Opportunities for one-to-one contributions were offered where space allowed, and participants were encouraged to share feedback during sessions so that needs could be accommodated in real time.

To reduce the reliance on spoken or written communication, we used a traffic light system worn on lanyards with coloured cards of green ("I am happy to join in"), yellow ("I need some support"), and red ("I need space and time"), allowing participants to signal and update their interaction preferences. 77 All research materials were provided in both standard and Easy Read formats. At Israac, materials were also available in Somali, translated by community group members. All workshop activities were explicitly multimodal. For example, sticky notes, stickers, drawing, art and craft materials, and verbal contributions scribed by researchers were all ways to engage. This ensured that being non-speaking or having language differences was not a barrier to contribution. We also funded support workers and translators to attend where requested.

3) Cultural and structural barriers to engagement

Structural and cultural factors shape not only who can attend research but who feels recognised and included within it. We applied culturally competent recruitment and inclusion criteria, adapting our approach when it became evident that cultural differences influenced how autism was perceived and disclosed across communities. 80, 81 For instance, in the workshop delivered with the Somali community organisation, Israac, adults openly identifying as autistic were less common, but mothers felt comfortable, supported, and highly engaged when discussing the healthcare barriers that they experienced on behalf of their autistic children. While we recognise that caregiver and autistic perspectives are fundamentally distinct, we wanted to avoid excluding these voices from communities already facing significant barriers to research participation. 26, 27 We therefore broadened our inclusion criteria for this specific workshop to welcome parents as proxy informants, valuing their experiential knowledge without conflating it directly with autistic lived experience.

Management of the physical environment was prioritised to minimise sensory demands, with workshops held in familiar community venues wherever possible. Some spaces presented unavoidable acoustic challenges, such as high ceilings and echoing rooms, which we acknowledge as a limitation. To enhance accessibility, a dedicated separate space was available at all workshops so that participants could take time out at any point if they wished.

Evaluation Methods

Our evaluation of PCSM focused on assessing the method and process rather than outputs in isolation. We measured success against our three core aims: 1) reaching and engaging underserved autistic communities; 2) whether they felt able to meaningfully contribute; and 3) translating their healthcare experiences into systems-level insight.

Data Collection and Analysis

Qualitative and quantitative data were collected from both the steering group and workshop participants using methods designed to minimise participant burden. These data sources were explicitly mapped to our three aims:

1) Reaching and engaging underserved autistic communities

Beyond our targeted recruitment of organisations serving underserved communities, we assessed our overall reach by measuring the self-reported demographic diversity of workshop participants against the NIHR INCLUDE framework. 55 Success was therefore defined as the active engagement of underserved autistic communities in the PCSM workshops, specifically those that are racially minoritised, living in areas of high economic deprivation, have a learning disability and/or who are non-or minimally speaking. This included both direct participation from autistic adults, and parents sharing the experiences of their autistic child in one workshop where this was the preference of a community.

To collect the demographic data of those who participated, we used an accessible "Blank

Character" template rather than a standard questionnaire. This was presented at the end of the workshop and featured a character outline surrounded by questions with open-ended text boxes (see Supplemental Figure 4), allowing participants to report aspects of their identity they felt important to share (e.g., ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and co-occurring conditions) in their own words or by drawing. Socioeconomic status was derived from the postcodes provided using the Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD), where a lower decile indicates a higher level of deprivation (a score of 1 being the most deprived and 10 being the least deprived). 82 The data were summarised using descriptive statistics to characterise the demographic diversity and determine whether we had successfully recruited participants from the underserved autistic communities we approached.

2) Ensuring underserved autistic communities felt able to meaningfully contribute

The accessibility and inclusivity of the PSCM workshop process were captured by evaluating whether participants understood the session's purpose, felt valued, and felt able to contribute. We adopted an interactive, low-burden form of evaluation using a physical "Postbox" system at the end of the workshop (see Supplemental Figure 5). Participants gave feedback by placing tokens with faces (Happy, Neutral, Sad) into 5 postboxes to indicate their rating on a 3-point Likert Scale, with the option to leave open-ended comments.

Each postbox displayed a different evaluation question designed to map onto a domain from the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR; see Supplemental Table 5). 83, 84 CFIR provides a well-established, theory-informed structure for examining the multilevel factors that shape whether an innovation (i.e., PCSM) can be effectively implemented. It highlights key barriers and facilitators across five interconnected domains:

Innovation Characteristics (features of the innovation itself, such as complexity and adaptability), Outer Setting (external context and influences on implementation), Inner Setting (organisational context, including culture, communication, and resources), Individuals (characteristics of those involved, including knowledge, beliefs, and motivation), and Implementation Process (strategies and activities used to plan, execute, and refine implementation). We calculated descriptive statistics of Likert responses, and qualitative comments were compiled and summarised, with exemplar quotes reported.

3) Translating underserved autistic communities' healthcare experiences into systemslevel insights

We sought to provide proof of concept for PCSM as a method by demonstrating that participants' lived healthcare experiences could be feasibly translated into systems-level insights. To do this, the researchers synthesised the live artist's hand-drawn feedback loops into a cross-workshop systems map, focusing exclusively on the feedback loops participants voted to prioritise and reflect on in each workshop. These loops were digitised into CLDs using Kumu, 85 a relationship-mapping software, by drafting the initial components and causal relationships from the artwork. We cross-referenced this against researcher notes, audio recordings, and storyboards, using CFIR domains to guide our interpretation of the systemic barriers and facilitators to healthcare access raised by participants. The research team then collaboratively refined and adjusted component names and the direction of connections to ensure they faithfully reflected what was shared by the group. External drivers (i.e. factors that influence a component of the map but are not influenced by it) discussed by participants were also incorporated as incoming components, 39 indicating community priorities for change (i.e., potential leverage points for intervention).

Established PSM approaches would then typically involve another round of member reflection. 66, 86 However, within the project constraints, we could only present the steering group's digitised CLD back to them for this extended process. We systematically reviewed each component and causal relationship together, and steering group members confirmed or refined these elements using their preferred communication methods, such as talking, writing on printouts, or typing.

While the resulting cross-workshop, researcher-synthesised map is provided as a supplementary resource for interactive viewing in Kumu (see Supplemental Link 1), it is not necessary to understand the core messages of the paper. Instead, we focus on the specific CLD generated by the autistic steering group as our primary "proof of concept.

Results and Lessons Learned

1) Reaching and engaging underserved autistic communities

Detailed demographic data is provided for the autistic adults directly involved in each workshop, as well as autistic people whose experiences were discussed by proxy informants (i.e., their parents) in Workshop 3 (see Table 1). We did not collect demographic information on the proxy informants themselves.

The cohort included substantial representation from racially minoritised groups, with 35.5% (n = 11) self-or proxy-reporting as Somali or Black African, and from areas of neighbourhood economic deprivation with a median IMD decile of 2 (Range: 1 [lowest] -9 [highest]).

The most frequently reported IMD deciles in the total sample were 1 (n= 9, 29%) and 2 (n = 8, 25.8%). Likewise, although we partnered with a learning disability service (Burton Street Foundation) for one workshop and autistic adults with co-occurring learning disabilities participated, only one person self-reported this (3.2%).

The Misalignment-Invalidation Cycle

The value of PCSM is most clearly shown in the primary reinforcing loop. The cycle starts with Communication Barriers experienced by the autistic person (i.e., service user), such as communication differences or difficulties identifying and describing how they feel Furthermore, PCSM showed a reciprocal relationship between Appointment Preparation Support and the Ability to Self-Advocate. Tools that help the autistic person prepare for the appointment (e.g., symptom diaries or having questions in advance) enable them to advocate for themselves. At the same time, when the autistic person is able to self-advocate, they feel more confident using these preparation tools. However, this relationship relies heavily on Available Energy (or "Spoons") 87 as a key driver. If the autistic person's Ability to Self-Advocate is constantly undermined, they reach a state of Disempowerment, which can cause them to stop using the healthcare system entirely. A methodological strength of PCSM is that it can identify priorities for change, rather than just simply pointing out problems. As shown, Clear and Accessible Communication (e.g., providing written information, avoiding jargon, and easy-read formats) helps reduce both Recall Challenges and Misaligned Understanding of Symptoms by supporting the autistic person to remember relevant information and answer questions. Similarly, providing Alternative Communication Methods (e.g., typing on a phone, using text-to-speech) improves Misaligned Understanding of Symptoms as the autistic person can choose to communicate in the way that works best for them. Finally, the clinician's attitude plays a vital role. Clinician Bias/Prejudice (i.e., making assumptions based on a person being autistic or their appearance) contributes to Clinician Dismissal/Invalidation. In contrast, Clinician Warmth and Receptivity can break the negative cycle, reducing dismissal and creating more equitable care.

Key Reflections

When engaging with traditionally underserved and marginalised communities, researchers must move beyond rigid project timelines to embrace a flexible, relational approach to co-production. Drawing on our experience from this project, we offer two key recommendations to guide meaningful engagement with underserved autistic populations in participatory research.

1) Investing in Relational Infrastructure

Meaningful engagement with underserved groups relies heavily on cultivating relational networks and an appropriate approach, in addition to sufficient time and resources (cited as the main barriers to engaging underserved groups by professionals in the UK). 88 This type of community work fundamentally "moves at the speed of relationships". Our project was co-led with a community partner (Sheffield Voices), building upon a relationship established well before the grant-writing phase. During the year of the project, we visited and engaged with community groups multiple times before data collection began, holding information sessions and attending community events. This duration was necessary to move beyond transactional research and towards genuine, long-term relationships. 33 Throughout this process, the research team also engaged in reflexive practice through regular team discussions to examine our own positionality and address any implicit biases held from an outsider perspective. 89 Resource allocation is also essential to cover the costs associated with accessibility. We funded support workers and translators to attend workshops, both of which were required so participants could meaningfully share their experiences in their preferred language or with necessary accommodations. 40,81

2) Being Flexible and Adapting

Community groups operate in a dynamic environment that can conflict with the rigid processes of academic research. Success required us to relinquish control and let the community groups guide the pace and format of engagement where feasible. Practically, this meant conducting sessions in familiar community spaces rather than university settings and organising workshops around school or childcare commitments. 33, 88 Promoting agency meant respecting community boundaries and organisational capacity. For example, our intention had been to work with autistic adults directly within each community group, but at Israac, the Somalian community centre, we instead worked with parents as proxy informants for their children, as this was the preference of the group.

We also recognised that not every attempt at engagement would succeed. Despite our best efforts and the support of our community partner, one group stepped back after initial meetings, and we were unable to find a time that worked for another group despite willingness on both sides. Community groups may be overstretched, under-resourced, have competing priorities, or simply may not perceive the immediate value of the proposed research. 89 Researchers must therefore remain mindful of not only avoiding extractive relationships, but also depletive ones, recognising that research involvement can draw heavily from an organisation's finite capacities. Respecting these boundaries and reassuring partners that they can step back without consequence is an essential part of maintaining a respectful and ethical relationship.

Discussion

In this paper, we describe and evaluate PCSM, an innovative emerging practice that combines CBPR with PSM and creative methods. Our evaluation demonstrated that meaningful engagement with underserved autistic people is achievable but requires more than just adequate resourcing (i.e., time and accessibility budgets). Success resulted from working in partnership with community organisations and taking a relational approach grounded in continued listening and trust-building.

By utilising multimodal contribution via the creative methods of persona development, storyboarding, and live artist visualisation, PCSM was accessible to diverse communication styles and first languages. This approach allowed for real-time, intuitive member reflection on components and causal relationships. Crucially, we demonstrated that all respondents who gave feedback on sharing their ideas and experiences felt able to do so, implying that the integration of creative methods successfully mitigated the cognitive and verbal demands that standard systems mapping can typically impose.

Beyond the methodological success of PCSM as a viable approach for participatory research with underserved autistic people, the steering group's "Clinical Misalignment Invalidation Cycle" CLD provides a participant-informed reflection of how structural features of healthcare systems can produce reinforcing patterns of misunderstanding, avoidance, and disengagement. By making these structural failures visible, PCSM challenges deficit-based views of autistic people, which typically place the blame on the individual without considering the wider context. Instead, barriers such as Misaligned Understanding of Symptoms may arise from inadequate autism-specific provider training and insufficient support for autistic people to communicate in ways that work for them. 20 The Double Empathy Problem offers a useful framework for understanding this misalignment, highlighting how differences in communication and interpretation between autistic and non-autistic people contribute to a mutual breakdown in understanding. 90, 91 In healthcare settings, these difficulties can compound into a Triple Empathy Problem, as medical jargon, assumptions and institutional structures create additional barriers to shared perspective-taking between autistic patients and non-autistic doctors, further marginalising autistic people. 92 Within this system-level insight, the external drivers identified in the "Clinical Misalignment Invalidation Cycle" causal loop function as the steering group's priorities for disrupting the reinforcing loop. For instance, while the need for Clear and Accessible Communication and Alternative Communication Methods is well-established within the literature, many autistic people continue to report a lack of such accommodations in practice. 93, 94 Within the logic of the causal loop, the systematic implementation of these adjustments is not merely helpful but essential to breaking cycles of invalidation and avoidance. Consequently, this offers both policymakers and healthcare professionals targeted areas to explore for potential service improvements. While many of the identified barriers manifest as behaviours and practices, shifting them at scale will require broader policy implications, such as the need to structurally mandate, fund and support coordinated and multidisciplinary action to achieve systemic change.

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this project lies in the authentic co-production we achieved with our community partner and the steering group, which was maintained throughout the lifecycle of the project. 40, 56 This partnership addresses field-wide issues with the depth of autistic involvement in participatory research, as our multi-level engagement spanned from identifying the initial research question through to study design, data analysis, and paper writing. 31 Furthermore, formal evaluation of the co-production process is rare in autism research. By embedding evaluation into the development of PCSM, we gathered perspectives on inclusion and accessibility within the workshops, providing a transparent assessment of our approach. We achieved a level of engagement and sample size appropriate for PSM workshops, 66 while capturing intersections of race and neighbourhood deprivation that are systematically underrepresented in autism research. 30 This highlights that when methodology is culturally and structurally adapted to community needs, underserved groups are willing and able to contribute complex systemic insights. Moreover, by following the GRIPP2 and GUIDED checklists, 57, 67 we also ensured transparency and consistency in reporting both our emerging practice and co-production processes.

Despite these strengths, several limitations must be acknowledged. The project was geographically localised to South Yorkshire (specifically Sheffield and Rotherham). This was an intentional methodological choice to facilitate meaningful and trusted engagement by leveraging existing community and voluntary sector infrastructure in these ethnically and socially diverse localities. [58] [59] [60] While we successfully developed and evaluated the PCSM methodology with a range of underserved autistic communities, healthcare access in the UK varies significantly due to regional commissioning differences (commonly known as the "postcode lottery"). 95 Consequently, our current mapping outputs may confound universal systemic experiences with localised service dynamics. Future applications of PCSM would therefore benefit from a wider geographical reach to distinguish universal systemic barriers from local service configurations. Additionally, recruitment relied heavily on autistic people already engaged with community organisations, likely excluding individuals who are more socially isolated. This represents an important challenge, given the established association between social isolation, loneliness, and poorer health outcomes. 96 Limitations were also evident in the representation of participants with co-occurring learning disability and non-or minimally speaking populations. Although the sample included a diverse range of communication styles and our use of creative methods allowed for multimodal contributions, we only heard about the experiences of non-and minimally speaking autistic people from parents acting as proxy informants (12.9%). While valuable, proxy accounts are distinct from the lived experience of autistic people and should not be relied on exclusively. 24 Through reflexive discussions with the wider research team and steering group, we recognise that not proactively and visibly integrating a broader range of Augmentative and Alternative Communication tools (e.g., Makaton, symbol boards) into the workshop design may have been an unintended barrier to inclusion. Non-speaking autistic people and those who support them may have assumed the workshops were "not for them", contributing to their absence. Moreover, parent proxies within the Somali community group introduced paediatric healthcare perspectives alongside adult experiences, blending two structurally distinct healthcare systems. Demographic data also indicated low self-report of learning disability despite recruitment from community groups specifically serving this population. This likely reflects a trade-off between our co-produced materials and standardised data collection. The "Blank Character" form was designed to be accessible and sensitive to underserved identities by encouraging participants to self-identify "other developmental conditions" rather than explicitly asking about learning disabilities. 97 This may have led to under-reporting of relevant demographics. Future work should consider further refinements to demographic measures to better capture learning disability and communication differences without compromising on the inclusive ethos of the work.

Finally, we must distinguish between the feasibility of generating CLDs and conducting extended member reflection across a full systems map. While our steering group engaged in member reflection to refine the "Clinical Misalignment" CLD as a proof of concept, within the scope of the current project we were unable to extend this process to the other community groups' CLDs or the consolidated cross-workshop map. Consequently, the latter currently represents a researcher-led synthesis based on our interpretations of the data. Participatory systems mapping relies on relational and qualitative processes to assess how faithfully a map represents the system of interest. 98 This requires re-engaging community groups for evidence triangulation or "disconfirmation", as well as testing causal relationships and leverage points with wider stakeholders. Future iterations of PCSM must factor in the substantial additional time and resources required for this extended member reflection before a map is used to inform or develop policy, service design or systems-level interventions. 66

Implications

The value of PCSM extends beyond just healthcare. Medical systems interface dynamically with housing, employment, education, and justice. 99 As autistic people disproportionately rely on these interconnected systems, 10 inaccessibility in one sector can ripple into others, creating a compounding ecosystem of exclusion. 100, 101 PCSM should be applied across sectors, moving beyond isolated service silos to capture the full, complex reality of autistic people's experiences. 45 Moreover, as methodologies such as PCSM are applied more widely and research practices become more inclusive, the definition of who is underserved will inevitably shift. Researchers must therefore remain reflexive and responsive to the perspectives which remain absent.

To move from proof of concept to actionable recommendations, future work should also proactively couple PCSM with implementation science frameworks during the formative development of system-level interventions. Specifically, CFIR can be used to anticipate multi-level factors across both outer (e.g., funding structures) and inner settings (e.g., organisational culture) from the outset, ensuring that resulting interventions are viable, sustainable, and effectively implemented. 83, 84

Conclusion

In this paper, we established PCSM as an innovative methodology that combines CBPR and PSM with creative methods. Through community partnerships, we provided an actionable roadmap for participatory research with underserved autistic people that can generate high-quality, systems-level insights. Ultimately, PCSM offers a pathway to close the research-implementation gap, driving the shift toward a healthcare system that is not only accessible but fundamentally equitable.

Because of this, Clinician Dismissal/Invalidation occurs, where the autistic person's symptoms are downplayed or ignored. The autistic person then experiences the appointment as a Perceived Waste of Time (Futility), which creates a Negative Emotional Impact on them where they may feel shame, self-blame, or anger and frustration at the healthcare system. As a result, the autistic person has a Reluctance to Seek Care/Avoidance in the future when they are unwell. If they do seek care, they experience Anticipatory Anxiety, often worrying about and imagining all the potential negative outcomes. This anxiety worsens the original Communication Barriers in their next encounter, closing and strengthening the negative loop. Beyond the main cycle, PCSM helped reveal the hidden mental effort required from the autistic person during this process. When a Misaligned Understanding of Symptoms occurs, it increases the autistic person's Information Processing/Cognitive Load because they must work much harder to translate their experience into medical terms while also trying to understand complex questions from a clinician. This creates a cognitive drain that reduces the autistic person's Ability to Self-Advocate, as they don't have the mental energy needed to speak up or challenge Clinician Dismissal/Invalidation.
