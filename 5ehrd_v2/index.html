Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Purpose: Word reading fluency continues to develop after accuracy is achieved, yet which early skills drive this development beyond earlier attainment remains unclear. We examined the longitudinal development of word reading fluency in Greek from Grade 2 to 6 to find out (1) how stable fluency is across these grades, (2) which early cognitive and linguistic skills predict Grade 6 fluency beyond prior attainment, and (3) whether the suppression effects commonly reported in concurrent designs hold longitudinally.

Greek children (n = 268) were assessed at the beginning of Grade 2 on serial and discrete naming (digits, objects, words, pseudowords), phonological awareness, orthographic knowledge, articulation rate, morphological awareness, vocabulary, and nonverbal ability, and on word reading fluency at the end of Grades 2, 3, and 6. Longitudinal structural equation models with autoregressive paths were used to identify unique predictors.

Results: Fluency was highly stable (β = .88 from Grade 2 to 3; β = .64 from Grade 3 to 6). Grade 2 fluency was predicted mainly by discrete word naming. Beyond Grade 3 fluency, serial digit naming, orthographic knowledge, and articulation rate predicted Grade 6 fluency, accounting for an additional 13% of the variance, but only when discrete digit naming was included as a suppressor. General cognitive and linguistic measures made no contribution.

The predictors of word reading fluency appear to change across the primary school years, from word-level naming to sequential processing, orthographic knowledge, and articulatory speed. Word reading fluency may thus be better understood not as a unitary skill that simply improves quantitatively over time, but as a developmentally shifting construct.

Long-Term Predictors of Word Reading Fluency Beyond the Early Elementary Grades Introduction

Although word recognition accuracy is typically achieved within the first years of formal schooling in transparent orthographies (e.g., Altani et al., 2019; Landerl & Wimmer, 2008; Lerkkanen et al., 2004; Soodla et al., 2015), reading fluency continues to develop well into the later primary school years (e.g., Georgiou et al., 2014 Georgiou et al., , 2021;; Tobia & Marzocchi, 2014; Vaessen & Blomert, 2010). What drives this continued development beyond early attainment, however, is not fully understood. The present study examines the longitudinal prediction of word reading fluency from early Grade 2 to Grade 6 in Greek, using a multivariate longitudinal model that includes a broad set of cognitive and linguistic skills.

Reading fluency is widely regarded as a defining marker of skilled reading and stands among the core educational priorities of elementary education (National Reading Panel, 2000). Despite its central importance, reading fluency has been defined and operationalized in different ways, with definitions ranging from the rate of word decoding (Breznitz, 2006) to the joint product of accuracy, automaticity, and prosody that together support comprehension (Kuhn et al., 2010). A key distinction concerns whether fluency is assessed through oral reading of connected text (text reading fluency, often termed oral reading fluency; e.g., Good & Kaminski, 2002) or through the rapid reading of lists of unconnected and unrelated words (word reading efficiency; as in the Test of Word Reading Efficiency; Torgesen et al., 1999).

Although these two formats are often regarded as essentially identical at a surface level (e.g., Fuchs et al., 2001; Hudson et al., 2009; Kim et al., 2012) and are sometimes treated as interchangeable indices of the same underlying skill, they differ in an important respect: Oral reading fluency is confounded with comprehension and other supralexical processes such as syntactic parsing and semantic integration, which are engaged when reading connected text but not for word lists (e.g., Eason et al., 2013; Jenkins et al., 2003; Kim, 2015). As a result, oral reading fluency performance may reflect a blend of word-level decoding efficiency and text-level comprehension skills, making it difficult to isolate the contribution of decoding efficiency. Word reading efficiency, by contrast, offers a purer index of word-level processing efficiency, relatively free from the influence of text-level skills, and may therefore provide a more direct window onto the cognitive and linguistic skills underlying decoding efficiency (Protopapas et al., 2018). For this reason, the present study focuses on word reading efficiency rather than oral (i.e., passage) reading fluency as the target of longitudinal prediction.

The ability to read individual words both accurately and rapidly is considered central to the development of reading fluency (e.g., Ehri, 2005; Hudson et al., 2009; Kim, 2015; Stanovich, 1980). Yet accuracy and speed do not emerge simultaneously or develop at the same rate. As children develop as readers, individual differences in word reading shift from the accuracy dimension to the speed dimension, with this transition occurring once a minimum level of word reading accuracy has been established (Altani et al., 2020; Juul et al., 2014; Karageorgos et al., 2020). In transparent orthographies, where decoding accuracy is typically mastered early, this shift occurs relatively quickly, such that large individual differences in reading ability come to be expressed primarily in speed well before the end of primary school (Eklund et al., 2015; Landerl & Wimmer, 2008; Mouzaki & Sideridis, 2007).

Despite this developmental progression, reading fluency shows strong autoregressive stability over time across orthographies (Georgiou et al., 2021; Khanolainen et al., 2020; Salins et al., 2026; van Viersen et al., 2018; Wei et al., 2015). However, this stability is imperfect and there is evidence for systematic variation in developmental trajectories. Specifically, in addition to the stable trajectory, cumulative (Stanovich, 1986), compensatory (Baumert et al., 2012; Catts et al., 2012; Psyridou et al., 2020; Torppa et al., 2015), and late-emerging (Catts et al., 2012; Etmanskie et al., 2016; Leach et al., 2003; Psyridou et al., 2020; Torppa et al., 2015) trajectories have been documented, in which further fluency development is not fully accounted for by earlier fluency performance. These trajectories have also been linked to different early cognitive profiles. For example, longitudinal evidence from Finnish (a highly transparent orthography) suggests that persistent difficulties were associated with broad weaknesses in phonological awareness, rapid naming, and letter knowledge, whereas lateemerging difficulties were most clearly marked by slow rapid naming, and resolving difficulties by weaknesses confined to the preschool years (Torppa et al., 2015). One possibility is that stability reflects trait-like continuity in a single homogeneous construct where a stable underlying ability drives performance throughout the developmental trajectory. In this scenario, deviating patterns would be accounted for by extenuating factors disrupting an otherwise set progression. Alternatively, the superficial long-term stability that is typically observed may belie a dynamic developmental process in which the cognitive and linguistic underpinnings of fluency shift across grades. In this scenario, both the apparently stable and the deviating patterns could be attributed to the same processes, leading to convergent or divergent outcomes as a result of their developmental interlocking. Addressing this question requires longitudinal designs that simultaneously track multiple cognitive and linguistic predictors to identify unique contributors to fluency development across grades.

Here, we take a first step toward this long-term goal by asking what early skills predict reading fluency development beyond prior attainment.

Serial naming (commonly termed "rapid automatized naming" or RAN) is among the most robust concurrent predictors of reading fluency across orthographies and developmental stages (e.g., Georgiou et al., 2016a,b; Moll et al., 2014, see also Araújo et al., 2015 for a meta-analysis). It is also a robust longitudinal predictor of reading fluency measures across orthographies, even when measured prior to reading instruction (although fewer studies have investigated the longitudinal relations and most findings have only examined a limited developmental range; e.g., Georgiou et al., 2012; Landerl et al., 2019; Lervåg & Hulme, 2009; McWeeny et al., 2022; Pan et al., 2011). This is important from a theoretical perspective because the cognitive processes underlying performance in reading fluency tests may change drastically during development. Although one can always administer a passage or word list and obtain a superficially comparable metric of correct words per minute at any age, this does not mean that the task is performed in a comparable manner. Children in early grades are likely to read many words by piecewise phonological recoding, whereas this is decreasingly likely at higher grades. Moreover, children in early grades are likely to focus all their processing resources on one word at a time, essentially reading one word after the previous one has been completed, whereas children in higher grades are likely to overlap their processing across multiple successive words as they achieve actual fluency (Protopapas et al., 2013 (Protopapas et al., , 2018 (Protopapas et al., , 2024)), eventually reading as fast as they can speak rather than as fast as they can decode. If the processes dominating test performance change during development, this means that different skill domains will dominate individual differences in reading fluency measures in different grades. For this reason, different kinds of predictors may be longitudinally associated with reading fluency performance at different stages in reading development, and findings from early development (such as early predictors of Grade 2 reading fluency) cannot be generalized to later development (in late elementary or higher grades). In this context, it is remarkable that serial naming has been found to be a strong and unique longitudinal predictor of reading fluency measures, beyond earlier attainment, both in early (from preschool to Grade 3, controlling for Grade 1; Diamanti et al., 2024) and later (Grade 2 to Grade 5; Romero et al., 2024) grades, in different orthographies (Norwegian and English, respectively).

When considering naming tasks as predictors of reading fluency, a key theoretical distinction concerns whether naming is assessed in a serial format, where multiple stimuli are displayed simultaneously and named as quickly as possible from left to right, or in a discrete format, where stimuli are presented one at a time. Serial naming has consistently been shown to be a stronger predictor of reading fluency than discrete naming across languages and grades (e.g., Altani et al., 2020; Logan et al., 2011; Protopapas et al., 2013), suggesting that processes specific to the serial format, beyond the speed of individual item retrieval, are critical for its relationship with reading. When both formats are included in the same model, discrete naming acts as a suppressor variable, removing shared item-level retrieval variance and thereby sharpening the unique contribution of serial naming to reading prediction (Logan et al., 2011; Logan & Schatschneider, 2014; Protopapas et al., 2013; van Viersen et al., 2024). This suppression effect reveals a specific sequential processing component of serial naming that is not shared with discrete naming. It has been proposed that this component concerns the ability to overlap processing stages across multiple successive items beyond single word recognition, referred to as cascaded processing (Protopapas et al., 2018).

Critically, however, this suppression effect has to date been demonstrated only in concurrent designs. Whether it extends to longitudinal prediction, and whether the sequential component of serial naming carries a lasting influence on fluency development beyond early attainment, has not yet been examined.

Establishing the unique longitudinal contributions of serial and discrete naming therefore requires a multivariate model that simultaneously accounts for other cognitive and linguistic predictors of fluency.

Beyond naming speed, two additional predictors are examined here whose longitudinal contributions to reading fluency have received relatively little attention: orthographic knowledge and articulation rate. Orthographic knowledge has been found to contribute significantly to efficient word reading across elementary grades, after controlling for reading skills, at least in transparent orthographies (Georgiou et al., 2012; Giazitzidou et al., 2025; Rothe et al., 2015). It can be assessed with an orthographic choice task, which requires children to identify the correctly spelled word from a pair of phonologically identical alternatives. Some researchers have suggested that this task measures word recognition rather than a distinct orthographic process, as readers who make correct orthographic choices probably have highly specified orthographic representations for those words, formed through orthographic learning (Nation & Castles, 2017). However, evidence suggests that orthographic choice performance is not fully determined by the ability to read words in isolation and that it captures not only item-specific orthographic representations but also more general sensitivity to the patterns and regularities of the writing system (Compton et al., 2020). Whether this sensitivity carries unique longitudinal predictive value for word reading fluency beyond prior attainment and naming speed remains to be examined.

Articulation rate, on the other hand, is typically assessed by asking children to repeat sequences of words as rapidly as possible. Since word reading efficiency involves overt articulation, articulation constraints may influence reading efficiency measures, so its inclusion allows its contribution to be properly separated from that of other predictors. Two prior studies have found articulation rate to be a non significant predictor of reading development (Georgiou et al., 2021; Parrila et al., 2004). However, these studies did not include discrete naming as a suppressor variable, leaving open the possibility that shared variance with naming speed may have obscured any unique contribution of articulatory efficiency to fluency development.

A broader set of cognitive and linguistic skills is also examined here within the same longitudinal framework. In the early phases of reading development, phonological skills seem to be particularly important (e.g., Georgiou et al., 2008; Puolakanaho et al., 2007); however, as decoding becomes automatized in later grades, their unique contribution to fluency prediction diminishes, especially in more transparent orthographies like Greek (e.g., Georgiou et al., 2008; Landerl et al., 2019). Alongside phonological skills, morphological awareness, a multidimensional component of the linguistic system that shares common variance with phonological and syntactic awareness (Levesque et al., 2021), has been proposed to influence literacy skills through multiple pathways. However, evidence for a direct contribution of morphological awareness to word reading fluency is mixed. In Greek, longitudinal studies have generally found no unique contribution once other predictors are controlled: Morphological awareness assessed in kindergarten and Grade 1 did not predict Grade 1 or Grade 2 word reading fluency after controlling for vocabulary and naming speed (Diamanti et al., 2017; Manolitsis et al., 2017), nor did morphological awareness at the start of Grade 2 predict fluency at the end of Grade 2 beyond phonological awareness and naming speed (Desrochers et al., 2018). Cross-sectional evidence suggests its contribution may be indirect, operating through phonological awareness (Giazitzidou et al., 2024). Similarly, in Norwegian, preschool morphological awareness did not predict word reading fluency in Grades 1 or 3 after controlling for phonological awareness and naming speed (Diamanti et al., 2024). In contrast, morphological awareness has been found to predict later reading fluency in less transparent orthographies when assessed well after the onset of literacy instruction (Desrochers et al., 2018), which is more difficult to interpret.

Finally, general cognitive skills (receptive vocabulary and nonverbal reasoning) are also added as control variables to ensure the specificity of the findings. Examining all these skills within a single longitudinal model provides a more comprehensive picture of the early cognitive and linguistic predictors of word reading fluency across the primary school years and allows their respective contributions to be properly evaluated.

Aim and Rationale of the Present Study

The present study examined the longitudinal prediction of word reading fluency in

Greek from Grade 2 to Grade 6. Using a longitudinal structural equation modeling approach, we aimed to examine: (1) to what extent word reading fluency is stable from Grade 2 to Grade 6, (2) which early skills (assessed in Grade 2) uniquely predict later word reading fluency after accounting for prior attainment, and (3) whether the suppression effect of discrete digit naming on serial digit naming, previously reported in concurrent designs across languages, extends to longitudinal prediction.

Greek is a relatively transparent orthography in which grapheme-phoneme correspondences are largely consistent (Protopapas & Vlahou, 2009). Although children typically achieve high levels of decoding accuracy early in reading development, substantial individual differences remain in the rate and efficiency with which words are processed (Protopapas, 2017). Understanding which early skills contribute to later fluency is therefore important for clarifying the mechanisms underlying the development of reading efficiency in transparent orthographies. To address this issue, the present study examined a broad set of cognitive-linguistic skills assessed at the beginning of Grade 2 as potential predictors of later fluency. These included receptive vocabulary, nonverbal reasoning, phoneme awareness, syllable and phoneme blending, morphological awareness, orthographic knowledge, articulation rate, serial naming of digits and objects, and discrete naming of digits, objects, words, and pseudowords.

By examining multiple naming formats alongside linguistic and cognitive predictors within a single longitudinal model, the study aimed to clarify which early skills uniquely contribute to the development of word reading fluency over time. Based on the results from previous longitudinal studies in transparent orthographies (e.g., Eklund et al., 2018; Georgiou et al., 2014 Georgiou et al., , 2021;; Khanolainen et al., 2020; Landerl & Wimmer, 2008; Psyridou et al., 2025), we expected word reading fluency to show high stability across the primary school years, even after accounting for measurement error, yet remain further predictable by early cognitive and linguistic skills beyond prior attainment. Prior research suggests that the cognitive skills driving reading fluency may shift across development; phonological skills and letter knowledge are particularly important in the early phases of reading acquisition (e.g., Georgiou et al., 2008; Puolakanaho et al., 2007), while later fluency comes to be predicted increasingly by naming speed as decoding becomes automatized (e.g., Eklund et al., 2018; Torppa et al., 2012; van Bergen et al., 2014). Thus, we expected the skills that uniquely predict fluency to differ between Grade 2 and Grade 6. Finally, based on concurrent evidence across languages showing that discrete digit naming suppresses the contribution of serial digit naming to reading fluency (e.g., Logan et al., 2011; Logan & Schatschneider, 2014; Protopapas et al., 2013), we expected a similar suppression structure to operate longitudinally, isolating the sequential processing component of serial naming as a specific and lasting predictor of fluency development.

Method Participants

The sample comprised 268 students from 17 public elementary schools from a socioeconomically diverse sample of neighborhoods in the Athens metropolitan area. All participants were native speakers of Greek. Written informed consent was obtained from parents or guardians prior to participation. The study received ethics approval from the ethics board of University of Alberta, Canada (study ID Pro00065133), and the directorate of primary and secondary education of the Greek Ministry of Education (Φ15/85312/91827/Δ1).

In accordance with Greek law, the permission to conduct the study in the public schools was renewed annually.

Participants were assessed across four waves: at the very beginning (fall) of Grade 2 in 2017, spring of Grade 2 (2018), Grade 3 (2019), and Grade 6 (2021 -2022). No assessment was undertaken in Grades 4 and 5 because of school closures due to the COVID-19 pandemic. By Grade 6 the sample was reduced to 199 participants due to some children changing schools, moving, or withdrawing from the study. There is no evidence that the final sample was a nonrandom subsample of the original (as Grade 6 missingness is not predictable by logistic regression from early Grade 2 measurements; χ2 = 22.44, df = 16, p = 0.129).

Measures

All predictors were assessed individually at the beginning of Grade 2 (fall); serial naming was also repeated in the spring of Grade 2. Word reading fluency was assessed at the end of Grade 2 and in Grades 3 and 6. Table 1 summarizes each task, its format, scoring, and internal consistency in the present sample; full descriptions of items and administration procedures are provided in the Supplemental Material (S1). Computerized tasks were administered with DMDX (Forster & Forster, 2003), and naming response times were obtained from the recorded audio with CheckVocal (Protopapas, 2007).

Statistical Analysis

Latent factors were constructed for all variables. Word reading fluency was assessed with one task per time point (end of Grade 2, Grade 3, and Grade 6). To account for measurement error, we specified dummy latent variables with a single indicator each, with residual variances fixed based on an assumed reliability of 0.94 based on prior data (Protopapas et al., 2007). Error variance was then specified as (1 -reliability) × observed variance at each wave, yielding fixed (unstandardized) residuals of 0.002, 0.003, and 0.003 for Grades 2, 3, and 6, respectively. To match the scale of the naming variables, fluency scores were also expressed as items per second (dividing raw scores by 60).

All predictors (receptive vocabulary, nonverbal reasoning, morphological awareness, phoneme awareness, syllable and phoneme blending, orthographic knowledge, articulation rate, discrete naming of words, pseudowords, objects, and digits, and serial naming of objects and digits) were also modeled as latent variables. Most constructs were represented using odd-even split parcels as indicators, with loadings constrained to be equal across the two parcels of each construct. Specifically, this applied to receptive vocabulary, nonverbal reasoning, phoneme awareness (combining phoneme deletion for words and pseudowords), syllable and phoneme blending, orthographic knowledge, and all four discrete rapid naming tasks. Morphological awareness was represented by three observed subtask scores (analogy for words, analogy for sentences, derivation). Articulation rate was represented by the five inverse-time scores (one per triplet). Serial naming of digits and serial naming of objects were each represented by two indicators corresponding to fall and spring of Grade 2, with loadings across the two time points constrained to be equal. Latent variances for all predictor constructs were fixed to 1. Additionally, the residual covariances between the two phoneme deletion parcel indicators, and between Grade 2 spring serial digit and serial object duration scores, were freed to be estimated, to accommodate shared indicator-specific variance.

To examine the longitudinal development of word reading fluency, we first estimated a simple autoregressive model with paths from Grade 2 to Grade 3 and from Grade 3 to Grade 6 fluency. This model served as the baseline for evaluating the incremental prediction of Grade 2 predictors over and above prior fluency attainment.

Next, to evaluate the bivariate associations between each Grade 2 predictor and reading fluency across time, we estimated a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) model in which all predictor-fluency associations were specified as nondirectional covariances. This model provided measurement-error-corrected latent correlations between Grade 2 predictors and reading fluency at each time point.

To determine which Grade 2 skills uniquely predicted Grade 6 reading fluency beyond autoregressive stability, we first estimated a multivariate structural model in which fluency at Grades 2, 3, and 6 was simultaneously regressed onto all predictors, while retaining the autoregressive paths. To identify a parsimonious set of longitudinal predictors, we proceeded through a structured model-trimming sequence. We first established the maximum proportion of variance in Grade 6 fluency that could be explained by all predictors simultaneously (the full model). Then predictors that made negligible contributions to Grade 6 fluency were removed sequentially while monitoring changes in R² at each step. Predictors were removed when their coefficients at Grade 6 were non-significant and their removal resulted in negligible changes in R². Conceptually related predictors were evaluated in groups where appropriate. The final trimmed (parsimonious) model was compared to the full model using a chi-square difference test.

The trimming sequence suggested potential suppression effects for some Grade 2 predictors, specifically for phoneme deletion, discrete digits, and discrete words. To examine the nature and magnitude of these effects, we fit a series of supplementary models in which suspected suppressors were removed from the Grade 6 regression equation individually and in combination, while substantive predictors (serial naming of digits, orthographic knowledge, and articulation rate) were retained. The primary focus of this analysis was on the Grade 6 outcome, given the aim of identifying longitudinal predictors beyond prior reading fluency skills. Discrete word naming was treated separately from the other two suppressors because its role varied depending on model specification: It functioned as a significant predictor of early word reading fluency (Grades 2-3) but made no independent contribution to Grade 6 fluency, and its inclusion or exclusion affected the suppression dynamics of other predictors in Grade 6. Model fit was evaluated using the chi-square value, the comparative fit index (CFI), the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and the standardised root mean square residual (SRMR). Good model fit was indicated by CFI and TLI ≥ 0.95, RMSEA ≤ 0.06, and SRMR ≤ 0.08 (Hu & Bentler, 1999). All analyses were conducted in R using the lavaan package with parameters estimated using maximum likelihood (ML). Additional packages included semTools, tidySEM, and lavaanPlot. Missing data were handled using full-information maximum likelihood (FIML) under the assumption that data were missing at random (MAR).

As noted above, discrete naming of digits and, to a lesser extent, phoneme deletion, appeared to act as suppressor variables in the prediction of Grade 6 word reading fluency. This suggests that the longitudinal predictive value of the three substantive predictors may depend critically on the presence of the suppressors.

In this case, predicted variance increased modestly (Grade 6 word reading fluency: R² = 0.481) compared to a model with no suppressors (R² = 0.454), and orthographic knowledge remained significant (β = 0.26, p = 0.035). Discussion

The present study examined the longitudinal stability of word reading fluency in Greek and the early cognitive and linguistic skills that uniquely predict fluency development from Grade 2 to Grade 6. Fluency was highly stable across both transitions (Grade 2 to Grade 3: β = .88; Grade 3 to Grade 6: β = .64), indicating that earlier word reading fluency is the strongest single predictor of later word reading fluency. Beyond the Grade 3 autoregressor, three early Grade 2 skills uniquely predicted Grade 6 word reading fluency: serial digit naming, orthographic knowledge, and articulation rate. Their contributions depended on the presence of discrete naming as a suppressor: Serial digit naming and articulation rate were no longer significant without discrete digit naming, and orthographic knowledge without either discrete digit or discrete word naming. Phoneme deletion acted as a weaker, secondary suppressor. General cognitive and linguistic measures (nonverbal ability, vocabulary, morphological awareness, blending) made no contribution once fluency itself and the naming measures were in the model. Of the three predictors, articulation rate emerged as the most robust as it remained significant across all model variants in which discrete naming was retained.

Stability Incorporates Qualitative Change

The results are consistent with autoregressive growth across Grades 2 to 6 with a constant standardized autoregressive path between successive grades of about .86 (as .86foot_0 ≈ .64). 3 This is hypothetical, as we have no data for Grades 4 and 5, but if it holds then it means that about three quarters of word reading fluency variance at any given grade is attributable to individual differences in the previous year. Given that the reliability of this type of measure exceeds .9, this leads us to expect that at least 15% and up to 20% of word reading fluency variance in each grade is not attributable to previous fluency attainment but must be attributed to other systematic causal factors.

Prior research on the development of word reading fluency has indicated strong stability over time, with autoregressive coefficients ranging from 0.75 to 0.97 across Grades 1 to 9, depending on the study, grade level, and time interval (Khanolainen et al., 2020; Manolitsis et al., 2017; Salins et al., 2026; van Viersen et al., 2018), so the present estimates are in line with the literature. However, despite this high mean-level stability, there is substantial variability in developmental word reading fluency trajectories at the individual level. A proportion of systematic variance beyond the autoregressor is therefore to be expected.

Consistent with this expectation, the early Grade 2 predictors accounted for about 13% of Grade 6 variance beyond prior attainment at Grade 3, although we do not know how much of this would survive the statistical control of a Grade 5 autoregressor. In addition, the early skills that uniquely accounted for this variance differed between Grade 2 and Grade 6: Discrete naming of words was the strongest predictor at Grade 2 but did not contribute to Grade 6 word reading fluency, where serial digit naming, orthographic knowledge, and articulation rate emerged as the main predictors. Although much systematic Grade 6 variance is left to be accounted for by other factors, the fact that such a substantial proportion is attributable to long-term prediction from early skills strongly indicates that different skill domains contribute to word reading fluency growth at different developmental periods.

This also means that the well-established longitudinal stability of word reading fluency should not be conceived of as expression of a stable trait. After all, a stable trait would lead to approximately equal pairwise correlations between any two grades, in stark contrast to the apparently autoregressive growth suggested by the present data. Twin data are consistent with this picture. Heritability of reading fluency is high and largely stable from age 7 to 16 (h2 = .73, .71, and .64), and the genetic influences at successive ages overlap substantially (rg = .84 from 7 to 12 and .78 from 12 to 16), with only small new genetic effects emerging at later grades (Tosto et al., 2017). Most of the systematic variance at any age is therefore carried forward from earlier ages rather than newly arising. If fluency at each grade builds on fluency at the previous grade together with the skills that are relevant at that stage, then the shift in predictors from Grade 2 to Grade 6 is the developmental counterpart of this statistical pattern. This raises the possibility that, even where mean-level stability is high, word reading fluency itself undergoes qualitative change across development, with the same measure tapping partly different underlying processes in early and later grades, shifting toward more automatized processing as children progress through primary school.

The Importance of Serial Naming

Among the early skills examined, serial digit naming emerged as a robust unique predictor of fluency at both Grade 2 and Grade 6, with its contribution strengthening rather than weakening over time. This finding is consistent with a substantial body of research showing that serial naming is a stronger correlate of reading than discrete naming across a range of languages and developmental stages (e.g., Altani et al., 2017; Georgiou et al., 2013; Logan et al., 2011; Protopapas et al., 2013; Vaessen & Blomert, 2010). The serial format requires not only rapid retrieval of individual item names but also the efficient scheduling and coordination of multiple processes occurring simultaneously across a continuous sequence, demands that are absent in discrete naming where items are presented and named one at a time (e.g., Logan et al., 2011; Protopapas et al., 2013).

When both formats are included in the same model, discrete naming has been shown to act as a suppressor variable, removing shared item-level retrieval variance and thereby sharpening the unique contribution of the sequential component captured by serial naming (Logan et al., 2011; Logan & Schatschneider, 2014; Protopapas et al., 2013; extensively discussed by van Viersen et al., 2025). Critically, this suppression effect has so far been demonstrated only in concurrent designs. The present study extends this work by showing that the same suppression structure operates longitudinally in Greek and suggests that the effect may be even stronger longitudinally than concurrently. Discrete digit naming assessed at the beginning of Grade 2 suppressed the association between serial digit naming and word reading fluency measured four and a half years later at Grade 6. Removing discrete digit naming from the model resulted in a substantial drop in explained variance at Grade 6 (ΔR² = 5.6%), with serial digit naming no longer reaching significance. This suggests that the sequential, coordinative component isolated by suppression is not merely a transient correlate of concurrent reading skill but reflects a more stable individual characteristic with a lasting influence on word reading fluency development.

One possible interpretation is that both serial and discrete digit naming involve familiarity with digit names and automatic access to their phonological forms. However, serial naming additionally captures processes specific to the sequential nature of the task such as rapid eye movement control and the efficient scheduling of multiple items, that drive its relationship with reading (e.g., Georgiou et al., 2013; Gordon & Hoedemaker, 2016; Kuperman et al., 2016; Protopapas et al., 2024) beyond the automaticity of name retrieval (e.g., Stanovich et al., 1983; Logan et al., 2011). By statistically controlling for the shared symbol naming component via discrete digit naming, the unique contribution of serial digit naming (i.e., the sequential processing component) to the longitudinal prediction of fluency is isolated and strengthened.

Moreover, the strengthening of the unique contribution of serial digit naming from Grade 2 to Grade 6 is consistent with the suggestion that word reading fluency undergoes qualitative change across development as children progress through primary school, moving beyond initial decoding toward more automatized word recognition and the efficient cascading thereby afforded (Protopapas et al., 2018 (Protopapas et al., , 2024)). Both serial naming and word list reading share the demand of sustaining rapid, sequential responses across a continuous series of items. As decoding becomes more automatized in later grades, the ability to efficiently maintain and coordinate this sequential processing may become increasingly critical for fluency, which may explain why the sequential component of serial naming, isolated through suppression by discrete naming, emerges as a stronger predictor of word reading fluency at Grade 6 than at Grade 2. The predictive shift itself may be qualitative in nature, meaning that serial naming may predict word reading efficiency for partially different reasons at different stages of literacy development: indexing intraword processes early on and interword processes later on, as fluency develops (de Jong, 2011; Protopapas et al., 2013; van den Boer et al., 2026).

In sum, the consistent suppressive effect of discrete naming in the relationship between serial naming and reading, in both concurrent studies (previously) and the present longitudinal one, confirms that the prediction of reading fluency by serial naming is probably more about the "serial" than about the "naming" aspect of serial naming. Whenever a pure naming measure (i.e., discrete naming) has been employed in conjunction with a RAN-type measure (i.e., serial naming) in an alphabetic orthography, a suppression effect has emerged, indicating that the relationship between RAN and reading cannot be primarily about whatever is shared with discrete naming. This realization calls for a reconsideration of conceptualizing naming per se as a critical reading subskill and should perhaps moderate reference to naming deficits per se as potential contributing factors to reading difficulties.

The Nature of Orthographic Choice

Whereas the suppression effect of discrete digit naming operated on the sequential processing pathway, another suppression pattern emerged for orthographic knowledge, involving phoneme deletion awareness and discrete word naming as suppressors. The suppression effect of phoneme deletion awareness alone was relatively modest (removing it while retaining discrete digit naming led to only a small reduction in explained variance at Grade 6 of approximately 1%, and orthographic knowledge remained significant, though attenuated). However, the suppression effect of phoneme deletion was larger when discrete word naming was also present as a predictor of reading fluency than when it was excluded entirely, pointing to a three-way interplay among early phoneme deletion awareness, discrete word naming, and orthographic knowledge as predictors of long-term reading fluency. One possible interpretation is that the longitudinal contribution of orthographic choice reflects sensitivity to the regularities and structure of the writing system. Phoneme deletion and discrete word naming seem to capture phonological decoding variance that overlaps with orthographic knowledge but is not relevant for long-term fluency development. When this shared variance is partialed out through suppression, the orthographic-specific contribution of orthographic knowledge becomes more apparent. When discrete word naming was examined as a substitute suppressor in the absence of both primary suppressors (discrete naming of digits and phoneme deletion), orthographic knowledge remained significant but serial naming of digits and articulation rate did not recover significance. This suggests that the suppression effect of discrete word naming operates primarily on the orthographic pathway rather than on the sequential processing or articulatory pathways. This is consistent with the nature of the task, as discrete word naming is itself a word-reading task and therefore shares lexicalorthographic variance with orthographic knowledge. This interpretation should be treated with caution, however, as the predictors were all measured at the same time point, so the suppression pattern reflects their shared variance rather than a directional relationship.

In the early phases of reading development, phonological decoding is effortful and relies heavily on phonological skills and letter knowledge (e.g., Georgiou et al., 2008; Georgiou et al., 2012). In transparent orthographies, as decoding becomes automatized relatively early, later reading fluency comes to be driven increasingly by other cognitive skills, particularly naming speed (e.g., Landerl et al., 2019; Moll et al., 2014; Torppa et al., 2012). Yet, the unique longitudinal contribution of orthographic knowledge to Grade 6 fluency, beyond prior attainment and naming speed, points to a specific role for orthographic knowledge in the development of reading efficiency that is independent of the sequential processing demands captured by serial naming. This is consistent with concurrent evidence from Greek showing that orthographic knowledge contributes significantly to efficient word reading in both early and later primary school grades (Georgiou et al., 2012; Giazitzidou et al., 2025). One possibility is that early performance in the orthographic choice task functions as a proxy for print exposure or reading experience (see also Burt, 2006). Children who have been exposed to more print have more opportunities to encounter correct spellings and, if continued, this advantage may compound over the intervening years between Grade 2 and Grade 6. Print exposure and home literacy environment have indeed been shown to correlate with reading fluency (e.g., Mol & Bus, 2011; Torppa et al., 2020; van Bergen et al., 2018).

However, the longitudinal independent effects of print exposure or home literacy environment on reading fluency are modest to negligible (e.g., Khanolainen et al., 2020; Silinskas et al., 2020) or indirect through phonological awareness, letter knowledge, and vocabulary (Inoue et al., 2020; Manolitsis et al., 2013). This suggests that even if performance on the orthographic choice task partly reflects print exposure, it captures additional variance that is more proximally relevant to reading fluency than print exposure per se.

A critical question is what this additional variance represents. Researchers have suggested that the orthographic choice task measures word recognition rather than a distinct orthographic process, as readers who make correct orthographic choices probably have highly specified orthographic representations for those words, formed through orthographic learning (Nation & Castles, 2017; see also Burt, 2006). Consistent with this view, using concurrent data, Hagiliassis et al. (2006) showed that word identification loaded on the orthographic processing factor, alongside the phonological processing factor. Further, Deacon et al. (2019) found in a longitudinal study that static orthographic knowledge and word reading formed a single latent factor, suggesting they largely capture the same variance at the construct level. However, Compton et al. (2020) showed that performance on an orthographic choice task is not fully determined by the ability to read the target words in isolation. Even when words were read incorrectly, children chose the correct orthographic form at a rate well above chance. Moreover, measures of general orthographic knowledge, phonemic awareness, word frequency, and orthographic neighborhood size accounted for significant variance in orthographic choice performance beyond item-specific word reading skill. This suggests that the orthographic choice task captures not only item-specific orthographic representations but also more general sensitivity to the patterns and regularities of the writing system, a component that is distinct from word reading.

Importantly, Deacon et al. (2019) further found that it is dynamic orthographic learning capacity, rather than static orthographic knowledge, that predicts later word reading fluency, suggesting that what drives reading development is the ability to form new orthographic representations efficiently rather than the store of existing ones. This distinction is important in the context of the present findings: If orthographic choice were simply a proxy for word reading skill, its contribution at Grade 6 would be absorbed by the autoregressor. The fact that it is not suggests that the general orthographic sensitivity component (beyond what is shared with word reading) is what carries the unique longitudinal predictive value. The general orthographic sensitivity captured by our orthographic choice task may be indexing this dynamic capacity: Children with stronger sensitivity to orthographic patterns at Grade 2 may be more efficient orthographic learners going forward. Consistent with this, studies on the self-teaching hypothesis (Share, 1995) have shown that prior orthographic knowledge (as measured using spelling choice tasks) significantly contributes to orthographic learning after controlling for decoding skills (Bosse et al., 2013; Cunningham, 2006; Cunningham et al., 2002; Nation et al., 2007). In transparent orthographies, by Grade 6, decoding becomes automatic for most children and fast word recognition relies less on phonological mediation.

Children who enter formal schooling with stronger general orthographic sensitivity may therefore continue to build richer and more precise word representations from subsequent reading experience, supporting this transition toward orthographic access and enabling faster and more fluent word recognition by Grade 6.

Beyond individual word representations, orthographic choice may also partly capture morpho-orthographic knowledge. In Greek, many spelling distinctions are morphologically motivated (i.e., the correct spelling of a word ending often depends on its grammatical category or morphological structure; Protopapas et al., 2013). Orthographic choice may therefore be partly tapping morpho-orthographic knowledge, reflecting sensitivity to the systematic patterns and regularities of the Greek writing system, including morphoorthographic structure, which becomes increasingly more important (e.g., Beyersmann et al., 2012; Brossette et al., 2024; Rastle et al., 2019) as the vocabulary of written texts grows in complexity across the primary school years.

The Limiting Role of Articulation Rate

Articulation rate emerged as a significant unique predictor of Grade 6 word reading fluency, remaining significant across all model variants in which discrete digit naming was retained. This is a novel finding as, to our knowledge, no prior study has demonstrated a unique longitudinal contribution of articulation rate to word reading fluency beyond autoregressive stability and naming speed. Indeed, most prior studies that have included articulation rate as a predictor have consistently found null results (Georgiou et al., 2021; Georgiou & Rothou, 2024; Parrila et al., 2004; but cf. Nikolopoulos et al., 2006). For example, Parrila et al. (2004) reported that articulation rate measured in kindergarten and Grade 1 did not predict unique reading variance in Grades 2 or 3 when phonological awareness or naming speed were included, concluding that, when measured at these time points, articulation rate is not a useful predictor of reading development in unselected samples. Similarly, Georgiou et al. (2021), using the same speech rate task in a 10-year longitudinal study of Greek children, found that articulation rate did not make an independent contribution to the growth of word reading fluency in either early or later primary school. However, neither of these studies included discrete naming in their models. In the present study, articulation rate was also non-significant in the absence of discrete digit naming; it is the suppressor effect of discrete digit naming (i.e., removing from both serial digit naming and articulation rate the variance they share with discrete digit naming, including the time taken to articulate each item) that reveals the unique longitudinal contribution of articulation rate. This suggests that the predictive variance of articulation rate may have been obscured in prior studies by its shared variance with naming speed that was not absorbed by a discrete naming suppressor. Georgiou et al. (2021) reported that controlling for articulation rate was important in their study precisely because oral reading fluency involves articulation and their results showed that RAN's contribution to reading fluency remained significant and independent of articulation rate. This is consistent with the view that serial naming and articulation rate tap partly overlapping but distinct processes: Serial naming captures sequential processing efficiency across a continuous array, while articulation rate captures the efficiency of articulatory execution. Because discrete naming response times were measured to response offset, they include articulation time. The suppression by discrete digit naming therefore removes not only shared naming speed variance but also the time needed to articulate a single item. What remains in articulation rate after this may not be just articulatory speed. It may instead reflect the flexibility of articulatory planning when multiple distinct articulatory patterns need to be produced in continuous succession, a demand shared with the speech rate task and list reading but is absent from discrete naming. This component may constitute a specific constraint on reading fluency that becomes visible only at this level of model specification. Fluent word list reading requires the rapid and sequential articulation of a series of items. Children who can execute these articulatory sequences more efficiently may experience fewer bottlenecks in producing successive words, supporting faster and more fluent reading.

The fact that this effect emerges specifically at Grade 6 and not at Grade 2 is consistent with a developmental shift in the determinants of fluency. In the early grades, when decoding is still effortful, articulatory efficiency may be less of a bottleneck as individual differences in fluency are more strongly driven by phonological decoding. By Grade 6, as decoding becomes more automatized for most children, the speed at which words can be articulated in sequence may become an increasingly limiting factor for individual differences in fluency.

Thus, fluency tasks eventually become "articulation-limited" once readers are sufficiently skilled to read aloud as fast as they can articulate, evidenced by the lack of silent intervals in fluently read passages or serial word reading tasks (Protopapas et al., 2024). This pattern suggests that individual differences in articulatory speed may already have been present at Grade 2 but only became a relevant constraint on fluency once the decoding bottleneck was largely overcome. Whether articulatory speed also becomes more stable as an individual difference across the primary school years, which would also provide a complementary explanation for the Grade 6 specificity of the effect, cannot be addressed with the present data, as articulation rate was measured only at Grade 2. To our knowledge, no longitudinal studies have tracked the development of articulation rate across the primary school years, making it difficult to evaluate this possibility. Future research examining its developmental trajectory alongside reading fluency would be needed to fully understand why articulatory speed emerges as a unique predictor only in the later primary school years and to establish whether the present finding replicates in other samples and orthographies.

Limitations and Caveats

Despite some strong findings with relatively clear potential interpretation, the present study is not without limitations. First, word reading fluency was assessed with a single task at each time point, and residual variances were fixed based on an assumed reliability of .94 derived from a prior study of Greek children using the same test (Protopapas et al., 2007).

Although this is a widely used approach for single-indicator latent variables, the assumed reliability was based on a different sample assessed at overlapping but not identical grades (Grades 2, 3, 4) and not longitudinally. Second, reading fluency was assessed using a word list reading task rather than a text reading task. However, Altani et al. (2020) showed that word list reading fluency becomes increasingly more similar to text reading fluency than to isolated word reading speed across development. This suggests that the present findings may extend to text reading fluency, though replication using passage reading measures is needed.

Third, orthographic knowledge was assessed with a single word-level orthographic choice task. Although the pattern of findings suggests that its longitudinal contribution reflects general sensitivity to the regularities of the writing system rather than item-specific orthographic representations, the present design cannot separate these two components, as both contribute to performance on every item. Finally, although not a limitation of the design, the unique longitudinal contribution of articulation rate to Grade 6 word list reading fluency beyond prior attainment and naming speed is novel and, to our knowledge, has not been reported in prior longitudinal studies. As such, it should be treated with caution until replicated in independent samples.

Conclusions

The present study examined the longitudinal prediction of word reading fluency in

Greek from the very beginning of Grade 2 all the way to Grade 6, using a longitudinal structural equation modeling approach that allowed the unique contributions of a broad set of early cognitive and linguistic skills to be examined simultaneously. The results revealed high autoregressive stability in word reading fluency across the primary school years, even after accounting for measurement error, with early skills accounting for substantial variance beyond prior attainment. Notably, the skills that uniquely contributed to this prediction differed between the early and later grades, from discrete word naming in the early grades to serial digit naming, orthographic knowledge, and articulation rate in the later grades. This shift suggests that reading fluency in early and later primary school may be supported by partly different underlying processes, with word recognition moving from effortful phonological decoding toward more automatized processing subject to efficient cascading as children progress through primary school. Critically, the unique contributions of these predictors were revealed through a suppressor structure involving discrete digit naming and phoneme deletion awareness, extending previously reported concurrent suppression effects to a longitudinal design and demonstrating that the sequential component of serial naming, as well as orthographic knowledge and articulatory speed, each carry specific and lasting influences on fluency development over and above prior attainment and naming speed. These findings contribute to a growing body of evidence on the cognitive and linguistic foundations of reading fluency, and demonstrate that the unique longitudinal contributions of serial naming, orthographic knowledge, and articulation rate to fluency development are only fully revealed when suppressor variables are included in the model to purify their specific contribution, underscoring the value of examining competing predictor formats simultaneously within a single longitudinal framework. Together, these methodological and substantive findings suggest that reading fluency is not a unitary skill that simply accumulates over time, but a developmentally shifting construct whose cognitive and linguistic underpinnings change across the primary school years. Phoneme deletion (words).

Children were presented with 25 spoken words and asked to repeat each word with a specified phoneme or segment omitted. On each trial, children first repeated the whole word and then produced it with the target segment removed. Two practice items preceded the test items. The examiner was permitted to provide clarification on the first twotest items only; no further assistance was given thereafter. No discontinuation criterion was applied. Items were arranged in order of increasing phonological complexity. The first seven items required deletion of a morphological constituent or syllable. Items 8-13 required deletion of the initial or final phoneme from CVC words. Items 14-19 required deletion of the initial or second phoneme from CCVCV words. Items 20-25 required deletion of one of two medial consonants from CVCCVC words. The score was the number of correct responses (maximum = 25). Internal consistency for the present sample was high (Cronbach's α = .86, McDonald's ωT = .88).

Phoneme deletion (pseudowords).

This task was identical in format to the word version, except that all stimuli were pseudowords. Children were informed that the items were unfamiliar and meaningless. As in the word version, children repeated each pseudoword before producing it with the target segment omitted. The examiner was permitted to provide clarification on the first two test items only. The task comprised 24 items with no discontinuation criterion. Items followed the same progression of phonological complexity as the word task: deletion of a constituent or syllable (items 1-6), deletion of the first or final phoneme from CVC pseudowords (A:B::C:?, e.g., to run : runner :: to climb : [climber]). Three practice items with corrective feedback preceded the test items. No feedback was provided during the testing phase, and the task was discontinued after three consecutive errors. The task comprised 14 test items. Half of the items required an inflectional transformation (plural-to-singular, tense alteration, passive-toactive voice) and half a derivational one (adjective-to-verb, adjective-to-noun, noun-to-verb, verb-to-agent noun, verb-to-process noun, and prefixation with "ξε-" [/kse-/], which denotes reversal or undoing of an action, similar to English "un-"). The inflected condition preceded the derived condition because children acquire knowledge of inflected word types earlier than knowledge of derived ones (Carlisle, 1995; Casalis & Louis-Alexandre, 2000; Grigorakis, 2014).

The score was the number of correct responses (maximum = 14). Internal consistency for the present sample was high (Cronbach's α = .84, McDonald's ωT = .87).

Sentence analogy task.

Children were presented with sentence pairs following the same analogy format as the word analogy task (A:B::C:?), but at the sentence level. Two practice items with corrective feedback preceded the 10 test items. No feedback was provided during the testing phase, and the task was discontinued after four consecutive errors. Eight items required tense transformations of the target verb and two items required a change between active and passive voice (one in each direction). The score was the number of correct responses (maximum = 10). Internal consistency for the present sample was high (Cronbach's α = .82, McDonald's ωT = .85).

Morphological derivation of words.

Children were presented with a target word and asked to produce a morphologically related word from the same word family to complete a spoken sentence (e.g., farm, My uncle is a [farmer]). Prior to the task, the examiner introduced the concept of word families (i.e., groups of words sharing form and meaning). Three practice items with corrective feedback preceded the 10 test items. No feedback was provided on test items, and the task was discontinued after four consecutive errors. Items required transformations between parts of speech, including noun-to-verb, agent noun-to-base noun, and verb-to-process noun derivations. The score was the number of correct responses (maximum = 10). Internal consistency for the present sample was moderate (Cronbach's α = .69, McDonald's ωT = .76).

Orthographic choice

Orthographic knowledge was measured with the Orthographic Choice task that was modeled after Olson et al. (1994). The task was administered using DMDX software (Forster & Forster, 2003). On each trial, two words were presented simultaneously on screen, one correctly spelled and one containing a spelling error. The spelling errors involved substitutions of orthographically distinct but phonologically equivalent or plausible letter sequences, targeting children's sensitivity to the conventional spelling of Greek words. Children were asked to select the correctly spelled word by pressing a left or right key. Four practice items preceded the 30 test trials. Each trial remained on screen for a maximum of 15 seconds before automatically advancing, with a 100 ms delay between the participant's response and the onset of the next trial.

The score was the number of correct responses (maximum = 30). Internal consistency for the present sample was moderate (Cronbach's α = .67, McDonald's ωT = .71). In the model, scores were represented by two odd-even split parcels forming a single latent factor.

Speech rate.

Articulation rate was measured with the Speech Rate task (Das et al., 1994). Children were presented with five triplets of three words each and were asked to repeat each triplet as quickly as possible for ten consecutive repetitions. The sequences comprised 14 disyllabic and one monosyllabic word across the five triplets. One practice item preceded the test items and no discontinuation criterion was applied. The examiner timed each triplet from the onset of the first word and recorded the total time required to complete the ten repetitions. Articulation errors were noted during administration. If consecutive errors occurred across two or more repetitions, the child was corrected and the trial was restarted. If errors persisted on the second attempt, that triplet was discontinued and the examiner moved to the next. Speech rate was calculated as repetition cycles per second, adjusted for accuracy by dividing the number of correct repetitions by the total time required to complete each set of repetitions. This yielded five speed scores (one per triplet), which were used as indicators of the latent articulation rate factor.

Serial naming.

Two rapid serial naming tasks were administered: serial naming of objects and serial naming of digits. Both tasks were computerized and administered using DMDX software. In both tasks, children named items presented in a grid as quickly and accurately as possible. All rapid naming scores were expressed as items per second.

Serial naming of objects.

An array of 36 pictures drawn from a set of four objects (time, hand, gift, ball) was displayed simultaneously on screen in a 4 × 9 grid. A fixation cross appeared briefly in the upper left of the screen prior to stimulus onset to attract the gaze to the location of the first item. Children were instructed to name all pictures as quickly and accurately as possible, proceeding from left to right across each row. Total naming time was recorded. The task was administered twice, in the fall and spring of Grade 2. At each occasion, the score was expressed as items per second (the inverse of total naming time). The two occasions served as indicators of a single serial naming of objects latent factor.

Serial naming of digits.

An array of 36 digits drawn from a set of four (2, 3, 5, 6) was displayed simultaneously on screen in a 4 × 9 grid. A fixation cross appeared briefly in the upper left of the screen prior to stimulus onset. Children were instructed to name all digits as quickly and accurately as possible, proceeding from left to right across each row, self-paced. Total naming time was recorded. The task was administered twice, in the fall and spring of Grade 2. At each occasion, the score was expressed as items per second (the inverse of total naming time), and the two occasions served as indicators of a single serial naming of digits latent factor.

Discrete naming.

Four discrete naming tasks were used: discrete naming of objects, digits, pseudowords, and words. All tasks were computerized and administered using DMDX software (Forster & Forster, 2003). Stimuli were presented individually in the centre of the screen, and the examiner advanced to the next item following each response. Four practice items displayed in blue preceded the 36 test items in each task. Children were instructed to respond as quickly and accurately as possible.

Response times, measured from the appearance of the visual stimulus to the offset of the verbal response (therefore including both onset latency and articulation duration) were determined from the recorded audio using CheckVocal (Protopapas, 2007). For each discrete task, the geometric means of odd and even items were separately calculated to serve as two parcel variables indicating the task latent; mean times were inverted and expressed as items per second, to match the serial naming variables.

Discrete naming of objects. Stimuli consisted of the same four object pictures as the serial object naming task (time, hand, gift, ball). A short practice run preceded the task, in which children were told the required name for each object, if necessary, to ensure consistent responses.

Discrete naming of digits. Stimuli consisted of the same four digits as the serial digit naming task (2, 3, 5, 6).

Discrete naming of words. Stimuli were common Greek words selected to be familiar to the children. The words were group-matched to the four number words in length, printed frequency, and phonological complexity (Protopapas et al., 2018).

Discrete naming of pseudowords.

Stimuli were pseudowords created from the word list by same-type phoneme substitution and/or syllable exchange, so that they would match the words in length and orthographic and phonological complexity. Children were informed that the items were unfamiliar and meaningless but could be read normally.

Word reading fluency.

Children's word reading fluency was assessed using a word reading efficiency task similar to the Test of Word Reading Efficiency-2 (TOWRE-2; Torgesen et al., 2012; see Protopapas et al., 2007, for more details about the construction of the test). The task was administered at the end of Grade 2 and in Grades 3 and 6. Children were presented with a list of 112 words arranged in columns in order of increasing difficulty and were instructed to read them aloud as quickly as possible. Reading proceeding from top to bottom within each column before moving to the next.

The examiner timed each attempt for 45 seconds and circled the last word read when time expired. Children were permitted to skip unknown words. Words read with incorrect stress but otherwise correctly were counted as correct. The score was the number of correctly read words within the time limit, expressed as items per second.
