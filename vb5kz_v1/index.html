Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p = .054 This analysis yielded a pooled effect of g = 0.16, 95% CI [-0.00, 0.33], p = .054, with I² = 63.3%.
p = .162 In this combined sensitivity analysis, the pooled effect was g = 0.10, 95% CI [-0.04, 0.23], p = .162, with I² = 56.7%.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Introduction

Executive function (EF) has become one of the hallmark constructs of developmental science (Blair, 2016). Across the past two decades, an extensive body of research has shown that individual differences in EF are associated with a wide range of developmental outcomes, including school readiness and academic achievement (Ahun et al., 2024; Willoughby et al., 2019), emotion understanding and emotion regulation, prosocial behavior, peer acceptance, the ability to deceive strategically, internalizing and externalizing symptoms, and inattentive and hyperactive behaviors (Diamond, 2013; Stucke & Doebel, 2024). Given these broad associations, EF is widely regarded as a fundamental predictor of children's adaptive behavior across cognitive, social, emotional, and educational domains.

Although different theoretical frameworks emphasize distinct components and mechanisms, there is broad agreement that EF refers to top-down control processes that support goal-directed behavior by enabling individuals to regulate their thoughts and actions in accordance with current goals (Cirino, 2023; Doebel, 2020). The influential framework proposed by Miyake et al. (2000) conceptualizes EF as comprising three core components: inhibitory control, which allows individuals to suppress dominant or automatic responses; working memory, which supports the maintenance and manipulation of goal-relevant information while resisting interference; and cognitive flexibility, which enables adaptive switching between tasks, mental sets, or goals. Other processes, such as planning, relational reasoning, monitoring, and attentional control, have also been proposed as components of EF, although their status remains the subject of ongoing theoretical debate (Baggetta & Alexander, 2016; Bunge, 2024; Diamond, 2013; Starr et al., 2022).

Given the broad range of outcomes associated with EF, understanding its underlying nature has become a major goal of developmental science. For many years, the prevailing assumption was that EF reflects a domain-general capacity, supported by a common set of top-down control processes that can be flexibly deployed across different situations and task contexts (Diamond, 2013). Under this view, improvements in a specific executive process should generalize to other activities that rely on the same underlying mechanisms, regardless of superficial differences between tasks or contexts (Diamond & Lee, 2011). This assumption has been highly influential, motivating the development of numerous interventions designed to strengthen children's executive functioning, with the expectation that gains would transfer beyond the trained activities and ultimately promote broad improvements in children's cognitive, academic, and socioemotional functioning (Diamond & Lee, 2011; Diamond, 2025). However, a growing body of evidence has challenged this prediction. Recent meta-analyses (Gobet & Sala, 2023; Kassai et al., 2019; Takács & Kassai, 2019) have consistently shown that, although interventions often improve performance on tasks that closely resemble the trained activities, evidence for generalized transfer to untrained measures is typically small or absent. These findings have raised fundamental questions about the nature of EF, the mechanisms through which executive skills develop and transfer, and about how intervention effects should be evaluated.

Importantly, interventions designed to improve EF are far from homogeneous. Diamond and Lee (2011) identified a broad range of approaches, including computerized cognitive training, aerobic exercise and sports, martial arts and mindfulness practices, and school curricula designed to foster executive and self-regulatory skills. These approaches differ substantially in both their proximal targets and the contexts in which executive skills are practiced. Computerized training typically involves intensive and adaptive practice of relatively specific processes, such as working memory or inhibitory control, whereas physical activity interventions aim to support EF through cognitively and physically demanding activities. Other approaches, including mindfulness and martial arts, combine executive demands with attention regulation, self-monitoring, and behavioral control. Comprehensive school curricula, in contrast, embed opportunities to practice EF within classroom routines, academic activities, and social interactions, potentially allowing executive skills to be recruited repeatedly in contexts resembling those in which children are ultimately expected to use them (Diamond & Lee, 2011).

Evidence from performance-based outcomes suggests that these approaches may also differ in their effectiveness and in the extent to which their benefits generalize. Diamond and Lee (2011) noted that successful programs tended to involve repeated practice, progressively increasing demands, and sustained opportunities to exercise executive skills, while also emphasizing that different approaches varied in the breadth of their effects. Subsequent meta-analytic evidence has reinforced the importance of distinguishing among intervention approaches. Takács and Kassai (2019), for example, found that intervention effects varied according to intervention type and the executive outcome assessed, while evidence for far transfer was generally limited. Such variability is theoretically important because interventions that train EF in relatively narrow contexts may produce different patterns of transfer from programs that embed EF within broader educational, social, or self-regulatory activities. Thus, evaluating only an overall intervention effect may obscure meaningful differences in whether particular approaches translate into changes beyond the activities directly trained.

The ongoing debate regarding the generalizability of intervention effects is based largely on evidence derived from performance-based laboratory tasks. Most influential meta-analyses evaluating the effectiveness of EF interventions have relied exclusively on these measures to estimate both near-and far-transfer effects (Kassai et al., 2019; Takács & Kassai, 2019). Laboratory tasks are administered under highly standardized conditions and typically require participants to produce a single correct response or optimize response speed according to explicit task rules (Dang et al., 2020). Classic examples include the Stroop task as a measure of inhibitory control (Scarpina & Tagini, 2017), the Corsi Block-Tapping Test as a measure of working memory (Corsi, 1973), and the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test as an index of cognitive flexibility (Grant & Berg, 1948).

Although these paradigms have substantially advanced the understanding of EF, they are designed to capture children's performance under optimal testing conditions rather than the way EF is expressed during everyday activities. Moreover, individual laboratory tasks frequently suffer from task impurity, reflecting multiple EFs simultaneously, and often show only modest reliability and weak correlations with one another, particularly when used in isolation to represent a single executive component (Baggetta & Alexander, 2016; Bunge, 2024; Dang et al., 2020; Miyake & Friedman, 2012). These measurement characteristics have important implications for how the intervention literature has conceptualized transfer. If performance-based tasks capture only one manifestation of EF, then the absence of transfer from a trained task to a different laboratory task cannot, by itself, establish that an intervention failed to produce broader changes in executive functioning. Children may show little improvement on an untrained cognitive task while nevertheless becoming better able to regulate their behavior, pursue goals, or manage executive demands in everyday contexts. Thus, the prevailing conclusion that EF interventions show limited generalizability may partly reflect the outcomes through which generalization has traditionally been evaluated. Assessing transfer therefore requires examining not only whether training improves performance on other laboratory tasks, but also whether intervention-related changes become observable in children's functioning outside the laboratory.

Report measures, completed by caregivers, teachers, or trained observers, capture how children deploy EF during daily activities, providing a window into the application of EF in ecologically meaningful contexts (Toplak et al., 2013). Although these measures were historically viewed as less objective than performance-based tasks, this assumption has increasingly been challenged. Recent methodological work has argued that directreport measures often offer important advantages over indirect performance indicators, including greater reliability, stronger predictive validity, and increased flexibility for capturing behaviors as they naturally occur (Corneille & Gawronski, 2024). These characteristics may be particularly relevant when the primary question is not whether a child can demonstrate EF under optimal testing conditions, but whether executive skills are successfully translated into everyday goal-directed behavior.

Among behavioral measures of executive function, the Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function (BRIEF) (Gioia et al., 2000) is by far the most widely used instrument and has been extensively employed in both clinical and intervention research.

Other questionnaires have since been developed to assess executive functioning in everyday contexts, expanding the range of available measures. More recently, Obradović and colleagues (2026) introduced the Executive Function from Observation and Reflection Tool (EFFORT), a freely available instrument that differs from many existing measures by adopting a strengths-based approach, emphasizing children's successful use of executive skills in everyday situations rather than focusing primarily on executive difficulties. The development of measures such as EFFORT reflects a broader shift toward assessing how EF is expressed in real-world contexts and across diverse cultural settings (also see Laurence & Bailey, 2026).

The growing recognition of behavioral report measures as valuable indicators of EF raises an important conceptual question: to what extent do they assess the same construct as performance-based laboratory tasks? Although both approaches are intended to measure EF, they differ substantially in what they require from children and in the contexts in which behavior is elicited or observed. Performance-based tasks assess how children deploy executive processes in response to explicit goals and constraints imposed by a highly standardized testing situation. Importantly, performance in these tasks should not necessarily be interpreted as reflecting a child's maximal executive capacity, as it may also depend on motivation, engagement, familiarity with the testing context, and other situational factors (Doebel, 2020). Behavioral reports, in contrast, aggregate observations of how children regulate their behavior across the varied demands of everyday life, including home and school contexts (Toplak et al., 2013). Consequently, rather than providing interchangeable estimates of an underlying executive ability, the two approaches may capture distinct, albeit related, manifestations of executive functioning.

Consistent with this view, studies have repeatedly found only modest associations between laboratory tasks and behavioral ratings of executive function (Toplak et al., 2013; Dang et al., 2020). Rather than indicating that one approach is superior to the other, these findings suggest that each method provides unique information (Friedman & Banich, 2019; Laurence et al., 2026). Laboratory tasks are particularly useful for assessing the efficiency with which individuals recruit specific EFs under controlled demands and may be especially informative for predicting performance in situations requiring the successful completion of clearly defined goals. Behavioral reports, in contrast, integrate executive functioning across diverse situations, goals, and environmental demands and have often demonstrated stronger associations with broader patterns of everyday functioning and clinically meaningful outcomes (Toplak et al., 2013; Dang et al., 2020). Consistent with this distinction, Laurence et al. (2026) found that laboratory EF tasks were more predictive of goal achievement during a naturalistic task, whereas self-reported EF was more strongly associated with broader patterns of attentional engagement. Taken together, these findings reinforce the view that performance-based tasks and behavioral reports provide complementary, rather than interchangeable, information about executive functioning.

If laboratory tasks and behavioral report measures capture related but distinct manifestations of EF (Dang et al., 2020; Friedman & Banich, 2019; Laurence et al., 2026; Toplak et al., 2013), an important gap in the intervention literature becomes apparent.

Despite the growing recognition that behavioral ratings provide valuable information about children's executive functioning in everyday contexts, no previous meta-analysis has focused primarily on questionnaire-and rating scale-based outcomes. This omission is particularly important because intervention effects may differ depending on how EF is assessed. Improvements that are not readily detected by performance-based tasks may nevertheless become apparent in children's everyday functioning. Conversely, improvements on trained or closely related laboratory tasks may reflect near transfer without producing meaningful changes in how children regulate their behavior at home, at school, or in other everyday contexts. Thus, establishing that an intervention improves performance on EF tasks is not equivalent to demonstrating that it improves children's executive functioning in daily life. Existing quantitative reviews based predominantly on performance measures may therefore provide only a partial picture of the extent to which EF interventions translate into meaningful behavioral change.

The present study addresses this gap by conducting a systematic review and metaanalysis of interventions designed to improve EF in typically developing children, focusing exclusively on behavioral report measures as intervention outcomes.

Specifically, we synthesized evidence from studies using questionnaires, inventories, and rating scales completed by caregivers, teachers, or other informants. By shifting the focus from laboratory performance to everyday executive functioning, this study provides a complementary perspective on the effectiveness of EF interventions and contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of how intervention gains manifest in children's daily lives.

Evaluating intervention effects through behavioral reports introduces another potential source of variability: who observes the child's behavior. Parents, teachers, children themselves, and trained observers encounter children in different contexts and may therefore have access to different manifestations of executive functioning. Parent ratings predominantly reflect behavior within family and home environments, whereas teachers observe children under the sustained cognitive, behavioral, and social demands of the classroom (and also have good reference to compare it with other children). Ratings may consequently differ not because one informant is necessarily more accurate, but because executive behavior is expressed differently across settings and because informants have different opportunities, expectations, and reference points for evaluating it. Informant effects may be particularly consequential in intervention studies, where teachers or parents may also be aware of, or directly involved in, the intervention.

Accordingly, determining whether estimated intervention effects vary according to the source of behavioral ratings is important both for understanding the contextual expression of EF and for evaluating potential methodological influences on intervention estimates.

Informants were classified as parents, teachers, researchers, or children. This classification was performed because the perspective from which children's executive functioning is evaluated may influence observed intervention effects and therefore represented a theoretically relevant moderator.

Effect Size Computation

The primary outcome measure was the standardized mean difference corrected for small sample bias (Hedges' g). Because the included studies reported intervention effects using different statistical metrics, all outcomes were converted to a common effect size metric to ensure comparability across studies. Positive effect sizes indicated better executive functioning following the intervention.

When post-intervention means and standard deviations were available, standardized mean differences were computed directly. Studies reporting F statistics were converted to t-values and subsequently transformed into Hedges' g. When standardized effect sizes (e.g., Cohen's d) were reported, these values were converted directly to Hedges' g. For studies reporting pre-post designs without sufficient post-test statistics, standardized mean change scores were calculated using the procedures implemented in the metafor R package, assuming a pre-post correlation of r = .70 when this value was not reported. This assumption is consistent with recommendations for synthesizing repeated-measures outcomes when correlations are unavailable.

Whenever multiple behavioral report outcomes were reported within the same study, all eligible effect sizes were retained for analysis rather than averaging outcomes or selecting a single measure. This approach preserved the available information while allowing the statistical dependence among effect sizes to be modeled explicitly through the multilevel meta-analytic framework described below.

Statistical Analysis

Because many studies reported multiple behavioral report outcomes derived from the same sample, data were synthesized using a three-level random-effects meta-analysis.

Behavioral report measures of executive functioning frequently yield multiple correlated scores, such as subscales assessing inhibition, working memory, cognitive flexibility, emotional control, or global executive function. Consequently, a single study often contributed several eligible effect sizes derived from the same participants and, in many cases, from the same measurement instrument. These effect sizes cannot be considered statistically independent because they share common sources of variance.

Traditional meta-analytic approaches typically address this dependency by averaging multiple outcomes into a single effect size or selecting only one outcome per study. Although these approaches simplify the data structure, they also discard potentially valuable information regarding different dimensions of executive functioning. To retain all eligible outcomes while appropriately accounting for their statistical dependence, the present study employed a three-level random-effects model.

The three-level model simultaneously estimates variance at three hierarchical levels: sampling variance (Level 1), within-study variability among multiple effect sizes reported by the same study (Level 2), and between-study heterogeneity (Level 3). This approach allowed all eligible behavioral report outcomes to be included in the quantitative synthesis without violating the assumption of independence among effect sizes.

Overall intervention effects were estimated using inverse-variance weighting under a random-effects framework and are reported as Hedges' g with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic, representing the proportion of total variability attributable to true differences in effect sizes rather than sampling error. All analyses were conducted in R (Version 4.6.0; R Core Team, 2026) using the meta package (Balduzzi et al., 2019).

Potential publication bias was evaluated through visual inspection of funnel plots.

Funnel plot asymmetry was examined to assess the possibility of small-study effects or selective publication. Because the number of included studies was relatively modest, publication bias was interpreted cautiously and considered alongside the overall pattern of evidence.

Assessment instruments

The BRIEF was by far the most frequently used behavioral report measure, appearing in 15 of the 19 studies included in the meta-analysis (78.9%).

Thus, the evidence synthesized in the present review was largely based on BRIEF scores, with comparatively limited representation of alternative behavioral measures of executive functioning.

Parents and teachers were the primary sources of information about children's executive functioning. Parent ratings were included in 10 studies, whereas teacher ratings were included in nine. Two studies used child self-reports, and one relied on ratings completed by researchers. Sensitivity Analyses

Three sensitivity analyses were conducted separately to evaluate the robustness of the primary findings, followed by a combined analysis applying all three exclusion criteria simultaneously (see Table 2). First, the study in which behavioral ratings were completed by non-blinded researchers was excluded to examine the potential influence of detection bias. The pooled effect was g = 0.12, 95% CI [0.01, 0.24], p = .040, with I² = 60.9%. Second, interventions delivered indirectly through adults rather than directly to children were excluded. This analysis yielded a pooled effect of g = 0.16, 95% CI [-0.00, 0.33], p = .054, with I² = 63.3%. Third, the unusually large GEC effect reported by Spawton-Rice and Walker (2022), which could not be independently verified, was excluded while retaining the remaining outcomes from the study. The resulting pooled effect was g = 0.16, 95% CI [0.01, 0.31], p = .031, with I² = 58.3%. Finally, all three exclusion criteria were applied simultaneously. In this combined sensitivity analysis, the pooled effect was g = 0.10, 95% CI [-0.04, 0.23], p = .162, with I² = 56.7%. Overall, effect estimates across the sensitivity analyses ranged from g = 0.10 to 0.16, with heterogeneity remaining moderate across models.

Discussion

Principal Findings

The present meta -analysis examined whether interventions targeting EF produce changes that are detectable in children's everyday behavior, as assessed through questionnaires and behavioral rating inventories.

Across 19 studies, 83 effect sizes, and 2,142 participants, EF interventions produced a small but statistically significant positive effect (g = 0.17). This finding provides evidence that intervention-related improvements are not necessarily restricted to performance on structured cognitive tasks but can also be observed, albeit modestly, in children's functioning across everyday settings. The effect remained statistically significant after excluding the only study in which non-blinded [0.00, 0.33] [0.04, 0.23] researchers completed the outcome ratings, although the pooled estimate decreased to g = 0.12. Thus, the overall conclusion was not entirely dependent on this potentially biased study. This result is particularly noteworthy when considered alongside previous metaanalytic findings. Takács and Kassai (2019), for example, did not find convincing evidence that EF interventions generalized consistently beyond the skills directly trained. By focusing specifically on behavioral reports, the present review addressed a different but closely related question: whether intervention effects become sufficiently generalized and sustained to be noticed by adults or children in ordinary environments. The small positive effect suggests that at least some interventions may influence everyday manifestations of executive functioning.

At the same time, its modest magnitude indicates that producing observable changes in daily behavior is considerably more difficult than improving performance under structured and highly supportive testing conditions. The substantial heterogeneity across effect sizes further qualifies this conclusion. Intervention effects were not uniform, and the pooled estimate likely summarizes programs that differed considerably in their content, duration, intensity, delivery agents, targeted skills, outcome measures, and implementation settings. Some interventions may have created repeated opportunities for children to apply EF skills across meaningful situations, whereas others may have trained relatively isolated processes with limited support for generalization. Differences in informants, measurement sensitivity, comparison conditions, participant characteristics, and risk of bias may also have contributed to the variability. Consequently, the average effect should not be interpreted as indicating that all EF interventions produce small improvements. Rather, it suggests that modest changes in everyday EF behavior are possible, while the conditions under which such changes emerge remain insufficiently understood. Importantly, the small magnitude of the effect does not necessarily imply that the interventions were inconsequential. Behavioral reports integrate observations across extended periods, multiple demands, and complex social environments. For an intervention-related improvement to affect these ratings, children must not only acquire or strengthen a cognitive skill but also recognize when it is useful, apply it across different goals and contexts, and sustain its use despite distractions, habits, emotional demands, and variations in adult support. Behavioral change therefore represents a demanding criterion for intervention success. From this perspective, the present findings offer cautious evidence of transfer to everyday functioning while simultaneously emphasizing the limits of the existing intervention literature. Everyday Executive Functioning Remains an Understudied Outcome One of the most striking findings of the present review was not the magnitude of the intervention effect itself, but rather the limited number of studies available for synthesis. Although systematic reviews of EF interventions have identified dozens of eligible randomized and quasi-experimental studies, only 19 met the criteria for the present meta-analysis because they included behavioral report measures of executive functioning. For example, Takács and Kassai (2019) identified approximately 90 eligible intervention studies, whereas fewer than one quarter of that number were available for the current review. This discrepancy suggests that, despite widespread interest in improving children's executive functioning, relatively little attention has been devoted to determining whether intervention-related gains become apparent in children's everyday behavior.

This imbalance has important implications for the field. Performance-based tasks and behavioral reports capture related but distinct aspects of executive functioning (Laurence et al., 2026; Toplak et al., 2019). Laboratory tasks typically evaluate children's ability to deploy EF under highly structured conditions, where goals are explicit, distractions are minimized, instructions are standardized, and experimenters provide continuous guidance and monitoring. In contrast, behavioral questionnaires require informants to judge how children apply executive skills across the complex, variable, and often unpredictable demands of daily life. Another important finding of this review was the overwhelming reliance on a single behavioral report instrument. Nearly 80% of the included studies used the BRIEF (Gioia et al., 2000), whereas only four studies employed alternative measures.

Consequently, the conclusions of the present meta-analysis largely reflect intervention effects as captured by the BRIEF rather than by behavioral measures more broadly.

Although the BRIEF has become the most widely used questionnaire for assessing EF in children, its predominance raises important questions regarding whether the field is relying on instruments that are optimally suited for evaluating intervention-related change.

The BRIEF was originally developed to identify EF difficulties in everyday life and has demonstrated substantial clinical utility across a variety of neurodevelopmental conditions. Its emphasis on identifying problems in children's everyday functioning has contributed greatly to both research and clinical practice. However, this orientation may also present challenges when evaluating interventions conducted with typically developing children. In community samples, many participants begin with relatively few executive difficulties, leaving limited opportunity for measurable reductions in deficits.

Consequently, intervention-related improvements may be more difficult to detect when the primary outcome measure focuses predominantly on the absence of problems rather than the development of increasingly sophisticated executive behaviors.

An additional consideration concerns how behavioral measures represent developmental change. The BRIEF is age-normed, such that standardized scores indicate children's executive difficulties relative to other children of the same age rather than their absolute level of EF. Consequently, normative improvements in executive behavior that occur as children develop are not necessarily reflected as improvements in standardized BRIEF scores. This feature is valuable for identifying clinically meaningful difficulties relative to developmental expectations, but it may be less well suited to characterizing the developmental progression of EF or detecting intervention-related gains that occur within the normative range.

Recent advances in questionnaire development suggest an alternative perspective.

Instruments such as the Ratings of Everyday Executive Functioning (REEF; Nilsen et al., 2017) and, more recently, the EFFORT (Obradović et al., 2026) were developed to assess children's successful application of executive functioning in everyday contexts rather than emphasizing executive dysfunction. EFFORT, in particular, was designed as a publicly available, culturally adaptable instrument that conceptualizes executive functioning as observable goal-directed behavior and evaluates the degree of independence with which children demonstrate these behaviors across everyday situations. Rather than asking whether children exhibit executive difficulties relative to age-based expectations, its response format focuses on how much adult support children require to successfully regulate their behavior. This approach may provide a more direct representation of EF as a developmental process, in which increasing competence is reflected not simply by the absence of difficulties but by children's progressively more independent deployment of executive skills.

Whether strength-focused measures are more sensitive to intervention effects remains an empirical question. At present, there is insufficient evidence to conclude that instruments such as REEF or EFFORT would yield larger intervention effects than deficit-oriented questionnaires such as the BRIEF. Nevertheless, these newer measures may offer important conceptual and methodological advantages for intervention research.

Measuring Everyday Behavior Comes with Methodological Challenges

Although behavioral reports provide an important window into children's everyday executive functioning, they also introduce methodological challenges that differ substantially from those encountered in studies relying exclusively on laboratory tasks.

Chief among these is the difficulty of maintaining blindness during outcome assessment.

Whereas many performance-based tasks can be administered and scored by assessors who are unaware of participants' intervention status-or even scored automatically through computerized procedures-behavioral questionnaires depend on informants who frequently know whether children have participated in an intervention.

This challenge is particularly evident in school-based intervention research.

Teachers often implement the intervention themselves or work closely with those who do, making complete masking practically impossible. Similarly, parents may receive intervention materials, participate in training sessions, or observe changes in their children's school activities, reducing the likelihood that they remain unaware of treatment allocation. Consequently, behavioral ratings may reflect not only genuine changes in children's executive functioning but also alterations in adults' expectations, attention to children's behavior, or standards used to evaluate that behavior. These influences are difficult to eliminate entirely and represent an inherent challenge when behavioral reports constitute primary outcome measures.

The findings of the present review illustrate this issue. Reporting of rater blinding was uncommon among the included studies. Only two of the 19 studies explicitly reported that behavioral ratings were completed by blinded raters, and one of these did not describe how blinding was achieved. Conversely, two studies explicitly stated that raters were not blinded to intervention status, while the remaining studies did not clearly report whether those completing the behavioral measures were aware of group allocation. This lack of information makes it difficult to determine the extent to which expectancy or detection bias may have influenced the available evidence. Notably, the only study in which researchers completed the behavioral ratings used non-blinded raters and produced substantially larger effects than studies relying on parent, teacher, or child reports.

Although this finding should be interpreted cautiously because it was based on a single study, it is noteworthy that removing this study reduced the overall pooled effect while leaving the general pattern of results unchanged. This suggests that the principal conclusions of the review were not driven solely by a potentially biased source of outcome assessment, while simultaneously highlighting the importance of carefully considering who evaluates intervention outcomes. Nevertheless, this pattern underscores both the importance of considering who evaluates intervention outcomes and the need for more transparent reporting of rater blinding in future studies.

Rather than suggesting that behavioral reports should be avoided, these findings underscore the need for more rigorous approaches to outcome assessment. Future intervention studies should, whenever feasible, separate intervention delivery from behavioral evaluation, incorporate independent or multiple informants, and complement behavioral questionnaires with objective measures such as laboratory tasks or structured behavioral observations. Converging evidence from multiple sources is likely to provide a more accurate picture of intervention-related change than reliance on any single type of assessment. Such multimethod approaches may also help distinguish genuine improvements in children's executive functioning from changes in how adults perceive and evaluate children's behavior following intervention.

Importantly, this challenge extends beyond EF research and reflects a broader issue in developmental intervention science. Whenever the outcomes of interest concern children's behavior in naturalistic settings, the individuals best positioned to observe those behaviors are often those most familiar with the intervention itself. The goal, therefore, should not be to eliminate behavioral reports-which remain indispensable for understanding children's functioning in everyday life-but rather to recognize their strengths and limitations and to interpret them alongside complementary sources of evidence.

Which Interventions Are Most Likely to Promote Everyday Executive

Functioning?

Although the relatively small number of studies within each intervention category precludes strong conclusions regarding comparative effectiveness, the moderator analyses revealed meaningful differences in the magnitude of intervention effects. EFspecific curricula produced the largest pooled effect, whereas computerized cognitive training demonstrated comparatively small and non-significant effects. Strategy teaching programs and mindfulness-based interventions yielded intermediate estimates, although confidence intervals frequently overlapped and substantial heterogeneity was observed within several categories. These findings should therefore be interpreted as suggestive patterns rather than definitive evidence of intervention superiority.

One possible explanation for these differences concerns the extent to which interventions encourage children to apply executive functioning across meaningful, everyday situations (Diamond, 2025; Zelazo et al., 2024). EF-specific curricula are typically embedded within children's regular educational experiences and provide repeated opportunities to practice planning, inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility, and self-regulation while pursuing authentic classroom goals. Rather than exercising isolated cognitive processes, these programs often require children to recruit executive functioning during activities that are socially meaningful, emotionally relevant, and repeatedly encountered throughout the school day. Such repeated opportunities for application may facilitate the transfer of executive skills beyond the immediate training context.

In contrast, computerized cognitive training generally emphasizes repeated practice of relatively specific cognitive processes under highly structured conditions.

Although this approach may strengthen performance on tasks that closely resemble the trained activities, generalization to children's everyday behavior has proven more difficult to demonstrate. The present findings are therefore broadly consistent with previous metaanalytic evidence suggesting that improvements observed during computerized training do not necessarily translate into meaningful changes outside the training environment (Takacz & Kassai, 2019). Importantly, however, the small number of computerized interventions included in the present review limits the strength of this conclusion, and future comparative studies are needed to determine under which conditions computerized approaches may promote broader behavioral transfer.

More broadly, the present findings align with contemporary theoretical perspectives that conceptualize executive functioning as fundamentally embedded within goals, contexts, and social environments rather than as a collection of isolated cognitive capacities (Doebel, 2020). From this perspective, successful transfer depends not only on strengthening EF but also on creating opportunities for children to recognize when those processes are relevant, recruit them across different situations, and gradually integrate them into habitual patterns of behavior (Zelazo et al., 2024). Interventions that explicitly support this process of generalization may therefore be better positioned to produce improvements that become visible in everyday functioning.

At the same time, the substantial heterogeneity observed within intervention categories indicates that intervention labels alone are unlikely to explain differences in effectiveness. Programs classified within the same category varied considerably in duration, implementation, delivery agents, intensity, and instructional approach.

Furthermore, with the exception of MindUP, every intervention program identified in this review was represented by a single study. This lack of replication highlights an important limitation of the current evidence base. Rather than systematically evaluating and refining existing interventions, the field has largely focused on developing new programs, making it difficult to determine whether observed differences reflect the characteristics of intervention categories or the unique features of individual programs (Diamond, 2025).

Greater emphasis on replication, direct comparisons among intervention approaches, and careful identification of the active ingredients responsible for behavioral change will be essential for advancing intervention science.

Directions for Future Research

The findings of the present review also highlight several priorities for advancing research on executive function interventions. First, the existing evidence base remains geographically concentrated. Nearly half of the included studies were conducted in the United States, with relatively few originating from low-and middle-income countries.

This distribution reflects broader trends in developmental science (e.g., Draper et al., 2022; Laurence, 2026; Segretin et al., 2025), where research has historically been conducted predominantly within high-income, Western populations. Because executive functioning develops within cultural, educational, and social contexts (Doebel, 2020), intervention effects observed in one setting may not generalize directly to others (Kassai et al., 2019). Expanding intervention research across a wider range of cultural and socioeconomic environments will therefore be essential for establishing the external validity of current findings and identifying contextual factors that influence intervention effectiveness.

Second, future studies should place greater emphasis on replication. With the exception of MindUP, every intervention identified in this review was evaluated in a single study. Consequently, many conclusions regarding specific intervention approaches rest on isolated findings rather than cumulative evidence. While the development of innovative intervention programs remains important, greater scientific progress is likely to come from systematically replicating, refining, and directly comparing existing approaches. Such efforts would allow researchers to distinguish robust intervention effects from findings that may be specific to individual samples, implementation procedures, or study designs.

Third, intervention studies would benefit from broader and more integrated Finally, future research should move beyond evaluating whether interventions work and focus increasingly on understanding why they work. The considerable heterogeneity observed across studies suggests that broad intervention categories may be less informative than the specific characteristics that promote behavioral change.

Identifying these active ingredients-including opportunities for repeated practice, contextualized goal pursuit, adult scaffolding, opportunities for transfer, and implementation fidelity-may ultimately prove more valuable than continuing to compare broad classes of interventions. Such an approach would contribute to the development of interventions that are both theoretically informed and practically effective across diverse educational and developmental contexts.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present review should be acknowledged when interpreting its findings. First, the relatively small number of eligible studies limited the precision of several analyses, particularly the moderator analyses. Some intervention categories and informant types were represented by only a few studies, and one subgroup (researcher ratings) included a single study. Consequently, comparisons across intervention approaches and informants should be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive.

Second, considerable methodological heterogeneity was present across the included studies. Interventions differed substantially in duration, intensity, implementation setting, delivery agents, and targeted executive function skills. Likewise, behavioral outcomes were assessed using different questionnaires completed by different informants. Although the use of a random-effects three-level meta-analysis allowed us to account for statistical dependence among multiple effect sizes within studies, the pooled estimate necessarily summarizes interventions and measurement approaches that varied in important ways. As a result, the overall effect should be interpreted as an average across diverse intervention contexts rather than as the expected effect of any specific program.

Third, the evidence synthesized in this review relied entirely on published studies.

Although publication bias appeared limited based on visual inspection of the funnel plot, unpublished studies and gray literature were not systematically included. Consequently, the possibility that intervention effects are modestly overestimated cannot be completely excluded.

Conclusions

The present systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized evidence regarding the effects of EF interventions on children's everyday executive functioning as measured through behavioral reports.

Across 19 studies and 83 effect sizes, interventions produced a small but statistically significant improvement in everyday executive functioning, suggesting that intervention-related gains can become observable in children's daily behavior. At the same time, the modest magnitude of this effect indicates that translating cognitive improvements into consistent behavioral change remains a substantial challenge. More importantly, this review highlights several limitations of the current evidence base. Research evaluating behavioral outcomes remains surprisingly scarce compared with the much larger literature relying on performance-based tasks. Existing evidence is heavily dependent on a single behavioral measure, geographically concentrated in high-income countries, and characterized by limited replication of intervention programs. Furthermore, the methodological challenges inherent in measuring everyday behavior underscore the importance of interpreting behavioral reports alongside complementary sources of evidence. Taken together, these findings suggest that the next stage of EF intervention research should move beyond demonstrating improvements on laboratory tasks alone and place greater emphasis on understanding whether, how, and under what conditions these improvements translate into children's everyday lives. Achieving this goal will require more diverse populations, more sensitive and conceptually appropriate behavioral measures, greater methodological rigor, and a stronger commitment to replication.

Ultimately, the success of EF interventions should be judged not only by children's performance in structured testing environments, but also by their ability to regulate their thoughts and actions in the complex settings in which development actually occurs.
