We left out 80 files that software creates by itself, not the authors: operating system (80). They hold nothing a reader can use, but they clutter the repository, and software can mistake them for real files (for example a ._ copy of a .csv for data).

Remove these: Operating system files: macOS adds a __MACOSX folder and a ._ copy of every file it puts in a zip, and .DS_Store files to folders; Windows adds Thumbs.db and desktop.ini. On a Mac, create zips from the command line with zip -r -X, or remove them afterwards with zip -d archive.zip "__MACOSX/*" "*.DS_Store". RStudio session files (.Rproj.user/) hold open tabs and unsaved edits; R history (.Rhistory) lists the commands typed in a session and can hold file paths and passwords; Jupyter checkpoints (.ipynb_checkpoints/) are automatic copies of notebooks; compiled Python files (__pycache__/, .pyc) are recreated when the code runs; open-document lock files (~$...docx, .~lock...#) are left while a document is open; editor backups (file~, .bak, .swp) and temporary files (.tmp, .temp) are copies a program made by itself.

Keep only on purpose: version control folders (.git, .svn, .hg) hold the project’s history, which can include earlier versions of files and data that was later removed; editor settings (.idea, .vscode) can hold paths on your own computer. A .gitignore based on GitHub’s templates (https://github.com/github/gitignore) keeps them out of a repository.

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Affective evaluations play a central role in risk perception, yet it remains unclear whether affect shapes judgments differently when risks are evaluated for oneself versus others. Across two studies, we tested whether hazard-directed affect exerts a differential influence on self-focused and other-focused risk judgments. Study 1 (N = 77) featured a repeated-measures design in which participants rated affect, perceived risk, and perceived benefit for 13 technologies for themselves and for a random other, allowing within-person comparisons of self-and otherfocused judgments. A linear mixed-effects model indicated that although negative affect predicted higher perceived risk and positive affect predicted lower perceived risk, the influence of affect did not differ across judgment targets. With the same design, Study 2 (N = 150) experimentally manipulated hazard-directed affect using negatively versus positively valenced images across five technologies. The positive affect manipulation lowered perceived risk while negative affect increased perceived risk. The influence of the manipulation did not differ across judgment targets. Additionally, we examined whether the inverse association between perceived risk and perceived benefit, an indicator of the affect heuristic, varied by target: evidence for a stronger inverse association for other-focused judgments emerged in Study 1, suggesting affective cues may be relied upon more readily when making judgements for others. This finding did not replicate in Study 2. Together, our findings refine affect-based accounts of risk perception by suggesting affective evaluations of hazards are applied similarly across judgment targets, even when self-other differences in risk level are present.

Affect and Self-Other Risk Judgments: Evidence for Target-Invariant Influence

Judgments of risk are strongly shaped by affective reactions. Alongside deliberative "risk-asanalysis" processes, people often draw on "risk-as-feelings," where experienced feelings provide rapid information about how risky a hazard seems (Loewenstein et al., 2001). In this tradition, affect refers to an evaluative reaction to a stimulus, experienced as its perceived "goodness" or "badness", and can guide judgments even when people have limited knowledge or when decisions are complex (Slovic et al., 2004). Separable from deliberate and experiential components, contemporary models treat affective influences as a distinct ingredient in risk perception (Ferrer et al., 2016). In other words, risk perception is not merely calculation; it is also an affectively infused judgment about potential harm.

A core framework for this idea is the affect heuristic, which proposes that people, instead of rationally reflecting on risks and benefits separately, use their immediate affective evaluation as a cue when judging both risks and benefits (Finucane et al., 2000; Slovic et al., 2007). When a hazard elicits positive affect, it tends to be judged as more beneficial and less risky; when it elicits negative affect, it tends to be judged as less beneficial and more risky (Alhakami & Slovic, 1994; Finucane et al., 2000). While societies seem to accept higher risks for more beneficial activities (Starr, 1969), people perceive that beneficial activities are less risky (Fischhoff et al., 1978; Slovic et al., 2004). As argued by Slovic et al. (2004), because high risks are generally only societally accepted if their benefits are also high, a rational hazarddirected risk and benefit judgment of societal risks (e.g., nuclear energy, vaccines, hydroelectric dams, high-speed rail networks) ought to be positively correlated. The inverse perceived risk-benefit association is thus commonly interpreted as an indicator of an affectbased heuristic being relied upon in decision-making. Supporting this interpretation, Skagerlund et al. (2020) finds this association largely stable across elicitation methods and domains of risk, although its strength varied with individual differences in cognitive reflection abilities. This inverse correlation between perceived risks and benefits has been replicated (Efendić et al., 2021) and expanded upon (Skagerlund et al., 2020) in studies within the affect heuristic literature. Recent experimental work in risk communication research shows affective cues can shape perceived risk: Keller et al. (2006) found that participants exposed to images of flooded houses perceived greater flood risks compared to controls. Complementing this experimental evidence, Västfjäll et al. (2014) showed that incidental negative affect following a natural disaster was associated with higher perceived risks and stronger risk-benefit associations, although this effect varied across domains. Together, this literature indicates affective evaluations, whether measured directly or induced through contextual cues, systematically shape judgments of perceived risk and perceived benefit.

Importantly, risk judgments are not only made for oneself, but also for other people. A broad literature on self-other decision-making suggests judgments and choices for others often diverge from those made for oneself, although this finding is not uniform across different contexts. Rather, meta-analyses suggest self-other differences in decision-making are domaindependent, with no consistent tendency for people to make either more or less risky choices for themselves compared to others (Batteux et al., 2019). Whereas Batteux et al. (2019) work finds no consistent directional self-other discrepancy, this work primarily examined the self-other differences within risky choice paradigms and not the perceived risk level of specific hazards depending on judgment target. Looking beyond risky choice paradigms, self-other distinctions appear in judgments of vulnerability, safety, and health (Stone et al., 2013; Yeater et al., 2009).

Crucially, self-other differences are observed when people judge both risks and benefits. In the case of smoking, smokers perceived greater benefits and lower risks pertaining to smoking for themselves when compared to other smokers (Zlatev et al., 2010). This pattern, in which people perceive their own risk as lower compared to the risks faced by others, is commonly labeled as unrealistic optimism or a self-other discrepancy (Shepperd et al., 2013; Weinstein, 1989) and recent meta-analytic evidence suggests this is reflected in climate-related risk perceptions across datasets and countries (Sandlund et al., 2026). Further, Sandlund et al. (2026) demonstrates this gap between self and other increases as the judgment target becomes more general (e.g., the average person in your country) as compared to more specific (e.g. your neighbor). Thus, prior work shows that self-and other-focused risk judgments often diverge, especially when the "other" is more abstract or socially distant.

Taken together, prior work suggests that affective cues shape judgments of risks and benefits and that risk perceptions differ depending on whether they concern the self or another person. What remains unclear is whether affective cues are weighted differently across these types of judgments. Existing theory suggests two competing possibilities. One possibility is that affective evaluations exert a weaker influence when people judge risks for others. Risk-asfeelings accounts emphasize that affective influences are strongest when outcomes are vivid and personally experienced (Loewenstein et al., 2001). Judgments about personal risks may therefore provide the most experientially rich reference point, whereas judgments about others hinge more on deliberate or analytical processing. This line of argument aligns with research showing that self-other differences in risky choices increase in magnitude as the target of the judgment becomes more general or psychologically distant (Hsee & Weber, 1997). Relatedly, this account is consistent with work on hot-cold empathy gaps and predictions of others' risky preferences, suggesting that individuals underestimate the influence of affective states when these states are not personally experienced, leading to more deliberate or analytically mediated rather than affective judgments about others (Faro & Rottenstreich, 2006; Loewenstein, 1996).

A different line of thinking follows classic affect-as-information accounts. When people judge risks for general or underspecified others, a lack of concrete individuating information about judgment targets may make one's feelings about the hazard itself more accessible (Schwarz & Clore, 1983 , 2003). From this perspective, hazard-directed affect need not be attenuated by psychological distance or abstraction; it may instead be applied similarly across judgment targets. A stronger interpretation would imply that because hazard-directed affect can serve as a general cue for decision-making, underspecified or low-information targets may increase reliance on affective cues when less information is available for deliberate risk assessment (Finucane et al., 2000; Slovic et al., 2007).

Prior work within risky choice paradigms has largely examined choices pertaining to the risk preferences of others, while affect-heuristic research has examined how affective cues influence the perception of risks and benefits. Moreover, empathy-gap and risky-choice studies often concern the projection of one's own preferences or affective evaluation onto another person, rather than the influence of hazard-directed affect toward specific societal risks. This gap in current knowledge, together with the competing theoretical accounts outlined above, leaves an unresolved question: Do affective evaluations shape perceived risk differently depending on whether the judgment is self-focused or other-focused? Answering this question is theoretically relevant to understanding the role of affect in risk judgments concerning others. It is also practically relevant in contexts where risk judgments about others inform policy interventions and risk communication (Slovic, 1987).

To address this research question, we tested whether affective evaluations of hazards operate similarly across targets (self vs. other), and whether a key marker of affect-heuristic processing, the inverse relationship between perceived risk and perceived benefit, differs across targets. Across two studies, participants evaluated technologies and provided perceived risk and benefit judgments for themselves and for another person (a random person in the United States). Study 1 assessed self-reported affective evaluation of each technology; Study 2 experimentally manipulated affect via valenced imagery. Given the theoretical ambiguity outlined above, we tested two non-directional hypotheses: (H1) affective influence on perceived risk differs as a function of target (self vs. other), and (H2) the inverse risk-benefit relationship differs as a function of target. a a The data were originally collected as part of a preregistered project addressing a different research question and aim. The present article uses the same dataset to examine a distinct research question that was not specified in the original preregistration.

Study 1 Method

This within-subjects online survey followed a randomized 13 (technology) x 2 (Target: self & other) design with perceived risk as the outcome variable.

Participants

Seventy-seven human participants aged 19-79 years old (M = 39.61, SD = 14.53) from the United States were recruited via prolific (www.prolific.com), 48 of which identified as female, 28 identified as male, and one identified as non-binary. The average completion time for the survey was approximately 17 minutes and data collection occurred on the 20th of March 2025 via Qualtrics (Qualtrics, 2025). Inclusion criteria included being fluent in English, residing in the United States, and being above 18 years of age. All participants received monetary compensation amounting to £12/hour for their involvement in accordance with Prolific fair pay guidelines and institutional ethical standards. Participants were informed of the purpose, aims, and the potential risks and benefits of the study when giving informed consent. The final sample size used in analysis was (n = 77) as all participants fully completed the survey. No power analysis was conducted for this study, post collection a power analysis and simulation concluded that this study was powered to find a small effect size (see OSF).

Procedures and Materials

Participants were presented with 13 different technologies in a randomized order (Table S1).

These technologies were presented one at a time with a short description about what the technology is, and how it works. To measure affect, participants were asked to indicate how positive or negative they felt about the technologies on a nine-point Likert scale (1 = extremely negative,9 = extremely positive) with the prompt "How do you feel about [technology]?". To measure perceived risk and perceived benefit, participants made judgments for both themselves and another random person in the United States: "[For you personally/for a random person in the USA], how [risky/beneficial] would it be if [technology] was implemented in large-scale?". These judgments were made on nine-point Likert scales for both risk (1 = extremely low risk,9 = extremely high risk) and benefit (1 = extremely low benefit,9 = extremely high benefit).

The use of the word "risky" as well as the formulation of the levels of the Likert scales were chosen as they are similar to previous works within the field (Alhakami & Slovic, 1994; Finucane et al., 2000). The order in which these items were presented was randomized and the exact formulation of the items and their Likert points can be found in Table S2.

Data Analysis

Data analysis was conducted using the statistical application R-studios (R Core Team, 2024).

Before any analyses were conducted the dataset was inspected for missing values, of which there were none, and descriptive statistics were generated for demographic variables (age & gender). Given the nested repeated-measure nature of the data in which participants provided multiple ratings across different technologies and targets, a linear mixed-effects model (LMM) approach was appropriate to test both hypotheses. As there may be asymmetric effects of affect valence: for example, negative affect may steeply increase perceived risk, whereas positive affect may reduce risk judgments more gradually, we employed a piecewise (spline) LMM to account for the potentially distinct slopes for negative and positive affect rather than assuming a single linear relationship. As such, the affect variable was split at the midpoint: Negative affect (values below the neutral midpoint, re-coded to reflect increasing negativity) and Positive affect (values above the midpoint, re-coded to reflect increasing positivity). Perceived benefit was grand mean centered prior to analysis to increase interpretability and reduce potential multicollinearity issues. All t-tests used Satterthwaite approximation with REML estimation. Hypothesis 1 used perceived risk as the outcome variable, both affect variables and target were entered as fixed factors along with the Affect(positive/negative) × Target(self/other) interaction. For Hypothesis 2, the outcome variable was perceived risk, fixed factors were perceived benefit and target along with the Benefit × Target interaction. Random intercepts were fit for type of technology and participants in models testing hypothesis 1 & 2.

As a robustness check, an additional analysis for hypothesis 2 was conducted in which positive and negative affect were included as covariates. This analysis examined whether the moderation of the risk-benefit relationship by target remained when affective evaluations were statistically controlled.

Because of the lengthiness and repeated measures nature of the study there were concerns about potential fatigue effects, thus an exploratory LMM analysis was conducted to investigate fatigue. We computed a risk-optimism score (Risk !" #$ -Risk %&'"( ), where negative values indicate lower perceived risk for self than for a random other. Risk-optimism was modeled as a function of viewing order, with random intercepts for participant and technology.

Results

Hypothesis 1. The LMM revealed that perceived risk was strongly predicted by affect: negative affect was associated with higher perceived risk (b = 0.67, p < .001) and positive affect with lower perceived risk (b = -0.61, p < .001). The main effect of target was not significant (b = 0.07, p = .545), indicating no overall difference between self-and other-focused risk judgments when holding affect constant. This pattern was consistent with a baseline model including only target as a predictor, in which target did not significantly predict perceived risk.

More importantly for our hypothesis (H1), neither of the affect (Negative & Positive) × Target interactions were significant (Negative Affect × Target: b = 0.06, p = .467; Positive Affect × Target: b = 0.03, p = .490), indicating that the strength of the affect-risk relationship did not differ significantly between self-and other-focused judgments (Figure 1). Discussion

Study 1 examined whether affective evaluations of hazards shape perceived risk differently depending on whether judgments are made for oneself or for another person.

Consistent with core predictions from the affect heuristic, both negative and positive affect strongly predicted perceived risk. Interestingly, one standard deviation higher in negative affect toward the target hazard increased perceived risk by 0.26 standard deviation while one standard deviation higher positive affect reduced perceived risk with 0.39 standard deviation. This emphasizes that risk assessment is not only driven by negative affect toward the hazard but also from positive affect. This pattern aligns with extensive evidence that affect serves as an evaluative cue in judgments of both risk and benefit, often producing the characteristic inverse relationship between perceived risk and perceived benefit (Alhakami & Slovic, 1994; Finucane et al., 2000; Keller et al., 2006; Slovic et al., 2004 Slovic et al., , 2007)).

Contrary to the prediction that affective influence should differ as a function of target (H1), affect did not interact with target: the affect-risk association was statistically comparable for self-and other-focused judgments. This null interaction is theoretically informative because "risk-as-feelings" accounts are often interpreted to imply stronger affective influence when judgment targets are more concrete and experientially grounded (Loewenstein et al., 2001).

Similarly, research on self-other discrepancies in risky choice suggests that abstraction can reduce reliance on one's own feelings when predicting others (Hsee & Weber, 1997). The present findings suggest that this general self>other expectation does not straightforwardly extend to contexts where affect is directed toward a shared hazard object. One interpretation is that when affect is an evaluation of the technology itself, it provides a general input that is readily applied to judgments for both self and other.

In addition to this target-invariant affect effect, Study 1 showed evidence for self-other differences in the risk-benefit association: the inverse relationship between perceived risk and perceived benefit was stronger for judgments made for others than for judgments made for oneself. This pattern is consistent with the idea that judgments about unfamiliar others may sometimes show stronger reliance on heuristic signatures (Finucane et al., 2000; Slovic et al., 2007). Importantly, this interaction persisted after controlling for self-reported affect, despite the main effect of perceived benefit becoming non-significant. This suggests that while affective cues account for much of the overall inverse association between risk and benefit, we cannot conclusively say that the differences in the risk-benefit correlation are directly related to the affect heuristic. In other words, the stronger risk-benefit coupling for others in Study 1 is not easily explained as simply "more affect" for other judgments. Instead, it may reflect differences in how participants integrate information about benefits across targets, or differences in uncertainty about others that shift how benefit and risk judgments cohere.

Finally, an exploratory analysis suggested that self-other differences in perceived risk may have narrowed across the repeated sequence, consistent with potential fatigue or adaptation across repeated ratings. Although this effect was weak and should be interpreted cautiously, it highlights a design consideration for repeated-measures risk perception tasks: extensive repetition may reduce target differentiation over time.

To account for this potential limitation and experimentally manipulate affective factors, we designed Study 2 aimed at reducing intensity and testing the causal relationship between affect and perceived risk.

Study 2 Method

Study 2 employed a 2 (Affect: positive & negative) x 2 (Target: self & other) x 5 (Technology: electric flying, small modular reactors, hydropower energy production, biomass energy production, self-driving taxi) mixed design with target, technology, and manipulation as within-subjects. The outcome variable was perceived risk.

Participants

One-hundred-fifty participants aged 18-79 years old (M = 40.99, SD = 13.61) from the United States were recruited via prolific (www.prolific.com). Out of these, 74 identified as female and 76 identified as male. The average completion time for the survey was approximately eight minutes and data collection occurred on the 24th of April 2025 via Qualtrics (Qualtrics, 2025).

The inclusion criteria were identical to Study 1, and all participants received monetary compensation amounting to £13.69/hour for their involvement in accordance with Prolific fair pay guidelines and institutional ethical standards. Participants were informed of the purpose, aims, and the potential risks and benefits of the study when giving informed consent. The final sample size used in analysis was (n = 150) as all participants fully completed the survey. An apriori power calculation was conducted using a small effect size (d = 0.15) and revealed that n = 111 participants were needed to achieve 95% statistical power using the standard 0.05 alpha value.

Procedure and Materials

Drawing from previous methodology on affect manipulation (Efendić 2021; Keller et al., 2006), affect was manipulated through negatively and positively valenced images. Images were generated through the large language model ChatGPT (OpenAI, GPT-4o, 2025) and pre-tested in a separate data-collection procedure where participants (n=75) rated how negatively or positively valenced these images were perceived on a Likert scale (1 = extremely negative to9 = extremely positive). As the juxtaposition between negative and positive affect is paramount for contrasting conditions, technologies and images used as stimulus material were selected based on the difference in mean affect ratings for the most negative and most positive image pairs within each technology. The resulting affective contrast between these pairs of images was substantial (d = 2.02). For a detailed description of the pre-testing procedures, see section 3 in the supplementary materials.

Participants were presented with five different technologies with the largest difference in affect ratings for positive and negative images (electric air travel, biomass energy production, small modular reactors, self-driving taxis, and hydropower energy production) in a randomized block-wise order along with a short description of what the technology is and how it works. For each technology, participants were randomly assigned to either a positive or a negative block. As such, participants were not divided into positive and negative manipulation groups but had an equal probability to view either negative or positive images of any given technology. For positive blocks, participants viewed a positively valenced image of the technology and judged the perceived risks and benefits pertaining to it for themselves (selfjudgment) and for another random person in the United States (other-judgment). For negative blocks, the participants underwent the same procedure as in positive blocks but viewed a negatively valenced image. Perceived risk and perceived benefit were measured identically as in Study 1. See Figure 4 for an example of a negative and positive image for hydropower energy production. Note. Images generated through the use of ChatGPT (4o) (OpenAi, 2025).

Data Analysis

All analyses were conducted using the statistical application software R-studios (R Code Team, 2024). Before any analyses were conducted, the dataset was inspected for missing values, of which there were none, and descriptive statistics were generated for demographic variables (age & gender). As the data structure is identical to Study 1, i.e., repeated measures with random effects, the same methodological choices regarding estimation method and predictor centering were made. However, as this study induced affect through an experimental manipulation instead of self-reported affect, the models were constructed differently.

Hypothesis 1 used perceived risk as the outcome variable and fit the experimental manipulation (induced affect) and target as fixed effects along with the Manipulation × Target interaction.

Hypothesis 2 used perceived risk as the outcome variable and fit perceived benefit and target as fixed effects along with the Benefit × Target interaction. The affective manipulation was included as a covariate to isolate the effect of target on the risk-benefit association. Random intercepts were fit for type of technology and participants in models testing hypothesis 1 & 2.

This causal pattern is consistent with the broader affect heuristic claim that affective evaluations shape perceived risk (Finucane et al., 2000; Slovic et al., 2007), and with risk-asfeelings accounts emphasizing the primacy of affective reactions in risk judgment (Loewenstein et al., 2001).

Despite the successful affect manipulation, Study 2 provided no evidence that affective influence on perceived risk differed between self-and other-focused judgments. The manipulation × target interaction was essentially zero. Together with Study 1, this suggests that hazard-directed affect can influence risk perception robustly while remaining largely invariant to whether the target of judgment is self or other. This result stands in contrast to interpretations that psychological distance or abstraction should attenuate affective influence for others (Loewenstein et al., 2001) and to literature suggesting that abstract targets reduce the use of feelings when predicting others (Hsee & Weber, 1997). Instead, our findings are more consistent with the idea that affective evaluations of hazards may serve as a shared evaluative input that generalizes across targets.

Study 2 did show a self-other difference in overall perceived risk (others judged as more at risk than self), but unlike Study 1 it did not provide clear evidence that the inverse risk-benefit association differed across targets. One plausible methodological explanation is that Study 2 used fewer technologies and experimentally constrained affective variance, potentially changing the covariance structure among affect, perceived risk, and perceived benefit compared to Study 1. Conceptually, differences in whether the risk-benefit association varies by target may depend on how affect is positioned in the causal story: if affect is part of the mechanism generating the inverse risk-benefit pattern, then statistically conditioning on affect will tend to reduce that signature (Finucane et al., 2000; Slovic et al., 2007). This suggests that reporting models both with and without affect adjustment can be informative for interpreting whether target differences in risk-benefit coupling are independent of affect or carried by affect.

General Discussion

Across two studies, we tested whether affective evaluations of hazards influence perceived risk differently when judgments are made for the self versus for another person. In both studies, affect, measured via self-reports (Study 1) or induced experimentally (Study 2), robustly predicted perceived risk in ways consistent with the affect heuristic: positive evaluations were associated with lower perceived risk, and negative evaluations with higher perceived risk (Alhakami & Slovic, 1994; Finucane et al., 2000; Slovic et al., 2007). However, the central moderation prediction was not supported: affective influence on perceived risk did not differ meaningfully between self-and other-focused judgments. Hence, it seems when affect is object directed (i.e., an evaluation of the hazard/technology itself), its influence on perceived risk is target invariant. Affect scaled perceived risk to a similar degree for the self and for another person, even as overall risk levels were higher for "others".

This pattern refines how affect-based theories may be applied to comparative risk judgments. Risk-as-feelings accounts emphasize that affective reactions are strongest when outcomes are vivid, imaginable, and psychologically close (Loewenstein et al., 2001).

Relatedly, self-other research in risky choice suggests that abstraction can reduce the use of one's own feelings when predicting others' preferences (Hsee & Weber, 1997). Taken together, these ideas are often taken to imply that affect should matter more for self-judgments than for judgments about abstract others. The present findings suggest that this inference may not generalize to contexts where affect is integral and object-directed, that is, an evaluation of the hazard itself rather than incidental mood or target-directed emotion. When affect is attached to the technology, it may be judged as relevant to consequences for both self and other, leading to comparable affective influence across targets.

At the same time, both studies showed self-other differences in perceived risk level, with others often judged as more at risk than the self. Such self-other discrepancies are consistent with the broad literature on unrealistic optimism and comparative risk judgment (Shepperd et al., 2013; Weinstein, 1989) and with recent meta-analytic evidence that self-other discrepancies increase with more general or abstract comparison targets in climate risk perception (Sandlund et al., 2026). Our results indicate that self-other differences in baseline risk level can coexist with similar affective "slopes" across targets: people may set a higher baseline for others while still using the same affective input to scale perceived risk up or down across hazards. This separation suggests that comparative asymmetries in risk may not be explained simply by stronger affective weighting for one target than the other but may instead reflect target-related differences in comparative framing, uncertainty, or prior beliefs that shift overall levels.

The inverse association between perceived risk and benefit, often treated as a signature of affect-based judgment, was stronger for other-focused judgments in Study 1 but did not replicate in Study 2. This should be interpreted in relation to prior evidence that the inverse risk-benefit correlation is a stable feature of affective decision-making across elicitation methods domains of risk (Skagerlund et al., 2020). Although the negative association was present, and consistent with the broader literature, the stronger regression slope for otherfocused judgments is anomalous. We see two non-exclusive interpretations. First, design differences likely matter: Study 2 used fewer technologies and constrained affective variance through experimental pairing with valenced images, which can alter covariance among affect, risk, and benefit and reduce opportunities for the target-dependent differences to emerge.

Second, inference about risk-benefit coupling critically depends on how affect is positioned in the model. In Study 1, the stronger inverse association between risk and benefit in judgments for others remained even after positive and negative affect were entered as covariates, despite an attenuation of the fixed effect of perceived benefit. This suggest that target differences in risk-benefit coherence are not reducible to affect alone. Simultaneously, the absence of this interaction effect in Study 2 emphasizes that such differences are not robust across designs and may instead depend on task structure and affective variability. Taken together, these findings caution against treating the risk-benefit correlation as a unitary or contextually invariant signature of affect and highlight the importance of reporting both affect-unadjusted and affectadjusted models when investigating target differences in evaluative judgment. A transparent approach is to report both unadjusted and affect-adjusted models and interpret any target differences in light of that causal positioning.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations qualify the conclusions. First, "the other" was operationalized as a random person in the same country; this may not represent a strongly distant or abstract target.

Prior work suggests that self-other discrepancies can depend on whether the comparison target is abstract versus vivid and concrete (Hsee & Weber, 1997). Future work should systematically manipulate target specificity (e.g., neighbor vs compatriot vs humanity) to test whether affective influence changes as a function of abstraction, consistent with patterns seen in metaanalytic work (Sandlund et al., 2026). Second, the affect measured and manipulated here was integral to the hazard (technology-directed) rather than incidental mood; future research should separate hazard-directed affect from incidental affect and from target-directed emotions (e.g., worry-for-self vs worry-for-other), which may show different target sensitivity (Loewenstein et al., 2001; Schwarz & Clore, 2003). Third, Study 2 lacked a neutral baseline condition, limiting conclusions about whether positive imagery reduced perceived risk, negative imagery increased perceived risk, or both. Addressing these issues will help clarify when affect generalizes across judgment targets and when target differences emerge.

Conclusion

Across two studies, we tested whether affective evaluations of hazards influence perceived risk differently when judgments are made for the self versus for another person.

Affect strongly predicted risk perception in both studies, technologies evaluated more negatively were judged as riskier and those evaluated more positively as less risky, consistent with affect heuristic and risk-as-feelings accounts (Alhakami & Slovic, 1994; Finucane et al., 2000; Loewenstein et al., 2001; Slovic et al., 2007).

However, this influence did not differ by judgment target: affect shaped risk judgments to a comparable degree for self-and otherfocused evaluations. While others were rated as having higher risk vs self, these self-other differences were not explained by differential affective weighting, suggesting that affect may function as a broadly applicable evaluative input to risk perception even when comparative asymmetries in baseline risk are present. These results are of theoretical importance as they challenge the expectation that affective evaluations operate differently depending on judgment target.
