Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

This study addresses a gap in previous research by examining how semantic memory organization relates to conspiracy endorsement and political extremism. A total of N = 98 participants completed the Generic Conspiracist Beliefs (GCB) scale and an animal verbal fluency task (𝑀 𝑎𝑔𝑒 = 27.88, 𝑆𝐷 𝑎𝑔𝑒 = 11.45; 59.18% female; 69.39% European). Of these, n = 96 also completed a self-placement political extremism item. Participants were grouped according to their levels of conspiracy thinking (high or low) and political extremity (moderate or extreme). We hypothesized that 1) political extremism would predict conspiracy thinking, 2) that individuals high in conspiracy thinking would exhibit flexible semantic networks, similar to those found in creative individuals, and that 3) politically extreme participants would exhibit similar semantic patterns to participants high in conspiracy thinking. Semantic networks were constructed from verbal fluency responses, and we compared the respective group networks' clustering coefficient (CC), average shortest path length (ASPL), modularity (Q), and small-world-ness (S). Results showed that political extremism negatively predicted conspiracy thinking.

High conspiracists exhibited more flexible semantic networks, consistent with patterns previously observed in creative individuals. Politically extreme individuals showed efficient global connectivity but lacked local associative clustering. These findings suggest that semantic memory structure is meaningfully linked to conspiratorial and ideological beliefs. Longitudinal research is needed to clarify the causal direction of these associations.

When Creativity Goes Rogue: Semantic Networks in Conspiracy Thinkers and Politically Extreme Individuals

Conspiracy thinking and political extremism are two phenomena that attracted considerable attention in recent research (Douglas, 2021; Swami et al., 2024; van Prooijen et al., 2015). Conspiracy theories are beliefs that certain events or situations are secretly manipulated behind the scenes by powerful agents with malevolent intentions (Wunderlich, 2025). Conspiracy theorists claim that powerful actors intentionally manipulate important events out of self-interest; for example that global warming is a hoax created by fear-mongering scientists (Bale, 2007; Douglas, 2021; Wunderlich, 2025).

In contrast, political extremism refers to the endorsement of ideologically polarized positions and support for radical societal change (van Prooijen & Krouwel, 2019). Individuals with extreme political attitudes report a stronger perception of societal threat (van der Linden et al., 2020; van Prooijen et al., 2015) and exhibit reduced willingness to engage with opposing viewpoints (van Prooijen et al., 2015). Therefore, both conspiracy thinking and political extremism can lead to damaging social consequences, such as social polarization and a distrust of institutions and scientific bodies (Douglas et al., 2019; Lewandowsky et al., 2013). This calls for a deeper understanding of the psychological foundations of both ideological attitudes (Douglas et al., 2017; Del Vicario et al., 2016; Van Prooijen et al., 2015). Accordingly, current research is increasingly shifting towards a cognitive approach to conspiracy thinking, although not yet to political extremism (Del Vicario et al., 2016; Douglas et al., 2017; Stall & Petrocelli, 2022). The current study aims to fill this gap by investigating the semantic organization related to political extremism and conspiracy thinking.

1.1. Conspiracy thinking, political extremism, and semantic organization

Recent research showed that political extremism predicts conspiracy thinking (van Prooijen et al., 2015). This overlap stems from shared cognitive mechanisms, such as the need for certainty and control, and unwarranted confidence in subjective knowledge (van Der Linden et al., 2020; van Prooijen et al., 2015). Political ideology itself can be a motivating factor for conspiracy thinking (Ender et al., 2015). For example, populism, a political strategy based on social polarizing in binary oppositions (i.e., the common people vs. the elite), is an underlying motive for conspiracy endorsement and use (van Prooijen, 2018).

Both right-wing and left-wing populist politicians use conspiracy theories to mobilize support and explain perceived injustices (Christner, 2022). In an analysis of Dutch populist politician Geert Wilders' posts on X, Engel (2023) found that he strategically adjusted his conspiracy rhetoric based on his political party's (Partij Van de Vrijheid; PVV) status. Wilders' use of conspiratorial rhetoric increased pre-elections in 2021 but then declined post-elections. Engel suggests that Wilders used conspiracy rhetoric before the elections to gain votes and portray himself as the outsider against the current "elite" in the Dutch political ground.

Moreover, both conspiracy thinking and political extremism are coping mechanisms that thrive in conditions where participants feel a lack of control. They are deployed to re-establish a sense of control and coherence (Kruglanski et al., 2006). Interestingly, a lack of control is a trigger for creativity and illusory pattern perception (Mulatti & Treccani, 2023). Research also found illusory pattern perception to be a strong predictor of conspiracy belief (van Prooijen et al., 2017).

Recent literature has explored the link between conspiracy thinking and creativity, particularly divergent thinking. Divergent thinking refers to the generation of novel ideas by moving beyond established norms, and is widely used as an indicator of creativity in scientific research (Bruce et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2020). Some researchers have suggested that conspiracy thinking is a form of divergent thinking gone awry--a "dark side" of creativity (Bonetto & Arcizewski, 2021; Zwaan, 2022).

Similar to divergent thinking, conspiracy thinking rejects popular narratives and rests on offering alternative explanations (Bonetto & Arcizewski, 2021). This process involves pattern recognition and novel association-making, two key mechanisms in creativity (Müller & Hartmann, 2023; Mulatti & Treccani, 2023). In both conspiracy thinking and creativity, individuals incorporate seemingly unrelated information into a coherent output (Bonetto & Arcizewski, 2021). Given these similarities, there are theoretical accounts arguing that conspiracy beliefs are better understood as conspiracy narratives that are attractive to endorse (Adornetti, 2023; Chlup, 2023; Munro 2024; Zwaan, 2022).

Indeed, empirical research found a weak but positive correlation between creativity and conspiracy thinking (Dirzyte, 2023). Similarly, Huete-Perez et al. (2025) found that individuals who endorsed epistemically unwarranted beliefs exhibited similar reaction times to both related and unrelated semantic primes. The authors attributed this finding to possibly disorganized semantic network structures, which are also characteristic of creative individuals (Beaty & Kenett, 2023; Huete-Perez et al., 2025; Kenett et al., 2016; Kenett et al., 2014).

Although no studies to our knowledge directly investigated divergent thinking in relation to political extremism, Tyagi et al. (2018) found that creative personality predicts political party affiliations, where supporters of the democratic party tended to score higher on creative personality measures. Nonetheless, research in this area remains scarce.

1.2. Semantic organization as an indicator of creativity

A semantic network consists of nodes representing concepts, and edges, which are connections between nodes, representing semantic relationships between the concepts (De Barros Pereira et al., 2022). The nodes fish and water, for instance, are connected by an edge because they are semantically related through functional relations (i.e., fish live in water). Flexible semantic networks may support creativity by facilitating novel associations between concepts (Kenett et al., 2016). For example, more flexible semantic networks are associated with better performance on verbal fluency tasks, and thus higher creativity (Rastelli et al., 2020).

The theory of associative creativity proposes that creative individuals possess a higher capacity for association. These individuals establish novel connections between unrelated concepts (Beaty & Kenett, 2023). Differences in creativity are rooted in differences in semantic memory structures. Creative individuals make common connections (e.g., cat-dog) as well as uncommon connections (e.g., cat-seahorse) equally easily. In contrast, in less creative individuals common connections tend to be strong while uncommon connections are generally weaker (Beaty & Kenett, 2023). These differences in associative creativity are assessed using verbal fluency tasks (Cosgrove et al., 2021; Kenett et al., 2016). In an animal fluency task, participants often cluster responses based on a common environment and taxonomy (Shaoet al., 2014). These responses can be analyzed through graph theory to extract semantic network properties such as the clustering coefficient (CC; how interconnected a node's neighbors are), average shortest path length (ASPL; number of steps required to get from one node to another), Modularity (Q; the extent to which a network is divided into clearly defined subcommunities), and small world-ness (S; the ratio of CC and ASPL, how efficient a network is). A small-world network relies on a higher CC and a lower ASPL. This results in a flexible network that is both globally and locally efficient (Latora & Marchori, 2001). Kenett et al. (2016) applied semantic network analysis to verbal fluency data to investigate the structural properties of creativity. Their findings showed that individuals with higher scores on the Creative Achievement Questionnaire (CAQ) exhibited flatter associative hierarchies (Carson et al. 2005). This indicates an ability to activate weakly linked nodes within their semantic networks.

Consequently, these individuals' networks were more flexible, which facilitated the formation of novel associations. Notably, their networks were characterized by higher small-world-ness (S). This property is associated with flexible and non-rigid semantic structures (Kenett et al., 2016).

In contrast, individuals with lower creative achievement demonstrated steeper associative hierarchies. Their networks showed clustered associations and a tendency to rely on common links between concepts (Kenett et al., 2016). These individuals exhibited a higher clustering coefficient (CC), longer average shortest path length (ASPL), and higher modularity (Q), indicating more rigid and less efficient semantic structures. The overall pattern observed by Kenett et al. (2016) suggests that highly creative individuals possess more chaotic and flexible networks. This structure appears to support associative creativity (Bendek et al., 2017). Therefore, if the theoretical accounts on the relation between creativity and conspiracy thinking are supported, we can expect hyper-associative semantic structure in conspiracy thinkers, similar to that of creative individuals. Moreover, since conspiracy thinking and political extremism are related, we can also expect the semantic structure of political extremists to resemble that of conspiracy theorists.

1.3. The present study

The current study aims to apply the framework proposed by Kenett et al. (2016) to investigate semantic organizational properties in relation to conspiracy thinking and political extremism. Using verbal fluency data, we will compare the semantic network metrics of individuals scoring high and low on measures of conspiracy thinking and individuals who identify as politically moderate versus politically extreme. The literature discussed above suggests that conspiracy thinking may share cognitive characteristics with divergent thinking, specifically in terms of unusual associative patterns and a tendency to make uncommon connections (Bonetto & Arciszewski, 2021).

And although there is no clear link between political extremism and associative thinking, political extremism is related to conspiracy thinking through cognitive mechanisms such as the need for certainty and control (van Prooijen & Krouwel, 2019). Both conspiracy thinking and political extremism thrive when individuals feel a lack of control, which is a trigger for creativity (Bonetto & Arciszewski, 2021; Kruglanski et al., 2006).

Therefore, we formulated the following hypotheses: 1) Conspiracy thinking and political extremism will be significantly related, 2) Individuals highly susceptible to conspiracy thinking will exhibit flexible semantic networks, similar to creative individuals, 3) Individuals who identify as politically extreme will exhibit similar semantic organization to individuals highly susceptible to conspiracy thinking. The primary metrics of interest will be CC, ASPL, Q, and S.

2. Method

>Method

2.1. Participants

A total of N = 159 individuals participated in the survey. We recruited participants through social media platforms using a snowball sampling technique. They participated on a voluntary basis and were not compensated for their participation. All participants provided informed consent. The project was approved by the Research Ethics Review Committee of the Department of Psychology, Education and Child Studies at Erasmus University Rotterdam (application number ETH2425-0676).

We excluded participants who rated their writing English proficiency below CEFR level B1 (Council of Europe, 2020) to ensure reliable measurement of verbal fluency and accurate interpretation of the Generic Conspiracist Belief scale (GCBS; Brotherton et al., 2013). We also excluded participants who did not report their age or failed to complete the GCBS. This resulted in a sample of N = 98 participants, with a mean age of M = 27.88 years (SD = 11.45). The sample was predominantly European (69.39%), followed by North American (10.20%), middle eastern (4.08%), Latin American (3.06%), Asian (3.06%), and participants with mixed or other national backgrounds (10.20%). Most participants were non-native English speakers (80.4%). The majority (77%) obtained a higher education degree (associate, bachelor's, or master's), while 21.43% reported having only a high school education.

Regarding political views, 31.63% of participants reported "slightly leaning toward one side", 32.65% described themselves as "strongly committed to one side but open to discussion", 12.24% identified as "very extreme, I believe radical change is necessary", 21.43% described themselves as "very moderate, I avoid extreme positions", and 2.04% chose "other". Of the final sample (N = 98), we excluded participants who chose "other" on the political extremism item, resulting in n = 96 participants for the extremism analysis. See Table 1 for a descriptive summary.

2.2. Material 2.2.1 Demographic Questionnaire. Participants reported their age, gender, nationality, highest level of education, and criminal history. Participants self-assessed their English writing proficiency using verbal descriptions of CEFR levels A1 to C2 (Council of Europe, 2020). Participants also indicated their political extremism on a self-placement item ranging from 1 (very moderate, I avoid extreme positions) to 4 (very extreme, I believe radical change is necessary), or 5 (other). The political extremism item did not distinguish between far-left and far-right extremism. This is because the current study focuses on the intensity of the political belief instead of its content. In support of this, empirical research on political extremism and ideology reports no significant differences between the far-left and far-right in political strategies or underlying psychological mechanisms (Christner, 2022; van Prooijen et al., 2015; van Prooijen & Krouwel, 2015). The data for criminal history was included for different research purposes and is not reported on.

2.2.2. Semantic Fluency.

Participants completed two verbal fluency tasks adapted for online administration. In the Animal Fluency Task, participants were given 60 seconds to type as many animal names as they can (Moreno-Martínez, 2017). After 60 seconds, the survey automatically moved on to the next question. . In the Emotion Fluency Task, participants listed as many emotions as possible within 60 seconds (Valdivia-Moreno et al., 2025). The target category was shown right at the start of the timer to prevent mental preparation. We removed the "next" button so participants would spend exactly 60 seconds on the task. After that, the survey automatically moved on to the next question.

2.2.3. Generic Conspiracist Beliefs Scale (GCBS).

The GCBS consists of 15 items measuring general susceptibility to conspiracy beliefs (Brotherton et al., 2013). Participants gave their responses on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = definitely not true to5 = definitely true).

The GCBS is known to be reliable (α = 0.93; Brotherton et al., 2013). In the current sample, the GCBS demonstrated excellent internal consistency too (α = 0.92). Earlier research showed the GCBS is significantly correlated with measures of belief in event-based conspiracy theories (Belief in Conspiracy Theories Inventory; r = 0.86) and measures assessing belief in 9/11 theories, 7/7 theories, and Red Bull theories, indicating high criterion-related validity (Brotherton et al., 2013).

2.2.4. Malevolent Creativity Behavior Scale (MCBS).

Participants also completed the MCBS, which assessed their tendency to generate creative but socially harmful ideas (Hao et al., 2016).

2.3. Procedure

We conducted the study via an online Qualtrics survey (www.qualtrics.com). After providing informed consent, participants completed the demographic questionnaire, followed by the political extremism self-placement item. The animal fluency task was presented next, followed by the emotion fluency task The emotion fluency task was not reported on due to many non-emotion and idiosyncratic responses across participants. Both tasks were limited to 60 seconds each, with onscreen instructions and a countdown timer. Participants then completed the GCBS and the MCBS.

The MCBS was excluded from the data analysis as it is outside the scope of this thesis. At the end of the survey, we thanked participants for completing the survey.

2.4. Network Analysis

We categorized participants into high and low conspiracy belief groups based on a median split of their average GCBS score. Participants who scored below median were placed in the low conspiracy group, while participants who scored above or equal to the median were put into the high conspiracy group. For the political extremism analysis, we split participants into moderate versus extreme groups based on the extremity of the political self-placement. Due to sample size limitations, those who identified as very moderate or slightly leaning were combined into a single "moderate" group, while those who identified as strongly committed or very extreme were grouped into a single "extreme" category. We excluded two participants who chose "other" on this item, resulting in a sample of n = 96 for extremism. See Table 1 for subgroup descriptives. We performed the network analysis on the animal fluency responses. We cleaned fluency responses using the SemNetCleaner package in R (Christensen & Kenett, 2023). Cleaning included standardizing responses (lowercasing, trimming whitespace), removing duplicates and spelling errors, and validating responses using predefined semantic dictionaries for animals. We then constructed group-level semantic networks using the SemNeT package in R (Christensen & Kenett, 2023). Building on the semantic network framework developed by Kenett et al. (2016), we constructed semantic networks using Pearson's correlations as an association metric, which quantify the co-occurrence of word pairs across participants' verbal fluency responses.

We created a binary word-by-participant matrix. Each row corresponded to a participant and each column represented a unique word generated by the sample. We coded the cell as 1 if the participant produced the word and 0 otherwise. The Pearson's correlation between each pairs of word columns was calculated, resulting in a word-by-word correlation matrix. This matrix represented how similar words were used across participants, with higher correlations indicating that two words tended to be produced by the same individuals. The resulting correlation matrix was an unweighted, undirected semantic network, where nodes represent words and edges represent the strength of their co-occurrence across participants (Christensen & Kenett, 2023; Kenett et al., 2016).

To reduce the likelihood of including spurious associations, we employed Triangulated Maximally Filtered Graphs (TMFG) for network filtering (Massara et al., 2017). TMFG preserves strong correlations and filters out weak ones. This approach has the advantage of standardizing the ratio of edges to nodes (Christensen et al., 2018). For each group-level network, the ASPL, CC, AND Q were extracted. We then conducted a random network analysis comparison to extract the measures needed for the S index (Christensen et al., 2018; Kenett et al., 2016).

2.5. Statistical Analysis

We used SemNet Shiny for the statistical analysis (Christensen & Kenett, 2023). The SemNet Shiny application is an interactive tool for semantic network analysis and is part of the SemNet package (Christensen, 2023). For both the group comparisons of conspiracy thinking (high vs. low) and political extremism (extreme vs. moderate), we used a node-wise bootstrapping approach. This procedure repeatedly selects 50% of the nodes in each network without replacement and constructs partial networks for each group based on the same subset of nodes. This process was repeated 1,000 times. The underlying logic is that if the overall network of one group differs from that of another group, then any subnetwork from one group is likely to differ from any subnetwork of the other (Christensen & Kenett, 2023).

For each partial network, the procedure resampled individual participants' responses within each group to compute a distribution of ASPL, CC, and Q. We then conducted an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) to examine whether partial network structures differed between groups categorized by levels of conspiracy thinking or political extremism. Although TMFG standardizes the ratio of edges to nodes, the total number of edges in each network still depends on the number of unique responses provided (Massara et al., 2017). Consequently, slight differences in the number of nodes across networks can lead to differences in the overall number of edges and network density.

To account for these residual differences, we included the number of edges as a covariate in our ANCOVA analyses. This allowed us to statistically control for network size and density when comparing network metrics between groups (Christensen & Kenett, 2023).

Additionally, we conducted a Random Network Analysis using the SemNet Shiny application.

This procedure generated 1,000 randomized versions of each semantic network to serve as null models for baseline comparisons. These random networks preserved the number of nodes and edges but removed meaningful semantic organization by randomly rewiring the connections (Christensen & Kenett, 2023). We calculated the small-world-ness index (S), which quantifies the extent to which a network simultaneously exhibits high clustering and short ASPL, which are the marks of a small-world structure (Kenett et al., 2016). 4. Discussion

We investigated whether the semantic organization properties related to creativity also underly conspiracy thinking and political extremism. Our findings partially supported the first hypothesis. We observed a significant relationship between the tendency to endorse conspiracy theories and political extremism, but contrary to our expectations, this relationship was negative. In line with our second hypothesis, group-level semantic network comparisons revealed that individuals high in conspiracy thinking exhibited more flexible semantic structure, exemplified by a shorter average shortest path length (ASPL), a higher clustering coefficient (CC), and lower modularity (Q), similar to that found in creative individuals (Kenett et al., 2016). Finally, comparisons of political extremists and political moderates offered partial support for the third hypothesis. We found some overlap in global network integration between conspiracists and extremists, but not in local associations.

We observed a negative correlation between conspiracy thinking and political extremism.

The surprising direction of this relationship stands in contrast to previous research which suggests a positive association where individuals at both extremes show higher levels of conspiracy endorsement (van Prooijen et al., 2015). One plausible explanation lies in how political moderates function in the current sociopolitical climate. Although political moderation is traditionally considered a form of political centrism, it can also reflect forms of political disengagement, distrust or alienation from the political system (Ender & Uscinski, 2021). Research by Abalakina-Paap et al.

(1999) demonstrated that feelings of alienation and institutional distrust are positively related to belief in conspiracies. Although we did not test for a curvilinear association, in our sample, participants who identified as politically moderate scored higher on the GCBS than those who identified as politically extreme (see Table 2). This suggests that the political moderation we observed may not reflect political centrism, but rather disengagement from political ideology. These individuals may be less trusting of political institutions, parties, and perceived elites, all core traits of conspiracy theorists (Imhoff & Bruder, 2014).

In support of this view, some research suggests that many moderate or unaligned voters feel politically "homeless" and are disengaged with both the center-left and center-right, yet also unwilling to endorse far-left or far-right (Harrison, 2020). Unaffiliated or moderate voters in Europe are less likely to feel represented by mainstream parties, report lower political distrust, and show more disengagement during elections (Bunting, 2024; Hooghe & Kern, 2013; Hooghe et al., 2011).

Additionally, individuals who do not feel close to a political party tend to exhibit greater cynicism and alienation (Hooghe & Kern, 2013). This lack of ideological representation may create fertile ground for conspiracy thinking, which is particularly endorsed during times of uncertainty as a way of reestablishing psychological control and coherence (van Prooijen & Krouwel, 2019).

Another plausible interpretation of this negative correlation lies in different belief structures characteristic of each construct. Political extremism is associated with ideologically rigid, internally consistent worldviews (Kruglanski et al., 2006; van Prooijen & Krouwel, 2019). Politically extreme individuals tend to interpret the world through simplified, dichotomous categories and maintain belief systems that are tightly integrated within their ideological agenda (Lammers et al., 2016; van Prooijen & Krouwel, 2019). In contrast, conspiracy thinking is a monological belief system, wherein belief in one conspiracy increases susceptibility to others, even when they are logically contradictory (Wood et al., 2012). Conspiracy thinkers appear to prioritize associative pattern recognition over ideological consistency. For example, some individuals simultaneously believe that Princess Diana was murdered but also that she faked her own death (Wood et al., 2012). This apparent contradiction is ignored to serve the broader narrative of institutional deception (Wood et al., 2012).

Therefore, individuals with high levels of conspiracy thinking in our sample might resist aligning with any fixed political ideology, including extreme ones that offer rigid explanatory frameworks and thus constrain their flexible conspiratorial worldview.

The semantic networks of individuals high in conspiracy belief support the notion of a hyperassociative structure, consistent with our second hypothesis. Compared to low scorers on the GCBS, high scorers displayed shorter ASPL, lower modularity Q, and greater small-world-ness S. Together, these features indicate a network that is globally integrated and efficiently locally connected. These features may support lexical access to semantically distant concepts, an organization that is associated with creativity (Kenett et al., 2016).

We also observed an increase in clustering among high conspiracy believers, which indicates denser local connectivity (Beaty & Kenett, 2023; Benedek et al., 2017; Kenett et al., 2016). While some prior research linked lower clustering to more creative semantic structures, clustering alone in the current context cannot be considered a sign of cognitive rigidity (Kenett et al., 2016). When interpreted alongside lower modularity, shorter ASPL, and higher small-world-ness, the increased clustering suggests a semantic structure that is both locally associative and globally integrated, which may facilitate associative pattern generation (Beaty & Kenett, 2023; Kenett et al., 2016). This interpretation is supported by other studies showing that higher clustering is also indicative of higher small-world-ness and divergent thinking ability (Benedek et al., 201; Rastelli et al., 2020).

Theoretical accounts have described conspiracy thinking as a form of associative creativity, in which individuals make uncommon connections (Beaty & Kenett, 2023; Bonetto & Arciszewiski, 2021). Empirical studies showed that creative individuals exhibit a small-world network configuration, which facilitates both the clustering and recombination of ideas in novel ways (Kenett et al., 2016). Our findings suggest that conspiracy thinkers possess semantic organization similar to that of creative individuals. These findings support a growing body of literature describing conspiracy thinking as an unregulated form of associative processing (Adornetti, 2023; Bonetto & Arciszewski, 2021; Zwaan, 2022).

Our analysis of the semantic network structure of political extremists revealed a similar though somewhat different pattern, partially supporting our third hypothesis. Compared to politically moderate individuals, participants who identified as extreme or strongly committed exhibited shorter ASPL and lower Q, similar to high conspiracy theorists. Nonetheless, these individuals exhibited no significant differences in clustering. While we did not conduct a statistical comparison of small-world-ness, the small-world index (S) appears to be nearly identical across the politically extreme and the politically moderate group, suggesting no clear group-level difference in this measure. Our findings imply that these individuals exhibit a semantic organization that is globally integrated, which supports broad concept retrieval that is not compartmentalized (Kenett et al., 2016).

More importantly, the comparable clustering and small-world-ness indices between politically moderate and extreme individuals suggest that political extremity may not be related to hyper-associative thinking (Benedek et al., 2017). The absence of rich local clustering, despite increased global integration, is more consistent with goal-directed, top-down processing than with semantic flexibility (Bar, 2007). Indeed, recent research found that individuals with strong political views show stronger activation in areas linked to response inhibition (Amodoio et al., 2007; Kenett et al., 2016). This specific aspect lies in contrast to the flexible, locally rich structure we found in conspiracy theorists.

When considered together, the semantic network structures of conspiracy thinkers and political extremists reveal both similarities and differences. Both groups exhibited shorter ASPL and lower modularity compared to their respective comparison groups. This finding points to a globally integrated semantic network which suggests that both individuals high in conspiracy thinking and political extremism may possess a heightened capacity for making broad conceptual connections (Benedek et al., 2017). This is a property they share with creative individuals (Kenett et al., 2016).

However, only conspiracy thinkers displayed increased local clustering and small-world-ness. This distinction suggests that individuals with higher levels of conspiracy thinking possess a more flexible and associative semantic network organization.

While the present study is not without methodological limitations, the potential impact of these constraints is mitigated by the theoretical contribution and methodological innovation of our approach. First, the use of a unidimensional political extremism item did not allow for directional comparisons across the left and the right, which might have obscured ideological asymmetries in associative patterns. Nonetheless, our focus was on the structural cognitive features of political extremism as a general psychological phenomenon, regardless of political content. This abstraction is consistent with prior work demonstrating that both ends of the spectrum often share underlying cognitive and motivational traits (van Prooijen & Krouwel, 2019).

Nevertheless, future research on the semantic organization of political belief systems could benefit from incorporating multi-dimensional ideological measures that allow for comparisons across political content and direction. This could help clarify whether differences in semantic structure are symmetrical across ideological extremes or whether they vary across ideological commitments.

Similarly, while we collapsed participants into broader categories (strongly committed/extreme versus slightly leaning/moderate) to address sample size limitations, this strategy increased statistical power and preserved interpretability across semantic network metrics. Importantly, key differences in network properties remained detectable in two distinct analyses: A bootstrapping analysis, showing significant differences in comparisons between generated partial networks, and a random network analysis which confirmed that the patterns we found are meaningful and not random. This suggests that our findings point to genuine structural distinctions rather than artifacts of classification. Future research should aim to examine semantic network differences across comparisons between more sharply defined groups. If the effects observed in this study are reliable, they should be more pronounced in more extreme comparisons.

Another limitation lies in the cross-sectional nature of this study. We cannot infer the direction of causality in the observed data, and it remains unclear whether the observed semantic organization of conspiracists and extremists serves as a predispositional cognitive feature or whether it is shaped by sustained engagement with such belief systems. It is also plausible that there is a bidirectional dynamic, in which belief content and semantic organization co-evolve over time. For example, work by Lodge and Taber (2000) showed that motivated political reasoning influences memory encoding and retrieval. Therefore, future research on semantic organization of political and social constructs should consider longitudinal designs to mitigate this directionality issue.

The implications of this research are both theoretically and socially relevant. This study extends network-based models of cognition into the domain of ideological belief systems. We demonstrate that semantic network theory can be applied to the study of conspiracy thinking and political extremism. Theoretically, our findings suggest that these beliefs are not solely driven by social or motivational factors, but they can also manifest at the level of (semantic) cognition.

At a societal level, the findings offer insight into the psychological foundations of increasing polarization and ideological rigidity in contemporary discourse, particularly in political and social contexts. As partisanship and conspiratorial narratives continue to spread, understanding the underlying cognitive mechanisms which help shape or maintain these beliefs becomes increasingly important.

Our results suggest that targeting the cognitive styles through which these maladaptive beliefs emerge may offer a productive path of intervention. Since conspiracy thinking appears to be associated with hyper-associative semantic networks, interventions for conspiracists might focus on epistemic discipline and on encouraging these individuals to critically evaluate the connections they draw between concepts. On the other hand, individuals with extreme political views may respond better to interventions focused on cognitive flexibility that are designed to challenge rigid schemas.

5.1. Conclusion

This study offered novel insight at the nexus of conspiracy thinking, political extremism, and semantic organization. By combining individual differences measures with semantic network analysis, we, to our knowledge, provide the first empirical attempt to explore how semantic cognition relates to political ideology and conspiracy thinking. Our findings suggest that associative creativity plays a role in conspiracy belief and although political extremists share some of these structural features, their belief systems appear more selectively associative. Our findings support the growing proposition that conspiracy belief is not a pathological phenomenon but may stem from unregulated cognitive tendencies (Dirzyte, 2023; Huete-Pérez et al., 2025; Zwaan, 2022). Finally, the present study showed that network science provides a promising framework for understanding the cognitive mechanisms that underlie ideological fragmentation in modern society.
