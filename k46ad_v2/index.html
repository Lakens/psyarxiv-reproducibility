Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Abstract

A chief aim of the neuroscience of consciousness is to derive how states of conscious experience (i.e., qualia) are related to supporting brain states (e.g., patterns of neural activation within patterns of neural connectivity). This aim goes beyond identifying neuronal correlates of consciousness (NCC) on the one side, and beyond structural descriptions of conscious experience (e.g., qualia structure) on the other side. The ultimate goal is to link these two sides by mapping specific neuronal states or mechanisms to specific conscious experiences. Relating two or more phenomena in science often takes the form of mathematical mappings, such as equations (e.g., E = mc^2). This raises the possibility that there also are mathematical mappings that relate conscious states to brain states. Here we review empirical work on mapping neuronal spiking activity to conscious contents. We specifically discuss pioneering paradigms aimed at identifying "bridge loci", in the form of NCC, and paradigms aimed at identifying "bridge laws", such as relating psychometric to neurometric functions. We then examine how these paradigms are augmented by mapping structural descriptions of conscious experience to structural descriptions of neuronal activity.

1. Introduction

One of the central ideas of the Qualia-Structure Project is to mathematically relate structural descriptions of brain mechanisms to structural descriptions of conscious experience (qualia). In the following, we will discuss prior, mostly non-structural, attempts at mathematically relating brain mechanisms to conscious experience. We do so in order to provide greater historical and empirical context. This includes known mathematical relations between brain mechanisms and qualia as well as successes and limitations of non-structural mathematical approaches. This contextual knowledge is vital to fully appreciate the rationale and motivation as well as historical development of various approaches and methods utilized by the Qualia Structure Project.

1.1 The Mind-Brain (Mapping) Problem

Humans have long wondered how consciousness relates to our physical bodies, or brains. This question is called the mind-body problem (Van Gulick 2004) https://paperpile.com/c/nGV3XJ/dF25x or in modern terms, the mind-brain problem.

1.2 Why Link Consciousness to Brains?

There is much at stake. If the existence of our conscious experience depends on the existence of our brains, our consciousness will cease to exist once our brains cease to exist. On the flip side, consciousness after death seems of greater likelihood if consciousness does not depend on intact brains. These logical consequences of seemingly empirical questions are rarely discussed within scientific contexts. And perhaps as a consequence, a significant fraction of neuroscientists are at risk of holding two or more opposing philosophical beliefs about the mind-brain problem at once (Navarro-Peña et al. 2026 https://paperpile.com/c/nGV3XJ/h3mn. Yet, one has to admit that despite millennia of study, the relationship between our bodies (brains) and our conscious experience remains largely enigmatic (Chalmers 2010; Levine 1983).

Some people react to the state of ignorance outlined above by assuming that only philosophy or theology can provide satisfying answers. Given that there is no agreed-upon scientific explanation for how brains link to consciousness, this possibility cannot be ruled out. Yet, many questions once deemed exclusive to philosophy or theology, such as whether the universe has always existed, are now addressed by science (Smeenk and Ellis 2017). At the same time, the fact that science established the Big Bang theory, did not eliminate philosophical and theological debates on why and how our universe came into being. We may find ourselves in a similar situation when it comes to the mind-body problem. Indeed, there are several concrete questions that place the mind-brain problem squarely within (neuro)science (Crick and Koch 1995 , 1992 , 1998).

Consider that we do not currently have a complete scientific account for why or how general anesthesia causes loss of consciousness (Brown et al. 2010; Moody et al. 2021; Mashour and Alkire 2013). As a consequence, we have no reliable direct measure of whether a patient is conscious during surgery (Crosby 2011). During major surgical operations patients are often given paralytic drugs that prevent them from moving (Whittaker and Wedell 1991). Moreover, some anesthetic drugs cause (anterograde) memory loss (https://paperpile.com/c/nGV3XJ/pg59L. Liu et al. 2024). Hence, an awake patient might neither be able to alert physicians, nor remember they consciously experienced pain during surgery (Pryor et al. 2025). This so-called intraoperative awareness is rare (A. Smith 2017; Sebel et al. 2004 https://paperpile.com/c/nGV3XJ/brL9g+IvPAk. Yet, without a reliable direct measure of conscious experience in the absence of behavior (i.e., by measuring brain activity), some cases will remain undetected (Mashour and Hudetz 2018; Huang et al. 2018). We also have learned that some patients who get diagnosed as comatose are conscious, yet are unable to respond (Owen et al. 2006; Owen 2019; Owen et al. 2007). Here, too, we have no reliable scientific way to detect consciousness when behavioral responses are not available.

The potential of detecting conscious experience via brain mechanisms (i.e., neuronal activity embedded in connectivity, or how neuronal states affect subsequent neural states) provides hope that we can help people in these undesirable scenarios.

The problem, then, is to find a reliable relationship between brain mechanisms and conscious experience, be it between levels of consciousness, contents of consciousness, or both. There already are several promising approaches, but none of them have enjoyed universal success (e.g., (Massimini et al. 2009; Rosow and Manberg 2001; Maschke et al. 2023 Maschke et al. , 2024;; Nilsen et al. 2024; Yichun Gu et al. 2024)). Without first uncovering the relationship between brains and consciousness, remedies might remain elusive (after all, it is hard to fix a broken car without knowing how a car works in the first place). The brain-consciousness relation, thus, is not just of intellectual interest.

It is a practical (neuroscientific) problem that demands a solution.

1.3 Why Mathematics?

Having established that there is a relationship between brain mechanisms and conscious experience means that mathematical mappings between them are of interest.

In order to relate brain mechanisms to conscious states, it seems helpful to ensure that both sides of the link are using the same descriptors. One way to accomplish this is to translate conscious states into mathematics since it is mathematics that is often used to describe brain mechanisms (see (Wigner 1960) and (Steiner 2009) for a deeper reflection on the role of mathematics in science). Once both conscious experience and brain mechanisms are described mathematically, it might be possible to derive mathematical mappings between these two descriptions. This holds true even if a mathematical description of conscious experience (qualia) is deemed non-exhaustive.

That is, the mathematical mappings in question can be seen as purely methodological in nature (see (Ellia and Tsuchiya 2026) for discussion).

1.4 Mathematical Relations for Consciousness

In fact, we already know of several mathematical mappings between (behaviorally indicated) conscious experiences and physical phenomena. These mathematical laws have held up to decades of empirical research. The existence of these laws calls into question the notion that there can be no mathematical mappings for conscious experience.

In the following, we will examine key historical developments in studying such mappings. In particular, we will review the two main pioneering neuroscientific approaches that derived mathematical mappings between brain mechanisms and conscious experience. This historical context explains both some of the origins and rationale of current Qualia Structure research as well as related structural approaches.

The early work reviewed here was largely limited to determining one or two-dimensional relations between brain mechanisms and conscious experience, such as coefficients or functions. The Qualia Structure approach expands mathematical mappings between brains and conscious experience to much richer structures that can exceed two or three dimensions (though this is only one of many motivators for structural approaches; see (Tononi et al. 2025; Phillips and Tsuchiya 2024; Tsuchiya, Phillips, et al. 2022; Tsuchiya 2025; Fink and Lee 2026; Lee 2021; Kleiner 2024)). After discussing the neuroscientific work that led up to the current structuralist approaches, we will briefly compare the chief differences between them. We will stop short of an in-depth review of mappings between structural descriptions of conscious experience to structural descriptions of brain mechanisms, as we have already done so elsewhere (Maier and Tsuchiya 2026).

2. Mapping Neural Responses to Conscious States

In the sections above, we made the case that (neuro-)science is justified in attempting to derive mappings between brain states and conscious states. Somewhat surprisingly, this argument was not fully established, nor widely accepted, a few decades ago (Crick and Koch 1992 https://paperpile.com/c/nGV3XJ/QGxZ3. As a consequence early studies often shied away from using the term "consciousness" and instead stuck to less contentious terms, such as "perception", or "awareness".

An added complication that afflicts both these pioneering studies as well as much ongoing research on the neuroscience of consciousness is the focus on studying the consciousness of others rather than one's own. In practice, this "extrinsic" approach runs into at least two problems: (1) Measuring, and hence studying, behavior (such as verbal report or eye movements) rather than conscious experience directly. (2) Limited knowledge about the presence of an experience and what that experience was like.

Notably, the scientific method does not prohibit the study of one's own experience (https://paperpile.com/c/nGV3XJ/DbR9. In fact, the history of science provides ample precedence of single individuals performing experiments on themselves, including on their own experience (e.g., (Poldrack et al. 2015; Rivers and Head 1908; B. J. Marshall et al. 1985; McCance 1936)). Some of the most widely cited classic (psychophysical) perception research studied the authors' conscious perception exclusively (Campbell and Robson 1968). And some of these studies even replaced all measurements with phenomenal descriptions (Hubel 1997).

One likely contributor to the focus on "report" rather than perceptual experience per se was the reliance on animal models that dominated the early neuroscience of consciousness we review below (https://paperpile.com/c/nGV3XJ/9GeBX+bqTxR+fD6ld. Leopold et al. 2003; Carter et al. 2020; Crick and Koch 1998). As we will discuss, there have been attempts of dropping reports (no-report paradigms), or enriching reports beyond simple yes/no responses (e.g., similarity ratings, micro-phenomenology). However, as long as we study "other consciousness" rather than our own consciousness, the problem of "indirect" assessment remains.

There are benefits of studying "other consciousness", of course, such as that we can more easily find shared phenomena between individuals. And another factor is that measuring our own brains is not currently feasible on the single neuron level. Yet, the rapid development and adoption of brain implants (Greenspon et al. 2026; Patrick-Krueger et al. 2024https://paperpile.com/c/nGV3XJ/d4fN+vTCC+rTOJ+00gw suggests that this might change in the not-so distant future (Greenspon et al. 2024 (Greenspon et al. , 2025;; Valle et al. 2025; Osborn et al. 2025 ).

2.1 Why Single Neuron Responses?

Most brain responses to sensory stimuli also occur during general anesthesia where consciousness is unlikely to occur (Ku et al. 2011; Katlowitz et al. 2026).

Whatever is required to elevate sensory brain responses to conscious experience may be subtle and thus might have to be studied in fine-grained detail.

Given the expectation that NCC might require fine-grained resolution, much of the earliest research centered on measurements of single neuron spiking activity.

Another benefit of this approach is that we know that neurons affect each other causally via spikes. While we do not know whether it is spike-driven causality that provides the correct spatio-temporal grain for consciousness, spiking has been well established to be relevant to brain function in general, and many other potential grains (e.g., synaptic currents or the combined activity of cortical columns) are themselves in direct causal relation to spikes. In contrast, most non-invasive (neuroimaging) techniques indirectly probe for spike-related activity, such as electromagnetic fields that penetrate the skull (EEG/MEG) or local changes in the brain's blood supply (fMRI). The indirect nature of these techniques can pose interpretative challenges. For example, repeated findings demonstrate that local fMRI responses can deviate from local spiking measurements (Epp et al. 2025; Maier et al. 2008; Sirotin and Das 2009; Chen et al. 2026; Rauscher et al. 2026). This is not to say that indirect measurements lack merit, and the reader is advised to also seek out reviews on consciousness research using indirect techniques, such as: (Tononi and Koch 2015; Boly et al. 2013 Boly et al. , 2017;; Rees 2013; Cogitate Consortium et al. 2025; Maier and Tsuchiya 2021; Rees et al. 2002; Schmid and Maier 2015; Förster et al. 2020; Dembski et al. 2021; Filimonov et al. 2026 https://paperpile.com/c/nGV3XJ/BAGKH+zM9ht+tQJPQ+6zrVE+IpdD9+5MrYW+tRl6b+5Kxgp+i9ZN7+yPFvJ+6FDLc.

2.2 Neuronal Correlates of Consciousness (NCC)

One of the early approaches aimed at finding any relations between brain states and conscious states. The focus was on isolating brain structures that correlate with conscious experience (neuronal correlates of consciousness, or NCC) to differentiate them from brain structures that do not. The word "correlates" did not prescribe a mathematical procedure (such as correlation coefficients), but meant to indicate metaphysical noncommitment (i.e., to avoid notions of causality). While NCC can be, and were, extended to neuronal mechanisms, such as neuronal synchrony, most NCC research stuck to identifying structures, such as neurons, areas, or networks whose activation coincided with certain contents of consciousness.

An impressive amount of literature has been amassed on the topic of "neuronal correlates of consciousness", though the rate of new papers has somewhat plateaued. The initial wave of empirical studies on neuronal correlates of consciousness mostly used the following subtractive contrast for analysis (Kim and Blake 2005) https://paperpile.com/c/nGV3XJ/xfg4x

One reason for this approach is that it is largely theory-agnostic. That is, rather than testing specific hypotheses about which brain mechanism leads to consciousness in a specific way, the idea was to first discover candidate subsets of brain mechanisms.

The most common type of paradigm used for this kind of analysis rests on a stimulus that is consciously experienced only some of the time (ideally, half of the time).

Above-threshold stimuli were more widely used than at-threshold stimuli (just-noticeable stimuli) that are so faint that they are only consciously perceived some of the time. The reason for this preference is that conscious experience of at-threshold stimuli might be trivially explained by non-conscious factors such as spontaneous noise fluctuations at the receptor level, or fluctuating states of attention, arousal, or vigilance (https://paperpile.com/c/nGV3XJ/5MrYW. However, above-threshold stimuli also come with constraints (Kim and Blake 2005). For example, visual masking requires other stimuli to either precede or follow a test stimulus within brief periods of time. Another aspect of early work on NCC was that it is largely focused on contents of consciousness rather than levels of consciousness such as dreamless sleep or coma.

And, in its most common form, the content of consciousness was reduced to a binary variable, such as "aware" and "unaware". Given this reliance on contrasting two different states of consciousness, we will refer to the NCC approach as "state-based" (Figure 1).

The contrastive NCC approach primarily identifies structures (such as distinct neuronal populations), or a "bridge locus" of neuronal mechanisms linked to specific conscious states (Teller 1984). The rationale was to "map bridge loci first (where?), then determine the bridge laws (how?)". This logic rests on the assumption that NCC are static rather than dynamic. If an NCC fluctuates across neurons rather than involving the same neurons, the task literally becomes a moving target. Unfortunately, neurons sometimes are part of an NCC determined by the contrastive approach, and other times are not (Maier et al. 2007).

Another criticism of the contrastive NCC approach is that it is challenging to distinguish an NCC proper from upstream, or downstream activation that is unrelated to consciousness. Mental and behavioral changes that follow stimulus awareness, such as surprise, heightened attention, or memorization and preparation of a behavioral report will also be picked up by the contrast (https://paperpile.com/c/nGV3XJ/9GeBX+Z9dEo+1cJtl+nv8cT+ne8ZW+S0jra+3rUw2+jYJHe+zI3DT. 2.3 Perceptual Decision Making

Around the same time, several groups used a somewhat different approach. The interested reader might appreciate the following selection of papers for deeper studyt: (Newsome et al. 1989; Britten et al. 1992; J. While the core of their research also exploited the fact that one and the same bistable physical stimulus sometimes evokes one conscious experience and sometimes another, these researchers did not just compare these two conditions in isolation. Instead, they systematically (parametrically) varied the stimuli across trials to alter the likelihood of one conscious experience over another. This results in a (psychometric) curve of reported occurrences for one percept (which arguably equates conscious experience) versus another as a function of stimulus manipulation. These researchers then related these psychometric functions to a function of simultaneously collected single neuron spiking (neurometric functions). Given this focus on mathematical functions, we will loosely group these studies as "function-based" mappings (Figure 3). This literature is more commonly known as "perceptual decision making" (and the authors might object to being lumped into consciousness research). "Decision making" was chosen to emphasize observable behavior (although the added word "perceptual" seemingly implies conscious experience as an inferred variable). This approach does not just identify neurons that correlate with certain conscious experiences, but further provides a mathematical rule (bridge law) that links perceptual report to associated neuronal functions. In practice, however, the single data point where a stimulus evoked each of two mutually exclusive conscious experiences with equal likelihood was used for analysis. 3. Mapping Neural Structures to Qualia Structures

A central topic of this book is on the "structural turn" in consciousness science (Kleiner 2024https://paperpile.com/c/nGV3XJ/QqLrk+NQxvo+pqgy+CXXK7 Lee 2021; Maier and Tsuchiya 2026; Fink and Lee 2026) ; see also: (Kleiner 2026 https://paperpile.com/c/nGV3XJ/5ytI+F88u) for challenges and rebuttals. This emerging paradigm aims to enrich the pioneering two-valued approach (i.e., "aware" or "unaware") by using multi-valued structural descriptions of conscious content. We have recently reviewed priorhttps://paperpile.com/c/nGV3XJ/pqgy ongoing, and potentially future structure-based approaches of interest (Maier and Tsuchiya 2026) , and thus will only briefly summarize them here.

3.1 Perceptual Spaces and Representational Similarity Analyses

Several decades of pioneering work related structural descriptions of conscious experience to structural descriptions of neuronal activation (see (Maier and Tsuchiya 2026) for a recent in-depth review).

Perceptual similarity spaces form the basis for several techniques that we will discuss (i.e., representational similarity analyses and some of the more recent work of the Qualia Structure Project). The basic idea of using space to represent similarity between individual perceptual objects, such as colors or auditory tones, goes back several decades (e.g., (Shepard 1982; Attneave 1950). As the name suggests, perceptual similarity spaces are established by asking subjects to rate the similarity between two sensory stimuli. This numerical value then gets translated into a metric distance between them -the more similar, the less distance between them. Using these pairwise distances, sensory stimuli then can be placed in a metric space, thus forming a structure.

Representational Similarity Analyses (RSA) can be used to map between similarity spaces (Kriegeskorte et al. 2008). RSA quantifies how much the pattern of pairwise distances is similar across two different similarity spaces. In this context, the most interesting use of RSA is to map distances between the similarity of neuronal population responses to the distances between similarity ratings for the associated stimuli (Kriegeskorte 2009; Lin and Kriegeskorte 2024). However, standard RSA usually quantifies pairwise dissimilarity distances rather than providing a full structure-preserving map. That is, RSA can show that two spaces have similar geometry, but it does not identify the full mapping of one structure onto the other. The Qualia Structure approach, discussed next, aims to take this next step (Tsuchiya 2025).

3.2 Qualia Structure

Several companion chapters in this book review ongoing work on Qualia Structures. We will thus just briefly mention that this approach goes beyond traditional RSA by studying structural mappings between qualia structures, between neuronal information structures, as well as between qualia structures and neuronal information structures (Phillips and Tsuchiya 2024) (Figure 4). Much of the work to date focuses on mappings between qualia structures (Tsuchiya, Phillips, et al. 2022; Tsuchiya, Saigo, et al. 2022; Prentner 2025). Given the scope of this review, we will accept that structural descriptions of conscious states are achievable (or, at least, actively improved upon), and consider what kind of "neuronal mechanism structures" they could be mapped to (Northoff et al. 2019; Tsuchiya et al. 2016).

3.3 Neuronal Information Structures

A major shift in the study of spiking activity that differentiates the time period of the pioneering work on state-based and function-based NCC is that action potentials now routinely get measured across more than one neuron, or just a small number of neurons. Instead, most direct measures of neuronal responses now comprise hundreds (and sometimes thousands) of neurons that were measured at the same time (Stringer et al. 2019).

The associated shift from careful examination of responses of individual neurons to large neuronal populations has evoked a concomitant shift in neuronal data analyses, as well as novel insights into concerted neuronal activity (Ebitz and Hayden 2021; Rouse et al. 2023). Much of these new techniques and insights are fundamentally structural in nature, and sometimes use the exact same geometric, topological and graph-based structural constructs that structural approaches to conscious experiences employ (see (Maier and Tsuchiya 2026, 2021) for review).

Systems neuroscience increasingly leverages similar multi-valuedhttps://paperpile.com/c/nGV3XJ/uDQlc+7DUCk+0td8h structural descriptions of population neuronal activity (Rouse et al. 2023; Gallego 2025; Perich et al. 2025) , thus opening the door for an entirely new structure-based mapping between the two.

While mappings between structural accounts of conscious experience and structural accounts of population neuronal responses are still in their infancy, initial studies provide reason for optimism (Leung and Tsuchiya 2022; Leung et al. 2021 Leung et al. , 2025;; Robinson et al. 2025). This work is necessarily slower than behavioral data collection. But the fact that we are already witnessing first studies utilizing this novel approach (see (Maier and Tsuchiya 2026) for review) is encouraging.

4. Caveats

Most of the early work we discussed focused on "neuronal activation" (i.e., action potentials), with little regard to connectivity. Recent work increasingly acknowledges that neuronal mechanisms comprise more than action potentials devoid of connective context (Gidon et al. 2022). Yet, there are many open questions regarding "connectivity". Recent work in C. elegans suggests that the anatomical connections between neurons do not provide a valid prediction of the functional connectivity (Cook et al. 2019; Ripoll-Sánchez et al. 2023) between these neurons (i.e., which neurons tend to co-vary) (Uzel et al. 2022). Likewise, functional connectivity does not fully predict the effective connectivity of the same neurons (i.e., the causal effectiveness with which these neurons affect each other). More than that, functional connectivity dynamically changes over time (Flavell and Gordus 2022). Mathematical mappings between brain mechanisms and conscious experience that consider both the activation state of neurons as well as their connectivity (and thus causal potentiality) thus face empirical challenges that we are only beginning to understand.
