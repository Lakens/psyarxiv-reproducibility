How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Abstract

Phonetic studies sometimes infer how many categories a linguistic system contains using surface distributional diagnostics. This inference is examined by distinguishing four levels: distribution shape, labeled-group separation, statistical components, and linguistic categories. In an equal-proportion, equal-variance Gaussian mixture, two speech categories produce a unimodal pooled distribution when the distance between their means is less than two standard deviations. In many realistic contexts, unimodality of a pooled distribution alone does not establish the presence of a single linguistic category. Conversely, bimodality of a pooled distribution alone does not establish two linguistic categories. The standard mixture-variance decomposition also shows that a two-category mixture need not have greater variance than its more variable component.

Simulations show mode-based diagnostics can correctly describe surface modality yet poorly recover the categories that generated the data. Empirical cases further illustrate that distribution shape, labeled-group separation, and component structure capture distinct properties of the data and need not converge on the same category analysis.

These diagnostics should thus be interpreted at their own levels. Agreement among them can strengthen a description of surface structure, but they cannot determine categoryhood on their own. Category-level claims require converging linguistic evidence suited to the category under study.

Some of this work also uses perceptual, social, or historical evidence, so it should not be characterized as relying on an acoustic statistic alone. It nevertheless documents an operational step used to infer category structure from acoustic distributions. An important inferential point to note here is that the technique presupposes a category distinction and uses acoustics-based measures to quantify the realization of the categories already supplied; it does not independently establish whether those categories remain distinct units in the linguistic system.

Notably, forward and reverse inferences have different evidential status. Given a specified generating mechanism, we can derive or simulate the population densities it may produce. The inverse is less determinate because the same surface pattern may be compatible with multiple generating mechanisms. Quantitative classification research has long recognized that both the presence and the absence of bimodality admit multiple explanations (Murphy, 1964). Distribution shape therefore describes the observed or estimated density, not the number of latent or linguistic categories. Measures of labeled-group overlap or distance describe acoustic separation under labels whose status has already been assumed or established. Previous work (Smith, Sonderegger, & The SPADE Consortium, 2024) has examined the limitations of these measures as estimates of separation, including their sensitivity to unbalanced data and the difficulty of quantifying their uncertainty; Stanley and Sneller (2023) show that sample size plays a critical role in interpreting Pillai scores. However, even if the estimate of separation is reliable, the labeled groups do not necessarily correspond to distinct linguistic categories. Mixture models have the same issue. They estimate statistical components under specified distributional assumptions and selection criteria. Their solutions represent the data statistically but do not directly establish linguistic categoryhood.

Near merger makes this cross-level problem especially clear. For phonological categories, we must distinguish an abstract category from its phonetic exponence, or surface realization. Phonetic proximity concerns that exponence and does not determine whether the categories remain distinct (Labov, Karen, & Miller, 1991; Yu, 2007). Labov used this analysis to explain how the MEAT-MATE merger in sixteenth-century English could later reverse without hypercorrection: the relevant distinction had not completely collapsed (Labov, 1994; Labov, Yaeger, & Steiner, 1972). Near merger is particularly informative, but the problem is broader: evidence about a surface distribution must be interpreted at the level it directly addresses.

Linguistic behavior can reveal distinctions that acoustic distance does not. In Huai'an Mandarin, for example, a derived Tone 3 can trigger Tone 3 sandhi, whereas phonetically similar Tone 1 or Tone 4 forms do not (Du & Durvasula, 2022 , 2025). Moreover, the same holds even when neutralization is phonetically complete, at least impressionistically. In Nupe, consonants are palatalized before front vowels and labialized before rounded vowels. Before surface [a], however, both palatalized and labialized consonants occur, even though the vowel itself is phonetically identical. Hyman (1970) argues that these [a] vowels are underlying front and rounded mid vowels: they are neutralized with /a/ on the surface but still trigger palatalization or labialization, as unneutralized front and rounded vowels do. In both cases, the evidence comes from phonological patterning: forms that are close or even identical in the measured acoustic space behave differently in the linguistic system. Other category claims may require evidence from lexical processing, perception, production, or sociolinguistic evaluation. As far as possible, such evidence should be independent of the surface distributional measure being evaluated. This avoids using the same acoustic measure both to establish and to validate the labels.

This paper examines what surface distributional diagnostics can support in category inference. Distribution shape, labeled-group separation, and model-based component structure each answer a legitimate question, but problems arise when their outputs are treated as direct measures of linguistic categoryhood. Modality provides the central formal example because its relation to population structure can be derived explicitly. The contribution is methodological rather than mathematical: the paper integrates established distributional results, category-recovery simulations, and empirical cases within a single framework for evaluating cross-level inference. Section 2 uses modality and variance to establish the formal problem. Section 3 evaluates category recovery when the outputs of mode-based diagnostics and mixture models are interpreted as category number in finite samples. Section 4 applies the same distinction to empirical cases. Together, these analyses show how surface diagnostics can inform category analysis and why category-level claims require additional linguistic evidence.

The reverse inference is also insufficient. A bimodal surface density shows that two modes are present, but it does not identify the mechanism that produced them. As noted in Section 1, bimodality admits multiple explanations (Murphy, 1964). In linguistic data, two modes may well reflect two distinct populations of tokens, such as conditioned realizations of a single category, different speaker or task groups, or different measurement procedures, but these need not be the categories under investigation. The two errors are symmetrical. Unimodality of the mixture distribution cannot rule out multiple independently established speech categories, and bimodality of the mixture distribution cannot establish multiple linguistic categories without evidence that the modes correspond to distinct units in the relevant linguistic system. Modality constrains an account of category structure but does not uniquely identify it. The next subsection demonstrates the same point with variance.

3 Simulation

3.1 Modality and component recovery

The established result in Section 2.1 concerns population densities. Under the equal-proportion and equal-variance assumptions, the relation between d and 2σ determines whether two speech categories produce one surface mode or two. However, it is crucial to highlight that empirical analyses use finite samples, which introduce sampling variation and make the issue of inferring latent or linguistic categories that much harder than it is for abstract mixture populations. In these samples, sampling variation can add local irregularities to a unimodal pooled density or hide weak separation in a bimodal pooled density. The following simulations examine common diagnostics when the number of underlying speech categories is known but only the pooled acoustic observations are analyzed.

The simulations retain the idealized correspondence established in the formal example: two independently specified speech categories generate two Gaussian VOT populations. The mixing proportion is fixed at p = 0.5, and the shared standard deviation at σ = 12.5. The distance between the two conditional means, d, takes the values 10, 15, 20, 24, 26, 30, 35, and 40. Since 2σ = 25, the pooled population density is unimodal when d < 25 and bimodal when d > 25. Values close to 25 lie near the transition boundary.

For each value of d, we draw a pooled sample of 500 tokens and repeat the procedure 1,000 times. Each sample is analyzed with four diagnostics: (i) peak counting on a kernel density estimate (KDE), (ii) Hartigan's Dip Test, (iii) Silverman's test, and (iv) a Gaussian mixture model (GMM) comparing one-and two-component solutions by BIC. We evaluate these methods because they are commonly used to infer category structure from phonetic distributions. The methods do not estimate the same statistical object: KDE peak counting and the mode tests concern surface modality, whereas GMM concerns statistical components under a specified model. To compare them, we treat each output as an estimate of the number of underlying speech categories.

We define category-recovery accuracy, henceforth shortened to accuracy, as the proportion of replications in which this interpretation correctly recovers the two underlying speech categories used to generate the data. For KDE, a replication is counted as accurate when exactly two local maxima are detected; one peak or three or more peaks do not recover the two categories. For a formal mode test, it is counted as accurate when unimodality is rejected. For GMM, it is counted as accurate when a two-component model is preferred. This definition does not evaluate whether each method correctly answers its own statistical question. A mode test may correctly identify the pooled population density as unimodal while being counted as inaccurate under the category-number interpretation.

The four diagnostics are implemented as follows: 1.

Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) (Silverman, 1986): We estimate the density with the density function in R (Gaussian kernel, evaluated on a grid of 512 points) using Silverman's rule-of-thumb bandwidth (bw.nrd0), and count local maxima as sign changes in the first difference of the estimated density. KDE directly describes the shape of a smoothed density estimate, so the number of detected peaks depends on bandwidth selection.

2. Hartigan's Dip Test (Hartigan & Hartigan, 1985): We apply Hartigan's Dip Test Δ (Separation) using the diptest package (Maechler, 2024). The test measures the maximum distance between the empirical distribution and the best-fitting unimodal distribution. A p-value below 0.05 is treated as evidence against the null hypothesis of unimodality.

3. Silverman's Test (Silverman, 1981): We apply Silverman's test using the multimode package's modetest function (Ameijeiras-Alonso, Crujeiras, & Rodríguez-Casal, 2021) with method = "SI". The test is based on the critical bandwidth required to obtain a unimodal density estimate. Like the Dip Test, it evaluates surface modality rather than linguistic category number.

4. Gaussian Mixture Modeling (GMM) (McLachlan & Peel, 2000): We fit oneand two-component Gaussian mixture models using the mclust package (Scrucca, Fop, Murphy, & Raftery, 2016) and compare their BIC values (Schwarz, 1978). BIC balances model fit against model complexity. Unlike the preceding methods, GMM selects a representation in terms of statistical components. Its output depends on the assumed component distributions, the parameterization of their variances, and the model-selection penalty.

Modebased diagnostics therefore have no population-level bimodality to detect. A unimodal result correctly describes the pooled density but counts as inaccurate when it is interpreted as evidence for one underlying category. Low category-recovery accuracy in this region is consequently not a failure of modality inference. It follows from the fact that the number of modes does not correspond to the number of categories specified in the simulation.

When d is substantially greater than 2σ, the pooled population density has two increasingly separated modes. The two-mode structure then provides surface evidence compatible with the two underlying categories, and category-recovery accuracy generally increases. The most sensitive region lies near d = 2σ, where the density is close to the transition between one and two modes. Its central portion is relatively flat, so sampling, smoothing, and model-selection choices can affect the category-number interpretation of the observed data.

Figure 5 shows the effect of sample size. When d < 2σ, no sample size will help recover the underlying two-category structure because the pooled density itself is unimodal. For a fixed value above the boundary, a larger sample reduces sampling uncertainty and improves the detection of population bimodality by the Dip Test (at d = 35, from 42% at N = 1000 to 93% at N = 3000), and BIC-selected GMM recovers two components in nearly every replication from d = 26 onward once N ≥ 2000. KDE does not follow this pattern: above the boundary its accuracy decreases with sample size (at d = 30, from 76% at N = 1000 to 63% at N = 3000), because the narrower bandwidth produces additional sample-specific peaks, so that more samples show three or more maxima rather than two. Category recovery remains unstable near d = 2σ within the sample sizes examined here because the curvature at the midpoint approaches zero at the boundary. The center of the density is nearly flat, so relatively small sampling or smoothing differences can affect the observed number of peaks.

We also tested three additional mode-based methods: the HY test (Hall & York, 2001), the FM test (Fisher & Marron, 2001), and the ACR test (Ameijeiras-Alonso, Crujeiras, & Rodríguez-Casal, 2019). When d < 2σ, low category-recovery accuracy is structural because the pooled population density is unimodal. When the pooled population is weakly bimodal and the sample is finite, a test may also have limited power to reject unimodality. This second limitation concerns the statistical behavior of a particular test; it is distinct from the first, which follows from interpreting modality as category number.

KDE-based peak counting is also sensitive to analytical choices. Figure 8 compares category-recovery accuracy under different bandwidths. The resulting category-number interpretation is particularly sensitive near the transition boundary.

The simulations reveal two sources of low category-recovery accuracy. When d < 2σ, the source is structural: two categories generate a genuinely unimodal pooled population density. When the pooled population is weakly bimodal, finite-sample variation, bandwidth selection, test power, and model-selection penalties affect whether the output indicates two categories. A mode test may therefore characterize a pooled density correctly while failing to recover the two prespecified categories under the category-number interpretation evaluated here. This limitation matters most where category number is actually in dispute. Debates about mergers, near mergers, and incomplete neutralization typically concern categories whose acoustic differences are small relative to their variability. These are the cases lie near or below the 2σ boundary, where the diagnostics are least reliable. Note that even in this idealized setting, where the underlying categories are known, mode-based diagnostics and mixture models cannot serve as standalone evidence for category number in empirical data.

3.2 Variance

The standard variance decomposition applied in Section 2.2 shows that variance magnitude does not map directly onto the number of underlying categories. A mixture of two categoryconditioned populations may have variance either greater or smaller than its more variable component. Conversely, a large surface variance can arise from heterogeneity within a single category. Larger variance therefore does not establish more categories, and smaller variance does not establish fewer categories. The following simulation examines a finite-sample consequence of this structural non-correspondence. The simulation demonstrates one direction of the broader non-correspondence: two known speech categories do not necessarily produce a pooled variance greater than the larger conditional variance, and finite samples make this comparison less stable near the theoretical boundary. The reverse inference is also unsupported. A large variance may reflect separation between populations, but it may instead reflect variation among speakers, lexical items, phonological environments, tasks, or measurements within a single category. Variance magnitude alone therefore cannot distinguish one category from multiple categories.

Like modality, variance can describe and constrain an account of a surface distribution, but it cannot determine category number. A smaller pooled variance does not establish fewer categories, and a larger pooled variance does not establish more.

4 Empirical implications

The preceding sections distinguished surface properties from category structure through formal examples and simulations. The same distinction guides empirical analysis: distribution shape describes the modality of pooled observations, labeled-group metrics describe acoustic separation after labels have been assigned, and mixture models represent statistical components. These diagnostics may fail to recover a distinction established on independent linguistic grounds or may identify surface structure that does not correspond to a category distinction. The cases below illustrate both possibilities.

4.1 Distribution shape is not categoryhood

A first source of cross-level inference is the interpretation of speaker-specific distributional patterns. Morrison (2023) examines lax and tense realizations of /e/ in Ness Gaelic and reports differences among speakers: some density plots show stronger evidence of bimodality, whereas the Dip Test is non-significant for other speakers. Figure 10 shows the observed speaker-specific distributions, and Table 1 reports the evidence against unimodality after Holm-Bonferroni correction. Neither result establishes that the speakers have different underlying linguistic systems. 1Between-speaker differences in sampled patterns may arise even when the generating system is held constant. Sampling error has long been recognized as a source of apparently multimodal patterns in small samples (Murphy, 1964), so speakers who share the same underlying categories may show different sampled patterns. Figure 11 examines mode-test outcomes in repeated samples comparable in size to the Ness Gaelic speaker samples. The figure uses ∆µ for the same distance between category means denoted by d above. At each value, all replications share the same generating parameters, yet sampling variation produces different test outcomes. This result identifies sampling variation as a plausible source of apparent between-speaker differences. It does not show that the Ness Gaelic speakers have the same system; it shows that different density shapes or significance outcomes are insufficient evidence that their systems differ. American English vowels illustrate the converse problem. The vowel labels in the Hillenbrand, Getty, Clark, and Wheeler (1995) dataset represent distinctions that are independently established in the linguistic system. For the present analysis, data handling was limited to selecting the male-speaker observations from the original dataset; all statistics and density estimates reported here were calculated from these data. Table 2 compares multivariate separation, measured by Pillai's trace, with univariate modality, measured by Hartigan's Dip Test, for several proximal vowel pairs in these data. Several known vowel contrasts do not produce significant bimodality in either F 1 or F 2 . Under the category-recovery interpretation evaluated here, the method therefore fails to recover distinctions whose linguistic status is not in question.

The /ih/-/iy/ contrast illustrates this failure. Pillai's trace indicates multivariate separation, but Hartigan's Dip Test does not find significant bimodality in either F 1 or F 2 . Figure 12 shows the two labeled categories in the joint acoustic space, while Figure 13 shows their univariate estimated densities. The absence of two modes in either dimension does not remove the independently known vowel distinction. It shows that the number of categories cannot be recovered from univariate modality in this case.

Together, the Ness Gaelic and Hillenbrand cases show why speaker-level distribution shape cannot be read as linguistic structure. Different sampled patterns do not necessarily Δ (Separation) imply different underlying systems, and similar or unimodal patterns do not necessarily imply a single category. Density plots and mode tests remain informative about surface modality, but the move from modality to categoryhood requires additional evidence.

4.2 Labeled-group distance is not categoryhood

The Hillenbrand analysis also provides a transition to a second evidential level. Once vowel labels are available, Pillai's trace can evaluate their separation in a multivariate acoustic space. Measures such as Pillai's trace, Euclidean distance, and Bhattacharyya affinity answer this conditional question: given a set of labels, how strongly do the corresponding observations overlap or differ in the selected dimensions? They do not establish the linguistic status of the labels. The source of those labels is therefore important. In the Hillenbrand data, the vowel categories are independently known, and the acoustic analysis examines how they are realized. In other studies, speakers' merger-status labels may instead be created from a clustering solution (e.g., Zhang et al., 2022). When the same measurements are then used to validate those labels, the analysis becomes circular. Acoustic separation can support a description of labeled groups, but it cannot independently validate labels derived from that separation.

The magnitude of labeled-group distance is also conditional on the acoustic dimensions, sample size, and within-group variability. A weak difference in one dimension may coexist with separation in another dimension or in a multivariate space. It cannot establish that two independently motivated categories have merged. Conversely, a large acoustic difference shows that the assigned groups are separated in the measured space, but it does not show that the groups function as distinct units in the linguistic system. Labeled-group metrics evaluate the phonetic realization of labels; categoryhood requires evidence for the labels themselves.

4.3 Latent components are not categoryhood

A third evidential level concerns latent statistical components. A Gaussian mixture model compares representations with different numbers of components under assumptions about the component distributions, their variances or covariances, and the model-selection criterion. Its direct output is therefore a property of a fitted statistical model, not a count of linguistic categories.

In the simulations in Section 3.1, the speech categories and their one-to-one correspondence with Gaussian components are known by construction. In empirical data, however, the relationship between statistical components and linguistic categories is not known in advance. Multiple components may reflect conditioned realizations, speaker groups, styles, or measurement structure within one linguistic category. Dillon, Dunbar, and Idsardi (2013) illustrate this problem with Inuktitut vowels, which are realized differently before uvular consonants. A standard Gaussian mixture fitted to the acoustic data recovered the intended categories only unreliably, with the solution depending on the prior and the amount of data; the three phonemic categories were recovered more accurately when the model jointly learned the context-conditioned relation between allophones. Thus, each phoneme corresponds not to a single Gaussian component but to linked, context-dependent distributions. Conversely, one component may cover multiple categories when their acoustic distributions overlap or when the relevant distinction is realized in a dimension omitted from the model.

Model selection adds a further dependency. AIC, AICc, BIC, and ICL may prefer different component structures because they balance fit, complexity, and component separation differently. A one-component solution therefore cannot establish that a merger is complete, and a two-component solution cannot establish that two linguistic categories are present. Mixture models can identify statistical heterogeneity and generate hypotheses about its sources. The correspondence between a component and a linguistic unit must be demonstrated with evidence outside the component solution itself.

4.4 Linguistic evidence is needed for linguistic category inference

The preceding cases distinguish the existence of a category from the performance of a diagnostic. This distinction is also central to earlier critiques of quantitative classification (Murphy, 1964). An acoustic method may describe how well independently motivated categories are separated, but the same diagnostic alone cannot establish that those categories exist. Category-level claims instead require converging evidence suited to the type of category being proposed. Phonological categories may be supported by patterning and alternations; and sociolinguistic categories by social evaluation, style-shifting, and community-level meaning. As far as possible, this evidence should be independent of the surface distributional measure being evaluated.

Mandarin Tone 3 sandhi illustrates the role of such evidence in the case of phonological categories. A Tone 3 preceding another Tone 3 is realized with a rising contour that may be phonetically similar to Tone 2. Surface similarity or difference alone does not determine the underlying linguistic representation. Speakers use knowledge of Tone 3 sandhi in prosodic judgment (Lai & Li, 2022), and auditory priming evidence indicates that listeners access the underlying /T3 T3/ representation during lexical processing (Chien, Sereno, & Zhang, 2016).

The category-level analysis is supported by how the forms pattern in phonology and lexical processing, not by the shape of a pooled acoustic distribution.

Incomplete neutralization in German word-final devoicing provides a second example. German final devoicing has been analyzed as neutralizing underlying voiced and voiceless obstruents in word-final position, as in Rad "wheel", with underlying /d/, and Rat "council", with underlying /t/ (Roettger, Winter, Grawunder, Kirby, & Grice, 2014). Phonetic studies have nevertheless found small differences in preceding vowel duration between forms with underlying voiced and voiceless final obstruents (Nicenboim, Roettger, & Vasishth, 2018). Grawunder (2014) examines the stability of such incomplete-neutralization effects across varieties, including Austrian Tirol German. These data provide a test case for surface diagnostics because the underlying voicing distinction is established by morphological alternations, independently of the acoustic measure. The Dip Test results and density estimates reported here were computed from the original data without modification.

The acoustic difference is small relative to production variability. In the pooled duration data illustrated in Figure 14, Hartigan's Dip Test does not reject unimodality (D = 0.0098, p = 0.8423). This result addresses the modality of the pooled duration distribution. At the same time, it shows that a distinction known to exist in the grammar can leave no detectable trace in surface modality: as with the Hillenbrand vowels, a researcher relying on modality alone would recover a single category where the grammar has two underlying categories. Therefore, establishing such a distinction requires evidence from phonological patterning, lexical structure, processing, or other relevant behavior. A duration distribution can provide evidence about phonetic implementation, but it cannot decide the underlying category analysis by itself.

Together, the empirical cases support interpreting distributional evidence at the level it directly addresses. Surface shape, labeled-group separation, and model-based components constrain category analyses and identify patterns that require explanation. Independent linguistic evidence supports the inference to categoryhood.

5 Discussion

5.1 Summary of the argument

This paper examined cross-level inference from distribution shape, labeled-group separation, and model-based component structure to linguistic categoryhood. Modality provides the clearest formal example: two independently specified speech categories can produce a unimodal pooled population density when their conditional means are sufficiently close, while bimodality does not identify the mechanism that generated the modes. Variance exhibits the same non-correspondence. A mixture variance is at least as large as the weighted average of the conditional variances but need not exceed the larger conditional variance. Its magnitude therefore cannot determine category number.

The simulations evaluated the additional interpretation imposed when diagnostic outputs are treated as category number. When d < 2σ, a mode test can correctly characterize the pooled population density as unimodal while showing low category-recovery accuracy. Near and above the boundary, test power, sampling variation, and bandwidth selection affect recovery. GMM achieves higher accuracy under the simulated one-category-one-component correspondence, but that stipulated correspondence cannot be transferred to empirical data.

The simulated settings are more favorable than most linguistic datasets. In both simulations, the observations follow Gaussian distributions, and each category corresponds to one Gaussian component. In the modality simulations, the two categories also have equal proportions and conditional variances. However, empirical data may contain unequal frequencies and variances, non-Gaussian distributions, and clustering by speaker, lexical item, phonological environment, or task. These factors can alter surface shape and weaken the correspondence between statistical components and linguistic categories. The diagnostics already have difficulty recovering known categories under favorable conditions. They are therefore unlikely to perform better on empirical data. The empirical cases are consistent with this expectation. The Ness Gaelic case shows that the same generating parameters can yield different sampled patterns, and the Hillenbrand vowels show that established category distinctions may not produce detectable univariate bimodality.

5.2 Methodological implications

The results support using each quantitative diagnostic at the level it directly addresses. Mode tests evaluate surface modality. Labeled-group metrics evaluate separation after labels have been assigned. Mixture models compare component structures under particular distributional and selection assumptions. Agreement among these methods may strengthen a description of surface structure. However, it does not automatically strengthen a category-level claim because the methods do not provide independent evidence that the relevant modes, labels, or components are linguistic units.

This distinction is especially important in cases of near merger, incomplete neutralization, dialect contact, and category acquisition. When acoustic distributions are close, surface evidence is less decisive even though the linguistic distinction may be theoretically important. The appropriate response is not to abandon acoustic analysis, but to specify the inference that each result supports. A category analysis should state what type of category is proposed, what the diagnostic directly estimates, how any labels were obtained, and whether the same measurement is being used both to define and validate those labels. It should then identify the independent evidence that licenses a category-level interpretation and consider alternative mechanisms compatible with the observed surface pattern.

Independent category evidence must also be appropriate to the claim. Phonological patterning and alternations can support phonological categories; and social evaluation, styleshifting, and community meaning can support sociolinguistic categories. No single behavioral measure provides a universal criterion. Stronger category inferences arise from converging evidence whose relation to the proposed category is explicit and which is, as far as possible, independent of the surface distributional measure under evaluation.

5.3 Limitations

The formal results are deliberately simple. The 2σ threshold applies to a one-dimensional mixture of two Gaussian populations with equal proportions and equal variances, and the simulations also only use a single acoustic dimension. Multivariate analyses may change both surface shape and diagnostic performance. As noted above, however, the other simplifying assumptions of the simulations favor the diagnostics rather than weaken the argument.

The empirical cases also concern different kinds of linguistic distinction. A phonological category, a lexical representation, a dialect category, and a socially meaningful variant do not require identical validation criteria. The purpose of comparing these cases is not to propose a single test for categoryhood, but to show why evidence must be matched to the category claim. The present argument therefore limits the interpretation of surface diagnostics; it does not show that mode tests, labeled-group metrics, or mixture models are invalid for the statistical questions they directly address.

5.4 Conclusion

Surface distributional diagnostics provide useful descriptions and constraints. They can identify modes, measure labeled-group separation, and compare statistical component structures. By themselves, however, they cannot determine whether a linguistic contrast has been retained, lost, or reorganized. What is needed is not less quantitative analysis, but a clearer account of what each analysis measures and what additional evidence supports the move to categoryhood. We infer linguistic categories from how forms function in the linguistic system. Surface distributions contribute to that inference rather than determine it.
