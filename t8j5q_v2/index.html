Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Cognitive theories are among the leading conceptualizations of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD). The core perspective shared by these theories is that distorted thinking (e.g., "no one can be trusted") is involved in maintaining symptoms. However, the proximal mechanisms linking cognition and PTSD symptoms remains unclear due to two major methodological limitations: 1) lack of research on cognition-symptom dynamics at the daily level, and 2) reliance on self-reported thought distortions, which is a method ill-suited for capturing biases that people often do not have insight into or are outside of their awareness. The present study helps address these limitations in a sample of trauma-exposed patients (N=510) who completed daily video diaries and symptom ratings for 90 days (n=30,958 days). We used two natural language processing techniques to assess cognitive distortions from the diary transcripts: a lexica-based and large language model (LLM) approach. We then established the day-to-day sequencing of cognitive distortion and symptom fluctuations using dynamic structural equation models. Across the lexical and LLM models, distorted thinking did not predict worsening symptoms (s=-.02-.01). Instead, worsening symptoms predicted more distorted thinking (s=.03-.07). Also, symptoms and cognitive distortions tended to fluctuate together (s=.08-.41). The current findings suggest that cognitive distortions do not precede PTSD symptoms as many theories have assumed, but may instead be a consequence or correlate of them. If replicated, our results could help refine cognitive and related models of PTSD.

Cognitive theories are a leading conceptualization of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD). The core hypothesis shared by these models is that distorted thinking provokes and maintains symptoms over time. Cognitive distortions include biases such as catastrophizing (e.g., "if I go back there, I'll completely lose control") or overgeneralizing (e.g., "the world is fundamentally unsafe"). Although the specifics vary across theories, distortion-symptom mechanisms can be distilled into three basic temporal patterns. The most widely recognized of these is the hypothesis that cognition is a proximal precipitant of PTSD symptoms (i.e., cognitive vulnerability). [1] [2] [3] [4] That is, when people engage in maladaptive cognitive processes, these thoughts lead to symptoms like avoidance, hyperarousal, dysphoria, or re-experiencing. This hypothesized sequence of thoughts to symptoms is the reason cognitions are a primary target in many frontline PTSD treatments. [5] [6] [7] [8] Two other temporal patterns connecting cognitive distortions to PTSD symptoms are possible but have received less attention. A second mechanism is the reverse of cognitive vulnerability, such that symptoms lead to cognitive distortions (i.e., cognitive reactivity). 9, 10 Cognitive reactivity could reflect various processes including mood-congruent attentional and interpretive biases 11 or maladaptive appraisals about symptoms themselves (e.g., "my intrusions are a sign of going crazy"). 12, 13 Finally, a third mechanism may be that symptoms and cognitive distortions are elicited by same situations (i.e., cognition-symptom coactivation) rather than one driving the other. 14, 15 The mechanisms underlying these three temporal patterns are not necessarily mutually exclusive, and are intertwined with other behavioral responses, but they nonetheless form the foundation of cognitive theories. 14, 15

Empirical evidence supports an association between PTSD and cognitive distortions.

Cross-sectional research consistently shows that people diagnosed with PTSD and those with more severe symptoms endorse more distorted thinking (e.g., catastrophizing, personalization). 12, 16, 17 Longitudinal studies suggest that the severity of distorted thinking before or shortly after trauma exposure predicts the likelihood of developing PTSD weeks or months later. [18] [19] [20] [21] Results from treatment studies are more mixed, with results showing changes in cognitive distortions precede symptom changes (but not vice versa), changes in distortions both predict and follow changes in symptoms, cognition and symptoms change concurrently, and that cognitive changes do not predict symptom changes at all. 5, 6, [22] [23] [24] [25] Thus, the bulk of evidence suggests that distorted thinking relates to PTSD, but the relative strength of each mechanism is unclear or whether cognitive vulnerability is, in fact, as prominent as theories would suggest.

Methodological limitations have hindered a more definitive test of mechanisms proposed by cognitive theories. 26 Two are especially relevant: First, all longitudinal studies to date have included only a small number of time points (i.e., < 4) separated by weeks or months rather than ambulatory assessment or daily diary designs, which increases the influence of recall bias and misses short-term dynamics between thoughts and symptoms. 27, 28 Indeed, processes at a short time scale are considered especially important to understand according to cognitive theories, which posit that thoughts triggered by everyday reminders of the trauma event and its sequelae elicit symptoms and prolong PTSD. 2, 15 The only study that examined such short-term dynamics with an ambulatory assessment design found re-experiencing symptoms and cognitive distortions tracked together within a day, consistent with coactivation mechanisms. 29 However, because this study focused only on concurrent associations, the temporal order of cognitive distortions and symptoms at short time scales remains unknown.

The second limitation to date is that cognitive distortions in PTSD have only been measured with self-report surveys. One problem with this method is that the survey items are often very similar or even identical to PTSD symptom criteria. Most obviously, criterion D2 in the DSM for PTSD is "persistent exaggerated negative beliefs about oneself, the world, and others," 30 which is essentially the same as cognitive distortion survey items like "the world is a dangerous place" or "there is something wrong with me as a person." 13 Such overlapping content can blur the distinction between predictor and outcome, limiting our ability to infer mechanistic relationships. Another more fundamental problem is that self-report questionnaires are simply an ill-suited method for the construct because cognitive distortions are, by definition, automatic and often occur outside of conscious awareness. 31 Moreover, people often lack insight into their biases, [32] [33] [34] especially when they become ingrained beliefs as is often the case in PTSD. 4, 35, 36 A method that overcomes these problems with self-report measures is to assess cognitive distortions directly from the language people use when describing their unique experiences, with natural language processing. 37 In particular, people's responses to open-ended prompts may reveal subtle or implicit thinking patterns that they may have limited awareness of 38, 39 and would be missed by a self-report distortions measure. This feature makes analyzing cognitive distortions from language a uniquely strong fit to the construct. 40 Further, because it is an entirely distinct method from a questionnaire, it is unconfounded with reported symptoms. Two modeling approaches can be applied to measure cognitive distortions from natural language: 41 1) Lexical models that count the frequency of certain words or short phrases associated with thought distortions, and 2) zero-shot estimation with large language models (LLMs), which involves prompting an LLM to assess cognitive distortions from text without task-specific training. 42, 43 Reinforcing the value of this method for testing cognitive theory, cross-sectional work has shown language-based measures of cognitive distortions have strong associations with internalizing psychopathology (e.g., depression). [44] [45] [46] Despite this potential, language-based assessments of cognitive distortions have not yet been used to understand PTSD maintenance mechanisms. A strong test of cognitive theories could benefit from tracking cognitive distortions measured from language and symptom dynamics at a short time scale that matches the hypothesized processes.

Present study

This study capitalized on a unique dataset to address limits of prior work and rigorously test the three basic mechanisms proposed by cognitive theories: cognitive vulnerability, cognitive reactivity, and cognition-symptom coactivation. We studied a sample of trauma-exposed patients with elevated PTSD symptoms who completed daily video diaries and symptom ratings for 90 days. From these diaries, we measured daily cognitive distortions using two natural language processing approaches: a lexical model and zero-shot prompting of an LLM. Using dynamic structural equation models, we established the temporal order of cognitive distortions detected from language and day-to-day symptom fluctuations. These models allowed us to compare the strength of these mechanisms head-to-head, at the daily level, for the first time.

Methods

Participants and procedure

This was secondary analysis of data from a project aiming to develop tools for automated monitoring of PTSD symptom severity. Study procedures were approved by the Stony Brook University institutional review board. Data was collected between 2022 and 2025. Participants were recruited from two sources. Majority (71%) were World Trade Center disaster responders recruited from the Stony Brook World Trade Center Health Program. Remaining participants were trauma-exposed outpatients from the Stony Brook University Hospital system. To be eligible, participants had to have elevated PTSD symptoms (PTSD checklist for the DSM-5 [PCL] 47 scores > 12).

A subclinical symptom threshold was chosen to ensure a range of symptom severity were represented. Due to the study protocol's complexity, individuals with cognitive impairment were ineligible (Montreal Cognitive Assessment 48 (MoCA) score < 18).

Diverse trauma exposures were represented; all WTC responders had WTC-related exposures, and the general outpatients reported exposure to a range of qualifying traumatic events (see Table 1 for complete breakdown).

Participation involved an in-person laboratory session followed by a 90-day daily diary protocol. During the initial session, participants were consented and trained on the daily diary protocol, which consisted of recording a video diary on their personal smart device and completing a brief self-report survey of PTSD symptom severity each day. Participants received $5 per post (i.e., survey and video).

Analyses included 510 participants, with a total of 30,958 days (average days per person = 80.0, SD = 18.5). Demographics and descriptives for study variables are in Table 1.

Measures

Daily PTSD symptoms. PTSD symptom severity was assessed using 8 items adapted from the PCL-5. 47 This scale has been validated for use in ambulatory assessment settings and did not include cognitive symptoms. 49 Participants rated symptom severity on a scale from 1 ("not at all") to 5 ("extremely"). Total PTSD symptom severity was calculated by averaging all items.

Daily cognitive distortions.

Cognitive distortions were assessed from language in the daily video diaries. For these recordings, participants were trained to discuss emotionally relevant topics related to their day. 50 They could select their own topics or follow example prompts (e.g., "tell me about the worst part of your day," "describe when you felt most happy today"). Recordings were required to be 3-10 minutes long. Audio from the videos was transcribed using Whisper 51 and cognitive distortions were scored from the resulting text. The average per-video word count was 635.90 (SD=278.68).

We assessed cognitive distortions with two modeling approaches: lexical and zero-shot models. Lexical models were developed to detect words and phrases indexing 12 cognitive distortions related to depression. 52 Word spans were selected to be common expressions that capture the core of each distortion type. Cognitive distortion definitions and corresponding word spans are in Table 2. Multiple studies support the validity of these models, with prior work showing the lexical-based distortions are higher in people with depression. 44, 52 For our analyses, we calculated an overall cognitive distortions score by indexing the number of distortion types present in each transcript.

Zero-shot models involved scoring cognitive distortions using an LLM without taskspecific training (i.e., using just what was learned from the base training data). Specifically, we prompted GPT-5.1 (2025-12-31) to rate the overall prominence of 12 cognitive distortions (same types scored by the lexical models) in each diary text from 0 (not at all present) to 10 (present in most of the transcript). Full prompt is in Table S1. Prior work has shown a zero-shot approach can detect clinical constructs from daily diaries with high levels of accuracy (e.g., convergent rs > .50). [53] [54] [55] [56] Affect and emotional tone. In robustness analyses, language-based affect and sentiment measures derived from the daily diary transcripts were included as covariates. We used established lexical models for positive and negative tone 38, 57 as well as affective valence and arousal. 58

Analytic plan

Our analyses used dynamic structural equation modeling (DSEM), 59 conducted in Mplus (Version 8.11). 60 DSEM combines multilevel models with time series analysis to estimate within-person temporal dynamics. With DSEM, each daily variable (i.e., cognitive distortions and PTSD symptoms) is decomposed into between-person and within-person latent variables.

Between-person variables reflect each person's average level of cognitive distortions and symptoms, whereas within-person variables capture fluctuations in distorted thinking and symptoms from their average levels on a given day.

At the within-person level, we estimated temporal paths representing cognitive vulnerability, cognitive reactivity, and cognition-symptom coactivation. Model set-up is shown in Figure 1. Paths were modeled as random effects, which allows the strength of dynamics to vary across people. Cognitive vulnerability is the lagged association between cognitive distortions and changes in next-day symptoms. Cognitive reactivity is the lagged association between symptoms and changes in next-day cognitive distortions. Finally, cognition-symptom coactivation is the correlation between changes in cognitive distortions and symptoms on the same day. To ensure the lagged effects capture predicted change in next-day symptoms or cognitive distortions, autoregressive paths were estimated as random effects. At the betweenperson level, random intercepts for cognitive distortions and symptoms were correlated, which is akin to a cross-sectional association between distorted thinking and symptom severity. Separate models were estimated for lexical and zero-shot distortion scores.

DSEMs assume there are equal intervals between measurement occasions, but this was not always the case in our sample due to occasional missing days of data (due to e.g., incomplete surveys). We accommodated for this by coding missing days and modeling the missingness with full information techniques. 59 DSEM uses Bayesian Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), meaning point estimates are drawn from the posterior distribution of values generated for that parameter. Because there are no p-values with this approach, parameters are tested based on whether zero is within their 95% credibility interval (CrI).

Results

Variability in cognitive distortions and symptoms

There was substantial within-person variability in cognitive distortions and symptoms.

Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) indicated that there is more within-person (versus between-person) variance in distortions (ICClexical = .27; ICCzero-shot = .41), whereas there is slightly more between-person variance in symptoms (ICC = .61). In other words, these results suggest cognitive distortions are more situation-dependent, while PTSD symptoms are more trait-like.

Figure 2 depicts the dynamic interplay of distortions and symptoms for three randomly selected participants. These plots show both how much these variables fluctuate from day-to-day and that those fluctuations appear to track with one another.

Validity of language-based cognitive distortion measures

Given the novelty of this application of language-based cognitive distortions, we also evaluated their validity in several ways. First, we compared the language-based scores to human ratings. Seven trained research assistants rated the overall presence of cognitive distortions in a subset of 165 transcripts. Interrater reliability was adequate, with a two-way mixed absolute ICC of .62. Human ratings were strongly correlated with both the lexical (r = .31; 95% CrI [.17, .44]) and zero-shot scores (r = .68; 95% CrI [.60, .78]), further supporting their validity.

Second, we examined convergent validity between the two language-based methods.

There was clear evidence of convergence at both the between (r = .56; 95% CrI [.50, .63]) and within-person levels (r = .20; 95% CrI [.18, .20]), indicating they measure similar constructs.

Third, we replicated well-established cross-sectional associations between cognitive distortions and PTSD symptoms. At the between-person level, overall severity of distortions and symptoms were strongly correlated, for both language models (rlexical = .32; 95% CrI [.23, .40]; rzero-shot = .61; 95% CrI [.55, .67]).

Finally, we more closely evaluated the content validity of zero-shot scores because this method has not been applied to cognitive distortions before. Specifically, we examined GPT's rationale for ratings. Rationales were obtained during the scoring procedure by requesting GPT provide a brief explanation justifying the score, citing specific phrases from the transcript (full prompt in Table S1). Rationales are not direct evidence for how GPT made ratings, but are "posthoc" explanations generated by the model that can aid interpretability. 61, 62 Exemplars are shown in Table 2. The rationales also showcase the potential value of GPT's contextual reasoning to obtain more accurate scores than is possible with simple word or phrase counts. In particular, the rationales for low scores suggest an ability to differentiate general distress or superficial signs of distorted thinking from "true" distortions. More examples are in Table S2.

Dynamic models testing cognitive theory

The lexical and zero-shot DSEM results were highly consistent with each other. Results for the within-person level testing the three mechanisms are in Figure 1. Contrary to the cognitive vulnerability hypothesis, cognitive distortions did not predict next-day symptoms in either model. Instead, results were consistent with cognitive reactivity, such that worsening symptoms predict more next-day cognitive distortions (βlexical=.03; βzero-shot=.07). There was also support for cognition-symptom coactivation, with the strongest associations being between same-day cognitive distortions and symptoms (βlexical=.08; βzero-shot=.41).

Although not of focal interest to our study, it is worth noting that the autoregressive paths (shown in Figure 1) suggest there is less day-to-day "spill-over" in cognitive distortions (βlexical=.06; βzero-shot=.13) compared to symptoms (β=.30).

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted two sets of sensitivity analyses. First, we tested the specificity of our results. It is possible that the effects of cognitive distortion variables on symptoms were driven by expressions of negative emotions rather than distorted thinking per se. We tested this possibility with DSEMs estimating the unique associations between distortions and symptoms over and above language-based measures of positive and negative tone as well as emotional valence and arousal. In these models, distortions and an emotion variable were included as simultaneous predictors of same-day symptoms at the within-person level, and of overall symptoms at the between-person level. No lagged paths were included for a maximally conservative test focusing on the strongest associations in our main analyses (i.e., within-and between-person covariance). Results were unambiguous; cognitive distortions (both lexical and zero-shot) predicted symptoms over and above emotion language, at both within-and betweenperson levels. Moreover, the magnitude of association with symptoms changed very little after adjusting for emotional language (average  decrease within-person = .01; between-person = .05). Full results are in Table S3-S4. These findings reinforce that our main results speak to the unique role of cognitive distortions--not just emotions--in PTSD symptom maintenance.

Second, we tested the generalizability of results across populations. For these analyses, we estimated the main DSEMs separately for WTC responders versus the general traumaexposed outpatients. Discussion

This study provided an unprecedented test of cognitive theories in PTSD. In a head-to-head comparison of candidate mechanisms, at a timescale matching the hypothesized processes, and with cognitive distortion measures that can detect automatic, implicit thinking patterns, we find no evidence that distorted thoughts precipitate symptoms. Instead, our results suggest that cognitive distortions are the consequence or correlate of symptoms. If replicated, these findings can help articulate a more precise model for understanding and treating PTSD. 1 The only exception was that the 95% CrI for lexical cognitive reactivity included zero in the general outpatient sample. However, this discrepancy is likely due to reduced precision of the relatively small sample size (n = 146 vs. 364), and we consider the effect to have been replicated because the point estimate fell within the CrI for cognitive reactivity in the WTC responder sample.

Our principal finding is that a central hypothesis of many cognitive theories--that thoughts lead to symptoms--is not supported once key methodological limitations are addressed. While surprising, these results do help make sense of the mixed evidence for cognitive vulnerability in the literature to date. [63] [64] [65] The only real consistent finding has been that there is some association between PTSD and cognitive distortions when measured cross-sectionally or at weekly and monthly intervals. 12, [16] [17] [18] [19] [20] [21] Indeed, we replicated these results in our study, finding strong correlations between symptoms and distortions at the between-person level. Yet, when we zoomed into day-to-day symptom-distortion dynamics, we were able to clarify which short-term mechanisms are likely driving these cross-sectional and long-term associations found in prior work.

The strongest dynamic found was symptom-cognition coactivation. Previous ambulatory assessment studies of self-report cognitive distortions and depression suggested coactivation and cognitive vulnerability may be at play. [66] [67] [68] However, implications for cognitive theories were unclear because overlapping scale content and shared rater biases muddied the distinction between constructs. By assessing cognitive distortions in natural language, there was less opportunity for a methodological confound with symptom expression. Not only are the language-based measures an entirely different method from a self-report survey, but we showed they are not simply picking up on emotional language, reinforcing the distinctiveness from symptoms. All told, this method allowed for more conclusive evidence that distortions track with trauma-related distress, while not predicting future symptoms. This could indicate cognitive distortions are a symptom of PTSD, which would align with the DSM-5 definition that includes negative cognitions as a criterion. 30 Alternatively, this could imply cognitive distortions are merely an epiphenomenon of symptoms. Although our results alone cannot adjudicate these interpretations, they provide a solid empirical foundation to inform ongoing efforts to define the core pathology of PTSD.

According to our results, cognitive reactivity may be a key mechanism underpinning PTSD. This account for the role of distortions has been primarily studied in relation to depression, 10, 63, 69, 70 and here we find it may extend to trauma-related symptoms. There are several explanations for why symptoms would lead to cognitive distortions rather than the other way around. One possibility is that distress activates mood-congruent attentional and interpretive biases. 11 Another possibility is that many traumatized people have persistent, negatively biased cognitions, but these thoughts are usually corrected through effortful processing. When symptoms worsen though, regulating distress may deplete cognitive resources needed to correct thinking biases, leading to increases in cognitive distortions. 64 Yet another possibility is that symptoms trigger distorted thoughts directly, as people often have maladaptive appraisals of symptoms (e.g., "I should be able to handle my reactions better"). 71, 72 Investigating these explanations for cognitive reactivity in PTSD will be a productive direction for future research.

Implications

Our study presents an unprecedented test of mechanisms proposed by cognitive theories of PTSD. If the findings replicate, our theories must be updated. Namely, cognitive theories must be able to account for the observed coactivation and cognitive reactivity effects, and the primary role of cognitive vulnerability revised to reflect the absence of evidence for it. Another consideration for theory development our study underscores are that the time scale of cognitionsymptom processes is underspecified in current models. This issue limits the models' utility because it renders the core claims about mechanisms difficult to falsify. For example, we examined the daily timescale because processes are described as reactions to everyday events, but perhaps cognitions trigger symptoms on the order of seconds, minutes, or hours. Whether cognitive vulnerability (or any other mechanism) unfolds day-to-day versus minute-to-minute not only has vastly different conceptual implications, but it also dictates the appropriate study design. 73, 74 Specifying the timescale of processes will make cognitive theories more precise, testable, and ultimately useful for understanding PTSD.

Research wise, a top priority is to evaluate whether our results replicate. Another natural next step is to determine whether the findings generalize beyond PTSD. Cognitive theory is widely accepted as a generic framework that explains most psychopathology. 65, 75, 76 According to a generic cognitive model, various psychological dysfunctions result from distorted thinking, but disorders are distinguished by specific situational triggers and thought content. 75 For example, social anxiety would relate to self-conscious thought content whereas health anxiety relates to health-related content. Assessing distortions from open-ended narratives enable a true test of the generic cognitive model by separately analyzing thought content from biased processing. Models in our study assessed distorted thought processes, and core content domains (e.g., self-consciousness, health) could be assessed with zero-shot or another language-based method like topic modeling. 77 With these methods, it becomes feasible to test whether a) all psychopathology symptoms are driven by cognitive processes and b) thought content differentiates disorders.

Our results may also have significant clinical implications. Frontline psychotherapies for PTSD--and many other psychiatric disorders--include interventions designed to reduce symptoms by changing thoughts. [78] [79] [80] If our results replicate and cognitions are actually a consequence or correlate rather than cause of symptoms, then this could help explain the limited efficacy of our frontline cognitive treatments. [81] [82] [83] Importantly, another implication is that we could potentially improve PTSD treatment by targeting cognitive reactivity (with e.g., mindfulness-based therapies) instead. 84, 85

Limitations

Limitations of our study should be considered. One limitation is that results may not generalize to all trauma-exposed populations. Although our sample we showed results were robust across two distinct populations (WTC responders and general outpatients), participants were disproportionately White and male. Analyses should be replicated in samples with greater racial and gender diversity to ensure generalizability of results. Another limitation is that we cannot directly compare our results to prior work because we did not have self-report cognitive distortion measures. Of course, our motivation for using language-based measures was to address shortcomings of self-reported cognition, so the lack of self-reports is also a strength.

Nevertheless, studies with both types of distortion measures administered in the same sample would allow for more direct connections to existing literature.

Conclusion

Despite being the dominant paradigm in PTSD theory and treatment, there have been gaps in knowledge about the maintenance mechanisms proposed by cognitive models. Our study begins to address this gap by applying novel methodologies uniquely suited to test the central hypotheses of cognitive theories. If replicated, our results call for a revision of cognitive theories in PTSD and suggest a path forward for redesigning treatment to be more effective. Note. Dichotomous thinking

Thinking that an inherently continuous situation can only fall into two categories.

only, the worst, nobody Repeated all-or-nothing statements: "really nothing... good," "everything... is bad," and "I don't feel happy at all" show black-and-white thinking.

Disqualifying the positives

Unreasonably discounting positive experiences great but, not that great, fine yet Positive moments are acknowledged ("I felt happy when my daughter said, 'Let's go to IHOP.'" ; "My daughter helps me"; "My boys are good"), but later dismissed: "Anything that cheered me today? Not really." and "What happened today you can feel thankful for? Not much."

Emotional reasoning

Thinking that something is true based on how one feels, ignoring the evidence to the contrary.

because I feel, but I feel, still feels Strongly present: "I know everything is fine... but... you can't shake that feeling." The speaker treats anxiety as evidence that situations are unsafe or unmanageable (crowds, neighborhoods), and concludes the day is "shot" based largely on feeling nervous rather than external evidence.

Fortune telling

Making predictions, usually negative ones, about the future. I will not, he will not, you will not Multiple definitive negative predictions: "they're going to deport me," "they're going to send a military ship on me," "I'm going to lose" Social Security and Medicare, "they're going to freaking find me," and "now we're going to suffer."

Labeling and mislabeling

Labeling yourself or others while discounting evidence that could lead to less disastrous conclusions.

a completely, he never, I am a Frequent global labels: "He's a real jerk," "This idiot takes care of the house," "He's such an idiot," "He's... paranoid... gullible," "Even though my son is a pain in the ass," and calling the cat a "little brat."

Magnification and minimization

Magnifying negative aspects or minimizing positive aspects worst, best, the only thing Amplifies negatives with hyperbole: "hurting bodywide," "I can't even freaking breathe," "burning burning burning," and "My cough is killing me."

Mental filtering

Paying too much attention to negative details instead of the whole picture if I only, nothing right, completely wrong

Focus is almost exclusively on negative details: conflict with son, screaming, chasing, family disagreements, service dog issues, and the repeated 'Don't have ...' refrain. Broader context or any positives are largely ignored.

Mindreading

Believing you know what others are thinking.

she will not believe, they will know, everyone will think Frequent assumptions about others' thoughts/intentions: "he enjoys the pain he caused me," "he had no concern for my health or safety at any point," "he just came back to mess me up more," and the Tinder date "trying to take my things."

Overgeneralizing

Making sweeping negative conclusions based on a few examples I am always, no one ever, all the time Sweeping conclusions from specific experiences: "none of us are ever happy," "every generation in my family," "my entire family is screwed up," and "my life has been... nothing but a waste" generalize across people and time.

Personalization

Believing others are behaving negatively because of oneself, without considering more plausible or external explanations for behavior.

all my fault, because of me, I caused Strong self-blame without clear evidence in the transcript: "I might have been the cause of their mother's death" and "I might have killed my wife and my daughter because I went to ground zero and brought something home," attributing others' illnesses primarily to his actions.

Should statements

Having a fixed idea on how you and/or others should behave.

should, ought, have to Explicit prescriptive rules: "things should be different," "you don't say their work here was done," and "Don't say anything. Say I'm here for you. Boom. That's all." These set rigid expectations for how events or others "should" be or behave.
