Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To improve Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Environmental cues can influence action through outcome-related information learned elsewhere, but whether features of the instrumental acquisition history associated with those outcomes remain behaviourally relevant when the outcomes are later predicted by Pavlovian cues is unknown. Across two experiments, one conducted in the laboratory (N = 54) and one online (N = 67), we separated instrumental acquisition under low-and high-effort response requirements from Pavlovian cue learning. Participants first learned that different actions produced outcomes under relatively low or high effort requirements and separately learned cues predicting those same outcomes. Pavlovian-to-instrumental transfer (PIT) was then tested under extinction, with neither outcomes nor response requirements present. In both experiments, reward-predictive cues elicited greater response vigour than a control cue. Critically, cues predicting outcomes previously obtained under high-effort response requirements elicited greater responding than cues predicting outcomes obtained under low-effort requirements. Specific PIT was robust under both acquisition conditions, with cues selectively promoting actions associated with their predicted outcomes. There was also suggestive evidence that high-effort acquisition amplified the magnitude of specific PIT, but this modulation was not consistently supported across experiments or statistical approaches. No corresponding effect emerged for initial outcome-congruent action selection. Overall, response vigour was modulated by instrumental effort history more consistently than outcome-specific action selection, indicating that features of prior effortful instrumental experience can remain behaviourally relevant when the associated outcomes are later predicted by separately learned Pavlovian cues.

1 Introduction

Goal pursuit often requires effort. Obtaining food, completing work, exercising, and pursuing long-term goals all require individuals to invest physical or cognitive resources in the service of desired outcomes. Effort therefore plays a central role in motivation, shaping both whether an action is worth pursuing and how much behavioural investment is mobilised to obtain its consequence (Salamone & Correa, 2024). Yet the consequences of effort are not straightforward. Although anticipated effort is commonly treated as a cost that discourages action, accumulating evidence suggests that effort already invested can continue to influence subsequent evaluation, learning, and behaviour (Inzlicht et al., 2018; Jarvis et al., 2022; Stähler et al., 2025).

This raises a broader question: does the history of how an outcome was obtained continue to influence behaviour even after the original effort requirement is no longer present?

Effort as cost, consequence, and learned history

Much of the effort literature conceptualizes effort prospectively as a cost. When more effort is required to obtain an otherwise equivalent reward, individuals are generally less willing to pursue it, a phenomenon commonly described as effort discounting (Białaszek et al., 2017; Chong et al., 2017; Escobar & Mitchell, 2025). Effort allocation, however, depends not only on objective response demand but also on expected reward, task difficulty, perceived attainability, and the expected benefit of further investment (Silvestrini et al., 2023; Vassena et al., 2019; Verguts et al., 2015). Reward and effort information may also be weighted differently depending on current task demands rather than being combined into a fixed scalar value (Vassena et al., 2019).

Effort is therefore better understood as an action cost whose motivational impact depends on both anticipated demand and the context in which behavioural investment is evaluated. In line with this conceptualization of anticipated effort as a cost, exerting (physical) effort can impose negative consequences for subsequent performance. For instance, repetitive finger movements can induce central motor fatigue (Arias et al., 2015), increase perceived fatigue (Soto-Leon et al., 2020), and, under high response requirements, produce slower responding (Bowyer et al., 2021). Accumulating fatigue can in turn influence subsequent motivation and effort-based choice (Matthews et al., 2023). From this perspective, greater prior effort exertion might therefore be expected to suppress rather than promote later responding.

The consequences of completed effort, however, do not always mirror those of anticipated effort. The effort paradox describes how effort is generally avoided prospectively but can, under some conditions, enhance the value of its consequences or increase later willingness to engage in demanding behaviour (Inzlicht et al., 2018; Clay et al., 2022; Job et al., 2024). For example, rewarding cognitive effort can increase subsequent preference for demanding activities even when the external reward is no longer available (Clay et al., 2022). Greater physical effort can decrease outcome value when considered prospectively yet increase it when evaluated retrospectively (Marcowski et al., 2025). Electrophysiological evidence further suggests that prior physical effort modulates neural responses during reward-outcome evaluation, with reduced reward positivity (RewP) during early feedback processing but enhanced late positive potential (LPP) during later processing (Wu & Zheng, 2023). These effects also depend on how effort is experienced: greater effort can enhance subsequent reward valuation when individuals perceive their actions as contributing to the outcome, whereas the opposite pattern can occur when perceived control is low (Harmon-Jones et al., 2024). More broadly, recent accounts propose that the value assigned to effort depends on its perceived instrumentality for attaining valued goals rather than on effort being intrinsically costly or rewarding in a uniform way (Job et al., 2024). Completed effort therefore does not uniformly increase or decrease outcome value.

Learning perspectives further suggest that these effort-based modulations can persist beyond the initial context in which the action was performed and evaluated. . Stimuli associated with greater preceding physical effort can subsequently acquire greater preference even when the effortful actions themselves remain less attractive (Alessandri et al., 2008; Klein et al., 2005). Learned-industriousness theory similarly proposes that reinforcement of high effort can condition value to effort-related experience and thereby promote subsequent persistence (Eisenberger, 1992). More directly, Jarvis et al. (2022) showed that physical effort altered reinforcement learning, strengthening learning from positive outcomes while attenuating learning from negative outcomes.

Together, effort requirements can therefore influence not only whether and how an action is undertaken but also how this implementation is evaluated and how it influences learning and subsequent behaviour. A complementary question concerns how information about effort requirements is acquired in the first place. In everyday behaviour, individuals are rarely given precise information about how demanding an action will be and must instead learn this through experience. Skvortsova et al. (2014) showed that expected physical effort can be updated through prediction errors, supported by neural systems partly distinct from those involved in reward learning. Hauser et al. (2017) similarly identified parallel effort-and reward-prediction-error signals that were subsequently integrated during action evaluation. Adaptive-effort accounts further propose that individuals learn when additional investment is worthwhile from the relationship between effort, reward, and task performance (Otto et al., 2022; Verguts et al., 2015). Recent work by Bi et al. (2026) extends this account by showing that learned expectations about required physical effort are combined with ongoing sensorimotor feedback and updated as environmental conditions change.

Effort expectations are therefore learned and dynamically updated rather than fixed properties of an action.

Together, these findings establish two complementary principles: effort requirements can be learned from instrumental experience, and prior completed effort can alter subsequent learning and evaluation. A broader theoretical question is whether such effects remain tied to the action through which effort was experienced or become incorporated into representations of the associated outcome and can therefore influence behaviour in a different context. This distinction is important because these alternatives imply different mechanisms: an action-bound effect would reflect learning specific to the effortful response, whereas transfer beyond the original action context would suggest that some feature of effort history is retrieved through the associated outcome. Addressing this question requires separating the later expression of behaviour from the action through which effort was originally experienced. Otherwise, any difference could reflect current response demand, residual fatigue, motor repetition, or direct learning about the effortful action itself. Pavlovian-to-instrumental transfer provides a way to make this separation.

Using PIT to test instrumental effort history

In Pavlovian-to-instrumental transfer (PIT), instrumental response-outcome and Pavlovian cue-outcome relations are learned separately. During transfer, Pavlovian cues can nevertheless influence instrumental responding despite never having been directly paired with the instrumental actions, typically under extinction so that newly delivered outcomes cannot shape behaviour during the test (Badioli et al., 2024; Cartoni et al., 2016).

PIT can be expressed in different aspects of instrumental behaviour. Specific PIT reflects outcome-specific action selection: a cue predicting a particular outcome preferentially promotes the action that previously produced that same outcome (Cartoni et al., 2016; Sennwald et al., 2020). General PIT is more closely associated with response invigoration, whereby reward-predictive cues increase overall instrumental activity (da Costa et al., 2020; Talmi et al., 2008). More broadly, these effects map onto two distinguishable dimensions of motivated behaviour: which action is selected and how vigorously behaviour is expressed (Salamone & Correa, 2012 , 2024). This mapping is not absolute: outcome-specific cues can also alter overall responding, and ostensibly general transfer can retain sensitivity to broader outcome properties (Mahlberg & Weidemann, 2024). In the present study, the distinction between action selection and response vigour therefore provides the primary framework for characterising the influence of instrumental effort history.

Outcome-mediated accounts propose that during transfer, a Pavlovian cue reactivates its predicted outcome, which in turn provides access to instrumental information associated with that outcome (Cartoni et al., 2016; Holmes et al., 2010).

Most PIT research has focused on outcome identity and value. Here, we extend this logic to instrumental acquisition history, asking whether obtaining an outcome under relatively low-or high-effort requirements subsequently changes how a cue predicting that outcome influences behaviour. Critically, the cue is never paired with either the instrumental action or its effort requirement. The shared outcome provides the experimentally established link between the two learning phases. If cues predicting outcomes previously obtained through high versus low effort subsequently elicit different behaviour, this would indicate that some feature of earlier instrumental acquisition history remains behaviourally relevant when the outcome is later predicted.

Such an effect would be consistent with instrumental history influencing behaviour indirectly through the shared outcome, although behavioural transfer cannot establish its precise representational format or retrieval mechanism.

Existing evidence also renders it plausible that such information could influence subsequent action. Reward and effort jointly influence action preparation, and effortrelated value can modulate motor excitability even in the absence of value-based choice (Verguts et al., 2015). Neural evidence from PIT further shows that outcomepredictive cues can modulate premotor activity before instrumental choice and that instrumental R-O learning can alter subsequent processing of the same Pavlovian cues within premotor and broader action-related systems even when overt responding is unavailable (Finotti et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026). Thus, information acquired during instrumental learning may remain relevant to action-related processing when the associated outcome is later predicted by a Pavlovian cue.

Where would an effect of effort history be expressed?

The distinction between action selection and response vigour leads to a more specific question: does instrumental effort history change which action a cue promotes, how vigorously it promotes responding, or both? An effect on response vigour would appear as a difference in overall responding to cues associated with high-versus low-effort outcomes without necessarily changing how responses are allocated between actions.

An effect on action selection, by contrast, would change the relative preference for the outcome-congruent over the incongruent response.

The direction of an effect on vigour is theoretically ambiguous. From a prospectivecost perspective, an instrumental history associated with high effort might retain a costrelated influence and suppress subsequent responding. High-effort options are generally avoided relative to otherwise equivalent low-effort alternatives unless increased reward compensates for their greater cost, and adaptive effort-investment accounts similarly propose that mobilisation depends on whether expected benefits justify anticipated demand (Chong et al., 2017; Escobar & Mitchell, 2025; Silvestrini et al., 2023; Vassena et al., 2019; Verguts et al., 2015). Cues linked indirectly with highrequirement acquisition might therefore elicit less responding. Conversely, retrospective-effort accounts suggest that successfully completed effort can enhance subsequent valuation, alter learning from rewarding outcomes, or increase willingness to mobilise effort again (Harmon-Jones et al., 2024; Inzlicht et al., 2018; Jarvis et al., 2022; Marcowski et al., 2025). If such consequences remain associated with the instrumental history of an outcome, cues predicting outcomes obtained under higheffort requirements could instead elicit greater response vigour. We therefore treated the direction of this effect as an open question.

For action selection, the question is whether instrumental history changes the strength of outcome-specific response bias. If acquisition under different effort requirements alters how strongly a predicted outcome favours its corresponding action, the difference between congruent and incongruent responding should vary between high-and low-effort conditions. If its influence is expressed primarily in vigour, overall response output may differ while relative response allocation remains comparatively stable. Initial action choice provides a complementary measure of selection that is less influenced by repeated responding within a trial.

The present study

Across two experiments (Fig. 1), we examined whether physical effort experienced during instrumental learning subsequently shaped cue-guided behaviour. Participants obtained different food outcomes through relatively low-or high-effort keypress requirements. In a separate Pavlovian phase, distinct cues predicted those same outcomes but were never paired with the instrumental actions or effort requirements.

Transfer was then tested under extinction, with neither outcome delivery nor the original effort requirements present.

We assessed two expressions of transfer. First, we tested whether cues predicting outcomes associated with high-effort acquisition elicited greater or lower response vigour than cues associated with low-effort acquisition. Because prospective cost and retrospective-effort accounts imply opposing effects, the direction was treated as an open question. Second, we tested whether acquisition history altered specific PIT, indexed by preferential responding towards the action associated with the cuepredicted outcome. If instrumental effort history changes outcome-specific action selection, the magnitude of specific PIT should differ between effort conditions.

Alternatively, if its influence is expressed primarily in response vigour, overall response output should vary while relative response allocation and initial action selection remain stable. These questions were tested first in a controlled laboratory experiment and then in an independent online experiment. By separating instrumental effort from Pavlovian learning and from the demands present at transfer, the study tests whether features of prior instrumental acquisition history continue to influence cue-guided behaviour after the original effort requirement is no longer present, and whether that influence is expressed in response vigour, action selection, or both.

2 Method

2.1 Design and Experimental Overview

Experiments 1 and 2 used the same experimental design and identical task procedure but differed in testing context. Experiment 1 was conducted in the laboratory, whereas Experiment 2 was administered online. Experiment 1 was conducted in a multicomputer laboratory, with a maximum of six participants tested concurrently in each session. To standardise instruction across participants and minimise differences between the laboratory and online experiments, the experimenter did not provide additional verbal task instructions. Participants instead read all instructions on screen and were required to answer comprehension questions correctly before continuing.

Participants wore headphones to minimise interference from other participants in the testing room. Experiment 2 followed the same procedure online. Participants completed the experiment independently on a desktop or laptop computer at a time and location of their choosing and were instructed to perform the task alone in a quiet environment. As in Experiment 1, progression through the relevant task phases required successful completion of the on-screen comprehension checks.

2.2 Ethics, transparency, and open science

The research was conducted in accordance with the General Ethical Protocol of the Faculty of Psychology and Educational Sciences at Ghent University. All participants provided informed consent before taking part in the experiment and were informed that participation was restricted to individuals aged 16-35 years. In Experiment 1, written informed consent was obtained using pen and paper before the testing session. In Experiment 2, participants provided electronic informed consent by selecting an "I agree" option before beginning the online experiment. The experiments were not preregistered. Anonymised data, analysis code, and experimental materials is publicly available on Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23833767).

2.3 Participants

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power (Version 3.1.9.6; Faul et al., 2009) for the critical Response × Cue interaction in a 2 × 5 repeated-measures ANOVA.

Both factors were manipulated within participants, comprising two instrumental responses (left vs. right) and five Pavlovian cues (CS1, CS2, CS3, CS4, and CS-).

Assuming an effect size of f = 0.20, an alpha level of 0.05, 80% power, a correlation of 0.50 among repeated measures, and a nonsphericity correction of ε = 0.75, the analysis indicated a minimum required sample of 38 participants. The Response × Cue interaction was selected because the primary outcome-specific transfer hypothesis concerned whether Pavlovian cue identity differentially biased response allocation between the left and right instrumental actions.

Experiment 1: Laboratory sample

A total of 57 participants were recruited through the Ghent University SONA participant pool and received one course credit per hour of participation. One participant withdrew from the study. Two participants were excluded because they did not meet the learning criteria described in the Exclusion criteria section, resulting in a final sample of 54 participants. Participants were aged 17-25 years (M = 18.70, SD = 1.37; female = 40).

Handedness was predominantly right-sided: 47 participants (87.0%) were righthanded, 4 (7.4%) mixed-handed, and 3 (5.6%) left-handed.

Experiment 2: Online sample

A separate sample of 78 participants was recruited through the Ghent University SONA participant pool and received one course credit per hour of participation. Eleven participants were excluded because they did not meet the learning criteria, resulting in a final sample of 67 participants. Participants were aged 18-32 years (M = 19.75, SD = 2.34; female = 41). Among the 65 participants with available handedness data, 51 (78.5%) were right-handed, 11 (16.9%) mixed-handed, and 3 (4.6%) left-handed.

Four cues (CS1-CS4) predicted the four food outcomes, whereas a fifth cue (CS-) served as a control stimulus and predicted no food outcome. The assignment of colours to cue identities was randomised independently for each participant.

2.5.1 Phase 1: Food selection

Participants first rated 40 food images presented individually in randomised order. For each image, they indicated how much they wanted to eat the depicted food at that moment using a slider ranging from -10 (not at all) to +10 (very much), with the cursor initially positioned at zero. Participants were required to move the slider before the continue button became available, preventing trials from being completed without an active response. Trials were self-paced. The program subsequently evaluated all possible combinations of four food images. For each participant, the combination with the highest mean wanting rating was selected. When multiple combinations had the same mean, the combination with the smallest standard deviation was selected, followed, if necessary, by the combination with the smallest rating range. This procedure was designed to identify four highly wanted foods with relatively similar initial ratings. The four selected foods were then randomly assigned to outcome identities O1-O4.

2.5.2 Phase 2: Pressing-capacity calibration

Following previous physical-effort paradigms in which effort requirements were individually calibrated (Arulpragasam et al., 2018; Gabay et al., 2024; Seaman et al., 2018), participants completed a pressing-capacity calibration to account for individual differences in motor response capacity. Participants completed 12 maximum pressing capacity (MPC) trials: six using the A key with the left index finger and six using the L key with the right index finger. Trial order was randomised. Each trial began with a slot machine displayed without response buttons for 1,000-1,500 ms. The two response buttons, progress bars, and an instruction indicating which key to press were then displayed. Participants were instructed to press the indicated key as many times as possible for 6 seconds. The 6-s calibration interval began with the first valid keypress, ensuring an equivalent period of active responding across participants.

Pressing capacity was calculated separately for the left and right response sides.

For each side, the highest number of presses recorded across the six calibration trials was defined as the participant's MPC. Low-and high-effort requirements were subsequently set to 20% and 80% of the corresponding side-specific MPC, respectively. Thresholds were rounded to the nearest whole number and constrained to a minimum of one press. This procedure yielded four instrumental response-effort conditions: left-low effort, left-high effort, right-low effort, and right-high effort (see Table 1).

2.5.5 Phase 5: Pavlovian training

Pavlovian training established one-to-one associations between four coloured cues (CS1-CS4) and the four food outcomes (O1-O4). A fifth cue (CS-) served as a control stimulus and predicted no food outcome. Cue colours were randomly assigned to cue identities for each participant.

As shown in Fig. 1c, each trial began with an unlit slot machine without buttons presented for 500-1,000 ms. A coloured cue was then displayed for 2,000 ms, after which the cue remained on screen while either its associated food outcome or a nofood symbol was presented for an additional 1,000 ms. Training comprised three blocks of 50 trials, yielding 150 trials in total. Within each block, each of the five cues was presented 10 times in randomised order. Each food-predictive cue (CS1-CS4) was followed by its corresponding food outcome on eight of the 10 presentations and by the no-food outcome on the remaining two presentations, corresponding to an 80% reinforcement probability. CS-was followed by the no-food outcome on all presentations.

To monitor engagement during training, attention checks were included after randomly selected sequences of five to eight Pavlovian trials. A red fixation cross appeared, and participants were required to press the space bar to continue. Because the interval between checks was randomised, the total number of attention checks varied across participants. At the end of each block, participants completed five selfpaced contingency questions assessing knowledge of the cue-outcome associations.

For each cue, they selected the outcome they believed was most likely to follow it from the four food images and the no-food symbol. All participants completed all three Pavlovian-training blocks; progression through training did not depend on contingencyquestion accuracy.

2.5.6 Phase 6: Post-training food ratings (T1)

Participants again rated their current hunger and completed the same five ratings for each of the four selected foods as at T0.

2.5.7 PIT transfer test

The transfer test assessed the influence of Pavlovian cues on freely chosen instrumental responding. It comprised six blocks of 15 trials, yielding 90 trials in total.

Within each block, each of the five Pavlovian cues was presented three times in randomised order, resulting in 18 presentations of each cue across the transfer phase.

As shown in Fig. 1d, each trial began with an unlit slot machine without buttons presented for 1,000-1,500 ms. The Pavlovian cue and both response buttons were then displayed simultaneously. Participants were free to respond using the A key, the L key, or to alternate between the two responses; simultaneous keypresses were not permitted. The first valid A or L keypress initiated a fixed 6-s response interval during which all subsequent keypresses and key releases were recorded. No responseinitiation deadline was imposed during transfer; therefore, the trial remained on screen until a valid response was made. No food outcomes were displayed during the transfer test. Participants were nevertheless instructed that their responses could still earn food rewards and no actual food reward in the current studies, such that transfer was assessed under nominal extinction, consistent with common procedures in human PIT research (Badioli et al., 2024; Finotti et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026).

3.5 Effort-related modulation of specific PIT was less consistent

Having established a robust effect of effort history on overall response vigour, we next tested whether effort history also altered the relative allocation of responding between outcome-congruent and incongruent actions. The primary analysis therefore examined whether the congruency effect (e.g., the difference between congruent and incongruent responding) varied between low-and high-effort conditions.

3.5.1 Experiment 1

Participants made substantially more congruent than incongruent responses (Fig. 3a), providing strong evidence of specific PIT (F(1, 53) = 125.72, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.703; BFinc = 1.00 × 10 44 ). There was also a main effect of Effort (F(1, 53) = 16.62, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.239; BFinc = 0.59) and a Congruency × Effort interaction (F(1, 53) = 7.63, p = 0.008, ηp² = 0.126), but the corresponding Bayes factor provided only weak evidence for the interaction (BFinc = 1.93). Specific PIT was robust under both low-effort cues (Congruent: M = 24.50, SD = 8.05; Incongruent: M = 6.60, SD = 7.32; pbonf < 0.001) and high-effort cues (Congruent: M = 28.72, SD = 8.96; Incongruent: M = 6.06, SD = 6.90; pbonf < 0.001). The congruency effect was larger for high-than low-effort cues (difference = 4.76 responses; t(53) = 2.76, p = 0.008). This difference reflected greater congruent responding under high effort (pBonf = 0.001), whereas incongruent responding did not differ between effort conditions (pBonf = 0.984). Thus, Experiment 1 provided frequentist evidence that high-effort acquisition amplified the magnitude of specific PIT, but the Bayesian evidence for this modulation was weak. The result therefore suggests an effort-related enhancement of the congruency effect without providing strong convergent evidence for it.

3.5.2 Experiment 2

Experiment 2 again showed a robust specific PIT effect (Fig. 3b), with substantially more congruent than incongruent responses (F(1, 66) = 59.92, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.476, BFincl = 1.73 × 10²⁹). There was also a main effect of Effort (F(1, 66) = 13.84, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.173, BFincl = 0.26). Unlike Experiment 1, however, the Congruency × Effort interaction did not reach significance (F(1, 66) = 3.57, p = 0.063, ηp² = 0.051), and the Bayes factor likewise favoured exclusion of the interaction (BFincl = 0.33). Specific PIT was evident under both low-effort cues (Congruent: M = 20.34, SD = 7.07; Incongruent: M = 8.88, SD = 7.05; pBonf < 0.001) and high-effort cues (Congruent: M = 22.47, SD = 8.96; Incongruent: M = 8.93, SD = 7.12; pBonf < 0.001). The descriptive pattern nevertheless resembled Experiment 1: the congruency effect was larger under highthan low-effort cues, although the difference was smaller and did not meet the conventional significance threshold. To characterise the descriptive pattern, exploratory comparisons showed greater congruent responding under high-than loweffort cues (pBonf = 0.010), whereas incongruent responding did not differ between effort conditions (pBonf = 1.000). Because the overall Congruency × Effort interaction was not reliable, however, these comparisons should be interpreted descriptively rather than as evidence that effort strengthened specific PIT.

Across both experiments, specific PIT was therefore robust, and the descriptive direction of the effort-related modulation was similar: high-effort cues produced a larger congruency difference, driven primarily by greater congruent responding. However, this modulation was statistically reliable only in Experiment 1, Bayesian support was weak there, and Experiment 2 provided evidence favouring exclusion of the interaction.

The data therefore provide suggestive but inconclusive evidence that instrumental effort history amplified the magnitude of specific PIT.

3.6 Initial action selection was unaffected by instrumental effort history

Initial action selection provided a complementary test of whether effort history altered which action was selected before repeated responding accumulated within a trial (Fig. 4a-b).

The proportion of left first choices differed strongly across cues in both Experiment 1 (F(2.77, 146.62) = 57.29, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.519; BF₁₀ = 5.23 × 10³³) and Experiment 2 (F(1.98, 130.80) = 34.36, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.342; BF₁₀ = 2.95 × 10²¹). In both experiments, participants preferentially selected the response associated with the cue-predicted outcome, and congruent first choices were above chance under both low-and high-effort cues (all psBonf < 0.001). Importantly, effort history did not alter the proportion of congruent first choices in either Experiment 1 (F(1, 53) = 1.47, p = 0.231, ηp² = 0.027; BF₁₀ = 0.39) or Experiment 2 (F(1, 66) = 0.04, p = 0.835, ηp² < 0.001; BF₁₀ = 0.19). The Bayes factors therefore favoured the model without an effect of effort history on initial action selection in both experiments. Thus, cue-predicted outcomes reliably guided which action participants selected, but this initial selection was unaffected by whether the outcome had previously been obtained under low-or high-effort requirements. Together with the response-count analyses, these findings indicate that instrumental effort history influenced response vigour more clearly and consistently than initial outcome-specific action selection, while leaving open a weaker modulation of the magnitude of specific PIT (see Supplementary Results S1).

4 Discussion

Across two experiments, Pavlovian cues predicting outcomes previously obtained under high-effort response requirements elicited greater response vigour than response-matched low-effort cues. This pattern was observed in both the laboratory and online experiments, even though the Pavlovian cues were never directly paired with the response requirements themselves. At the same time, specific PIT remained robust under both effort conditions. There was also some evidence that high-effort acquisition amplified specific PIT: the Congruency × Effort interaction was significant in Experiment 1 and showed a smaller effect in the same direction in Experiment 2.

However, this modulation was not consistently supported across experiments or statistical approaches, and effort history did not alter initial outcome-congruent action selection in either experiment. Thus, instrumental acquisition history associated with high-effort requirements influenced response vigour robustly, while its influence on outcome-specific action selection was weaker and less consistently expressed.

4.1 Effort history and cue-evoked vigour

Our main finding was that cues predicting outcomes previously obtained under higheffort requirements elicited greater subsequent response vigour than cues predicting response-matched outcomes obtained under low-effort requirements. This direction contrasts with what might be expected if prior effort operated only as a cost. In prospective effort-based decisions, increasing the physical or cognitive work required for a reward generally reduces willingness to pursue it and discounts its subjective value (Białaszek et al., 2017; Chong et al., 2017; Inzlicht et al., 2018; Salamone & Correa, 2024). In the present experiments, however, the effort had already been completed and was no longer required during transfer. The direction of the effect is consistent with evidence that the consequences associated with completed effort can differ from the prospective costs of anticipated effort (Cheval et al., 2025; Inzlicht et al., 2018; Stähler et al., 2025).

This pattern is broadly consistent with the retrospective side of the effort paradox: although effort is typically avoided prospectively, completed effort can sometimes enhance the later motivational significance of its consequences (Inzlicht et al., 2018).

Previous studies have found greater preference for stimuli following more effortful responses, potentially reflecting effort justification or within-trial contrast between the demanding action and its subsequent consequence (Alessandri et al., 2008; Klein et al., 2005; Zentall, 2013). The present findings extend this literature in two respects.

First, the effect was expressed in subsequent response vigour rather than direct preference. Second, the cues eliciting this difference had never been paired with effort; they were related to the effort manipulation only indirectly through their predicted outcomes. Thus, some feature of prior high-effort instrumental experience remained behaviourally relevant when behaviour was later elicited by a separately learned outcome-predictive cue.

Nevertheless, the findings should not be taken to indicate that prior effort uniformly enhances subsequent motivation. Retrospective effects of effort vary across tasks and conditions. For instance, Tsukamoto et al. (2017) found greater preference for stimuli following difficult tasks only when participants made a high proportion of incorrect responses, whereas Aridan & Schonberg (2021) observed no change in snack preferences after associating the items with different levels of grip effort. Other research similarly indicates that the effects of effort on subsequent reward evaluation can vary depending on the experimental context such as evaluation timing and perceived control (Harmon-Jones et al., 2024; Marcowski et al., 2025; Wu & Zheng, 2023). Whereas these studies primarily examined how previously exerted effort influences subsequent reward evaluation or preference, the present experiments demonstrate that instrumental effort history can also influence later response vigour indirectly through Pavlovian cues predicting the same outcomes. Although the generalisability of this effect across different task contexts remains to be established, its consistent expression across the laboratory and online experiments provides converging evidence that prior instrumental effort can enhance subsequent cue-guided behavioural activation, even when effort is no longer required.

Importantly, greater cue-evoked vigour should not be interpreted as evidence that high effort increased the explicit value of the food outcomes. Although wanting, deliciousness, and pleasantness generally declined after training, none of the rating dimensions showed reliable main effects of Outcome or Time × Outcome interactions.

Outcomes obtained through high effort therefore showed no selective increase in liking, wanting, pleasantness, deliciousness, or arousal. Whereas previous retrospectiveeffort studies have often assessed changes in preference or valuation (Alessandri et al., 2008; Klein et al., 2005; Marcowski et al., 2025), the present effect was expressed in later response output. The results therefore support the narrower conclusion that response vigour was modulated by instrumental effort history associated with the higheffort response requirements, without demonstrating either explicit outcome upvaluation or that effort itself was the uniquely responsible feature of acquisition history.

4.2 Effort history and action selection

The influence of instrumental effort history on outcome-specific action selection was less consistent than its influence on response vigour. Participants responded more on the action associated with the cue-predicted outcome under both effort conditions, demonstrating robust specific PIT. There was nevertheless some indication that this outcome-specific response bias was amplified following high-effort acquisition. In Experiment 1, the Congruency × Effort interaction was significant, with a larger difference between congruent and incongruent responding for high-than low-effort cues. Experiment 2 showed a smaller effect in the same descriptive direction, but the interaction did not reach conventional significance. Moreover, Bayesian evidence for the interaction was weak in Experiment 1 and favoured its exclusion in Experiment 2.

The two experiments therefore provide suggestive, but not conclusive, evidence that instrumental effort history may have amplified the response-count congruency effect, rather than consistently strengthening relative outcome-specific action selection.

The form of this pattern is informative. In both experiments, high-effort cues were associated primarily with greater congruent responding, whereas incongruent responding remained largely unchanged. Thus, additional responding elicited by higheffort cues tended to occur on the action already favoured by the cue-predicted outcome. However, a larger congruent-incongruent difference in response counts does not necessarily imply a stronger change in relative action selection, because response counts are also sensitive to overall response vigour. Given that high-effort cues reliably increased total response output, greater vigour could amplify responding on the already preferred congruent action without substantially changing the relative bias between competing actions.

The initial-choice results support this more cautious interpretation. Participants were more likely than chance to select the outcome-congruent action first under both low-and high-effort cues, but effort history did not alter the probability of making that congruent first choice in either experiment. Bayesian evidence likewise favoured the model without an effort effect on initial action selection, although the strength of this evidence was modest. Taken together, the results therefore do not support a simple conclusion that effort history either did or did not affect specific PIT. Rather, high-effort acquisition may have amplified the behavioural expression of an existing outcomespecific response tendency, while evidence that it changed the underlying relative preference between actions was considerably weaker.

This pattern reinforces an empirical distinction between action selection and response vigour. Pavlovian cues can bias which instrumental action is selected while also changing how strongly responding is expressed, and these effects need not vary together (Cartoni et al., 2016; Holmes et al., 2010). Specific PIT primarily indexes outcome-specific action selection, whereas general PIT primarily indexes response invigoration; neither form is defined exclusively by one behavioural dimension. Sennwald et al. (2020), for example, observed both outcome-specific transfer and broader increases in effort mobilization, whereas Lingawi et al. (2022) found that sensory-specific satiety abolished general invigoration while sparing specific PIT.

General PIT can likewise occur without outcome-specific transfer (Degni et al., 2022), although specific PIT can be sensitive to outcome value under some conditions (Eder & Dignath, 2016; Seabrooke et al., 2017 Seabrooke et al., , 2019)).

The present findings extend this dissociation by showing that a feature of prior instrumental experience can influence cue-evoked vigour more consistently than the relative outcome-specific allocation of responding or the action selected first. In motivational terms, this pattern broadly corresponds to stronger modulation of activational than directional aspects of motivation (Salamone & Correa, 2012 , 2024), without implying that these constructs are identical to response vigour and action selection. Importantly, the evidence does not exclude a more modest influence on outcome-specific action selection; rather, such an influence was less consistently supported across experiments and measures than the effect on overall response output. Similarly, although reward-predictive cues increased total responding relative to CS-, this comparison should not be interpreted as a pure measure of general PIT because all reward-predictive outcomes were also associated with instrumental actions.

4.3 Instrumental effort history across learning phases

The most distinctive feature of the present findings is that the effect of effort emerged across separately acquired instrumental and Pavlovian relations. Effort was manipulated while participants learned the response-outcome contingencies, whereas the Pavlovian cues were subsequently paired with the same outcomes in the absence of any action or effort requirement. During transfer, neither the outcomes nor the original effort requirements were presented. The greater vigour elicited by high-effort outcome cues therefore cannot be attributed to direct cue-effort learning. At the same time, the design does not establish that effort itself was retrieved when the Pavlovian cue was encountered. High-effort requirements also involved greater motor repetition and a longer response sequence preceding each outcome. The result therefore identifies behavioural relevance of instrumental acquisition history, rather than the specific feature of that history responsible for the effect.

This result is consistent with the central logic of PIT, according to which a predicted outcome connects independently acquired cue-outcome and response-outcome relations (Badioli et al., 2024; Cartoni et al., 2016). Forward cue-outcome relations support specific PIT, and transfer can vary with outcome value and expected outcome availability under particular testing conditions (Alarcón et al., 2018; Mahlberg et al., 2021; Seabrooke et al., 2019). Neural evidence further indicates learning-dependent differentiation in premotor and broader action-related systems during processing of outcome-predictive cues, including when overt responding is unavailable (Finotti et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026). The present findings extend this account by showing that the vigour of behaviour elicited by an outcome-predictive cue can also vary according to the instrumental acquisition history associated with that outcome.

The suggestive modulation of the specific-PIT congruency effect further raises the possibility that acquisition history may influence not only overall response output but, more modestly, the expression of outcome-specific response tendencies. However, the present evidence is not sufficiently consistent to establish this second effect. The present pattern is therefore consistent with some feature of instrumental acquisition history remaining behaviourally relevant when the shared outcome was later predicted by a Pavlovian cue. This extends the usual PIT question beyond which outcome and action are retrieved to whether characteristics of the instrumental history associated with that outcome can influence the later expression of cue-guided behaviour. It does not, however, establish that those properties were encoded as discrete features of the outcome representation.

Effort-learning research provides a possible framework for understanding how such information could be acquired. People can learn expected effort through predictionerror updating, and effort expectations can be integrated with reward information and ongoing sensorimotor feedback (Bi et al., 2026; Hauser et al., 2017; Skvortsova et al., 2014).

Repeated experience with stable low-and high-effort contingencies may therefore have established distinct expectations about the demands associated with obtaining each outcome. One possibility is that learned expectations about effort demand became associated with the outcomes and were later made behaviourally relevant through the shared predicted outcome. Such an account would provide one mechanism by which separately learned Pavlovian cues could gain access to information established during instrumental acquisition without requiring a direct cueeffort association. This interpretation remains provisional, however, because the present experiments did not estimate effort expectations trial by trial, model effort prediction errors, or manipulate uncertainty during effort learning. The results therefore show that some feature of instrumental acquisition history associated with high-effort response requirements remained behaviourally relevant during later cue-guided responding, but they do not establish that participants retrieved an explicit or computationally defined effort representation.

4.4 Limitations and implications

Several limitations qualify the interpretation of these findings. First, the operationalisation of effort confounded experienced response demand with motor repetition and response-sequence length. High-effort trials required 80% of participants' maximum pressing capacity, compared with 20% for low-effort trials, and therefore involved substantially more keypresses before the outcome was reached.

Although the block-completion criterion equated the target number of rewarded outcomes across response-effort conditions, high-effort response requirements involved substantially more repeated responding within each response-outcome episode. Repetitive finger movements can induce central motor fatigue and perceived fatigue and can slow subsequent responding (Arias et al., 2015; Soto-Leon et al., 2020), while accumulating fatigue can also influence subsequent motivation and effort-based choice (Matthews et al., 2023). Motor repetition and its associated fatigue may therefore have contributed to the later cue effect independently of experienced effort.

Accordingly, the present findings should be attributed to instrumental acquisition history associated with the high-effort response requirement rather than to effort alone.

Future studies should hold response frequency constant while manipulating physical demand through force or resistance. The effort contingencies were also stable and deterministic; manipulating average effort independently of noise or volatility would clarify whether cue-evoked vigour reflects expected demand, uncertainty about that demand, or both (Bi et al., 2026).

Second, subjective differentiation of the effort contingencies was incomplete, particularly in Experiment 2. Nevertheless, analyses restricted to participants who correctly differentiated the high-and low-effort outcomes reproduced the main pattern: More broadly, the findings connect two aspects of effort that are often studied separately. Effort-based choice research primarily examines whether anticipated costs discourage action, whereas research on the effort paradox examines how completed effort shapes later evaluation (Chong et al., 2017; Inzlicht et al., 2018; Marcowski et al., 2025; Salamone & Correa, 2024; Wu & Zheng, 2023). The present results show that instrumental effort history can remain behaviourally relevant when the corresponding outcomes are later signalled by separately learned cues. This influence was expressed most clearly and consistently in response vigour. There was also suggestive evidence that high-effort acquisition amplified the magnitude of specific PIT, but this effect was weaker and less consistent across experiments and measures.

Thus, prior instrumental history appears to influence how strongly cue-guided behaviour is expressed more robustly than it alters which action is selected.

5 Conclusion

Across two experiments, Pavlovian cues predicting outcomes previously obtained under high-effort response requirements elicited greater subsequent response vigour than response-matched low-effort cues, despite never being directly paired with those requirements. Specific PIT remained robust under both effort conditions, and there was suggestive evidence that high-effort acquisition amplified its magnitude, particularly in response counts. However, the Congruency × Effort interaction was significant only in

Experiment 1, with Bayesian evidence varying in strength across analyses, and no reliable effort-related difference in initial outcome-congruent action selection was observed in either experiment. Instrumental acquisition history associated with higheffort response requirements was therefore expressed more clearly and consistently in response vigour than in relative action selection. These findings show that features of prior effortful instrumental experience can remain behaviourally relevant when the corresponding outcomes are later signalled by separately learned environmental cues, while leaving open whether that history also produces a smaller modulation of outcome-specific action selection. Experiment 1

First-choice allocation differed strongly across cues (F(2.77, 146.62) = 57.29, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.519; BF₁₀ = 5.23 × 10³ ³ ). Because these analyses were conducted in reduced subsamples selected on the basis of subjective effort ratings, they were treated as sensitivity analyses rather than replacements for the full-sample analyses.

S2.4 Summary

Overall, restricting the analyses to participants whose subjective effort ratings in their final completed training block differentiated both effort contingencies preserved the principal pattern observed in the full samples. High-effort cues continued to elicit greater overall response vigour, while robust specific PIT was observed under both effort conditions.

Evidence that effort history modulated specific PIT nevertheless remained inconsistent across experiments. In Experiment 1, the raw response-count analysis showed an Effort × Congruency interaction, with Bayesian evidence favouring inclusion of the interaction. In Experiment 2, the interaction was not reliable and Bayesian evidence favoured its exclusion. The sensitivity analyses therefore reinforce the overall conclusion that instrumental effort acquisition history influenced response vigour more consistently than outcome-specific action selection.

Response vigour.

Overall response vigour was defined as the total number of left and right responses emitted during each cue. Participant-level mean response counts were analysed using a one-way repeated-measures ANOVA across the five cue conditions (CS1, CS2, CS3, CS4, and CS-). Planned comparisons contrasted each rewardpredictive cue with CS-and compared each cue predicting an outcome previously obtained under a high-effort requirement with its response-matched low-effort counterpart. Specific PIT. Responses during CS1-CS4 were coded as congruent when the response had previously produced the outcome predicted by the cue and incongruent when it corresponded to the alternative response. Mean response counts were analysed using a 2 (Effort: low, high) × 2 (Congruency: congruent, incongruent) repeated-measures ANOVA. The main effect of Congruency indexed specific PIT, whereas the Congruency × Effort interaction tested whether instrumental effort history altered the relative outcome-specific response bias. Planned comparisons tested congruent versus incongruent responding separately within each effort condition and low-versus high-effort responding separately for congruent and incongruent responses. Initial action selection. Initial action selection was assessed using the first response emitted on each transfer trial. The proportion of trials on which the left response was selected first was analysed across the five cue conditions and compared with chance (0.50). For CS1-CS4, first responses were additionally coded as congruent or incongruent with the cue-predicted outcome. The proportion of congruent first choices was tested against chance separately for low-and high-effort cues and compared directly between effort conditions.

high-effort cues elicited greater vigour, and specific PIT remained robust under both effort conditions. The Effort × Congruency interaction was significant in Experiment 1, with Bayesian evidence favouring its inclusion, whereas the interaction was not significant in Experiment 2, with Bayesian evidence favouring its exclusion. Effort did not reliably alter initial action selection in either experiment. These sensitivity analyses therefore reinforced the robustness of the response-vigour effect while leaving the evidence for effort-related modulation of specific PIT inconsistent across experiments.Incomplete subjective differentiation therefore appears insufficient to explain the findings, although the present study cannot establish whether explicit knowledge of effort history was necessary. The absence of effort-specific changes in food ratings also limits conclusions about outcome value. Although explicit ratings may not capture every motivational consequence of effort, the present data provide no evidence that high-effort outcomes were consciously valued more positively. Future studies could include direct-choice and outcome-devaluation measures. An additional Pavlovian reward outcome without an instrumental association would also help distinguish general cue-evoked invigoration from effects that depend on the outcome's instrumental history. Finally, replication across laboratory and online experiments strengthens the vigour effect, but generalizability remains limited by the repetitive keypress manipulation. Replication using sustained force, resistance-based or cognitive effort, and more naturalistic effort histories is therefore needed.
