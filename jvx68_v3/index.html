To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

We found 1 imprecise p value out of 5 detected p values.

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p ≈ .077 The within-group interaction for repressors did not reach the conventional .05 level, F(1, 9) = 4.00, p ≈ .077, partial² = .31.

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

American Psychological Association (2020). Publication manual of the American Psychological Association, 7 edition. American Psychological Association.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

p = .622 The main effect of defensive group was not significant, F(1, 15) = 0.254, p = .622.
p ≈ .077 The within-group interaction for repressors did not reach the conventional .05 level, F(1, 9) = 4.00, p ≈ .077, partial² = .31.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Raphael Merz

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. 1. Introduction

The study of emotional influences on cognition has been transformed by experimental methods that permit inferences about processing without requiring participants to describe or evaluate emotionally significant information directly. One widely used family of methods presents information at competing locations or channels and measures the speed or accuracy of responses to nominally neutral probes. Differences in probe performance are then interpreted, subject to appropriate assumptions, as evidence about the allocation of attention (Bar-Haim et al., 2007; Yiend, 2010).

The most influential application is the visual dot-probe procedure reported by MacLeod, Mathews, and Tata (1986). Their participants viewed competing words and responded to visual targets appearing at locations previously occupied by those words. The task has subsequently supported a substantial research programme on threat-related attention in clinical and nonclinical populations, while also generating important debates about the reliability and interpretation of attentional-bias indices (Kappenman et al., 2014; McNally, 2019).

The development of this method also has an auditory history. In an undergraduate dissertation submitted at University College London in 1981, I described and implemented a modified dichotic-listening experiment in which reaction times to brief, emotionally neutral tones were used to examine attention between competing speech channels. The emotional content of words in the attended channel and a measure of defensive style were the variables of theoretical interest (Halkiopoulos, 1981). The dissertation, originally submitted under the title Towards a Psychodynamic Cognitive Psychology, is available in the Open Science Framework archive (https://doi.org/10.31219/osf.io/6y3d8). MacLeod et al. (1986) acknowledged that this unpublished research motivated the development of their visual paradigm. Subsequent published accounts also placed the auditory study within the intellectual background of the dot-probe method (Eysenck, 1989; Mathews, 1990; Borkovec, 2004).

The present article has three purposes. First, it reconstructs the theoretical and methodological route by which I arrived at the 1981 experiment. Second, it reports its design and findings in a form accessible to contemporary readers, while explicitly acknowledging the limitations of a small historical study. Third, it places the auditory procedure in the broader methodological history of attentional probes and considers what this comparison can contribute to current research. The focus throughout is the scientific development of experimental methods, not the evaluation of individuals or institutions.

2. Intellectual background and development of the procedure

>Intellectual background and development of the procedure

2.1. Perceptual defence, selective attention, and response bias

My interest in emotional attention emerged from the New Look tradition, which investigated how motivational and emotional factors might affect perception and recognition. Particularly important were discussions of perceptual defence and vigilance: the possibility that threatening material might, under different conditions or in different individuals, encounter elevated or reduced recognition thresholds (Byrne, 1961; Dixon, 1971; Erdelyi, 1974). As a psychology student at UCL, I was interested in whether selected psychodynamic propositions could be expressed as testable claims about ordinary information-processing mechanisms. Norman Dixon supervised the dissertation and provided a particularly relevant intellectual environment.

A central methodological difficulty concerned the interpretation of traditional perceptual-defence findings. If participants are slower to report a threatening word, is that because less information about it was perceptually processed, because they adopted a different criterion for reporting it, or because they hesitated to produce the response? Dixon (1971) and Erdelyi (1974) discussed versions of this problem. Dixon's earlier work offered an instructive alternative: rather than asking participants to report the emotionally significant stimulus, he measured the threshold for a neutral light signal presented in conjunction with subliminal material of differing emotional significance (Dixon, 1958). The general methodological lesson was that an emotionally neutral response might provide indirect evidence about the effects of emotional information.

I approached the same problem through selective attention. Cherry's (1953) dichotic-listening procedure allowed two streams of speech to be presented concurrently while participants shadowed one and ignored the other. I reasoned that a threat-related change in attention might be detectable as a redistribution of processing between the speech channels. The challenge was to obtain an index of that redistribution without simply replacing one emotionally laden recognition response with another.

2.2. Neutral probes as an indirect measure

The solution I developed was to introduce short neutral tones into the ongoing dichotic-listening task. A tone could occur in the ear carrying the shadowed message or in the opposite ear, following a neutral or a threatening word in the attended message. Participants would press the same response key as quickly as possible whenever a tone occurred, regardless of which ear received it or what kind of word preceded it. Thus, the response requirement did not refer to the threatening word or vary according to its emotional content.

The key assumption was that probe detection would ordinarily be faster in the attended than in the unattended ear. If a threatening word prompted a shift of attention away from the shadowed message, that attended-ear advantage should diminish. If it produced stronger concentration on that message, the advantage should increase. The theoretical value of the procedure lay in the interaction between the location of the probe and the emotional significance of preceding material, rather than in the response to either factor in isolation.

The dissertation tested predictions derived from contemporary accounts of defensive style. Individuals classified as relatively repressive or inhibitory were expected to display a reduced attended-ear advantage after threat; those classified as relatively sensitising or facilitative were expected to show an increased advantage. These were hypotheses about styles of managing threatening information as indexed by Ullmann's (1962) facilitation-inhibition measure. They should not be treated as interchangeable with a simple division into high-and low-trait-anxiety groups.

The procedure was implemented in audition for practical reasons: the required dichotic recordings could be prepared with the available technical facilities. A visual implementation was also conceivable, but it was the auditory experiment that was developed and documented in 1981. My account of the route to this design is a retrospective reconstruction, whereas the dated dissertation provides contemporary evidence for the procedure and its underlying rationale.

2.3. Relation to earlier attention-probe methods

Neutral probes and reaction times had been used to study attention before 1981. Posner and Boies (1971), for example, employed auditory probe responses during an ongoing visual task to investigate components of attention and processing demands. Flaherty and Coren (1974) used reaction time in dichotic listening to compare attended and unattended inputs, including tonal targets. Hink and Hillyard (1976) presented irrelevant auditory probes during attention to competing speech messages and measured selective processing electrophysiologically through evoked potentials. These studies are relevant methodological precedents and demonstrate that individual elements of a probe-based approach were already established.

They did not, however, constitute the intellectual route by which I devised the 1981 experiment: I was not aware of these particular probe studies when designing it. The distinction matters because methodological precedence and historical influence are separate issues. The distinctive feature of the 1981 arrangement was the conjunction of competing auditory speech streams, emotional variation in the attended material, neutral lateralised probes, an invariant speeded behavioural response, and predictions about defensive differences in attentional distribution. The contribution is therefore best characterised at the level of this integrated experimental design and its theoretical application, rather than as the invention of probe measurement in attention research generally.

In correspondence following his reading of an account of the study, Steven A. Hillyard, whose earlier work with Robert Hink provided a close electrophysiological comparison, offered this qualified assessment of the 1981 procedure and expressly authorised its quotation: Your undergraduate study is indeed a cool paradigm, and as far as I know it's the first of its type-using RT to neutral auditory probes as a measure of assessing the direction of auditory attention to speech that includes emotional words.

(S. A. Hillyard, personal communication, October 21, 2026; reproduced with permission.) Hillyard also noted that the existence of earlier probe techniques does not preclude originality in a different experimental setting. His assessment is a useful specialist perspective on the distinctiveness of the auditory arrangement, rather than independent evidence of the historical path from the 1981 study to the later visual procedure.

3. The 1981 auditory experiment

>The 1981 auditory experiment

3.1. Participants and classification

Eighteen undergraduate psychology students began the study; 17 contributed to the reported analysis. On the Ullmann (1962) Facilitation-Inhibition Scale, administered after the experimental task, 10 were assigned to the relatively inhibitory (here termed "repressor") group and seven to the relatively facilitative (here termed "sensitiser") group. The more familiar repression-sensitisation terminology of Byrne (1961) is retained to describe the theoretical contrast, although the actual classification instrument was Ullmann's scale. The available report does not provide sufficient information for a modern assessment of cut-off selection, recruitment procedures, or statistical power.

The distinction between defensive style and trait anxiety is important for interpreting these groups. Weinberger, Schwartz, and Davidson (1979), for example, distinguished repressive coping from genuinely low anxiety by considering both reported anxiety and defensiveness. The 1981 study instead divided participants using Ullmann's facilitation-inhibition measure alone and did not independently assess trait anxiety or social desirability. Its results therefore cannot determine whether any group differences arose from defensive style specifically, from correlated differences in anxiety, or from other characteristics associated with the scale. The historical labels used here describe the original theoretical contrast, not a contemporary diagnostic classification.

3.2. Task and materials

Participants heard synchronised pairs of spoken words, one word to each ear, at approximately one pair every 1.5 seconds. They were asked to repeat aloud the words in a designated ear (the attended channel) while ignoring the other stream. The ear designated for shadowing was determined at random. Half the words in the attended stream were emotionally negative and the remainder neutral; all words in the unattended stream were neutral. A male speaker recorded the material. Word frequency was considered using the norms of Kučera and Francis (1967).

The main experimental sequence comprised 40 word pairs. Eight words in the attended channel were followed immediately by a brief, clearly audible pure tone: four designated as neutral (chairs, sale, silver, actor) and four as threatening (grave, fail, cancer, snake). The tone occurred either in the attended or in the unattended ear, with the probe locations distributed across word types. Participants pressed one response key as quickly as possible following each tone. The response was the same for all probes, irrespective of location or preceding word. The word list included words of differing syllabic lengths; the original preprint's characterisation of all selected words as disyllabic has therefore been removed.

Practice was provided before the main sequence. The practice materials included an initial shadowing-only block and a further block containing tones; the protocol allowed repeated attempts to achieve accurate shadowing. The final word lists, recordings, probe duration and intensity, detailed trigger timings, and triallevel responses are not available in the materials examined for the present reconstruction. No new trial-level analysis has therefore been attempted.

3.3. Design and hypotheses

The design crossed a between-participants factor (defensive style: repressor versus sensitiser) with two within-participants factors (tone channel: attended versus unattended; preceding attended-word type: threatening versus neutral). The first prediction was a general reaction-time advantage for probes delivered to the attended ear. The second was a three-way interaction: relative to neutral words, threatening words were expected to reduce the attended-channel advantage among repressors and increase it among sensitisers.

Only eight words were probed in the 40-pair main sequence. If those eight probes were distributed equally across the four combinations of tone channel and word type, each participant contributed just two observations to each condition. This is a limitation of the historical experimental implementation and makes individual condition means sensitive to trial-level variation.

4. From the auditory procedure to the visual dot probe

The auditory dissertation was submitted in March 1981. The visual dot-probe article appeared five years later, in 1986 in (MacLeod et al., 1986)). Although the studies differed in sensory modality, participant samples, and the manner in which competing information was presented, they shared an important inferential arrangement: emotionally differentiated material was related to a neutral probe, and the participant's response to that probe was used to index the allocation of attention.

For clarity about the primary record, the auditory task discussed in this article was completed and reported in the UCL BSc dissertation submitted in 1981, and an archived copy of that document is available online (Halkiopoulos, 1981). The 1986 acknowledgement refers to the work in the context of subsequent unpublished research; the dated dissertation makes the original design directly inspectable.

The relationship between the two experiments is documented in contemporary publications. The acknowledgement in MacLeod et al. (1986, p. 15) states that my unpublished research "motivated the development of the current paradigm. " Eysenck, MacLeod, and Mathews (1987) subsequently described the auditory task and discussed a related visual procedure. Eysenck (1989, p. 147), in an account of work on selective processing, introduced the historical sequence as follows: "The first of a series of studies was carried out by Christos Halkiopoulos, in unpublished research."

Eysenck characterised the earlier findings as "potentially of great interest, because at last we have convincing evidence of threat avoidance by low-anxiety individuals and threat approach by high-anxiety individuals as originally proposed by Byrne". He added: "Of course, it would be desirable to replicate such an important finding, and this has been done by MacLeod, Mathews, and Tata (1986)" (Eysenck, 1989, p. 147). These assessments are reported to document how the findings and subsequent research were understood at the time; they do not remove the empirical limitations considered in Section 5. In particular, the original UCL experiment assessed defensive style rather than independently measuring trait anxiety. Mathews (1990, p. 459) offered a particularly direct account of the methodological transition:

In an initial unpublished experiment by a student of Michael Eysenck (C. Haliopoulos), high trait anxious Ss were found to detect tones in a dichotic listening tape more rapidly if these followed a threatening word. Converting this to the visual modality, we found a similar effect in clinically anxious (GAD) Ss.

This retrospective description identifies the change of modality as part of the research sequence. The original experiment had been completed at UCL under Norman Dixon's supervision and had classified participants by Ullmann's defensiveness-related scale; those distinctions remain relevant to interpretation. In a later recollection reproduced in Borkovec's (2004) biographical chapter, Mathews described the exchange of ideas in these terms:

Then Michael Eysenck made contact, and we picked up the idea for the dot probe method from his student, Chris Haliopoulos. I certainly remember that being a really fun time.

(Andrew Mathews, quoted in Borkovec, 2004, p. 13.) The spelling of my name is preserved as printed in the cited source. Read together with the earlier publications, these recollections contribute evidence about the route through which the auditory task became relevant to the development of the visual dot-probe method.

These published accounts are considered here for the historical information they provide. They are consistent with the earlier auditory experiment forming one identifiable contribution to the development of the visual task, alongside other relevant conceptual and methodological influences. As the next section explains, the two procedures also differ in important operational respects. The aim is to describe that relationship accurately, rather than infer a single uninterrupted history for the many forms of attention-probe research.

The two tasks should not be regarded as operationally identical. My auditory experiment used an explicitly attended speech stream, continuous shadowing, and occasional tones in one of two ears; the visual dot-probe task used spatially separated visual material followed by a target at one of the relevant locations. The common feature is a more abstract methodological logic: a neutral probe response is used to make inferences about attention in the context of emotionally differentiated information. In this sense, the auditory experiment constitutes an early documented implementation of a recognisably related emotional attentional-probe arrangement, and the visual task represents a consequential subsequent development.

This history is also compatible with the broader development of probe research. The auditory and visual attention literatures supplied different techniques for manipulating selective attention, creating target signals, and interpreting reaction-time or electrophysiological differences. The methodological history is best understood as one of partly converging lines of work rather than a single uninterrupted lineage. What can be established with particular clarity in the present case is the design documented in 1981 and the later published acknowledgement of its contribution to the visual method.

5. Methodological interpretation and limitations

>Methodological interpretation and limitations

5.1. What the original findings show

The 1981 study addressed a substantive question that remains of interest: whether emotionally threatening input changes the distribution of attention between channels in a way that differs across individuals. The observed three-way interaction is compatible with such a possibility. The accompanying directional pattern suggests that sensitisers devoted relatively more attention to the shadowed channel after threatening words, whereas repressors showed a reduced advantage for that channel. However, probe reaction time is an indirect measure. Differences can reflect perceptual selection, alerting, preparation, competing task demands, and response-related factors, and the original data cannot uniquely distinguish among these explanations.

The original reasoning was framed in terms of perceptual defence and repression. Later theories of repressive coping proposed that vigilance and avoidance could occur at different stages of processing (Derakshan et al., 2007), but the present experiment did not measure a temporal sequence of such stages. It is therefore better to describe its findings as broadly compatible with hypotheses about defensive attention than as a direct demonstration of a vigilance-avoidance mechanism.

5.2. Constraints of the historical experiment

Several limitations are particularly important. First, the analysed sample comprised only 17 students, making the estimates uncertain and the subgroup comparisons especially fragile. Second, the small number of probe trials per condition limits the precision and potential reliability of reaction-time measures; a conventional psychometric reliability analysis is not available. Third, the set of probed words was very restricted and not adequately matched across all potentially relevant properties, so differences in item features, acoustic realisations, and shadowing demands cannot be separated cleanly from emotional valence. Fourth, the surviving description does not permit a detailed contemporary assessment of synchronisation, temporal intervals, counterbalancing, exclusion criteria, or trial-level data processing. Fifth, the defensive-style grouping was not accompanied by independent measurement of trait anxiety. The study was not preregistered, as would be expected for much contemporary experimental work; its original analyses are presented as historical results, not as a new confirmatory evaluation.

A further conceptual limitation is that one cannot infer the absence of response bias simply because the response was emotionally neutral. The design reduced reliance on direct emotional judgements but did not eliminate every possible source of measurement bias. These qualifications affect the strength of conclusions about the effects of threatening words, not the fact that the dissertation specified and implemented the method.

The later dot-probe literature offers an instructive perspective. Reviews and measurement studies have identified limited internal consistency and test-retest reliability of some conventional probe-based bias indices (Kappenman et al., 2014; McNally, 2019). More recent task variants, including a dual-probe procedure, have been designed partly to improve psychometric performance (Grafton et al., 2021). Any present-day implementation of the auditory task should benefit from these developments rather than reproduce the original 1981 protocol unchanged.

5.3. Auditory extensions and renewed applications

The case for studying emotional attentional bias in audition does not rest solely on the historical experiment. Later investigators have examined spatial orienting towards emotional spoken words using auditory beep probes. Bertels, Kolinsky, and Morais (2010), for example, presented concurrent spoken words from spatially separated sound sources followed by a lateralised beep and reported emotion-related spatial effects. Asutay and Västfjäll (2015) likewise considered the ways negative emotion can guide auditory spatial attention. These studies demonstrate the feasibility and continuing relevance of auditory approaches, while involving procedures that are not simple replications of the original dichotic-shadowing task.

A contemporary research programme could systematically vary whether emotional information occurs in the explicitly attended or competing stream, the timing of probes relative to word onset, the emotional properties of naturalistic speech, and the stability of probe-derived attentional indices. It could also compare behavioural reaction-time measures with electrophysiological measures of selective processing, extending the kind of comparison invited by the earlier work of Hink and Hillyard (1976). Larger item sets, repeated assessments, and independently validated stimuli would allow more reliable estimation of individual differences.

6. Conclusion

The 1981 UCL dissertation documented a specific experimental response to a long-standing problem in emotion research: how to investigate attentional consequences of threatening information without requiring participants to report the threatening material itself. Its solution combined dichotic shadowing and emotionally differentiated speech with neutral tone probes delivered to either ear and an invariant speeded response. The original findings were suggestive but limited, and their substantive interpretation requires caution.

The historical significance of the experiment lies primarily in the methodological configuration it established and its documented place in the subsequent development of emotional attentional-probe research. Earlier probe methods form an important part of the broader history of attention; the 1981 experiment applied related measurement principles to a distinctive theoretical question and experimental arrangement. The emergence of the visual dot-probe task in 1986 and later auditory variants illustrate the continuing adaptability of that general approach. This history offers both a fuller account of the development of a widely used research method and a starting point for further work on emotion and auditory selective attention.

Historical-data and transparency note

This article reports the descriptive statistics and statistical tests documented for a study completed in 1981 and reconstructs its methodology in the context of subsequent published work. The original BSc dissertation is available via the OSF deposit identified in the references. No new participants were recruited and no new participant-level statistical analysis was undertaken. Numerical p values are stated to three decimal places where they can be obtained from the reported F statistics and degrees of freedom; partial ² for the three-way η interaction was calculated from those summary statistics. Original recordings, complete trial-level data, and stimulus-level response records were not available for reanalysis when preparing this manuscript. The experiment is therefore evaluated as an exploratory historical study. Its place in methodological history is assessed separately from the reliability of its substantive empirical results.
