Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

The discovery of covert consciousness (CC) and cognitive motor dissociation (CMD) has revealed a conceptual inconsistency at the heart of disorders of consciousness (DoC) nosology. Advanced neuroimaging and electrophysiological studies have demonstrated that behavioral unresponsiveness does not necessarily imply the absence of consciousness or cognitive capacity, yet contemporary diagnostic categories continue equating unresponsiveness with unconsciousness. This equivalence is embedded throughout DoC nomenclature, where behavioral syndromes such as the vegetative state (VS) and minimally conscious state (MCS) are defined by what patients are inferred to experience (i.e., their state of mind) but operationalized on the basis of what they are observed to do (or not do). We argue that this category error now impedes the translation of over two decades of scientific advances into a coherent clinical framework. First, it has fostered the widespread but incorrect conflation of covert consciousness (CC) and cognitive motor dissociation (CMD), despite the fact that the two concepts identify different patient populations and may carry distinct implications for diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment. Second, it renders contemporary DoC nosology incapable of coherently incorporating modern neuroimaging and electrophysiological findings. Because the absence of consciousness is an entrenched, definitional component of the VS, its behavioral phenotype cannot be reconciled with covert neurophysiological phenotypes such as CC and CMD. As a result, current nomenclature impedes the development of a phenotype-endotype framework in which bedside behavioral classifications are complemented by biologically informed categories derived from advanced neuroimaging and electrophysiological assessments. We trace the origins of this problem to a historical shift whereby the definitions of VS and MCS evolved from descriptions of observable behavior into claims about a patient's mental state. Disentangling responsiveness from consciousness, we argue, is essential if the field is to incorporate contemporary evidence and develop a coherent, biologically informed nosology of DoC, which, in our view, is best pursued within a phenotype-endotype framework.

Introduction

There is now abundant evidence that a substantial proportion of brain injury survivors who do not immediately recover may harbor a level of consciousness and cognitive function that far exceeds what can be detected in conventional, behavior-based clinical assessments. [1] [2] [3] [4] This evidence has revolutionized our understanding of the assessment of disorders of consciousness (DoC) 5, 6 such as coma, the vegetative state (VS, also referred to as Unresponsive Wakefulness Syndrome), and the minimally conscious state (MCS), and has been instrumental in promoting an agenda of evidence-based competency in managing these conditions that is now codified in international guidelines. 7, 8 Nonetheless, this evidence has generated substantial confusion with respect to the interpretation and implication of such results with respect to the nosology of DoC, 9 something that can have complex consequences for patients and other stakeholders.

Several terms have been proposed to describe patients whose level of awareness or cognitive capacity exceeds what is detectable at the bedside, such as covert awareness (CA), 10, 11 covert consciousness (CC), 12, 13 or cognitive motor dissociation (CMD), 14 among others. 9 Rather than representing isolated terminological disputes, we argue that the challenges raised by these labels reflect a deeper conceptual problem within contemporary DoC nosology: the tendency to define diagnostic categories by what patients are inferred to experience (i.e., their state of mind) rather than by the behavior on which that inference rests. This tendency, as we discuss below, was absent from the original description of the VS 15 and was only embedded into its definition many years later. As a result, current diagnostic frameworks obscure important distinctions among emerging neuroimaging and electrophysiological findings and hinder their integration into a coherent clinical framework. This category error has at least two important consequences for the field. First, we highlight the common but incorrect practice of using the labels Covert Consciousness/Covert Awareness and Cognitive Motor Dissociation interchangeably. This conflation is both erroneous, because the two categories of terms do not identify the same subset of DoC patients (i.e., are not coextensive), and unwanted, because it robs the field of an important opportunity for developing novel assessments and patient-tailored interventions. Second, we discuss the complex yet paramount challenge of accommodating novel concepts, such as CC, CA, and CMD, within a coherent and modern DoC nomenclature and evaluate the advantages and difficulties of two possible frameworks that achieve this goal. Finally, we trace both issues to a common historical origin: the gradual redefinition of the notion of VS (and MCS) from a behavioral description into an inference about a patient's state of mind. While this historical shift is, as we discuss, the root of both issues, in what follows we deliberately begin by distinguishing CC and CMD because this is likely to be the most consequential and immediately actionable issue for current practice. The ensuing nosological discussion, on the other hand, shows how this distinction forces the field to make a decision as to the best path forward, and the historical analysis explains how the field has come to need such a decision.

A brief history of covert awareness, covert consciousness, and cognitive motor dissociation Since the landmark case published in 2006 by Owen and colleagues, 16 several names have been used to refer to the phenomenon whereby the extent of a patient's responsiveness at the bedside, typically evaluated with neurobehavioral protocols such as the Coma Recovery Scale Revised (CRS-R), 17 is exceeded by what they demonstrate through advanced, task-based, imaging and electrophysiology assessments. i The names used to refer to this include CA, 10, 11 CC, 12, 13 CMD, 14 along with some now less used terms, such as functional locked in syndrome (fLIS) 18 and "non-behavioral" minimally conscious state, 19 among others. 9 While being a measure of the rapid development of this field, such panoply of names is generating confusion concerning the exact subset of patients each picks out (i.e., what is the exact definition of each label) and how such terms ought to be integrated in a modern DoC nosology, limiting opportunities for further understanding of these conditions.

The clearest instance of such confusion is the fact that the term CA is often used interchangeably with the term CMD. 3, [20] [21] [22] This, we argue, is a harmful and factually incorrect conflation of terms.

Covert consciousness is not and should not be the same as cognitive motor dissociation

Despite describing different phenomena, the terms CC and CMD are now often taken to be synonymous, 3, [20] [21] [22] possibly as a symptom of the fact that, in the absence of a means to objectively measure consciousness in an individual, we pragmatically rely on voluntary behavior as a proxy for its presence. 5, 32 There are at least two reasons for this conflation. First, CC and CMD have been, in practice, operationalized in the same manner (i.e., a positive response to an active AIE paradigm), so that the two labels have had no occasion to come apart. Second, a behaviorally unresponsive patient who is nonetheless conscious calls for an explanation of the phenomenon, which is exactly what the CMD proposal provides: a dissociation between the cognitive systems that initiate willful action and the motor system that executes it. 31 Yet, conflating these two terms is both incorrect and undesirable. It is incorrect because the two terms cannot be coextensive (see Figure 2). As originally defined, 14 CMD can be true of patients with a (behavioral) diagnosis of Coma, VS, and MCS-whereas CC can only be understood as applying to Coma and VS patients.

For, if a patient can demonstrate behaviorally that they are conscious (i.e., MCS-), it makes little sense, should they also be able to respond to verbal commands as detected with fMRI or EEG, to refer to them as being covertly conscious. An MCS patient is, by definition, overtly conscious, no matter how subtle the behavioral signs of awareness might be. Thus, referring to such a patient as covertly conscious is as much a contradictio in terminis as was the rightfully deprecated term coma vigil. 33 In other words, CMD implies the presence of consciousness. Where that consciousness is not behaviorally manifest (i.e., patients meeting the behavioral criteria for coma or VS), CMD must therefore also imply CC. However, where the presence of consciousness is already overt (i.e., patients meeting the behavioral definition of MCS-), the CC label would be contradictory and does not apply (cf. Figure 2). Notably, the Curing Coma Campaign's working group on CMD reached the same conclusion: while adopting the original operational definition of CMD, it noted that, although the two concepts overlap, covert awareness and CMD apply to different subsets of patients, the former being restricted, by definition, to behaviorally unresponsive patients and the latter extending to patients in MCS-. The group also noted that a patient may be aware yet fail to meet the definition of CMD. 30 In addition, foregoing the distinction between CC and CMD is undesirable for the field because it fails to recognize the fact that a patient might well be covertly conscious while being unable to meet the definition of CMD. It is perfectly possible for a patient to be conscious but unable to demonstrate so via active MRI/EEG tasks due to the multiple cognitive processes that must be intact and operate in concert to complete the tasks. 5, 34 As originally discussed by Owen and colleagues, 34 active 27 (e.g., "motor imagery") tasks are very cognitively demanding, requiring sustained allocation of executive attention, working memory, intention, and rule-based decisionmaking. 1, 32 Yet, patients with a DoC are very likely to have, with high prevalence, sensory, attentional, and linguistic impairments, 35, 36 and, even where such processes are intact, they might still have insufficient arousal regulation which would prevent them from performing cognitively demanding tasks over the long durations required for AIE assessments. 37 Unsurprisingly, active paradigms are well known to suffer from low sensitivity to a state of consciousness, 5, 27 with only 38% of patients who follow commands behaviorally generating a response to task-based MRI or EEG. 1 Failing to distinguish between CC and CMD thus robs the field of the opportunity to develop approaches that can detect the presence of a state of consciousness in the absence of both overt and covert response to command. Consider, for example, the rapidly developing technique of contemporaneous transcranial magnetic stimulation and electroencephalography, and its derived measurement, the Perturbational Complexity Index (PCI). 38, 39 Extensive work has now shown that there exists a specific threshold, known as PCI*, capable of discriminating, with 100% sensitivity and specificity, conditions in which a benchmark population did (i.e., wakefulness, dreaming during REM sleep, ketamine-induced dissociation) versus did not (i.e., dreamless NREM sleep, anesthesia with propofol, midazolam or xenon) later report having been conscious. However, this benchmark is, by construction, anchored to retrospective report rather than to consciousness as such. 40 This technique also has a 94.7% sensitivity for detecting states of awareness in behaviorally MCS patients and returns above threshold values in approximately 20% of behaviorally unresponsive patients, 39 intriguingly consistent with recent published rates of CMD. 1, 30 Novel approaches leveraging inter-subject correlation (ISC) of electrocardiographic, 41 metabolic, 42 and electroencephalographic 43 bio-signals in response to narratives also show that it might well be possible to detect the presence of awareness, which is necessary for such inter-subject correlations to emerge, in patients who do not demonstrate overt (or covert) signs of consciousness. The same holds for the 'global effect' in the local-global auditory paradigm, a late, widespread electrophysiological response to violations of a global regularity that is thought to require conscious access and whose presence, although not highly sensitive, is reasonably specific to preserved consciousness. 44, 45 Although the evidentiary value of "passive" techniques such as PCI, local-global, and ISC for assessing consciousness is less straightforward than observing a voluntary (i.e., "active") response -whether motor-based or brain-based 27 -these results point to the existence of a clinically important, distinct DoC presentation: patients who are behaviorally unresponsive on conventional bedside examination (i.e., presenting in coma or VS), who fail to demonstrate command-following via active imaging or electrophysiological paradigms, and yet exhibit biomarkers highly specific to conscious awareness. Such a patient could not possibly be said to have CMD, since they are not demonstrating covert response to command, but might well be covertly aware ii . Not distinguishing between CMD and CC makes this group invisible (i.e., the hatched blue area in Figure 2) and robs the field of the conceptual space needed to recognize and investigate such cases, potentially foreclosing therapeutic opportunities (e.g., Brain Computer Interfaces). [46] [47] [48] Such distinctions are also likely to carry substantive prognostic implications similar to those already demonstrated for behaviorally subtle differences [49] [50] [51] and provide a clear framework for testing the prognostic significance of well-characterized biomarker thresholds strongly associated with the presence of consciousness (e.g. PCI>0.31, 39 metabolic index >3.18 52, 53 ).

Has the field outgrown the standard DoC nomenclature?

The second major issue raised by CMD and CC is one of nosology. How should we classify a patient who fulfills the behavioral criteria for VS, yet demonstrates command-following through neuroimaging? At first glance, the answer seems obvious: following the logic of conventional neurobehavioral assessments, detection of command-following is indicative of (at least) MCS+, so the patient should be classified accordingly. Nonetheless, the standard DoC nomenclature is anchored in readily apparent levels of behavioral responsiveness. The emergence of AIEs therefore raises a broader question: should diagnostic categories originally defined by overt behavior be extended to include evidence of awareness derived from brain activity alone? The Curing Coma Campaign has explicitly left this question open, noting that forthcoming mechanistic, epidemiological, and outcome data "will determine whether the nosology of DoC requires modification." 30 Broadly speaking, there appear to be at least two approaches to answering this question which, most crucially, differ in what each proposes to serve as the primary classifier: the observable behavioral phenotype versus the inferred level of consciousness.

Phenotype-endotype approach

Under a phenotype-endotype approach, 57, 58 the nosology of DoC would continue being anchored in behavioral phenotypes, thus maintaining its current behavior-based diagnostic framework (i.e., coma, VS, MCS-, MCS+), while refining them with progressively sharper endotypes afforded by the newfound sophistication of AIEs (e.g., CC, CMD, PCI>PCI*, Covert Cortical Processing, 21 etc). This approach would mirror similar efforts in other domains of medicine, [59] [60] [61] [62] including moderate to severe traumatic brain injury, 63 and would represent a welcome step towards addressing the high heterogeneity of DoC patients, with the aim of enabling more precise, individualized prognostication and improved stratification for intervention selection. 64 Indeed, a framework of this kind has already been articulated for DoC by the Curing Coma Campaign. The Advanced Classification of Consciousness Endotypes (ACCESS) framework 65 retains bedside phenotypes as a first tier, adds functional measures (EEG, fMRI, PET) in a second tier summarized by an Arousal-Volition-Cognition-Mechanisms (AVCM) score that deliberately separates clinically observed arousal and volitional motor output from cognitive capacity ascertainable either behaviorally or through AIEs, and envisions a third, multidimensional tier tracking trajectories over time. A framework of this kind is also the natural home for the proposal to organize DoC classification around cognitive capacities, whether probed overtly or covertly. 66 In ACCESS, for example, the 'cognitive content' item of the AVCM score is rated on a single scale, from none to complex cognition, whatever the source of the evidence (i.e., clinical examination, task-based AIE). Cognitive capacities are precisely the kind of endotype such a framework is meant to capture, and keeping them distinct from the behavioral phenotype is what allows a covertly demonstrated capacity to be recorded without re-labelling the patient's behavioral state. In addition, this is also where the distinction drawn above between the question of whether a patient is conscious (i.e., creature-consciousness) and what their consciousness contains (i.e., conscious contents) pays off: the former is captured by the CC designation, whereas the capacities and contents a patient's consciousness comprises are captured by the cognitive endotypes, with the added benefit of allowing the field to pursue this understudied issue 55, 66, 67 without disturbing the answer to the first question.

Under a phenotype-endotype approach, then, the hypothetical patient described above, who fulfills the behavioral criteria for VS while demonstrating command-following through AIEs, would thus be labeled as "VS with CMD". "VS" to reflect the absence of any observed motorbased purposeful response and "CMD" to indicate the presence of a positive response to an AIE assessment.

This immediately highlights a critical issue with applying a phenotype-endotype approach to the existing DoC nosology: by the most recent international guidelines iii , VS is defined as a "state of wakefulness without awareness" 68 whereby patients "are therefore considered unaware of themselves and their surroundings" 8 due to the presence of "[s]pontaneous eye-opening signaling wakefulness, but [absence of] evidence of purposeful behavior suggesting awareness of self or environment". 7 In light of these definitions, it becomes inescapably contradictory to characterize a patient as being both unaware of themselves and their surrounding (i.e., VS) and, at once, capable of intentionally modulating their brain activity in response to command (i.e., CMD). In this sense, the label of VS (and, as we point out below, that of MCS) suffers from an erroneous, 5 yet deeply entrenched, inference to the unobserved state of awareness of a patient.

Given the associations that have accrued to the terms VS, UWS (as discussed below), and MCS, it is not easy to imagine them amenable to this phenotype-endotype approach. Perhaps, the field could consider replacing the conventional diagnostic labels with novel ones that describe the clinically observable phenomena key to the DoC diagnosis: wakefulness and responsiveness. A coma patient might, for example, be described as unarousable (i.e., without eye opening, spontaneous or stimulus-induced) and limited to reflexive responses, whereas a VS patient could be described as wakeful albeit behaviorally reflexive. An MCS patient, instead, could be described as wakeful and behaviorally intentional without (i.e., MCS-) or with intact basic language function (i.e., MCS+). A nomenclature as in this example would be in the spirit of the AVCM score, 65 whose arousal and volition dimensions characterize what is observed at the bedside without committing to an inference about awareness (although it is notable that ACCESS itself retains VS/UWS and MCS as its clinical phenotypes, thus inheriting the tension discussed above). Importantly, under such a nomenclature, it would no longer be problematic to describe a patient as having an unarousable reflexive (i.e., coma) or wakeful reflexive (i.e., VS) phenotype and a Covert Consciousness or Cognitive Motor Dissociation endotype.

Consciousness-first approach

An alternative to the phenotype-endotype approach is to retain the standard DoC nomenclature and focus diagnosis on the detection of level of consciousness, an approach that seems implicit in the 2020 EU guidelines for the diagnosis of coma and other DoC. 8 In this approach, each novel AIE test is just another item in an extended, multimodal, assessment in which "a given patient should be diagnosed with the highest level of consciousness as revealed by any of the […] approaches (clinical, EEG, neuroimaging)". 8 The consciousness-first approach recognizes that the almost totality of novel AIEs developed for DoC have been focused on the important question of detecting the presence of consciousness in patients whose behavior is not sufficient to demonstrate it. 69 In this approach, then, the hypothetical patient described at the beginning of this section (classified as "VS with CMD" under the phenotype-endotype approach) would be considered, simply, MCS.

As advantageous as it might seem to accommodate CMD and CC within the traditional DoC categories, doing so would make the patient's phenotype dependent on an endotype that is challenging to obtain, not yet validated or standardized, and of unknown upper limit. For, 20 years after the first reported case, AIEs remain in a state of endorsement without implementation, 70 with very few clinicians worldwide having access to such techniques. 71 While this limitation is practical and may well prove temporary, two others would remain even if access were universal. First, considering the characteristically fluctuating responsiveness of DoC patients 72 and that misdiagnosis rates are known to decrease dramatically when assessments are repeated, 73 a phenotype that hinges on a single AIE result (given AIE assessments are rarely repeated) would be significantly more fragile than one based on serial bedside assessment.

Second, and most importantly, behavioral and AIE-based categories constrain a patient's capacities from opposite sides. A behavioral diagnosis of MCS carries a floor as well as a ceiling: it implies the presence of some intentional behavior and the absence of behaviors indicating emergence, respectively. A positive active paradigm, on the other hand, only establishes a floor, command following and hence capacities at least at the level of MCS+, with no upper limit on what else the patient may be capable of. While any label can underestimate a patient's capacities, the specific problem with relabeling such a patient as just MCS is that it asserts an upper limit (i.e., absence of evidence of emergence) not supported by the evidence, by importing a category with a ceiling (i.e., behaviorally defined MCS) onto evidence that has none (i.e., AIE defined MCS). A label such as 'VS with CMD', on the other hand, asserts no such limit: the phenotype records what was observed at the bedside and the endotype what was demonstrated by other means, and neither bounds the other. In addition, the consciousness-first approach is challenged by the finding that AIEs can reveal clinically important information also in patients who are behaviorally in MCS. 25 This is telling: if the value of an AIE result were truly exhausted by its raising a patient's diagnosed level of consciousness, a positive result in a patient known to be (behaviorally) in MCS would have to carry no information. Yet initial data suggest that MCSpatients with CMD improve on the CRS-R at 3 months at substantially higher rates than MCSpatients without CMD. 25 Failing to distinguish between phenotype and endotype also risks antagonizing the basic promise of a more granular understanding of DoC, a more patient-tailored categorization, and a precision medicine approach to prognosis and intervention selection. 64, 65 Weighing the two approaches, we favor the phenotype-endotype framework. While what we described here should be seen as an example of how such an approach could work, we do argue that a framework in which bedside behavioral categories describe what is observed, while advanced imaging and electrophysiology define endotypes that qualify, rather than overwrite, those descriptions, 65 is the one most likely to deliver to DoC the benefits that other areas of medicine have derived from similar schemes: 57-64 a more granular characterization of patients, testable hypotheses about the prognostic and therapeutic significance of each endotype, and a path toward precision medicine that does not require first settling what consciousness is and how best to measure it.

Three clarifications are in order. First, the advantage of the phenotype-endotype framework does not lie in how many distinctions it draws. Either approach can be kept coarse or made fine: a consciousness-first scheme could stop at conscious versus unconscious or subdivide consciousness into levels and dimensions, just as a phenotype-endotype scheme could stop at four bedside categories or subdivide each by endotype (e.g., VS with CMD, VS with PCI>PCI*, VS with neither observed, VS with neither tested). The advantage lies in what the additional distinctions are anchored to (i.e., observed behavior and measured biology rather than an inferred state), and in the fact that they can be added only as the evidence warrants, as biomarkers prove prognostically or therapeutically informative. Second, the framework takes no stand on whether the boundary between reflexive and intentional behavior also marks a boundary in consciousness itself, as some have questioned. 66 Rather, it uses that boundary as a phenotype and as an evidential threshold, not as a claim about awareness. Third, any such scheme must serve families, clinicians, researchers and ethicists, who read labels differently and require different things from them. 30, 56 This might favor an architecture in which a stable, descriptive bedside phenotype can be communicated in plain language, while the endotype layer carries the technical qualifications that clinicians and researchers need, and it is also where a plain-language umbrella term such as 'covert consciousness', as proposed previously, 56 can do its work without erasing the distinctions beneath it.

Chronology of an original sin

Given the transformative advances that the DoC field has witnessed over the last few decades, how did the notion of a VS come to be defined by an inferred state of mind as opposed to clinically observable behavior? When Jennett and Plum coined the term vegetative state in 1972, they were careful about the epistemological limits of their proposal. For, they explicitly noted that the name should not imply more than is known, and that the clinical syndrome "describes behaviour." 15 They also acknowledged that, in these patients, the inference from behavior to the presence or absence of conscious experience is uncertain, noting that one can only "infer the presence or absence of conscious experience in another person." 15 Importantly, their caution was thus not the generic observation that all minds other than our own are known only by inference (i.e., the problem of other minds), but the specific one that in these patients it is unclear whether the inference to the absence of awareness is warranted, and that it should therefore not be written into the name. It was over 20 years later, that the Multi-Society Task Force on the Persistent VS redefined the vegetative state as "a clinical condition of complete unawareness of the self and the environment" (emphasis added), 74 despite recognizing that Jennett and Plum described it as a "state of wakefulness without detectable awareness." While the diagnostic criteria set by the Task Force were behavioral in nature (e.g., no evidence of purposeful behavior), the definition itself equated behavioral phenotype (i.e., unresponsiveness) with mental state (i.e., unawareness), something which they reasoned was supported by multiple lines of evidence. 75 Although the Task Force overtly grappled with the limitations tied to the inferential nature of the definition, they nonetheless concluded that VS patients "are noncognitive, nonsentient, and incapable of conscious experience" 74 (emphasis added). The inferential nature of the VS diagnosis was already established in the field even before the Task Force codified it. In 1992, for example, a member of the Multi-Society Task Force acknowledged that "our only means of ascertainment of another person's cognition and awareness is by inference," and specifically raised the possibility of false-positive VS diagnostic error, that a patient "could retain a rudimentary level of awareness that our crude tests cannot detect." 76 Yet, foreshadowing the 1994 guidelines, this issue was considered as an irreducible epistemic limitation to be managed, not as a reason to reconsider the VS definition. Although a seemingly subtle change, by 1994, the inference had become the definition. The following year, the American Academy of Neurology also codified this same definition by stating that "[t]he vegetative state is a clinical condition of complete unawareness of the self and the environment" 77 (emphasis added). While, again, behavioral criteria were offered as the means of diagnosis, what was being diagnosed is a state of unawareness. To be clear, the problem here is that in the case of VS specifically, the inference from unresponsiveness to unconsciousness is invalid (i.e., is based on interpreting absence of evidence of awareness as evidence of its absence), 32 as demonstrated empirically, 1, 78 and yet it has come to be the very definition of the category. In this sense, the same concern does not apply to positive inferences drawn from overt or covert responses which, when detected, imply the presence of a state of awareness (assuming one agrees that the demonstrated behavior is of the kind that licenses such inference; e.g., willful responses). This connotation of the term Vegetative State, defined by the absence of awareness, has never left the field since the Task Force's work. Indeed, by 2002, the Aspen criteria had fully absorbed it and distinguished MCS from VS on the basis of "partial preservation of conscious awareness." 79 Although, again, it is recognized that the diagnostic separator between the two categories is the presence of behaviors associated with conscious awareness, what is being diagnosed is the state of absence of consciousness (cf., Ref 79 , Table 1, p. 350). This connotation, however, did not go unnoticed. In 2010, Laureys and colleagues proposed renaming the condition as Unresponsive Wakefulness Syndrome (UWS), explicitly choosing 'the clinically descriptive term unresponsive rather than the misleading unaware'. 33 While we share the spirit of this proposal, the definition of UWS remains the same as that of VS (i.e., wakefulness in the absence of awareness), making our criticism equally applicable to the new label. Indeed, none of the recent guidelines suggests UWS as the recommended label. Rather, the new term is either rejected outright 68 or simply appended to the VS abbreviation (i.e., VS/UWS), while retaining 'wakefulness without awareness' as the definition for both. 7, 8 Furthermore, as discussed elsewhere, 68, [80] [81] [82] the label "unresponsive" is incorrect at the bedside (i.e., UWS patients do show responses, albeit at a reflex level 68 ) and in light of the AIE evidence (i.e., some UWS patients can respond in active tasks 1, 4 ) which renders the name less diagnostically precise. 81 Indeed, the only sense in which the label is accurate is when read, as intended, 33 as the absence of any behavioral response that evidences awareness, which is exactly the inference-laden content the label was supposed to shed. Thus, while superficially appealing, UWS simply shifted the inference from 'unaware' into 'unresponsive' without removing it. Most importantly, the relabeling does not touch the problem at issue here; that is, 'UWS with CMD' is as much a contradiction in terms as is 'VS with CMD', the former denying the response, the latter denying awareness.

Another 30 years since the Task Force's landmark work, the most recent guidelines 7, 8, 68, 83 still suffer from the deep echoes of the equation of unresponsiveness with unconsciousness, framing the diagnostic process as a search for evidence of a patient's state of (un)awareness. The Royal College of Physicians' 2020 guidelines, for example, unambiguously define the VS as a "state of wakefulness without awareness" (cf., Ref 68 , Table 1.2 and Figure 1.2), even noting that families sometimes find comfort in the knowledge that their loved one "is not aware and not suffering". 68 The American Academy of Neurology's 2018 updated guidelines define the VS as featuring "[s]pontaneous eye-opening signaling wakefulness, but no evidence of purposeful behavior suggesting awareness of self or environment" (emphasis added; cf., Ref 83 ,

Table e-1). Similarly, the European Academy of Neurology 2020 guidelines state that "[t]he term VS/UWS denotes a condition of wakefulness without (clinical signs of) awareness. Such patients […] are therefore considered unaware of themselves and their surroundings" (emphasis added; cf., Ref 8 , p. 743).

While the issue of how to properly detect a patient's state of awareness, given its inferential nature, is a cornerstone theme of all three guidelines, it is clear from the definitions that, much like in the 1994 Task Force report, what is being diagnosed is a state of mind, not a behavioral phenotype.

The vegetative state is not a state of mind 32, 76, 84 that permits treating unresponsiveness as a proxy for unawareness: when voluntary responses (whether overt or covert) are the only tractable signal for consciousness, the distinction between CC and CMD collapses, despite the fact that the two terms do not pick out the same subset of patients (cf., Figure 2). Furthermore, because the definition of VS is so deeply entrenched in the idea of "absent awareness," 68 the phenotype of unresponsiveness can no longer be coherently reconciled, in a modern nomenclature, with the endotypes that AIE assessments are revealing.

To be clear, our aim is not to rehabilitate 'vegetative state' as a behavioral label, but to insist that whatever label is used denote a behavioral phenotype and not an inferred state of mind.

Two important corollaries emerge from this renewed understanding. First, one might also question whether the term Minimally Conscious State suffers from an analogous shortcoming and should be changed to something closer to the original Minimally Responsive State (MIN-R) 85, 86 label suggested by the American Congress of Rehabilitation Medicine (ACRM) in 1995 as "a descriptive term that refers to patients who are no longer comatose or vegetative" (emphasis added) and whose behavioral "responses are inconsistent but indicative of meaningful interaction with the environment." 87 Alternatively, in an effort to make the label more precise and transparent a term to the effect of Minimal Non-Reflexive Responsiveness could be adopted. Such a change would refocus the label for this cohort of patients on the observable phenotype rather than the inferred level of awareness and would be immediately accommodated in a modern phenotype-endotype approach (e.g., Minimal Non-Reflexive Responsiveness with CMD).

Second, behavioral scales such as the CRS-R 17 are often interpreted in a graded fashion with "[h]igher CRS-R scores representing higher levels of consciousness". 88, 89 Yet, while certain threshold behaviors pragmatically justify inferring the presence (versus absence) of consciousness, interpreting progressively higher CRS-R scores as reflecting progressively higher levels of consciousness is another manifestation of the incorrect equation of behavior with state of mind. While the CRS-R was explicitly developed around a hierarchical model of increasingly complex neurobehavioral responses, and substantial psychometric evidence supports its validity, 90 whether progression along this hierarchy should be interpreted as reflecting progressively higher levels of consciousness remains unclear. 67 Higher CRS-R scores are thus better understood as strengthening the evidence that a patient is conscious at all (i.e., creature consciousness), and hence the probability that consciousness is present, rather than as quantifying its level. Similarly, higher scores also document a wider repertoire of behaviors and, plausibly, of conscious contents and capacities. Neither reading, however, licenses treating the score as a quantity of consciousness: the CRS-R is an evidential scale, not a consciousness scale.

How to unwind such deeply embedded associations across specialists and the general public alike is a key challenge. Yet unless this distinction is made, the field risks continuing to mistake evidence of consciousness for consciousness itself, obscuring the interpretation of emerging biomarkers and hindering the development of a modern precision-medicine framework for disorders of consciousness. In the meantime, two steps are available now. The first is to stop using covert consciousness and cognitive motor dissociation interchangeably, reserving the former for the presence of awareness in behaviorally unresponsive patients and the latter for covert command-following, whatever the behavioral phenotype. The second is for the field to start discussing what labels would best fit a phenotype-endotype nosology in which bedside categories describe what is observed, while advanced imaging and electrophysiology evidence defines endotypes that qualify, rather than overwrite, them.
