Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Spatial skills are consistently associated with mathematical performance, and numerical magnitude representation may partially explain this relationship. However, it remains unclear whether spatial anxiety is similarly associated with mathematics. We sampled 221 adults and assessed spatial skills, spatial anxiety, unbounded number line estimation, math anxiety, and mathematical performance. Spatial skills were assessed using mental rotation and surface development tasks, while spatial anxiety and math anxiety were measured with self-report scales. We evaluated numerical magnitude knowledge using an unbounded number line estimation task, and we measured mathematical performance across arithmetic, fractions, algebra, and percentages. Higher spatial anxiety was associated with poorer spatial skills and lower mathematical performance. Moreover, spatial skills accounted for part of the association between spatial anxiety and mathematics, whereas unbounded number line estimation did not, even after controlling for math anxiety. These findings suggest that spatial anxiety may be associated with poorer mathematical performance without a corresponding association with numerical magnitude knowledge as measured by unbounded number line estimation. The results highlight potential differences in the cognitive operations underlying spatial skills and spatial anxiety in relation to mathematics.

Introduction

Spatial skills demonstrate a consistent association with mathematical learning and performance across developmental stages. Individuals possessing stronger spatial skills typically achieve higher performance across various mathematical outcomes, and meta-analytic evidence supports a reliable positive association between these domains (Atit et al., 2022; Geer et al., 2026; Hawes et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2020). Despite the established cognitive relationship between spatial skills and mathematics, considerably less is known about whether affective responses to spatial demands are related to mathematical performance. Some individuals experience apprehension, tension, or discomfort when they encounter tasks that require spatial thinking. This response is referred to as spatial anxiety and may arise during navigation, visualization, or the mental manipulation of objects and spatial relations (Lyons et al., 2018).

Most research on spatial anxiety has focused on spatial performance (Ferguson et al., 2015; Lyons et al., 2018). A smaller body of research has included spatial anxiety in broader analyses of performance across cognitive domains, but it has rarely treated the relationship between spatial anxiety and mathematics in adults as the central question (Daker et al., 2022). Research with children has not found a relation between spatial anxiety and math performance (Geer et al., 2026; Lauer et al., 2018), but Ashkenazi and Velner (2023) did find a relation between spatial anxiety and math performance in adults and suggested it worked indirectly through math anxiety.

This study tests for the relation between spatial anxiety and math performance and then considers what indirect associations could account for it. Previous research has associated greater spatial anxiety with poorer spatial performance (Lyons et al., 2018) and documented overlap between spatial anxiety and math anxiety (Daker et al., 2022). Stronger spatial skills are associated with better mathematical performance (Atit et al., 2022), whereas greater math anxiety is associated with poorer mathematical performance (Barroso et al., 2021). Together, these findings suggest several possible explanations for an association between spatial anxiety and mathematics. If we further know that the relation between spatial skills and math performance is itself mediated by number line performance (Ayesu et al., 2026; Gunderson et al., 2012; Tam et al., 2019), then the pattern of indirect associations between spatial anxiety and math performance could be even more complicated. In this study, we ask the question of how much the relation between spatial anxiety and math performance is explained by math anxiety, spatial skills, and/or unbounded number line performance.

Spatial Anxiety

Spatial anxiety is a multidimensional construct. The Spatial Anxiety Scale developed by Lyons et al. (2018) distinguishes three related components: imagery anxiety, mental manipulation anxiety, and navigation anxiety. Imagery anxiety concerns situations that require the generation or recall of visual images. Mental manipulation anxiety concerns situations that require objects or spatial configurations to be transformed mentally. Navigation anxiety concerns finding one's way through familiar or un-familiar environments. Although these components involve different spatial demands, they reflect a broader affective response to situations that require spatial processing. Previous research has demonstrated that spatial anxiety is associated with poorer spatial performance. Individuals who report higher spatial anxiety tend to perform less accurately on tasks involving mental rotation and other forms of spatial transformation (Ferguson et al., 2015; Lyons et al., 2018). Anxiety may interfere with performance by increasing worry, reducing confidence, or diverting attention from task relevant spatial information. It may also encourage avoidance of spatial activities, thereby reducing opportunities to practice and develop spatial skills. Conversely, repeated difficulty with spatial tasks may contribute to spatial anxiety. Although the direction of the relationship remains uncertain, existing research demonstrates a reliable association between spatial anxiety and spatial performance.

There are theoretical reasons to expect the influence of spatial anxiety to extend into mathematics. Fractions require the coordination of part to whole relations. Percentages require quantities to be rescaled relative to a base of 100. Algebra requires the representation and transformation of relations among quantities. Even arithmetic may involve spatial organization, place value, and internal representations of numerical magnitude. If spatial anxiety interferes with the efficient use of spatial information, it may also be associated with poorer mathematical performance. The present study examines this possibility across several mathematical domains.

Distinguishing Spatial Anxiety from Math Anxiety

Any examination of spatial anxiety and mathematical performance must also consider math anxiety. Math anxiety refers to apprehension or tension associated with mathematics related situations. It is reliably associated with lower mathematical performance, avoidance of mathematics courses and activities, and reduced participation in fields involving substantial quantitative demands (Ashcraft & Moore, 2009; Barroso et al., 2021; Hembree, 1990). Math anxiety may disrupt performance by occupying working memory resources, weakening attentional control, and increasing intrusive thoughts during mathematical problem solving (Ashcraft & Kirk, 2001; Eysenck et al., 2007). Lauer et al. (2018) examined math, spatial, and verbal anxiety in 394 children between 6 and 12 years of age. Their primary aim was to determine whether each form of anxiety showed a domain specific relationship with the corresponding area of performance. Spatial anxiety was associated with poorer spatial reasoning, but not with mathematics achievement or reading ability, in the correlation analyses. Moreover, spatial anxiety did not uniquely predict spatial reasoning after math and verbal anxiety were considered. These findings provided evidence that spatial anxiety was more closely connected to spatial performance than to mathematics in childhood. Daker et al. (2022) investigated the specificity of anxiety and performance relationships in two adult samples. Their focal analyses tested whether math anxiety predicted mathematics after general and spatial anxiety were controlled and whether spatial anxiety predicted spatial performance after general and math anxiety were controlled. Spatial anxiety did not account for the association between math anxiety and mathematics performance and did not explain unique variation in mathematics beyond math anxiety. In contrast, math anxiety accounted for part of the relationship between spatial anxiety and spatial performance. This asymmetric pattern indicates that math anxiety and spatial anxiety are related but not interchangeable. Spatial processing has also been implicated in individual differences in math anxiety (Sokolowski et al., 2019), and other evidence suggests that spatial anxiety may be associated with complex mathematics indirectly through math anxiety (Ashkenazi & Velner, 2023). Together, these studies establish the importance of cross domain comparisons, while leaving the mechanisms connecting spatial anxiety with different forms of mathematical performance largely unresolved.

Spatial Skills

Spatial skills provide one possible explanation for an association between spatial anxiety and mathematics. Spatial anxiety is associated with spatial performance, and spatial skills are associated with mathematical performance (Atit et al., 2022; Ferguson et al., 2015; Xie et al., 2020). Consequently, any relationship between spatial anxiety and mathematics may partly reflect individual differences in spatial skills.

Several possibilities should be considered. Spatial anxiety may interfere with performance on spatial tasks, and lower effective spatial performance may subsequently be associated with poorer mathematics. Alternatively, individuals with lower spatial skills may experience more difficulty during spatial activities, contributing to the development of spatial anxiety. Spatial anxiety and spatial skills may also arise from shared experiences, including differences in education, exposure to spatial activities, beliefs about ability, or avoidance of spatially demanding situations.

Directly measuring spatial skills permits a more informative examination of these possibilities. If spatial anxiety continues to predict mathematics after spatial skills are controlled, it would provide information beyond objective spatial performance. If the association is substantially reduced, spatial skills may statistically account for part of the relationship. Because cross sectional data cannot establish temporal order, such an indirect association would identify a plausible explanatory link rather than demonstrate a causal sequence.

One possible spatial mechanism connecting anxiety to mathematics involves numerical magnitude representation. Spatial representation accounts propose that numerical magnitudes are organized along a mental number line (Dehaene et al., 1993; Siegler & Lortie-Forgues, 2014). Number line estimation is associated with mathematical achievement and may partly connect broader spatial skills with mathematical performance (Booth & Siegler, 2008; Gunderson et al., 2012; Schneider et al., 2018; Tam et al., 2019).

If spatial anxiety interferes with spatial representations of nu-merical magnitude, individuals with higher spatial anxiety may produce less accurate number line estimates. In turn, poorer number line estimation may be associated with lower mathematical performance. This possibility makes number line estimation a theoretically relevant mechanism. This body of evidence prompts a critical question: Does the numerical magnitude process that links spatial skills and mathematics also help explain a potential relationship between spatial anxiety and mathematics? Since higher spatial anxiety has been associated with poorer spatial skills, it is plausible that spatial anxiety may also relate to less accurate numerical magnitude representations. If so, unbounded number line estimation could mediate the association between spatial anxiety and mathematical performance. However, current evidence casts doubt on this relation. Leonard and Santos (2025) examined spatial anxiety and math anxiety in three experiments with adults in the United Kingdom. Their results indicated that math anxiety predicted different kinds of number line performance, including unbounded number lines, whereas overall spatial anxiety was not reliably associated with these number line tasks. These findings suggest that the spatial demands of numerical tasks do not necessarily align with the affective demands captured by spatial anxiety.

Collectively, these findings indicate that while spatial skills are associated with numerical magnitude representation, spatial anxiety may not exhibit a comparable association. Nevertheless, it remains uncertain whether spatial skills or numerical magnitude representation explain any potential relationship between spatial anxiety and mathematical performance.

The Present Study

The present study examined the relationships among spatial skills, unbounded number line estimation, spatial anxiety, math anxiety, and mathematical performance in adults. The first objective was to confirm that there is a relation between spatial anxiety and math performance. The second objective was to confirm other expected findings that serve as potential explanations for this relation. That is, we expected that spatial anxiety would predict spatial skills, spatial skill would predict math anxiety, math anxiety would predict math performance, and spatial skills would predict math performance, with unbounded number line estimation mediating this relationship.

The third objective was to test the relations between spatial anxiety and mathematical performance, and whether this relation was best explained by spatial skills, unbounded number line, or math anxiety. Overall mathematical performance, derived from arithmetic, fractions, algebra, and percentages, served as the primary outcome, while individual mathematical domains were considered secondary outcomes.

Method Participants

A total of 221 adults were recruited through Prolific. Three participants were removed because of technical computer errors, 2 withdrew consent, 23 failed at least 1 of 4 attention checks (such as an instruction to select strongly agree), and 3 were removed because they completed both sessions too quickly (i.e., less than 30 minutes). Completion in less than 30 minutes was considered insufficient to support conscientious responding. The final sample included 95 women and 95 men, with a mean age of 27.4 years (women: M = 27.1; men: M = 27.8). Educational backgrounds were diverse: 38.4% held an undergraduate degree, 28.4% had completed high school or A-level qualifications, 17.4% had graduate-level education, 10.0% reported technical or community college education, and 5.3% reported secondary school education. By ethnicity, 63.2% identified as White, 21.6% as Black, 8.9% as mixed race, 4.2% as another ethnicity, and 2.1% as Asian. All participants provided informed consent before beginning the study.

Measures

Spatial Anxiety Scale

Spatial anxiety was assessed using the 24-item Spatial Anxiety Scale (Lyons et al., 2018). Eight items assessed navigation anxiety, 8 assessed imagery anxiety, and 8 assessed mental manipulation anxiety. Participants rated the anxiety associated with each spatially demanding activity on a 5-point scale from 0, not at all, to 4, very much. Item responses were averaged within each subscale and across all 24 items. Higher scores indicated higher spatial anxiety. The primary analyses used the total score because the research questions concerned the shared spatial anxiety construct. Internal consistency was high for mental manipulation anxiety, ω = .94, navigation anxiety, ω = .89, and imagery anxiety, ω = .87.

Spatial Skills

Spatial skills were assessed with the revised Mental Rotations Test and the Surface Development Test. The revised Mental Rotations Test was based on the measure developed by Vandenberg and Kuse (1978) and redrawn by Peters et al. (1995). Each of the 24 items presented 1 target figure on the left and 4 comparison figures on the right. The comparison figures differed in orientation, and 2 depicted the target after rotation around its vertical axis. Participants identified both matching figures. Participants received 1 point for selecting both correct figures and 0 points for selecting an incorrect pair. Scores could range from 0 to 24, with higher scores indicating better mental rotation performance. The test had 2 parts. Participants had 4 minutes per part and 8 minutes total. Internal consistency was α = .89.

The Surface Development Test was drawn from the Kit of Factor Referenced Cognitive Tests (Ekstrom et al., 1976). The test assessed spatial visualization, the ability to mentally transform three-dimensional objects into two-dimensional representations and vice versa, using 12 items. Each item presented a net and a corresponding solid shape. Participants visualized the net folded along its dotted lines and matched the numbered edges of the net with the lettered edges of the solid. Participants received 1 point for each correct match and 0 points for an incorrect match. Higher scores indicated better spatial visualization performance. The test was divided into 2 parts. Participants received 6 min-utes for each part and 12 minutes in total. Internal consistency was α = .90.

We standardized and averaged mental rotation and surface development scores to form a spatial skills composite. The composite was subsequently standardized for the regression and mediation analyses. The two-indicator composite had an estimated reliability of α = .81.

Unbounded Number Line Estimation

Numerical magnitude knowledge was assessed with a 20-trial unbounded number line task adapted from Reinert et al. (2015). Each trial presented a line anchored at 0 with a unit reference but without a labeled endpoint. Participants placed a target number on the line, ranging from 0 to 115. Trials appeared on separate screens so that participants could not use earlier estimates as visible reference points. Figure 1 shows a sample item. Estimation accuracy was scored as mean percent absolute error (PAE), calculated as the absolute difference between the estimated and target values, divided by the 115-point scale, and multiplied by 100 (Booth & Siegler, 2008). Higher values indicated poorer magnitude knowledge. Internal consistency was ω = .80.

Abbreviated Math Anxiety Scale

Math anxiety was assessed with the 9-item Abbreviated Math Anxiety Scale (Hopko et al., 2003). Participants rated their anxiety when performing mathematics-related tasks on a 5-point scale from 0, not at all, to 4, very much. Responses were averaged, and higher scores indicated higher math anxiety. Internal consistency was α = .95.

Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale

General anxiety was assessed with the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (Spitzer et al., 2006). Participants rated the frequency of each symptom on a 4-point scale from 0, not at all, to 3, nearly every day. Responses were averaged, and higher scores indicated higher general anxiety. Internal consistency was α = .92.

Mathematical Performance

Mathematical performance was assessed using multiple-choice items adapted from the Mathematical Placement Test administered by the Department of Mathematics and Statistics at the university where the study was conducted. The items assessed arithmetic, fractions, algebra, and percentages. Mathematics educators designed the placement test to assess competence in core areas of mathematics through at least Grade 12, including arithmetic with integers and rational numbers, algebraic expressions, trigonometry, geometry, percentages, and fractions. The study used 45 items: 15 arithmetic, 15 fraction, 10 algebra, and 5 percentage items. Each question had 4 response options and required participants to apply conceptual or procedural mathematical knowledge. Two authors and a graduate student independently classified the selected items by mathematical domain, with 100 percent agreement.

Arithmetic items required computations and selection of a simplified result, percentage items required basic percentage cal-culations, fraction items required computations involving fractions and decimals, and algebra items required solving equations for an unknown variable. Performance within each domain was calculated as the percentage of correct responses out of the total number of questions assigned to that domain. Overall mathematical performance was calculated as the mean of the four domain percentage scores. Internal consistency (ω) was .73 for arithmetic, .72 for fractions, .66 for algebra, and .67 for percentages.

Procedure

Participants completed the study online in two sessions using standardized instructions, with four attention checks embedded across the sessions. We administered the mathematics in four separately timed domain blocks, presented in randomized order, and instructed the participants to do these assessments without a calculator. Participants were allotted 15 minutes each for arithmetic, fractions, and algebra, and 10 minutes for percentages. Correct responses received one point; incorrect and unanswered questions received zero points. Participants completed the unbounded number line trials individually on separate pages.

Exclusion of the 13 cases (n = 177) did not alter the pattern of results or the study's substantive conclusions. Consequently, all 190 participants were retained in the final dataset, with analysis-specific sample sizes determined by data availability.

Descriptive statistics and Pearson correlations were calculated prior to model estimation, using pairwise complete observations for correlations. Hierarchical linear regression models were then estimated separately for overall mathematics, algebra, fractions, arithmetic, and percentages. Model 1 included total spatial anxiety, sex, and general anxiety. Model 2 incorporated the spatial skills composite, and Model 3 included math anxiety. Discussion

This study investigated the relationships among spatial skills, spatial anxiety, numerical magnitude representation, and mathematical performance in adults. In the face of sparse and inconclusive evidence for a cross-domain association between spatial anxiety and mathematics in previous research, these data suggested that higher spatial anxiety correlated with poorer overall mathematics and with each of the four mathematical domains in bivariate analyses. Furthermore, in line with previous research, spatial skills demonstrated a positive association with mathematical performance, with unbounded number line estimation partially mediating this relationship. Spatial anxiety was negatively associated with both spatial skills and mathematics, with indirect effects observed through spatial skills but not through unbounded number line estimation. However, spatial anxiety did not account for unique variance in mathematics once math anxiety was included in the analysis. These findings provide preliminary evidence regarding the correlates of a cross-domain association between spatial anxiety and mathematics in adults.

Spatial Skills, Number Line Estimation, and Mathematics

The initial analyses confirmed the commonly reported positive association between spatial skills and mathematics. Individuals with stronger spatial skills performed better in arithmetic, fractions, algebra, percentages, and overall mathematics. This pattern aligns with meta-analytic evidence linking spatial and mathematical skills (Atit et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2020) and with longitudinal findings by Geer et al. (2026), who identified bidirectional associations between spatial and mathematical skills in first-grade students. These results extend this literature by showing that the association between spatial and mathematical performance persists into adulthood. Unbounded number line performance partially mediated the association between spatial skills and overall mathematics, consistent with previous findings in adults (Ayesu et al., 2026). Robust spatial performance was linked to better unbounded number line estimation, and, after controlling for spatial skills, sex, and general anxiety, higher unbounded number line performance was associated with improved overall mathematics. The indi-rect association remained statistically significant when we controlled for math anxiety and both math and spatial anxiety. These results suggest that numerical magnitude representation is a relevant process in the spatial skills-mathematics relationship, although the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions about causality.

The domain-specific indirect associations were less consistent. We found associations through unbounded number line estimation for fractions and percentages, whereas those for algebra and arithmetic were not statistically distinguishable from zero. This variability may indicate that numerical magnitude estimation is more closely related to some aspects of mathematical performance than to others. However, nonsignificant indirect associations do not show that number line estimation is unrelated to algebra or arithmetic. Formal comparisons of domainspecific effects would be necessary to determine whether their magnitudes differ. Taken together, the evidence supports an overall role for unbounded number line performance in the spatial skills-mathematics association, while suggesting potential variation across domains, measures, and samples.

Spatial Anxiety, Spatial Skills, and Mathematical Performance

After controlling for sex and general anxiety, spatial anxiety remained associated with overall mathematics, arithmetic, and percentages, but not with algebra or fractions. This pattern contrasts with the absence of an association reported in first-grade children by Geer et al. (2026), highlighting ongoing inconsistencies in the literature. Differences in age, tasks, measurement, and sample characteristics may explain these differences. The cross-sectional nature of the data precludes conclusions about developmental processes or causal direction.

Spatial skills demonstrated the most robust cognitive association in the relationship between spatial anxiety and mathematics. Individuals with higher spatial anxiety exhibited lower spatial skill performance. Furthermore, spatial skills positively predicted each mathematics outcome after controlling for spatial anxiety, sex, and general anxiety. Indirect associations involving spatial skills were observed for overall mathematics and each mathematical domain, and these associations persisted after accounting for math anxiety, although the confidence interval for the percentage outcome was close to zero. This pattern suggests that the association between spatial anxiety and mathematics may be partially attributable to differences in objective spatial performance.

The observed association involving spatial skills offers a potential explanation for the covariance between spatial anxiety and mathematical performance in this sample. Determining the directionality of these relationships will require longitudinal or experimental studies, particularly those assessing whether changes in spatial anxiety correspond with changes in spatial performance and mathematics.

Math Anxiety

Math anxiety was strongly associated with spatial anxiety and emerged as the more proximal affective correlate of mathematics in the final regression models. After including math anxiety, spatial anxiety explained no unique variance in any mathematics outcome. This finding aligns with Daker et al. (2022), who found that spatial anxiety explained no unique variation in mathematics beyond math anxiety across two adult samples. It is also partially consistent with Ashkenazi and Velner (2023), who reported indirect associations between spatial anxiety and complex mathematics through math anxiety.

The absence of a unique spatial anxiety coefficient should not be interpreted as evidence that spatial anxiety is unrelated to mathematics. Spatial anxiety remained associated with mathematical performance in bivariate analyses, and indirect associations involving spatial skills persisted when math anxiety was included. Rather, the final regression models indicate substantial overlap between spatial anxiety and math anxiety, with math anxiety exhibiting a stronger unique statistical association with mathematical performance. Future research should investigate whether these two forms of anxiety reflect overlapping affective responses, partially shared beliefs about mathematical and spatial competence, or reciprocal experiences of difficulty in spatially demanding mathematical contexts.

Spatial Skills and Number Line Estimation

The contrast between the findings for spatial skills and number line estimation is particularly relevant to spatial representation theories of mathematics. These theories propose that spatial skills support mathematical performance in part through numerical magnitude representation. Consistent with this perspective, unbounded number line estimation accounted for part of the relationship between spatial skills and mathematics. Although higher spatial anxiety was associated with poorer spatial skills, this did not translate into a corresponding association with numerical magnitude representation. Instead, spatial skills accounted for part of the spatial anxiety-mathematics relationship, while unbounded number line estimation did not. Spatial anxiety was only weakly related to unbounded number line performance, despite the association of that task with both spatial skills and mathematics. Leonard and Santos (2025) similarly reported no reliable relationship between overall spatial anxiety and number line performance.

One possible interpretation is that individuals with higher spatial anxiety may experience difficulties with spatial transformation tasks without exhibiting corresponding deficits in numerical magnitude knowledge, as measured by unbounded number line estimation. Therefore, the processes associated with spatial anxiety may not align directly with the magnitude representation process implicated in the relationship between spatial skills and mathematics. This distinction is theoretically significant, as it separates anxiety about spatial thinking from the spatial representations that support numerical performance. However, the lack of a supported indirect association does not confirm the absence of a relationship, and the separate models did not assess the complete sequence from spatial anxiety through spatial skills and number line estimation to mathematics. Employing a serial indirect model and directly comparing the indirect effects would provide a more rigorous test of this interpretation.

It is also worth noting that spatial skills still mediated the relation between spatial anxiety and math performance even after controlling for math anxiety. While, as noted above, spatial anxiety and math anxiety shared some common variance, this variance did not explain the connection between spatial anxiety and spatial skills. This contradicts the finding of Ashkenazi and Velner (2023), who suggested that the relation between spatial anxiety and math performance only worked indirectly through math anxiety.

Theoretical and Practical Implications

The findings indicate that spatial skills and spatial anxiety may exhibit different patterns of association with numerical magnitude representation and mathematics. Unbounded number line estimation accounted for part of the spatial skills-mathematics relationship, but not for the association involving spatial anxiety. This distinction implies that anxiety about spatial processing may influence performance on spatial tasks without necessarily reflecting the numerical magnitude knowledge assessed in this study. Further research should investigate whether this pattern generalizes to other measures of magnitude representation and spatial ability.

The potential educational relevance of spatial anxiety depends on whether its association with mathematics generalizes beyond the current sample. Consequently, it is premature to recommend spatial anxiety screening or interventions to improve mathematics based on these findings. Future longitudinal and experimental studies should assess whether the cross-domain association replicates, clarify the contribution of math anxiety, and examine whether modifying spatially demanding mathematical tasks influences spatial anxiety, spatial skills, or mathematical performance.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these conclusions. First, the cross-sectional design precludes inferences about temporal order or causality. Longitudinal studies are necessary to determine whether spatial anxiety precedes changes in spatial skills and mathematical performance, whether repeated difficulties increase anxiety, or whether these relationships are reciprocal. Second, spatial skills were assessed using two tasks, and spatial anxiety was primarily measured by a total score.

Larger samples could facilitate latent variable models that differentiate among imagery, mental manipulation, navigation, and other dimensions of spatial ability and spatial anxiety. Such models may also reduce task-specific measurement error and clarify whether distinct aspects of spatial anxiety relate differently to mathematical performance.

Third, some mathematical subscales demonstrated only modest reliability, and the percentage outcome exhibited a restricted range and negative skew. These measurement limitations may have attenuated associations or reduced comparability across domains. Future research should employ reliable measures that capture adequate variability across the full spectrum of mathematical performance. Additionally, the analysis of multiple mathematical outcomes and statistical models increases the risk of chance findings. Although overall mathematical performance was the primary outcome, domain-specific analyses involved multiple comparisons without formal adjustment. These results should be interpreted with caution, particularly when confidence intervals approached zero. Replication with larger and more diverse samples, along with appropriate adjustments for multiple comparisons, would enhance confidence in the stability and generalizability of the observed associations.

Conclusion

Spatial skills accounted for part of the association between spatial anxiety and mathematical performance. Furthermore, it seems that the part of spatial skills that was related to spatial anxiety was separate from the part of spatial skills that was positively associated with mathematical performance through unbounded number line estimation. In other words, spatial skills explained part of the association between spatial anxiety and mathematics, whereas unbounded number line estimation did not. In the one case (arithmetic skill) where spatial anxiety continued to be related to math performance after controlling for spatial skills, it no longer did so after controlling for math anxiety. Collectively, these findings suggest that spatial anxiety may be associated with mathematical performance without exhibiting the same relationship to numerical magnitude representation as spatial skills. Future research should investigate this distinction using additional measures of magnitude knowledge and models that directly test the proposed sequence of associations.
