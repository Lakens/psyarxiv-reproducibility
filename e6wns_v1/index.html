How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Introduction

Intimate partner violence (IPV) is recognized globally as a significant public health issue and encompasses any behavior occurring within intimate relationships that causes physical, sexual or psychological harm to the victims. 1, 2 Over the past few decades, scientific literature has predominantly focused on females as victims and males as perpetrators, [3] [4] [5] [6] leaving male victimization noticeably understudied. [7] [8] [9] Consequently, public health initiatives, legal frameworks, and support services have primarily centered around violence against women. 1, 10, 11 However, emerging evidence is revealing that men also experience substantial rates of IPV. [7] [8] [9] [12] [13] [14] [15] [16] [17] [18] [19] Despite this growing recognition, determining the exact prevalence of male victimization remains challenging, as rates vary widely depending on study methodology, geographical context, and how IPV is defined. 20 Globally, general estimates suggest that between 17% and 20% of men experience IPV during their lifetime. 7 Providing a closer examination of these figures, a review of 34 studies on male IPV victimization, by Nowinski and Bowen (2012) found that lifetime prevalence rates for heterosexual men ranged from 7.3% to 32.4%, while 12-month prevalence spanned between 0.6% and 29.3% across all forms of violence. 21 Importantly, these global estimates mask significant regional and cultural variations.

In Western societies, including Europe, United States, and Australia, lifetime prevalence rates among males are notably elevated, with studies indicating ranges from 10% to 55%. 8, 12, 13, 18 Conversely, reported rates are considerably lower in other cultural contexts, such as Asian (approximately 12%) and Arab societies (1.4%). 18 While these reported figures establish male IPV as a significant issue, the wide variance in prevalence rates necessitates careful interpretation. An important factor to consider when evaluating these results is that across all official statistics, the available data on men's IPV victimization are based on self-reports and thus the rates may significantly underestimate the true magnitude of the problem. 9 When examining the specific forms of IPV against male victims, the existing literature reveals a highly consistent pattern across studies. Evidence indicates that men most frequently experience psychological violence, followed by physical, and, to a lesser extent, sexual violence. 7, 13, 15 To provide empirical context to these figures, recent analyses of the World Health Organization (WHO) global dataset, comprising over 600 data points, indicate that approximately 9% of IPV studies focus on male victims. 13 Within this subset, the median lifetime prevalences reported for men reach 24.4% for psychological abuse, 17.1% for physical abuse, and 13.0% for sexual abuse. 13 Furthermore, help-seeking behavior differs markedly by gender, with men being substantially less likely to report their abuse or access support services than women. 13, 18 This lack of disclosure masks the actual scope of male victimization, often leading institutional stakeholders to dismiss the issue as a minor problem. 7, 22 A primary internal barrier driving this silence is men's failure to self-identify as victims or recognize their experiences as IPV. 7, 15, 17, 18 This reluctance to identify as a victim is deeply rooted in traditional masculine norms, which dictate that men must remain strong, stoic, emotionally controlled, and self-reliant. 7, 8, 15, 18 Consequently, acknowledging victimization is often perceived as weak or "unmanly", leading male victims to internalize their trauma through profound feelings of shame, embarrassment, guilt, and denial. 13, 15, 18, 22 Beyond these internal struggles, male victims face severe external and institutional barriers, most notably societal stigma and a pervasive fear of not being believed. 8, 13, 22 Because society and institutional networks are often predisposed to stereotype men as aggressive perpetrators and women as fragile victims, men fear that disclosing their abuse will result in their experiences being minimized, ignored, or resulting in false accusations that they are the actual abusers. 7, 13, 15, 17 Unfortunately, these fears are frequently validated by negative institutional responses, as men who report their abuse to police or support services are often mocked, told to "man up", ridiculed, or even arrested. 7, 13, 22 In addition, many men remain in abusive relationships or tolerate the violence to protect their partner, maintain family relationships, or out of fear of retaliation and losing custody of their children. 7, 13, 18 Because of this complex set of barriers, when men do decide to seek help, they rely predominantly on informal networks, such as friends and family, rather than formal public resources, which they frequently evaluate as unhelpful. 15, 18 Finally, evidence suggests that men rarely seek help unless physical violence is involved, underscoring the need to support victims of nonphysical forms of IPV, such as psychological or emotional abuse. 18 International research reveals severe, multi-dimensional consequences of IPV on male victims. Male survivors frequently report elevated levels of depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), alongside a markedly increased risk of suicidal ideation. 7, 15, 16, 18, 23 The literature emphasizes that psychological violence including coercive control act as a particularly strong predictor of these psychiatric outcomes, demonstrating robust associations with overall poorer mental health and severe PTSD. 22, 23 Beyond psychological distress, IPV significantly compromises men's physical well-being. Victims frequently endure serious physical injuries and a substantial reduction in their overall physical health status, with some findings indicating a 2.5-fold decrease in health metrics among male victims. 14, 16 Moreover, male survivors often manifest a range of physical and psychosomatic symptoms, including chronic illness, insomnia, weight loss, loss of appetite, and irritable bowel syndrome. 18, 22 Qualitative and quantitative data indicate that male IPV victims experience diminished self-worth, decreased self-efficacy, impaired concentration, and profound social isolation and loneliness. 7, 13 This distress is sometimes coupled with maladaptive coping mechanisms, such as alcohol and substance misuse. 7, 13 Literature on the association between IPV against males and mental health is very scarce. The absence of culturally and geographically relevant data hinders the development of inclusive social policies, specialized clinical guidelines, and targeted support services for men experiencing IPV in the country. In this context, we conducted a cross-sectional study to examine the association between IPV and multiple dimensions of mental health in a sample of males in Greece. To the best of our knowledge, the present study is the first to address this important research gap in the Greek population.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional survey was conducted in Greece in July 2026 using an online data collection approach. Data were gathered through a structured questionnaire created with Google Forms and disseminated via multiple social media platforms, including Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, and LinkedIn. The study population consisted of males with internet access who actively engaged with social media. Participation was voluntary, and no financial or material incentives were offered. Participants selfenrolled by accessing the survey link and completing the questionnaire. Eligibility criteria required participants to be males, to have been in an intimate relationship for at least the preceding 12 months, and to provide informed consent before participation. Recruitment was based on a non-probability convenience sampling approach, resulting in the acquisition of a convenience sample. The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. 24 To reduce selection bias, the participant information sheet described the study as an examination of intimate partner relationships without explicitly referring to abusive behaviors toward males. This approach was intended to promote participation among males regardless of their exposure to IPV, thereby minimizing the likelihood of disproportionate recruitment of individuals with victimization experiences.

Consequently, the risk of overestimating the prevalence of abusive behaviors within the sample was reduced.

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.2). The calculation was based on a multivariable analytical model incorporating 10 variables, including four independent predictors and six potential confounding factors.

Assuming a conservative small effect size (f² = 0.05), a significance level of 0.05, and a statistical power of 95%, the minimum required sample size was estimated at 262 males. The adoption of a conservative effect size was intended to ensure adequate statistical power for detecting small, yet potentially meaningful, associations between the predictor variables and the study outcomes.

Measurements

Demographic information was collected from participants, including age (continuous variable), marital status (single, married, cohabiting with a partner, divorced, or widowed), number of children (continuous variable), educational attainment (elementary school, high school, or college degree), and employment status (employed vs. unemployed). In addition, perceived economic status was assessed using a self-reported rating scale ranging from 0 (very poor) to 10 (excellent), with higher scores indicating more favorable perceptions of economic well-being.

IPV was assessed using the Woman Abuse Screening Tool (WAST). 25, 26 The WAST is a validated instrument designed to identify emotional, physical, and sexual abuse among males in intimate relationships, facilitating the early detection of abuse and appropriate referral to specialized support services. The instrument consists of eight items rated on a three-point Likert scale, with response options ranging from never (0) to often (2). Total scores range from 0 to 16, with higher scores indicating greater exposure to abusive behaviors. Consistent with the cut-off score recommended by the instrument's developers, participants with a total WAST score of ≥5 were classified as likely to be experiencing IPV, whereas those scoring <5 were considered unlikely to be victims of abuse. The validated Greek version of the WAST was employed in the present study. 27 In the current sample, the instrument demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach's alpha coefficient of 0.721.

The lifetime prevalence of IPV was evaluated using three self-reported dichotomous items (yes/no) assessing experiences of physical, psychological/emotional, and sexual abuse. Participants were asked whether they had ever experienced: (a) physical abuse by an intimate partner, such as slapping, punching, kicking, pushing, biting, hair pulling, assault with objects or weapons, confinement, physical restraint, strangulation, or suffocation; (b) psychological/emotional abuse, including ridicule, persistent criticism, humiliation, threats, blackmail, bullying, gaslighting, or excessive control over personal activities, financial resources, social interactions, or physical appearance; and (c) sexual abuse, encompassing any form of non-consensual sexual contact, rape, sexual harassment, or coercive sexting. Responses were recorded as either yes or no for each category of abuse. The lifetime prevalence of physical, psychological/emotional, and sexual IPV was calculated as the proportion of males reporting a positive response for each respective type of abuse within the study sample.

Anxiety-like and depressive-like symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4), a brief self-report screening tool comprising four items, including two items evaluating anxiety-like symptoms and two items assessing depressive-like symptoms. 28 It should be noted that the PHQ-4 measures symptoms suggestive of anxiety and depression rather than providing clinical diagnoses or directly assessing anxiety and depressive disorders. Responses are rated on a fourpoint Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). Subscale scores for anxiety-like and depressive-like symptoms range from 0 to 6, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. The validated Greek version of the PHQ-4 was used in the present study. 29 Internal consistency was good, with Cronbach's alpha coefficients of 0.802 for the anxiety-like symptoms subscale and 0.724 for the depressive-like symptoms subscale. Sleep quality was evaluated using the Single-Item Sleep Quality Scale (SQS), a brief measure of overall perceived sleep quality. 30 The instrument consists of a single question: "During the past 7 days, how would you rate your sleep quality overall?" Participants are instructed to consider various dimensions of their sleep experience, including sleep duration, ease of sleep initiation, frequency of awakenings, and the restorative nature of sleep. Responses are recorded on an 11-point scale ranging from 0 (terrible sleep quality) to 10 (excellent sleep quality), with higher scores reflecting better perceived sleep quality. The validated Greek version of the SQS was employed. 31 Global self-esteem was assessed using the Single-Item Self-Esteem Scale (SISE), a concise alternative to the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale. The SISE consists of a single item designed to measure overall self-esteem: "I have high self-esteem". 32 Responses are provided on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not very true of me) to 7 (very true of me), with higher scores indicating higher levels of self-esteem. Despite its brevity, the SISE has demonstrated strong convergent validity with the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale and comparable predictive validity across a range of psychological outcomes, supporting its use as a valid and reliable measure of global self-esteem in research settings. 32 Loneliness was assessed using the UCLA 3-Item Loneliness Scale (UCLA-LS-3), a brief instrument designed to evaluate subjective experiences of loneliness and social isolation. 33 The scale comprises three items rated on a three-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (hardly ever) to 3 (often). Total scores range from 3 to 9, with higher scores indicating greater feelings of loneliness. The validated Greek version of the UCLA-LS-3 was used in this study. 34 The scale demonstrated very good internal consistency within the study sample, with a Cronbach's alpha coefficient of 0.838. Life satisfaction was measured using the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS), a widely used five-item self-report instrument that assesses individuals' global cognitive evaluations of their overall life satisfaction. 35 The SWLS captures subjective well-being based on respondents' personal judgments and standards rather than objective life circumstances. Participants rate each item on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Total scores range from 5 to 35, with higher scores indicating greater life satisfaction. The validated Greek version of the SWLS was utilized in the present study. 36 The instrument demonstrated excellent internal consistency in the study sample, with a Cronbach's alpha coefficient of 0.964.

Discussion

Addressing a critical gap in the existing literature, the present study investigates the link between IPV and mental health outcomes among male victims in Greece. By examining how men's exposure to IPV correlates with different aspects of their mental health and general well-being, our research aims to directly inform public health policy. Consistent with prior research, 7, 13, 15 this study found that psychological/emotional abuse was the most commonly reported form of IPV, followed by physical and sexual abuse. This pattern mirrors our previous findings in a female sample, where psychological violence was similarly the most frequent form of IPV. 38 Specifically, the lifetime prevalence of psychological/emotional abuse among males was 50.0%, while physical IPV was reported by 15.6% of the participants.

Sexual abuse was the least common form, reported by 1.2% of the study sample.

Furthermore, our findings reveal a robust association between male victimization and both depression-and anxiety-like symptoms. Higher levels of recent abuse, as indicated by elevated WAST scores, correlated strongly with higher levels of these symptoms. Notably, this effect was not limited to recent abuse. Males who reported a lifetime history of physical and psychological/emotional abuse also exhibited significantly higher baseline levels of symptoms indicative of both anxiety and depression. These results align with previous studies linking IPV to the development of anxiety and depression. 23, [39] [40] [41] This dual impact highlights the cumulative and enduring nature of trauma. While recent abuse likely acts as an acute stressor that triggers immediate anxiety-and depressive-like symptomatology, lifetime exposure to physical and emotional violence appears to have a deeper impact on males' mental health by fundamentally altering the stress response system. 42 As a result, chronic trauma leads to severe psychological stress alongside endocrine and immuneinflammatory dysregulation, both of which are key drivers of depression. 42 This persistent physiological dysregulation was further reflected in reduced sleep quality. Our analysis showed that men with a lifetime history of physical and psychological/emotional abuse reported significantly poorer sleep quality than those without such experiences. This finding may be partly explained by the effects of chronic traumatic stress on the body's stress-response system, which sustains hyperarousal and hypervigilance, thereby interfering with normal sleep patterns. 43 Poor sleep may, in turn, further exacerbate emotional distress, creating a vicious cycle in which trauma exposure, psychological symptoms, and sleep disruption reinforce one another.

Beyond physiological impacts, violence severely diminishes an individual's selfesteem, 44 a finding clearly supported by our data. Participants who reported elevated WAST scores (recent abuse) and those who had experienced physical abuse at any point in their lives had significantly lower levels of self-esteem. This finding requires critical interpretation within the context of traditional gender norms. Societal expectations often dictate that men should be strong, capable of defending themselves, emotionally controlled and self-reliant. 7, 13, 15, 18 Experiencing IPV directly contradicts these internalized masculine ideals, inducing profound feelings of shame and guilt that ultimately lead to diminished confidence and reduced self-worth. 7, 13, 15, 18 Our multivariable models also showed that higher levels of recent abuse (WAST score) were significantly associated with increased feelings of loneliness. Τhis finding aligns with previous literature showing that male IPV victims frequently experience severe social isolation and heightened loneliness. 45 Specifically, recent research from Wales highlights that this loneliness and reduced psychological well-being are significantly exacerbated if fathers are separated from their children. 45 Driven by societal stigma and a pervasive fear of disbelief, male victims often suffer in silence and remain hesitant to seek help.

Consequently, this compounding burden of psychosocial and mental health consequences may considerably reduce victims' life satisfaction. In our study, recent abuse, as assessed by the WAST score, was a predictor of lower overall life satisfaction. Furthermore, we found a similarly detrimental effect among males who had experienced lifetime psychological/emotional abuse. Building upon our earlier results, it is highly probable that the cumulative effects of anxiety-and depressivelike symptoms, as well as feelings of loneliness, fundamentally undermine overall well-being, ultimately driving the observed decline in life satisfaction.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study.

First, the cross-sectional design does not permit conclusions regarding the temporal sequence or causality of the observed associations between IPV and mental health outcomes. Second, males were recruited through a non-probability convenience sampling strategy using social media platforms. As participation was voluntary and based on self-selection, the sample may not be representative of the broader male population in Greece. Third, because recruitment was conducted through publicly accessible online posts, the number of individuals who viewed the study invitation could not be determined. Consequently, an accurate response rate could not be calculated, making it difficult to evaluate the potential impact of non-response bias.

Fourth, all study variables were assessed using self-report measures, which are inherently vulnerable to information bias, including recall bias and social desirability bias. Fifth, the online methodology may have excluded certain population groups, including males with limited digital literacy, restricted internet access, older age, lower socioeconomic status, or residence in geographically remote areas.

Consequently, selection bias may have been introduced, further affecting the representativeness of the sample. Finally, although several demographic variables were measured and considered in the analyses, residual confounding due to unmeasured factors remains possible.

Despite these limitations, the study possesses several notable strengths. These include the use of validated instruments to assess multiple dimensions of IPV and psychological well-being, the inclusion of a relatively heterogeneous sample of males from different regions of Greece, and the examination of a topic that has received limited empirical attention in the Greek context. To our knowledge, this study provides some of the first evidence regarding the association between IPV and mental health outcomes among males worldwide. As such, the findings offer an important basis for future research, particularly longitudinal investigations and nationally representative studies aimed at further elucidating these associations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this cross-sectional study provides evidence of significant associations between IPV and multiple dimensions of mental health and psychosocial well-being among males in Greece. Greater exposure to abusive behaviors was associated with elevated symptoms indicative of anxiety and depression, increased loneliness, lower self-esteem, poorer sleep quality and reduced life satisfaction. These findings highlight the psychological and psychosocial burden associated with IPV victimization among men and underscore the importance of recognizing both recent and lifetime experiences of abuse when assessing male victims' well-being.

Despite growing recognition of male IPV victimization, the experiences and support needs of male victims remain insufficiently addressed. The findings emphasize the importance of developing inclusive, evidence-based screening and support approaches within healthcare and social care settings that acknowledge gender-related stigma and barriers to disclosure and help-seeking. Further research, particularly using longitudinal designs, is needed to clarify the relationships between IPV exposure and psychological and psychosocial outcomes, as well as to examine the distinct effects of different forms of IPV. By contributing to the limited evidence on IPV and mental health among males in Greece, this study highlights the need for targeted prevention strategies, appropriate clinical guidance, and accessible support services that address the complex and diverse needs of men experiencing IPV.
