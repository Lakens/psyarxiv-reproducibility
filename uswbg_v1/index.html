Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Compulsivity, the maladaptive persistence of behaviour despite adverse consequences, is central to obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) and has been increasingly conceptualised as a transdiagnostic dimension spanning multiple neuropsychiatric disorders. Research within computational psychiatry has linked dimensional compulsivity to two candidate mechanisms: impaired goal-directed control and elevated metacognitive confidence. Here, we take a clinical perspective addressing how insights from these findings can be brought into practice. We first ask how dimensional compulsivity measures should be interpreted with respect to clinical presentations and discuss conflicts between the general population findings and clinical data. We highlight several challenges facing clinical implementation: effect sizes are small, goal-directed control has not been shown to change with either pharmacotherapy or psychological therapy, and the overconfidence associated with dimensional compulsivity contrasts with the reduced confidence reported in patients with OCD. We argue these discrepancies are informative rather than disqualifying and set out a translational roadmap for mechanistically informed detection and transdiagnostic intervention. Specifically, we suggest that screening and digital monitoring of dimensional compulsivity, combined with cognitive and mechanistic phenotyping to guide psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy, may ultimately improve outcomes across diagnostic boundaries.

A randomised controlled trial is underway to determine whether behavioural metrics can personalise antidepressant choice to improve clinical outcomes 16 . These approaches have also been used to provide novel insights into the mechanisms behind existing psychological treatments for depression [17] [18] [19] . However, within compulsive disorders, computational psychiatry has yet to make inroads in the clinic, despite having made significant progress in clarifying candidate mechanisms underpinning its key symptoms 1, 20, 21 . Here, through reviewing the literature on compulsivity as a transdiagnostic construct and exploring its potential for early detection and personalised intervention, we will highlight how we might bridge this gap.

There is a growing consensus that existing psychiatric diagnostic systems are inadequate 22, 23 . Current diagnoses are syndromes -clusters of co-occurring symptoms defined without reference to underlying biological mechanisms -so patients sharing a diagnosis may differ in symptoms, pathology, course, and treatment response. Conversely, the same symptom can appear across diagnoses, and a single treatment (e.g., SSRIs) can work across many of them. Applying the existing diagnostic approach in clinical practice is reasonable in the absence of an understanding of the underlying biological mechanisms. Nonetheless, development of novel treatments has stalled under this paradigm, and current clinical practice has for decades relied upon serendipitous discoveries 24,25 rather than interventions targeted at disorder-relevant mechanisms informed by basic science. While there is some precedent for a more mechanistically-informed pathway in compulsive disorders-behavioural treatment through exposure and response prevention was developed based on the proposed mechanisms from learning theory 26-29 -significant advances in neuroscience and cognitive science in recent years are yet to be translated into any new treatments. This may reflect the inappropriateness of diagnostic categorisation for identifying mechanisms that serve as effective targets for interventions.

Introduction

Compulsions involve repetitive behaviours or mental acts performed in response to an irresistible urge regardless of negative consequences 1, 2 . Compulsions are central to obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), but also relevant across multiple conventional diagnostic entities -including body dysmorphic disorder 3 , tic disorders, eating disorders 4 , and substance use disorder 5 . Despite the availability of effective pharmacological and psychological interventions, treatment outcomes are highly variable. Diagnosis is frequently delayed, which correlates with increased severity and poorer outcomes 6, 7 . Therefore, early detection and intervention have the potential to improve long-term outcomes. It is hoped that through employing such a dimensional approach, a greater understanding of the cognitive, neural, and biological mechanisms underlying symptoms will emerge. In turn, this approach could lead to the development of novel or repurposed therapeutics targeted to the individual patient based on an appreciation of the underlying psychopathological mechanisms.

Two influential frameworks have proposed alternative approaches to categorical diagnosis: the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP) 22 and the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) 23 . HiTOP and RDoC take opposing approaches -HiTOP derives dimensions from the covariance of self-reported symptoms but is agnostic to mechanism 30 , while RDoC starts from neurobiologically defined constructs (e.g., potential threat, reward learning, cognitive control) that have proved hard to map back onto clinical presentations. In this review we will focus on a new approach -computational factor modelling 21 -which sits between HiTOP and RDoC, determining symptom dimensions from questionnaires and then directly relating them to theory-driven models of behaviour.

What is transdiagnostic compulsivity?

The most influential characterisation of transdiagnostic compulsivity emerged from a study by Gillan and colleagues 31 . They recruited a large sample (~1,400) of unselected online participants. Participants completed nine well-established selfreport questionnaires (with 209 items) commonly used to screen for psychiatric diagnoses including OCD, depression, and substance use disorder (Figure 1C). Despite not targeting clinical populations directly, this approach provided a broad range of symptom severity. Responses were entered into exploratory factor analysis, finding the data were best summarised by a three-factor solution. Based on item loadings (Table 1), the researchers named these "Anxious-Depression", "Compulsive Behaviour and Intrusive Thought", and "Social-Withdrawal". This approach has had a significant impact on the research field and has numerous advantages 21 . It has allowed researchers to investigate the relationship between the dimensional compulsivity score and various cognitive processes in large samples drawn from the general population, avoiding the logistical and ethical challenges of clinical recruitment and confounds related to ongoing treatment, while improving the generalisability of findings. There is also evidence that this approach demonstrates a stronger relationship between dimensional compulsivity and cognitive parameters than if a screening questionnaire for an individual disorder were used 31, 32 . These findings suggest that dimensional compulsivity may reflect underlying cognitive computational mechanisms better than existing diagnostic frameworks.

While many heavily weighted items derive from the OCI-R, there is also significant influence from the EAT-26 and AUDIT, reflecting the transdiagnostic nature of the factor.

Furthermore, as a self-report screening tool, the OCI-R total score is heavily influenced by symptom breadth (the number of different domains affected). In contrast, the Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS)-the gold standard clinician-rated measure for OCD-assesses functional impairment regardless of the number of symptom types. To illustrate with an example, a patient housebound by severe contamination rituals but lacking other OCD subtypes related to checking, ordering or symmetry would score near the maximum on the Y-BOCS yet might fail to reach the clinical threshold on the OCI-R. This is a general property of a dimension formed by the weighted sum across symptom domains -a person with mild symptoms across many domains and a person severely impaired in a single domain can obtain similar scores. Therefore, at the individual level, dimensional compulsivity operationalised in this way could be considered a transdiagnostic marker of trait propensity to compulsivity across multiple disorders / symptom domains (e.g. OCD, eating disorder, alcohol use disorder), as opposed to a classical clinical metric indexing the severity or disability resulting from compulsivity. This dissociation is relevant when considering discrepancies between how cognitive processes relate to categorical diagnoses and to transdiagnostic compulsivity, and in determining the clinical threshold for intervention.

Goal-directed control vs. habitual responding

There is an extensive literature linking OCD with impaired goal-directed control and an over-reliance on habits 1 . On this account, compulsions are automatically triggered by certain cues and are associated with an urge to habitually perform a stereotyped behaviour inconsistent with current goals. It is proposed that the cue-triggered response is initially goal-directed, and is strengthened through either positive or negative reinforcement and repeated performance 1, 33 . Through this strengthening, the behaviour eventually becomes habitual. Classically, in an experimental setting, a deficit in goal-directed control is shown by demonstrating continued performance of a pre-learned "habitual" response when contingencies change or following outcome devaluation 34, 35 . There has been comparatively little exploration of how habits are initially acquired 36, 37.

From a computational perspective, goal-directed control and habitual responding can be formalised as resulting from model-based and model-free reinforcement learning systems respectively 38 , based on the former's reliance on an internal "model" of the environment. The model-based system is therefore more flexible but cognitively demanding, while the model-free system is simpler but less costly. In people with compulsive disorders, there is hypothesised to be a deficit in the model-based system 39 , or alternatively a deficit in arbitrating when to be guided by model-based vs. model-free systems 40 , leading to a tendency to instead rely on model-free, habitual behaviour.

The relative influence of model-based and model-free systems on choices can be probed with a commonly used two-step decision-making task (Figure 1A) 41 . Voon and colleagues demonstrated that choices made by OCD patients were less influenced by the model-based algorithm relative to healthy controls 42, 43 (Table 2), and that this deficit related to the severity of compulsions on the Y-BOCS 43, 44 . However, this effect was not specific to those with a diagnosis of OCD -it was also present in patients with binge eating and methamphetamine use disorder 42 . Subsequent studies have also demonstrated the same pattern in schizophrenia -a disorder which is not typically classified as compulsive 45 . Furthermore, there was no difference in model-based planning between those with a diagnosis of generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) and OCD 46 . This suggests that impairments in goal-directed control cut across existing diagnostic categories.

Utilising a dimensional approach, many studies have demonstrated that individuals with higher levels of compulsivity perform two-step decision-making tasks with a reduced model-based influence on choices 31, [47] [48] [49] [50] [51] (Table 2). Crucially, transdiagnostic measures of compulsivity were shown to be stronger predictors of a deficit in goaldirected control than scores on individual questionnaires 31 , or an OCD vs. GAD diagnosis 46 . Furthermore, the relationship has generally been shown to be specific to dimensional compulsivity -it was not observed for Anxious-Depression or Social-Withdrawal dimensions. If we consider model-based planning a fundamental algorithmic component of human cognition, these findings suggest the transdiagnostic dimensional compulsivity score may lie closer to underlying cognitive computational mechanisms (and their derangement) than conventional categorical psychiatric diagnoses.

Metacognition and confidence

Metacognition is another cognitive process which has been associated with both compulsive disorders and dimensional compulsivity 20, 52 . Broadly, metacognition refers to our insight into our own cognitive processes and decisions. Clinically, cognitive distortions hypothesised to underlie compulsive symptoms -such as thought-action fusion (the belief that thinking something makes it more likely to happen), or an overinflated responsibility for egodystonic intrusive thoughts -could be formulated as impaired metacognition [53] [54] [55] . In cognitive neuroscience, metacognition has been studied experimentally through eliciting confidence estimates following perceptual decisions (Figure 1B). To reconcile the different levels of abstractness between clinical and experimental metacognition, a metacognitive hierarchy has been proposed whereby local confidence in individual decisions reciprocally interacts with generalised confidence in one's abilities and a more global sense of self-worth 56 . An impairment in laboratory-measured metacognition may represent an under-or overestimate of one's performance (metacognitive bias), or a poor correlation between choice accuracy and confidence (metacognitive sensitivity).

While research in clinical populations has generally demonstrated lower confidence in OCD patients compared to controls 57, 58 (Table 3), this trend is not disorderspecific 57 . Similar deficits appear across diagnoses, including depression and generalised anxiety disorder. Using a transdiagnostic approach, Rouault and colleagues showed a positive relationship between confidence ratings and dimensional compulsivity 59 : more compulsive individuals generally rated their confidence level as higher at the end of each perceptual decision-making trial. There was also a significant negative relationship between confidence ratings and the Anxious-Depression dimensional score. We focus on these findings relating symptom dimensions with metacognitive bias, as they have been replicated in multiple subsequent studies [60] [61] [62] [63] [64] [65] . Much like the relationship between compulsivity and goal-directed control, effects are weaker when confidence ratings are related to individual-disorder questionnaires rather than to the transdiagnostic factor 59, 60, 62 highlighting the merits of a transdiagnostic approach.

Despite the consistency of these findings, the higher levels of confidence with high dimensional compulsivity appear to contradict prior research in patients with OCD 57, 58, 66 and clinical intuition which characterises OCD as a 'disorder of doubt'. This contradiction represents a significant challenge to the validity of taking a transdiagnostic approach -where an important assumption is that the subthreshold dimensional symptoms represent an attenuated form of clinical phenotypes. Confidence may also be inefficiently used to guide subsequent behaviour: OCD patients 67 and highly compulsive individuals 68 show decoupling between confidence and action updating. However, a recent comparison found this decoupling (together with overconfidence) in highly compulsive individuals but not in OCD patients (who were underconfident) -providing further dissociation between clinical and transdiagnostic populations 69.

One explanation for these discrepancies is that OCD patients are likely to score highly on the Anxious-Depression dimension, given the frequent co-morbidity of OCD and affective disorders, and its negative relationship with confidence would counteract the effect of dimensional compulsivity 68 . However, a recent study demonstrated lower confidence ratings in OCD patients relative to controls, even when controlling for co-morbid anxiety and depression 66 . An alternative explanation is that OCD patients could have elevated levels of local confidence even if they have lower levels of confidence further up the metacognitive hierarchy 63 . However, this would not explain the low levels of local confidence observed in recent studies or the findings from a recent meta-analysis 58, 66, 70 . An additional consideration is that dimensional compulsivity captures features of other diagnoses -such as gambling disorder -which may be associated with overconfidence 71.

From a clinical perspective, it is important to note that OCD is not a single entity 72 but comprises distinct symptom domains (e.g. obsessions/checking; symmetry/ordering; contamination/cleaning). There is evidence that different symptom domains are associated with distinct metacognitive belief profiles 73 . Furthermore, amongst clinical populations there is variability in insight. It is possible that local under-confidence would be over-represented in patients with checking symptomatology and underrepresented in those with poor insight -findings which would help to reconcile the current differences in the transdiagnostic and clinical literatures. Larger studies in clinical populations, which calculate dimensional compulsivity and record symptomatology domains, would help to address these important unresolved questions.

Translating insights to the clinic

Given the promising foundational findings outlined above highlighting how cognitive processes relate to compulsive symptoms, an important question is how these insights may be used clinically. One potential application is to use individualised behavioural metrics to predict relapse 14, 15 and inform the selection of treatments with pre-established efficacy for particular diagnoses 16 . While the computational approaches discussed may be incorporated into existing clinical approaches to guide individualised treatment decisions, a more ambitious vision is for mechanisminformed transdiagnostic treatment. We propose a framework whereby psychotherapeutic and pharmacological interventions reliably shown to shift relevant cognitive mechanisms could be used as targeted treatments. For instance, a drug or therapy approach shown to shift a goal-directed control impairment could be selectively beneficial in patients cognitively phenotyped as demonstrating this deficit. Alternatively, a different patient phenotyped as showing a metacognitive bias may benefit from a different pharmacological agent or therapy (Figure 2). In the following section, we outline a roadmap towards mechanism-informed early detection and treatment of transdiagnostic compulsivity.

Psychological treatments

The current most effective psychological approach for OCD and BDD is a type of behavioural therapy: exposure and response prevention (ERP), whereby the patient is exposed to an anxiety-provoking thought or stimulus that would ordinarily prompt a compulsion. Preceding psychoeducation sessions explain that the anxiety response will eventually habituate and that preventing the compulsion is important to 'break the cycle'. Interestingly, similar exposure-based approaches are employed transdiagnostically in the treatment of anxiety disorders -including in panic disorder and social anxiety.

During ERP, patients are effectively being encouraged to exert goal-directed control over habitual responding. One may therefore infer that successful ERP would lead to an increase in model-based planning. Surprisingly, previous results did not support this hypothesis. In a small-sample two-timepoint longitudinal study, ERP improved OCD symptoms but there was no change in goal-directed control on the two-step decision-making task 44 . A further study showed that internet-based self-guided CBT (pooled across various therapeutic techniques and diagnoses) did not alter modelbased planning metrics 48.

Interestingly, various factors seemingly unrelated to psychopathology can influence model-based planning. These include increasing the monetary incentive for correct performance 49 , providing explicit instructions about task structure 76 , laboratoryinduced stressors 77 (but see also 78 ), and adding additional tasks performed simultaneously to strain cognitive resources 79 . Aside from the two-step task, time pressure has also been shown to increase the expression of habitual responses following a sensorimotor remapping 80.

Central to this approach is mechanistic cognitive phenotyping using gamified goal-directed control and metacognitive tasks (examples adapted from 47, 70 ). Screening youth populations: we propose a role for dimensional symptom profiling and extracting cognitive biomarkers in youth populations to facilitate early identification of compulsive symptoms. While awaiting clinician evaluation, those at high clinical risk could benefit from digitalised symptom monitoring (depicted as logging handwashing behaviours), along with brief digital psychotherapeutic interventions. In those requiring formal psychological treatment, the approach (balancing between behaviour-based exposure and cognitive approaches) would be informed by individual phenotyping. In those requiring pharmacological intervention, drug choice could be informed by computationallyderived mechanistic insights. This work requires further input from experimental medicine studies to characterise how candidate drugs influence relevant mechanisms. In treatment-resistant cases, brain stimulation -initially non-invasive methods -could precisely target deranged neural circuitry at the individual level. Both pharmacological and brain stimulation work should be informed by translation from animal models.

One explanation for the failure to show a relationship between CBT-ERP-induced symptom improvement and laboratory-measured model-based planning could be that the increase in goal-directed control gained with ERP is context-specific (to the obsession / compulsion) and does not extend to an abstract two-step decisionmaking problem. If so, an open question is whether building analogues of the factors described above (incentive, instruction, stress, cognitive load) into ERP would facilitate or impair therapy. A related question concerns patients' ability to generalise their learning from ERP tasks. For some patients, particularly in youth, the structured ERP approach is sufficient to resolve OCD symptoms and prevent recurrence, while for others new obsessions / compulsions emerge. This may represent a failure to generalise goal-directed control across contexts.

An alternative explanation for the absence of an effect of CBT-ERP on model-based planning is that this cognitive process is a trait-like quality (as opposed to state) which predisposes one to develop compulsive symptoms 48 . In that case, the clinical role for model-based planning assays may lie in early detection (discussed below). There may also be a role for pharmacological and psychotherapeutic synergywhereby a drug which can increase model-based planning could facilitate stronger learning in ERP sessions to update behavioural policy and reduce compulsions. This synergistic approach mirrors clinical evidence in youth populations that the combination of SSRIs and ERP is more effective than either treatment alone 81 . Regardless, there is clearly a need for larger longitudinal studies, particularly in youth and at-risk groups, to determine the temporal relationship between compulsive behaviours and impaired model-based planning.

In addition to the primarily behavioural ERP approach, extended CBT treatment models incorporate additional cognitive insights 82 . The Salkovskis model advocates modifying how intrusive thoughts are appraised -an approach which could be characterised as improving metacognition. Presently, when treating patients with compulsive disorders, the balance between behavioural and cognitive approaches may be influenced by the patient symptomatology, patient preference, or therapist experience. A new approach could be phenotyping patients using computational metrics of model-based planning and experimental metacognitive tasks -as a predictor for which form of therapy they would be better suited to (Figure 2). Preliminary evidence of the potential value of taking such an approach comes from a recent longitudinal study which demonstrated that in a transdiagnostic sample of patients receiving online CBT, an improvement on scores in Anxious-Depression scores correlated with an improvement in under-confidence in perceptual decisions 61 . This result is consistent with the hypothesis that improvements in metacognition underlie the effects of CBT on anxious-depression and could be explored further in a longitudinal study of the effects of CBT-ERP.

There is a clear role for a dimensional approach, as opposed to a categorical diagnostic approach, when determining psychotherapeutic interventions. For instance, online self-help platforms already leverage this framework by providing modularised components of CBT tailored to individuals experiencing subthreshold symptoms. This transdiagnostic perspective is particularly relevant for conditions scoring highly on dimensional compulsivity. Within this dimension, there is significant overlap in the exposure-based protocols used to treat OCD, BDD, and hoarding disorders. Paradoxically, however, substance use disorder -which also loads heavily onto dimensional compulsivity -is classically treated with a seemingly contradictory clinical approach, prioritising the avoidance of triggers rather than direct behavioural exposure. The fact that dimensional compulsivity loads items whose disorders traditionally have opposing treatments could be interpreted as a challenge to a proposed single treatment-relevant mechanism across disorders. An alternative view is that these insights may provide a mechanistic grounding and additional momentum for trialling novel approaches which could prove effectivesuch as cue exposure therapy in substance use disorder 83, 84.

Pharmacological treatments

Current evidence supports the use of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) for first-line pharmacological treatment for OCD and many of the compulsive disorders 85 . The choice of a particular SSRI is subjective and may be guided by clinician or patient preference. Low-dose antipsychotics (dopamine antagonists) can be trialled in OCD if a second SSRI is unsuccessful, and they are effective in the pharmacological management of tic disorders. Once an effective therapy is established, the consensus is generally to continue indefinitely to reduce relapse risk -partly because there are few individual predictors of relapse. There are clearly multiple steps in existing treatment algorithms where it would be beneficial to have reliable predictors of who would respond to a particular SSRI, antipsychotic augmentation, or relapse on treatment discontinuation.

There is a highly influential body of theoretical work proposing distinct roles for serotonergic, dopaminergic, and noradrenergic neurotransmitter systems in modelbased planning and metacognitive processes like uncertainty estimation [86] [87] [88] . To implement a mechanism-guided transdiagnostic approach, further evidence of the causal roles of cognitive mechanisms in the pathological process is needed, along with reliable pharmacological mediators. While clearly in the early stages, it is worth considering the existing evidence of how pharmacological agents impact relevant cognitive mechanisms and how it relates to what is known clinically.

Although animal studies support a role for serotonin in model-based planning [89] [90] [91] , evidence from human interventional studies is more mixed. Tryptophan depletion, a model of reduced serotonergic function, decreased model-based planning in healthy volunteers 92 . However, this finding was specific to a reward-maximising version of the two-step task and has not been subsequently replicated 93 . Furthermore, three weeks of SSRI administration in healthy volunteers had no effect on model-based planning 94 . Consistent with this, a longitudinal observational study of patients starting an antidepressant (most likely an SSRI, although drug and diagnosis were not reported) found no change in model-based planning at four weeks 48 . Together, these findings suggest that SSRIs are unlikely to act by correcting a goal-directed control deficit, although longer treatment periods than those studied are typically required for clinical response in compulsive disorders.

There is perhaps stronger evidence for the effects of dopaminergic modulation on model-based planning, with several studies demonstrating that levodopa (a dopamine precursor) leads to an increase in the degree of model-based behaviour 95, 96 (see also [97] [98] [99] [100] ). However, there is no evidence that dopamine agonism improves compulsive symptoms -with dopamine antagonists showing therapeutic benefit in OCD and tic disorders. Furthermore, dopaminergic therapy may lead to punding (repetitive stereotyped non-goal-directed behaviours) in Parkinson's disease 101.

Several studies have also investigated the effects of acute pharmacological manipulation upon metacognition in healthy volunteers. These studies have demonstrated inconsistent results about the effects of noradrenergic and dopaminergic blockade on metacognitive performance 98, 102, 103 . However, preliminary clinical findings are more promising; in the longitudinal "antidepressant" study mentioned previously, there was an improvement in metacognitive confidence ratings which correlated with improvement in dimensional 'Anxious-Depression' scores 61 . There is therefore a need to clarify the specific pharmacological agents associated with improved metacognitive confidence and the causal nature of this relationship.

Brain stimulation

Deep-brain stimulation (DBS) is an effective, although rarely used, intervention for treatment-resistant OCD 104, 105 with some evidence of effectiveness in other compulsive disorders 106 . The white matter of the ventral capsule / ventral striatal area is predominantly targeted, with stimulation likely to spread throughout cortico-striatothalamo-cortical loops. Activity in these same brain areas has been shown to be abnormal in compulsive disorders 1 and implicated in goal-directed control and habitual behaviour. While there has been speculation that the effects of DBS for OCD may be mediated by increased confidence 107 , there is not currently a sufficient understanding of its therapeutic mechanism. Advances in non-invasive deep-brain stimulation, neuromodulation, and lesional techniques -such as temporal interference and focussed ultrasound -may provide greater mechanistic insight. However, we suggest it will be important to keep cognitive processes at the heart of such work with carefully characterised behavioural paradigms providing the necessary mechanistic bridge from neural circuitry to psychiatric symptomatology 108, 109 . These technologies also offer the opportunity to noninvasively trial a localised circuit-level intervention for effectiveness within an individual patient before proceeding to the more invasive surgical DBS, although this approach would require further evidence of a shared stimulation mechanism.

Translation through animal models

Studying analogous cognitive tasks in animal models also offers the opportunity to further develop a mechanistic understanding of compulsivity and test new therapeutic strategies. The most prominent animal model of OCD, the Sapap3 knockout mouse, was established due to the observation that the genetic deletion caused mice to display persistent grooming behaviours which could be ameliorated with an SSRI 110 . However, it is debatable how analogous this phenotype is to human OCD, and the human mutation is not strongly linked to OCD. The Sapap3 mouse has been shown to display deficits in reversal learning [111] [112] [113] , a concept related to cognitive flexibility and to the balance between goal-directed and habitual control. However, it is important to note that results of impaired reversal learning in OCD patients are inconsistent 114 . Recent studies have begun to demonstrate animal versions of multi-step decision-making tasks [115] [116] [117] , and manipulations to measure confidence 118 . Future work should seek to back-translate cognitive deficits which have been consistently observed across the compulsive dimension into animal models. Once an effective animal model is established, it provides a promising testbed for preclinical drug screening and neuromodulation interventions.

Early detection and symptom monitoring

A more speculative role for dimensional approaches is in population screening and symptom monitoring. Effective early detection and intervention are key to improving outcomes for people with compulsive disorders. These conditions can be challenging for parents, teachers and even non-specialist clinicians to identify -with appropriate interventions often only beginning once significant symptoms are experienced. This process is likely delayed further through existing clinical approaches whereby symptoms must reach a certain threshold before a categorical diagnosis is made. However, it is likely the predisposing cognitive computational mechanisms emerge before a young person meets diagnostic criteria or even before they show external symptoms 119 , and it has been suggested that deficits in goal-directed control may reflect a trait-level vulnerability for compulsive symptoms 51.

We envisage a role for remote data collection to improve this process and act as a screening tool in youth populations. Clinical questionnaires could be used to determine dimensional compulsivity, and gamified behavioural tasks could provide individualised mechanistic insights (Figure 2). These data complement each other: the behavioural tasks indicate an underlying psychopathological process that can be targeted for intervention, and the questionnaires indicate how psychopathology manifests in everyday life (along with its associated distress and functional impairment).

A key strength of this approach is its affordability and scalability -with recent advances meaning remotely-administered behavioural tasks can gather reliable data within 5-10 minutes 47, 65 . Evidence suggests children may be more willing to engage in gamified tasks than disclose compulsive symptoms to a clinician 120, 121 . A dimensional approach could identify a wider range of young people at risk of developing various diagnoses without the issue of dismissing those not currently reaching the threshold. However, future work would be required to determine the sensitivity and specificity of a screening approach and prospectively establish improved clinical outcomes. Analyses would need to account for developmental increases in model-based 119, 122 and metacognitive abilities 123, 124.

Once high compulsivity is detected, mechanism-informed psychoeducation and brief digital psychotherapeutic interventions could be offered while awaiting clinical review. Digital monitoring could help clarify the pattern of symptoms and their triggers, their relationship with underlying cognitive mechanisms, and individualised longitudinal trajectories. However, care would need to be taken not to perpetuate excessive thought monitoring 125 . A strength of using validated cognitive tasks to monitor symptoms would be that they are less prone to desirability effects than the repeated administration of questionnaires. A promising recent study demonstrated the potential clinical relevance of remotely collected metacognition measures, as reduced confidence preceded OCD symptoms 70.

Limitations

While the approaches we are advocating have many advantages, it is important to also explore some of the limitations to this body of work and identify the necessary further evidence required to take innovations into the clinic.

Studies linking dimensional compulsivity with cognitive parameters must consider possible spurious drivers of these relationships. Recent work has highlighted how inattentive responding while completing symptom questionnaires could result in elevated dimensional compulsivity 126, 127 , which in turn could explain a correlation with overconfidence in metacognitive tasks. While these flaws could equally apply to clinic, laboratory and online-based data collection, it will be important for future studies to ensure inattentive responders are effectively excluded.

While conclusions from existing dimensional studies have been widely replicated, the relationships between dimensional scores and cognitive parameters require large samples and show small effect sizes (Tables 2 and 3). The modelling approach used can also have a significant impact on test-retest reliability 128 . This provides a significant challenge for how existing behavioural metrics can be used at the individual patient level. It is possible that, through combining multiple metrics in multivariate machine learning models, future studies may demonstrate stronger predictive power. Furthermore, longitudinal intra-individual tracking may have significant utility through allowing the patient to serve as their own control and provide a sensitive baseline to monitor treatment response or clinical decline.

We have argued that the stronger relationship between behavioural metrics and transdiagnostic compulsivity-rather than classical diagnostic categories-suggests that transdiagnostic compulsivity better reflects underlying cognitive computational processes. Although this in turn implies a closer relationship with underlying neurobiological processes, the neural basis of compulsive behaviour is relatively underexplored. The majority of studies on the neural basis of compulsivity have utilised structural, resting-state, or functional connectivity measures -as opposed to task-based correlates 1 . Further neurophysiological studies of compulsivity 50, 129, 130 , incorporating mechanistic behavioural signatures, are required. Bridging the gap from synaptic-level dysfunction to transdiagnostic symptomatology will likely require neurophysiological markers that can be extracted across experimental models (e.g., translationally across animals, non-invasively in patients, and from DBS recordings).

Another important question is the relationship between cognitive markers and fluctuations in symptoms. Cognitive markers may be mediators (the premise of CBT), fixed traits that predispose to symptoms, state-dependent correlates, or noise. For instance, the impairment in model-based planning in compulsive disorders could be an epiphenomenal consequence of obsessional thoughts straining cognitive resources. The ideal cognitive marker would be one in which a fluctuation drives symptoms -which in turn guides a particular intervention. To identify such markers there is a need for more longitudinal studies (particularly in youth populations), ecological momentary assessments 70, 131, 132 and symptom provocation studies to characterise the temporal sequence of cognitive processes and symptomatology.

A more fundamental challenge to taking a dimensional approach is whether compulsivity can be represented as a continuous spectrum, or whether the psychopathology in clinical compulsive disorders represents a distinct, qualitative state severed from "normal" experience. For OCD, the general consensus from taxometric approaches is that symptoms are dimensional in nature 133, 134 . This assumption is crucial when utilising general population samples to draw mechanistic inferences about compulsivity. As discussed, transdiagnostic compulsivity scores may better represent symptom breadth rather than symptom severity. This poses a challenge if clinicians take a dimensional approach -as an additional marker of clinical severity would be needed to determine the threshold for intervention.

Finally, it is important to acknowledge that current dimensional approaches are somewhat limited by the use of symptom questionnaires. In existing clinical approaches, the questionnaires may be utilised as screening measures as opposed to a means to confirm a diagnosis. They identify areas which a clinician may prioritise for exploration in a subsequent interview. If we consider the item with the second highest loading on the dimensional compulsivity factor (Table 1), 'I am upset by unpleasant thoughts that come into my mind against my will' -a patient could agree with the statement for a variety of different reasons. Subsequent exploration may reveal this distress is from egodystonic thoughts the patient recognises as their own, or alternatively thoughts which are nefariously inserted by an external agent. Existing diagnostic approaches would classify these two cases as fundamentally distinct experiences. While this is not a new critique of the drawbacks of screening questionnaires, it is important to note that in computational factor modelling there is no opportunity to unpack the nuance in a subsequent interview. The approach will always be limited by how accurately and precisely the questionnaires assess psychopathology. To overcome this limitation, future dimensional approaches must move beyond rigid, structured scales. Integrating unstructured behavioural datasuch as Natural Language Processing (NLP) metrics derived from spontaneous speech-could allow computational models to capture the contextual and qualitative markers essential for a more precise determination of psychopathology 135, 136.

Conclusions

Dimensional approaches to transdiagnostic compulsivity using computational factor modelling have provided a significant advance in research into compulsive disorders. They have produced replicable associations between two cognitive mechanismsimpaired goal-directed control and overconfidence -and compulsive symptoms. This is a considerable step forward for a research area typically reliant on small-sample case-control studies lacking replication, and for psychiatric disorders where existing treatments were identified serendipitously, agnostic to mechanism. This research approach has also demonstrated how to reach large sample sizes and developed innovative technological approaches so that gamified behavioural assays can be completed within 5-10 minutes.

From a clinical perspective, while there is clearly overlap between dimensional compulsivity and compulsive disorders, they are not the same. As discussed, people with severe dimensional compulsivity are likely to have a breadth of compulsive symptoms across classical diagnostic entities, whereas a clinician's concept of a severe disorder is significant distress and functional impairment. There are important discrepancies to resolve -such as the paradox between apparent under-confidence in OCD and over-confidence in dimensional compulsivity. However, applying a clinical perspective as we present here -including highlighting how heterogeneous OCD symptom domains and variable levels of insight may relate to metacognitive confidence -can help to reconcile these issues as opposed to merely identifying inconsistencies. Moving forward, we hope large longitudinal studies in clinical populations collecting symptomatology, severity, dimensional compulsivity and behavioural data will help to resolve these disparities.

Developing mechanistically informed early detection and clinical interventions offers great promise for people with compulsive disorders (Figure 2). Early detection using gamified behavioural metrics and dimensional symptom scores offers unprecedented reach, scalability, and the opportunity for early intervention. Mechanism-informed psychotherapy would be an advance on current systems whereby the psychotherapeutic approach is largely informed by availability. For conditions with no new first-line pharmacological class since the emergence of SSRIs 40 years ago, identifying fundamental cognitive mechanisms opens the door to a novel set of rationalised therapeutic targets. Cognitive paradigms also hold promise in providing a behavioural readout to enhance understanding and titrate stimulation for neuromodulation. Realising this will require longitudinal studies in at-risk populations, establishing reliable metrics at the individual level, and verification of the effects of screening interventions. There is a need for experimental medicine studies exploring the effects of pharmacological agents and neuromodulation upon candidate cognitive mechanisms -spanning animal, healthy volunteer, and patient populations -across clinically relevant timescales.

Compulsive disorders are debilitating conditions where a significant proportion of patients do not respond to first-line treatment. Computational phenotyping offers a set of mechanistically grounded measures that could improve clinical decisions at several points along the care pathway and facilitate the development of novel mechanistically informed transdiagnostic treatments.
