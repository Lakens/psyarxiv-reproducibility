Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

INTRODUCTION

The sudden death of a parent is one of the most profound and disruptive stressors a young individual can experience, often triggering acute and enduring turmoil in mental health, family functioning, and developmental adaptation . Given that more than 2.9 million children under the age of 18 have experienced parental loss in the United States , research has increasingly focused on identifying its psychological and behavioral consequences. Parentally bereaved youth are at increased risk for depression, post-traumatic stress disorder, substance use, functional impairment, and health-risk behaviors, with some difficulties persisting into adulthood . However, although sadness and yearning are commonly recognized manifestations of grief , anger and physical aggression may also represent important, yet relatively understudied, responses to parental loss.

14 5 2,3,68 (American Psychiatric Association, Edition, 2013)

Parental death may increase vulnerability to anger and physical aggression by disrupting the family and social resources that support emotion regulation . Bereavement can impair the functioning of the surviving caregiver, and reduce family cohesion and parental monitoring . These disruptions may limit opportunities for youth to process the loss and regulate intense emotions, such as anger. Indeed, longitudinal research provides evidence that parental bereavement may increase vulnerability to aggressive and other externalizing behaviors. One study found that parental death before age 16 was associated with greater prevalence of delinquent behavior, particularly when the death occurred during adolescence . Similarly, another study found that parentally bereaved male and female adolescents exhibited greater delinquency and fighting than their nonbereaved counterparts . Although most group differences diminished over seven years, bereaved females continued to report greater involvement in fighting . Among maltreated youth, bereavement prospectively predicted increased externalizing problems from early to middle adolescence.

Research using criminal records has similarly linked childhood parental death with later violent behaviors. Parental loss was associated with approximately twice the risk of violent criminal violent behaviors among males followed from adolescence into adulthood . Large population-based registry studies also found elevated risks of violent criminal convictions following parental death, including assault, robbery, weapon-related offenses, threats, and arson . Risks were generally greater following external, unnatural, or suicidal parental deaths, although patterns differed according to the sex of the offspring and the deceased parent, and the age of the child at the time of loss. These studies provide evidence of an association between parental death and serious externalized violence, however, their reliance on criminal convictions captures only the most severe behavioral outcomes and offers limited information about the emotional expressions of anger and more subtle physical aggression.

Findings involving less severe forms of aggression and health-risk behavior have been more limited. At approximately 9 months after parental death, found no statistically significant differences between bereaved and nonbereaved youth in individual violence-related behaviors, including physical fighting and weapon carrying. However, when the same cohort was followed longitudinally, bereaved youth demonstrated persistently higher levels of health-risk behaviors across three years than nonbereaved controls, even after accounting for pre-loss and post-loss risk factors . In addition, suicide-bereaved children and adolescents have reported greater anger and more behavioral problems than youth bereaved by other causes of parental death . These findings suggest that the behavioral consequences of bereavement may not be fully evident at one cross-sectional assessment and may become clearer when trajectories are examined prospectively.

Importantly, the occurrence of parental death alone may not fully explain these outcomes. The severity of grief reactions may be more directly related to anger and aggression than bereavement status itself. Among persistently delinquent adolescents, bereavement-related distress predicted externalizing symptoms, particularly among boys, even after accounting for PTSD, maltreatment, and psychopathy-spectrum traits . Nevertheless, this study involved a small sample of incarcerated youth and examined broad externalizing syndromes rather than anger and physical aggression separately. More recent longitudinal evidence from homicidally bereaved adults demonstrated reciprocal associations between prolonged grief symptoms and state anger across four assessments, with prolonged grief symptoms predicting subsequent anger more strongly than anger predicted subsequent prolonged grief symptoms . However, this study's focus on adults exposed to violent loss limits its generalizability to parentally bereaved youth and does not clarify whether grief-related anger translates into overt physical aggression.

Thus, the current study aims to (1) Examine whether bereavement and prolonged grief are related to anger and physical aggression, cross-sectionally and longitudinally. We hypothesize that bereavement and prolonged grief will be associated with higher anger and physical aggression both cross-sectionally and longitudinally.

METHODS Participants

Bereaved young adults and adolescents aged 7-29 years old who lost a biological parent in the past year due to sudden death such as suicide, accident or natural death (within 24 hours of the initial incident) were recruited to a longitudinal study examining the effect of bereavement on children's mental health. The study was conducted from August 9, 2002 through December 31, 2013. All participants were identified via medical examiner reports or newspaper advertisements, with recruitment methods and specific reasons of death described previously (Melhem et al., 2007). 71% of those eligible to study were consented to participate. The University of Pittsburgh institutional review board approved this study, and all participants provided written consent or assent for their participation. Baseline assessment occurred on average, around 9 months after parents' death, while follow-ups occurred at 21, 33, 62, and 84 months, on average, after death. Three-hundred and eighty participants completed baseline assessments of anger and physical aggression and were included in the analysis, 47 were excluded from the analysis due to missing data on anger and physical aggression or had experienced another loss during the study. Compared with excluded participants, those included in the analyses were less likely to be bereaved, non-White, or have a history of ADHD. They also exhibited lower posttraumatic stress symptoms and higher global functioning. Their caregivers were less likely to be non-White and had higher global functioning. No significant differences were found in aggression or violence-related behaviors (Supplementary Table 1).

Of the sample included in the analysis, 206 were parentally bereaved and 174 were frequency-matched non-bereaved controls (Total N=380). Of the 206 parentally bereaved participants, 66 had parents who died by suicide, 49 by accident and 91 by natural death. Of the 380 participants included in the analysis, 299 (78.6%) were retained in the study and had complete data in all 4 follow-ups assessments. Compared with participants who were not retained, those retained in the study were less likely to be bereaved and reported lower aggression, violencerelated behaviors, and suicidal ideation, as well as lower rates of substance use disorders, better functioning, and higher family cohesion. Retained participants and their caregivers were more likely to be White, and their caregivers demonstrated better functioning and lower rates of bipolar disorder and PTSD. Their parents also had lower rates of bipolar and personality disorders (Supplementary Table 2).

Measures and assessments

Baseline and follow-up assessments were conducted through in person interviews by clinically certified study clinicians for participants and their surviving caregivers separately. Sociodemographic characteristics such as age, gender, race and socioeconomic status assessed by the Hollingshead's scale were measured at baseline for both participants and their surviving caregiver. Aggression and violence-related behaviors were assessed using the Aggression Questionnaire and the Youth Risk Behavior Questionnaire . The Aggression Questionnaire consists of 29 items assessing four domains: physical aggression, verbal aggression, anger, hostility and indirect. Items are rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (extremely uncharacteristic of me) to 5 (extremely characteristic of me), with higher scores indicating greater aggression. The Youth Risk Behavior (YRB) Questionnaire is an adaptation of the Youth Risk Behavior Survey , which assesses health-risk behaviors prioritized for surveillance by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, including behaviors contributing to unintentional injury and violence. The current study included two violence-related behaviors: carrying a weapon, including a gun, knife, or club, during the past 30 days (Item 6) and involvement in a physical fight during the past 12 months (Item 11). Items were rated using frequency-based response options ranging from 0 (0 times) to 4 (6 or more times). Items assessing sexual violence were not included in the analysis since they were outside the primary focus of the study on physical aggression, whereas other violence-related items assessed more specific aspects of weapon carrying and physical fighting already captured by the selected items. Prolonged grief. At the time the study was initiated, prolonged grief reactions were referred to as "complicated grief," a term now commonly described as "prolonged grief" . Prolonged grief for surviving caregiver and participants aged 18 years and above was measured by the 19-item inventory for complicated grief (ICG) , with a clinical threshold of 25 or higher indicating clinical prolonged grief . The 28-item Inventory for Complicated Grief-Revised for Children (ICG-RC) was used to assess prolonged grief in participants aged 18 years and under, with a clinical threshold of 68 or higher indicating clinical prolonged grief symptoms. Standardized scores of both inventories were used to reflect the severity of prolonged grief symptoms in both child and adult participants. Clinical prolonged grief was also used as a binary outcome using cut-off scores for both inventories. Circumstances of exposure to death (CED) a semi-structured interview was used to assess experiences surrounding the death, reflecting the participant's experience at and following the death of the parent . Psychiatric disorders prior to the death were assessed using the Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia for School-Age Children-Present and Lifetime Version and the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV (SCID) Axis I Disorders for children and adult participants, and their surviving caregiver, respectively. This was conducted as a retrospective report on psychopathology prior to the death. A psychological autopsy was also used to assess the deceased parents' lifetime history of psychiatric disorders . Personality disorders in adult participants were assessed with the Structured Interview for DSM-IV Personality Disorders.

General functioning for participants aged under 18 years was assessed using the Children's Global Assessment Scale (CGAS) , indicating functioning at home and school. The Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF) was used for those who are older than 18 years old and their surviving caregiver . High interrater reliability was maintained for the assessment of psychiatric diagnoses (kappa=0.74-0.85; N=107) and impairment (intraclass correlation= 0.92, 95% CI=0.90, 0.93; N=734) . Clinical symptoms, including depression, anxiety, posttraumatic stress, suicidal ideation severity, and negative coping, were assessed using the Beck Depression Inventory , Beck Anxiety Inventory , Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Symptom Scale-Interview (PSSI-I) , Suicidal Ideation Questionnaire-Junior (SIQ-JR) , and Ways of Coping Questionnaire , respectively. Protective factors, including self-esteem, social support, and family cohesion, were assessed using the Weinberger Adjustment Inventory self-esteem subscale , Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support , and Family Adaptability and Cohesion Evaluation Scales-II , respectively. Stressors, including physical and sexual abuse were measured by the post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) section of the psychiatric interview and the Abuse Dimensional Inventory . Negative life events were assessed using the Life Events Checklist for participants under 18 years old and the shortened Social Readjustment Rating Scale for participants aged 18 years and older.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis were conducted using R program . Independent samples t-test and Chi-square test were used to examine differences in sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the participants, surviving caregiver and deceased parent between those bereaved vs. non-bereaved, and the type of parent's death among bereaved participants.

For data reduction, we conducted an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) using baseline data for the Aggression questionnaire and the weapon carrying and physical fighting items from the YRB questionnaire. Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin and Bartlett's tests supported factorability. Varimax rotation was applied, and parallel analysis indicated two-factor-solution: Factor 1, reflected anger, and included hostility, indirect aggression, verbal aggression and anger. Factor 2, reflected physical aggression, included physical aggression, physical fighting, and weapon carrying; Supplementary Table 3). We conducted confirmatory factor analysis to determine measurement invariance across timepoints. Measurement invariance was not consistently supported across the configural, metric, scalar, and residual models based on all examined fit indices (Supplementary Table 4). Because the same two-factor solution emerged across timepoints (see Supplementary Table 5 for EFA at each timepoint), the overall pattern of factor loadings remained generally consistent, and the factors were clinically interpretable, we retained the same two-factor structure across timepoints for the longitudinal analyses.

To examine whether bereavement and prolonged grief were associated with higher levels of anger and physical aggression at baseline, separate linear regression models were conducted for anger (Factor 1) and physical aggression (Factor 2). In the full sample, bereavement was examined as the primary predictor of each outcome. Prolonged grief severity was examined separately within the bereaved sample only. Models were adjusted for age, sex, race, and socioeconomic status. Within the bereaved sample, additional linear regression models examined whether the type of parental death was cross-sectionally associated with each outcome.

To examine whether bereavement and prolonged grief were associated with changes in anger and physical aggression over time, longitudinal associations were examined using separate linear mixed-effects regression models for each outcome. Models included bereavement status, time, and the bereavement-by-time interaction to determine whether bereaved and non-bereaved participants differed in changes in anger and physical aggression over time. The models included repeated observations nested within participants and accounted for the clustering of participants within the same family. Days since baseline was used as the measure of time in these models. Within the bereaved sample, separate models included prolonged grief severity, time, and the prolonged-grief-by-time interaction to examine whether grief severity was associated with changes in each outcome overtime. Covariates were entered and refined using the same hierarchical and backward-elimination procedures applied in the crosssectional analyses.

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to longitudinally examine the associations of prolonged grief with anger and physical aggression among parentally bereaved youth. Bereavement alone was not associated with anger or physical aggression after adjustment for sociodemographic and clinical factors, either cross-sectionally or longitudinally. In contrast, clinical prolonged grief was associated with higher anger at baseline and increasing anger over time, but not with physical aggression. In addition, physical aggression increased more steeply over time among youth bereaved by sudden natural death than among those bereaved by suicide.

Our finding that bereavement was not independently associated with anger or physical aggression after adjustment for covariates suggests that parental loss alone may not be sufficient to explain these outcomes. This aligns with prior findings from the same cohort showing no significant cross-sectional differences between bereaved and non-bereaved youth in specific violence-related behaviors, including physical fighting and weapon carrying . However, it contrasts with research linking parental bereavement to delinquency, fighting, externalizing behaviors, and violent criminality . These discrepancies may reflect differences in the outcomes examined, as previous studies often assessed broad health-risk behaviors, delinquency, or criminal convictions rather than examining anger and physical aggression separately. Although bereaved youth from this cohort previously demonstrated persistently higher overall health-risk behaviors (including unsafe driving and substance use) across three years , the current findings indicate that changes in physical aggression over time did not differ significantly between bereaved and non-bereaved participants.

Our findings also indicate that bereaved youth with clinical prolonged grief exhibited higher anger at baseline and increasing anger over time, whereas anger decreased among those without clinical prolonged grief. This finding is consistent with the conceptualization of anger and bitterness as manifestations of the intense emotional pain that may accompany prolonged grief . It also aligns with findings among homicidally bereaved adults showing longitudinal associations between prolonged grief and anger, with prolonged grief more strongly predicting subsequent anger than the reverse direction . Similarly, bereavement-related distress has been associated with externalizing symptoms among persistently delinquent adolescents, even after accounting for PTSD, maltreatment, and other clinical factors . Extending this literature, our findings demonstrate that clinical prolonged grief is prospectively associated with increased anger among parentally bereaved youth.

Several mechanisms may contribute to the association between prolonged grief and anger. According to the integrative risk-factor framework, grief adaptation is shaped by pre-loss vulnerabilities, experiences surrounding the death, and post-loss individual and family processes . Beliefs that the death could have been prevented, perceived accountability, self-blame, and exposure to an unexpected or violent death have been associated with more severe grief symptoms . Such beliefs may sustain feelings of injustice, blame, frustration, and bitterness, thereby interfering with the integration of the loss and contributing to increasing anger. Disruptions in surviving-caregiver functioning, family cohesion, social support, and the caregiver-child relationship following parental death may further limit opportunities for youth to process the loss and regulate these emotions.

Importantly, prolonged grief was not associated with physical aggression, indicating that prolonged grief does not necessarily translate into overt aggressive or violent behavior. Anger represents the affective component of aggression, whereas physical aggression reflects its behavioral expression and may be shaped by additional individual and situational factors . Given that our study focused on a community-based sample, youth experiencing prolonged grief may express their distress primarily through anger without engaging in overt physical aggression, which may require the presence of additional vulnerabilities. Furthermore, our previous study examined the effects of bereavement on a broad range of health-risk behaviors rather than the specific effects of prolonged grief, whereas another study examined grief-related distress among persistently delinquent youth who were already at elevated risk for externalizing behavior. Thus, differences between these findings may reflect variation in sample characteristics, risk for behavioral disorders, and the specificity of the outcomes examined. Our findings suggest that within a community-based sample of bereaved youth, prolonged grief may be more directly related to the emotional experience of anger than to its behavioral expression through physical aggression.

We also found that physical aggression increased more steeply among participants bereaved by sudden natural death than among those bereaved by suicide, whereas changes in physical aggression over time following accidental death did not differ significantly from that following natural death. This finding was unexpected given prior studies reporting greater anger and behavioral problems following parental suicide and greater risks of violent criminality following external, unnatural, or suicidal parental deaths . However, this finding partially aligns a previous study , who found that violent criminality was more strongly associated with unnatural parental deaths other than suicide than with parental suicide, indicating that suicide bereavement may not necessarily pose the greatest risk for violent criminality. This study suggested that, in some cases, parental suicide may be preceded by recognizable psychiatric difficulties, allowing families or services to anticipate the possibility of a suicide death and initiate protective support . In addition, it is possible that families bereaved by suicide had experienced greater pre-loss psychiatric morbidity, family disruption, and reduced parental involvement, whereas sudden natural death may have represented a more profound disruption to previously stable family relationships, as indicated in a previous study.

Our results should be interpreted while considering several limitations. This is one of the few community studies with a 7-year follow-up period that examined multiple domains of highly relevant risk factors with relatively good retention. An important limitation concerns the representativeness of our sample and the characteristics of those retained. Participants who remained in the study were predominantly White, and although not significant after correcting for multiple comparisons, their surviving parents tended to have higher functionality and lower prolonged grief symptoms. This pattern may bias our findings and limit generalizability to more clinically severe and ethnically diverse populations. In addition, we included both adolescents and young adults in our study, known to differ developmentally. Different developmental forces may influence outcomes for youth who lose a parent in childhood compared with those who experience this loss closer to young adulthood . Even though age was not significantly associated with prolonged grief symptoms in our study, our sample size prevented us from examining age as a moderator and from determining whether outcomes differed between adolescents and young adults.

Taken together, this community-based longitudinal study extends prior research by distinguishing the emotional experience of anger from overt physical aggression and examining their trajectories across seven years following sudden parental death. Clinical prolonged grief, rather than bereavement alone, was associated with higher and increasing anger over time. The type of parental death, rather than bereavement or prolonged grief, was associated with increases in physical aggression over time, with steeper increases following sudden natural death than suicide. Those findings highlight the potential importance of strengthening family functioning and relationships after sudden parental deaths and the importance of assessing prolonged grief and anger among bereaved youth.
