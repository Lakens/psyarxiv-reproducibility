Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. Abstract

Affective science is the interdisciplinary study of phenomena such as emotions, moods, attitudes, and stress responses, which together make up a large part of the forces that energize and direct behavior. The field has developed rich theories of motivation and emotion and generated an impressively large empirical literature on affect in human and nonhuman behavior. In this paper, I argue that the field is now well poised for a systematic turn toward computational modeling. I begin by explaining what computational modeling is, trace how it has transformed neighboring disciplines, and lay out what affective science stands to gain from it. Many computational models of affect and motivation already exist, but this work remains scattered across fields that rarely speak to one another. I review this work across three interrelated domains: (1) models of specific, circumscribed affective phenomena; (2) applied models in computational psychiatry and affective computing; and (3) broader theoretical models that aim to explain what affect is and how it arises. What is Computational Modeling?

Before going further, it is worth pausing to clarify what I mean by computational modeling. I expect readers will come to this discussion from different starting points: some already fluent in computational modeling, many others engaging deeply with it for the first time.

Complicating matters further, the term itself has carried different meanings across history and disciplines. A shared working definition is, therefore, in order. Broadly, computational models in the behavioral sciences serve one of two aims. Explanatory models translate theories and hypotheses about how a phenomenon arises into precise equations and algorithms, with the goal of making those theories explicit enough to simulate and test. Predictive models, including many COMPUTATIONAL AFFECTIVE SCIENCE machine-learning approaches, are optimized to forecast an outcome without making strong claims about the process that generated it (Shmueli, 2010). These two aims are often complementary, since predictive models can surface new regularities that explanatory models must account for. Accordingly, throughout this paper, I use the term computational modeling in a deliberately capacious sense, to refer to any approach that translates ideas about affect from words into equations or algorithms in a way that advances our understanding of how affective processes arise and how they shape behavior and cognition.

Importantly, computational modeling is not a single method. Instead, it is useful to think of computational modeling as a spectrum defined by the tightness of the link between the psychological hypothesis and the mathematical form it takes. A model can take the form of a Bayesian model, a reinforcement learning algorithm, a neural network, or something else entirely, as long as it faithfully expresses the modeler's hypothesis about the phenomenon under study. The tightness of the link between the description of a hypothesized process and its mathematical formulation is what distinguishes a computational model from a statistical model (Guest & Martin, 2021). A researcher can fit a linear regression to a dataset to test whether two variables are related, without claiming that the structure of the equation mirrors the process that generated the data. But the same linear regression equation can be considered a computational model if the substantive hypothesis is that increases in quantity x drive changes in quantity y, and that this effect is constant across the range of x. Imagine, for example, walking into a local bakery and trying to figure out why different cookie boxes have different price tags. If you suspect that the price of a box of cookies is simply the price of a single cookie multiplied by the number of cookies in a box, then you may formalize your hypothesis as a linear regression and test your model by comparing its predictions against real data.

In many cases, hypotheses in psychology are richer than that. For example, we might hypothesize that the reason why a person who has just moved into the neighborhood buys a different cookie on each visit to a bakery during their first month, before eventually settling on double chocolate chunk every time, is that they are gradually updating their beliefs about the value of each option until they can identify a favorite. Finally, it is worth making explicit what building a computational model involves in practice, since the rest of this paper returns to these steps repeatedly. A model typically has four ingredients. The first is a set of variables: the quantities the model takes in and produces, such as the stimuli it sees and the choices or ratings it makes. The second is a set of rules (i.e., equations or an algorithm) that specify how inputs are transformed into outputs and that embody the hypothesis itself. The third is a set of parameters: adjustable quantities within those rules, such as a learning rate (how much a single experience changes one's expectations). The fourth ingredient is a way of evaluating the model: researchers simulate it to see what behavior it predicts, fit it to real data to estimate its parameters, and compare it against competing models that embody alternative hypotheses, asking which one best accounts for the data without unnecessary complexity.

Together, these distinctions offer a lens for the rest of this paper. For any model discussed, it is worth asking whether its primary aim is to explain or to predict, how tightly its COMPUTATIONAL AFFECTIVE SCIENCE mathematical form is tied to a psychological hypothesis, and what its variables, rules, parameters, and evaluations reveal about the phenomenon under study.

The Case for Embracing Computational Modeling in Affective Science

At its core, computational modeling is the practice of translating psychological hypotheses from whatever language we already use to guide experimental exploration and write papers into the language of math. Of course, anyone who has tried to carry out even a casual conversation in a second language-much less do rigorous science in it-knows how demanding such translation can be. Why, then, invest all this time and effort into learning another language when affective science already makes steady progress?

It is true that affective science has made remarkable progress in turning rich everyday phenomena into objects of scientific study and providing useful explanations for how they arise and influence behavior. However, as things currently stand, most such explanations exist at the level of verbal theories-empirically-grounded accounts, stated in natural language, of why people feel what they feel, and what roles those feelings play in everything from memory and decision-making to physical health and mental well-being. Over the years, these theories have guided measurement choices, motivated innovative experimental paradigms, and directly informed clinical interventions. They rest on a large body of rigorous experimental evidence. Yet an important limitation remains, and, with it, an important opportunity. Natural language is rich, expressive, and relatable, but it is also imprecise. When the goal at stake is to articulate the exact mechanisms and generate quantitative, falsifiable predictions about affective phenomena, it often falls short. To see why, let us consider research on anxiety as a case study both in how far the field has come and why computational affective science marks the next frontier.

This is precisely the kind of question where a verbal theory lets us leave important choices unstated. The first possibility is that anxiety shifts one's starting assumptions-or prior beliefs-about danger, so that an anxious person walks into a novel social situation already expecting it to go poorly. The second possibility is that anxiety changes how people interpret uncertainty. Someone laughs after they speak-are they laughing with them or at them? By lowering the threshold for treating ambiguous cues as threatening, anxiety pushes the interpretation toward the latter option.

Adjudicating Between Competing Theoretical Accounts

A compelling example of this affordance of computational modeling comes from one of the earliest attempts at formal theorizing in psychology. For many years, the dominant account of perception postulated that a signal-such as a quiet sound or a quickly presented image-either crossed a fixed sensory threshold or did not, rendering detection of said signal all-or-none. An alternative hypothesis put forth by Swets et al. (1961) proposed that every observation was instead a reflection of overlapping distributions of both signal and noise, with an observer playing the role of a decision-maker who is actively setting a criterion for determining whether a signal was present or not. Notice that the two hypotheses give rise to the same qualitative prediction: people are more likely to detect strong signals and less likely to detect weak ones.

However, once formalized, signal detection theory predicted that, as the criterion shifts from more liberal to more conservative, the resulting combinations of hit and false alarm rates should fall along a characteristic nonlinear receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, even as one's sensitivity to the stimulus (d') remains unchanged. Empirical data from studies that force participants to adopt deliberately strict or loose criteria (e.g., by incentivizing accuracy vs. speed) consistently matched this pattern. By cleanly separating what an observer can sense from how they choose to respond, this work paved the way for the study of perception, memory, and decision-making as we know them today.

Discovering New Hypotheses

One of the truly humbling features of computational modeling is that it often reveals novel or surprising hypotheses that would have been unlikely to emerge from a traditional verbal COMPUTATIONAL AFFECTIVE SCIENCE debate (Cowell et al., 2012). A striking example comes from the study of dopamine and reward.

For a long time, the dominant hypothesis in the field of learning was that midbrain dopamine scales with the amount of reward (Olds & Milner, 1954; Wise et al., 1978). Drawing on the foundational idea that learning is driven by surprise rather than reward itself (Rescorla & Wagner, 1972) and on temporal-difference learning algorithms (Sutton & Barto, 1998), Montague et al. (1996) developed a predictive model that formalized phasic dopamine activity as tracking not the reward itself but rather a moment-to-moment discrepancy between expected and realized reward-a signal that has come to be known as the reward prediction error. Crucially, this formalization generated a series of non-obvious predictions. First, the prediction that once a cue is reliably paired with the reward, the timing of the dopamine spike should shift from the presentation of the reward itself to the cue that predicts it. And second, that if an expected reward then fails to materialize, dopamine activity should dip below baseline. Notice that questions about the precise timing of a neural response, or about whether an effect grows linearly or nonlinearly over time, rarely arise from verbal theorizing because they seem too narrow to matter. Yet it is precisely such specific, non-obvious predictions that provide the strongest support for a proposed mechanism. Like a spotlight illuminating a previously neglected corner of the conceptual terrain, this model inspired researchers to measure dopamine activity at moments that previously seemed unimportant. Recordings from midbrain dopamine neurons revealed exactly the predicted pattern of activity (Schultz et al., 1997). The resulting framework profoundly reshaped theories of motivation and addiction, eventually paving the way for the introduction of the distinction between "wanting" and "liking" which transformed our understanding of mechanisms driving addiction (Berridge & Kringelbach, 2015). Testing Hypotheses That Are Off-Limits for Empirical Research

Formal models can let us explore scenarios that are effectively off-limits because of cost, duration, impossibility of experimental manipulation, or ethical constraints. We could never (thankfully!) systematically expose children to chronic stress to study its emotional toll. We can, however, examine related mechanisms "in silico." Many examples of this strategy emerged from the connectionist tradition, where researchers used artificial neural networks to probe mechanisms spanning multiple levels of analysis. For example, in order to study the etiology of acquired dyslexia experimentally, one would, in principle, need to lesion the brain regions that are believed to support reading. Instead, researchers trained neural networks to map spelling onto meaning and then "lesioned" the model directly by selectively removing connections after learning had taken place. The lesioned models reproduced the hallmark errors of deep dyslexia, including semantic confusions such as reading "cat" as "dog" (Hinton & Shallice, 1991; Plaut & Shallice, 1993). This work helped reshape neuropsychological accounts of dyslexia, and related connectionist approaches were subsequently extended to other disorders (see Olson & Humphreys, 1997). More broadly, this work can be viewed as part of a larger effort that propelled forward the basic understanding of semantic cognition by showing that category structure can emerge from a system that simply learns the statistical relationships between concepts (as opposed to a system that requires that such structure be imposed explicitly). These insights went on to inspire many productive lines of research on the acquisition of conceptual COMPUTATIONAL AFFECTIVE SCIENCE knowledge in children, language development, and memory, among others (Rogers & McClelland, 2004).

Taken together, these examples demonstrate how systematically applying computational tools can help adjudicate between competing accounts, surface surprising predictions, and probe questions that remain off-limits to empirical research. Computational Modeling in Affective Science: A Conceptual Review

At present, there is no single, coherent field that calls itself "Computational Affective Science." Nevertheless, attempts to formalize affective processes have been growing across psychology, cognitive science, neuroscience, psychiatry, and other disciplines. These efforts often proceed in isolation, using different terminology, publishing in different venues, and rarely speaking to one another despite frequently trying to solve the same problems. Recognizing these efforts as part of a shared enterprise would make it easier for ideas, methods, and findings to accumulate across traditional disciplinary boundaries and help build toward a more unified understanding of affect and motivation and, ultimately, intelligent behavior more broadly.

I synthesize the existing computational work on affect into three interconnected domains organized by their goals. First, models of specific phenomena seek to isolate and formalize circumscribed processes like emotion regulation or social emotion inference. Second, applied models are built to accomplish real-world goals that extend beyond scientific understanding, whether by detecting and measuring affect, intervening on it, or generating it in artificial systems (as in computational psychiatry and affective computing). Third, theoretical models attempt to synthesize across phenomena and applications in order to answer fundamental questions about the nature of affect and its role within the broader architecture of the mind. Great science often begins with a simple observation. We notice, for example, that people feel regret in the face of a missed opportunity. Observations like these serve as the foundation for our hypotheses. We hypothesize that missed opportunities lead to regret, and we create experimental settings-that either involve missed opportunities or not-to test our intuitions. But we rarely stop there. We might further hypothesize, for example, that it is not the missed opportunities per se that cause regret, but a particular type of appraisal: one that involves a counterfactual comparison between what happened and what could have happened (van Dijk & Zeelenberg, 2002). Notice that the second hypothesis is much harder to test. To establish the causal role of counterfactual appraisals in the experience of regret, the ideal experiment would randomly assign people to either think counterfactually or not while keeping the situation constant. Yet we cannot reach into a participant's brain and turn their "appraisal knobs" up or down, and we know that simply instructing people not to think counterfactually would be about as effective as instructing them not to think about a white bear (see Wegner & Schneider, 2003).

We can, of course, instruct some participants to think counterfactually and compare them with participants who receive no such instruction. Such designs do support causal inference, but they are imperfect, since the instruction offers only coarse control over how strongly the appraisal is engaged. Similarly, asking people to recall whether they appraised a situation in a certain way, or to explain why they chose one emotion regulation strategy over another, can yield biased or confabulated responses, particularly when people are asked about the causes of a specific reaction (Nisbett & Wilson, 1977; see also Ericsson & Simon, 1980). Self-reports remain indispensable, of course, but they are best treated as one source of evidence among several. Over the years, affective scientists have developed an impressive arsenal of clever experimental COMPUTATIONAL AFFECTIVE SCIENCE designs, neuroimaging methods, and ecological assessments to make headway on exactly these kinds of problems. Computational modeling offers a complementary path forward. Just as meteorologists build artificial tornadoes and dial the artificial humidity knobs, we can build artificial participants and directly manipulate their appraisals, beliefs, goals, and much more.

Computational modeling has already been brought to bear on a remarkable range of affective phenomena. Why do we reach for a funny video when we feel down (Gong et al., 2023)? How exactly does a sharp pang of regret spur us to make better choices in the future (Petrova et al., 2025)? How do people learn to label their own emotions (Smith et al., 2019)?

Why does our memory feel sharper when we are in a good mood (S. Park & Myung, 2014)? And why does an unexpected reward feel disproportionately good (Neville et al., 2021)? Each of these now has at least one computational model that turns a verbal "why" into an algorithmic "how." Other advances have been both more gradual and more cumulative. In work on social emotion inference and emotion regulation, computational approaches are increasingly providing shared formal frameworks that promise to unify disparate findings and reveal common underlying mechanisms. unobservable goals and beliefs give rise to observable actions, which the observer then uses to update their inferences (Baker et al., 2009; Cushman, 2024). By treating emotions as additional latent variables in this causal chain, a growing number of researchers have leveraged the same probabilistic machinery to understand how people predict, infer, and, eventually, intervene on others' affective states.

Social Affective

Foundational work in social affective cognition established that laypeople possess intuitive "theories of emotion" akin to naïve physics: just as one expects a dropped glass to break, people anticipate specific emotional reactions to arise in response to specific events (e.g., receiving a poor grade elicits sadness; Ong et al., 2015; Skerry & Saxe, 2015). Building on these early insights, a growing body of work has formalized social emotion inference within Bayesian theory-of-mind frameworks. Saxe and Houlihan (2017) argued that emotion concepts can be formalized within a Bayesian theory of mind, in which observers reason about how a target appraises events relative to their goals, beliefs, and values, and invert this generative model to infer emotions from observable expressions and actions (e.g., facial movements; self-reports).

Further elaborating these ideas, Houlihan et al. (2023) demonstrated that emotion prediction can be understood as probabilistic computation over a generative theory-of-mind model: observers use inverse planning to infer a target's beliefs and preferences from their actions, compute appraisals of how outcomes relate to those mental states, and map these appraisals onto distinct emotions. Notably, inferred monetary preferences alone could not account for observers' predictions, with inferred social preferences contributing to predictions of nearly every emotion.

Lay theories are now thought to guide emotion inference more powerfully than surfacelevel cues (such as facial or vocal expressions) alone (Goel et al., 2024; Saxe & Houlihan, 2017).

Several lines of work have focused on understanding how perceptual cues are integrated into 22 COMPUTATIONAL AFFECTIVE SCIENCE Bayesian models of social affective cognition. These approaches demonstrate that low-level facial feature dynamics feed into higher-level emotion representations (Han et al., 2024; Vitale et al., 2014). At the same time, converging evidence suggests that contextual information can exert as strong or a stronger influence on emotion inference than facial signals, which are often ambiguous in isolation (Aviezer et al., 2012; Goel et al., 2024). How these cues are weighed and combined also appears to depend on the task used to elicit emotion judgments (Anzellotti et al., 2021). These findings refine the traditional view that emotion recognition relies primarily on bottom-up decoding of facial or vocal signals (Wallbott, 1998).

Importantly, just as emotions continuously change and evolve in rich naturalistic contexts, emotional inferences themselves are not static. Instead, they are continuously updated as new contextual information becomes available, supporting a dynamic, probabilistic understanding of others' affective experiences (Ong et al., 2015 (Ong et al., , 2019)). Such a dynamic framework also makes it possible to move beyond passive inference and to model how agents can actively intervene upon others' affective states: people choose interventions on others' emotions by simulating their effects over an intuitive theory of emotion (Chen et al., 2024), a first step toward formalizing interpersonal emotion regulation as it unfolds in social interaction.

Taken together, this research program illustrates a powerful synergy between cognitive theories and computational modeling. The models reviewed here begin to bridge traditionally separate domains-emotion perception, theory of mind, social learning, and interpersonal emotion regulation-within a common computational language centered on dynamic belief updating and action selection.

Emotion Regulation. How do people choose which emotion regulation strategies to use at any given moment? In one example of a recently developed model, Petter et al. (2025) aimed COMPUTATIONAL AFFECTIVE SCIENCE to offer a mechanistic explanation for the robust effect of emotional intensity on regulatory strategy choice: the finding that people tend to favor reappraisal (an emotion regulation strategy that involves changing one's interpretation of the situation in order to change its emotional impact, McRae et al., 2012) at low stimulus intensities and distraction at high intensities (Matthews et al., 2021; Sheppes et al., 2011). The proposed model formalizes the idea that a successful reappraisal might be more effortful and time-consuming to generate for stimuli with high emotional intensity (because such stimuli have fewer affordances for reappraisal, Suri et al., 2018), keeping people in a heightened emotional state for longer periods of time. In contrast, distraction is stimulus-independent and should take more-or-less the same amount of time to implement regardless of the stimulus. The emerging hypothesis, then, is that opting for distraction leads to less overall time spent in a highly negative emotional state, making it a more desirable option in high-intensity situations.

Model simulations show that when this cost-benefit trade-off is built into the decisionmaking process of simulated participants, they spontaneously replicate the empirical pattern, preferring reappraisal at low intensities and distraction at high (Petter et al., 2025). Instead of being hard-coded into the model, the preference for distraction at high intensity emerges from the hypothesized trade-off between how long each strategy takes to work and how much time is then spent in a highly negative state. Because the model makes these assumptions explicit, alternative accounts (e.g., that distraction is preferred at high intensity simply because it is less cognitively demanding) can be formalized and compared against it on the same footing.

Importantly, this is not a result the model produces in a vacuum: it depends on decades of empirical work. Building on that foundation, the model yields a concrete mechanistic hypothesis that can be tested empirically by manipulating the time available to regulate-an experimental design that would be difficult to motivate without the structure that the model provides.

Extending the horizon from isolated instances of emotion regulation to lifelong learning, other models simulate how virtual agents learn which emotion regulation strategies are most effective in a given environment, depending on past outcomes and stable personality traits (Abro et al., 2015; Bosse et al., 2010). By instantiating the regulation process in a simulated environment with changing affective demands, these models provide insight into how agents balance short-and long-term benefits of different strategies. These models, too, yield concrete and testable predictions-such as which trait profiles should gravitate toward distraction in unpredictable environments, or how strategy repertoires should change with feedback-that can be evaluated in laboratory tasks and longitudinal designs.

Each model reviewed here formalizes the same core architecture: an agent in a dynamic environment, who has access to a limited repertoire of regulatory strategies; incurs costs for deploying those strategies; and must balance immediate emotional relief against longer-term stability and adaptation. This shared architecture, along with the shared formal language that speaks of value, dynamics, and learning, puts these models on a common footing, allowing insights to accumulate across models and levels of analyses.

Applied Models

The work reviewed so far seeks to understand affective phenomena for their own sake.

Applied models are defined by aims that extend beyond scientific understanding: they are built to accomplish goals in the real world, whether by detecting and measuring affect, intervening on it, or engineering systems that produce affective behavior of their own. Two areas are especially relevant to the emerging field of computational affective science: computational psychiatry, COMPUTATIONAL AFFECTIVE SCIENCE which formalizes affective dysfunction in order to predict and treat it, and affective computing, which builds emotion-aware algorithms and agents. Unlike the models reviewed in the previous section, which focused on explaining specific affective phenomena, the work reviewed here is organized around broader domains that use computational models to not only understand the affective mind but to achieve practical goals.

Computational Psychiatry.

Affect dysregulation lies at the heart of many psychiatric conditions, from depression and anxiety to schizophrenia and personality disorders (Aldao et al., 2010; Beck, 1971; Gross & Jazaieri, 2014). The emerging field of computational psychiatrywhich aims to formalize the mechanisms underlying mental illness-offers a particularly rich and relevant context for computational affective science. The goal of computational psychiatry is not only to understand affect, but to clarify its dysfunction, predict symptom trajectories, and, ultimately, improve treatment outcomes. Individual papers in this literature also vary in how close they are to the clinic, with some offering primarily theoretical accounts of a disorder and others developing tools for treatment selection. Given the breadth of this literature, what follows is a selective review highlighting advances that are most relevant for computational affective scientists.

One prominent area of work models specific symptoms that manifest as breakdowns in emotion generation or regulation processes. For example, reinforcement learning (RL) has been widely applied to characterize the mechanisms behind rumination (Bedder et al., 2024), dysregulation of effort-reward trade-offs in depression (Berwian et al., 2020), and anhedonia (Chase et al., 2010). These models have allowed researchers to isolate precise mechanisms through which various RL parameters (e.g., learning rates, value updating) can contribute to the development and maintenance of depression symptoms (for a review, see Chen et al., 2015).

Crucially, these models make direct contact with the basic phenomena reviewed earlier.

Anhedonia, for instance, can be captured as a downward shift in a single reward-sensitivity parameter, so that the same value-learning machinery used to explain ordinary reward responses becomes a model of their blunting (Chen et al., 2015).

Extending beyond depression, the computational toolbox has been applied to the study of other conditions (e.g., schizophrenia, Barch et al., 2024; ADHD, Ziegler et al., 2016). In anxiety disorders, for instance, researchers have explored how different types of models (e.g., RL vs.

Bayesian frameworks) might explain dysregulated threat responses (Yamamori & Robinson, 2023). Relatedly, Robinaugh et al. (2024) introduced a network model of panic disorder, offering a mechanistic account for how symptoms like heart palpitations and catastrophizing can spiral out of control in a self-reinforcing loop. Importantly, none of this work replaces the careful clinical observation and theory development that identified these symptoms as worth modeling in the first place; instead, it builds on those foundations. What these models share is the demonstration that psychiatric symptoms leave distinctive mathematical fingerprints that point to specific processes that have gone off track and where interventions can be fruitfully focused.

A second line of work uses modeling to guide intervention directly, a goal that has gained significant traction in the 2020s (for a review, see Yip et al., 2023). For example, the large-scale AID-ME study used machine learning to match patients with antidepressants, aiming to reduce the costly trial-and-error approach that dominates current practice (Benrimoh et al., 2025a). In a subsequent trial, patients whose clinicians had access to the decision-support tool were more likely to reach remission than patients in an active control condition (Benrimoh et al., 2025b). In another inspiring line of work, Wang et al. (2023 Wang et al. ( , 2024) ) developed a series of personalized prediction algorithms to forecast suicidal ideation with an impressive degree of accuracy, COMPUTATIONAL AFFECTIVE SCIENCE opening the door to scalable interventions that would not be possible without this computational work. Model-based measures have also begun to show prognostic value. Among patients in remission from depression, for example, parameters of a drift-diffusion model capturing sensitivity to anticipated effort predicted who would relapse after discontinuing antidepressants, information that could eventually help clinicians decide when it is safe to stop medication (Berwian et al., 2020).

A third line of work within computational psychiatry widens the viewing angle from isolated symptoms to a contextualized perspective on how they emerge, interact, and evolve over the course of an individual's life. For example, some models aim not only to explain moment-tomoment symptoms, but to capture how personality traits can shape symptom trajectories over time (Sharp et al., 2020). Other models extend this line of thinking to demonstrate how systemslevel interactions between psychopathology symptoms and physiological processes can be mapped and simulated using network models (Fried, 2020; Haslbeck et al., 2021). Finally, newer models are increasingly drawing on basic work in computational affective science (e.g., Rutledge et al., 2014) to formalize how the subjective experience associated with psychiatric symptoms may emerge from quantifiable parameters such as prediction errors and counterfactual reasoning (Kao et al., 2023).

Across these three lines of work-modeling symptoms, guiding treatment, and tracing trajectories-computational modeling is beginning to enrich clinical psychology and psychiatry by building on established clinical insight. For affective scientists, this work offers not only methodological tools but a reminder that understanding emotion in full requires engaging with its dysfunction; and just as computational psychiatry stands to benefit from basic affective science, affective science may find some of its most urgent applications in the clinic.

Affective Computing.

From affective robots, to emotion-detection software, to mentalhealth chatbots, affective computing has blossomed in recent years. Its primary goal has historically focused on the development of useful applications; engineering systems that can themselves recognize and express emotion, and, ultimately, engage in motivated behavior (Picard, 1997). A common belief in engineering, bringing us back to Richard Feynman, is that truly understanding something means being able to build it. In practice, though, the arrow points in both directions, with deeper understanding emerging as a by-product of trying to make a system work. In this section, I highlight several ways affective computing has begun to generate such understanding.

Emotion detection from facial expressions, natural language, and physiology has long been of interest to engineers and researchers in the field of affective computing. Many of the models in this space are so-called performance-driven models, that is, models with no expectation of interpretability but instead designed with the goal of optimizing performance (e.g., D'Mello et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2020; Martínez & Yannakakis, 2010; Shukla et al., 2017).

Yet even these relatively opaque models raise foundational questions: What is the ground truth (i.e., the standard against which a system's judgments are scored as correct) for emotion recognition? Should we privilege self-report or physiological signals when they conflict? Such tensions echo debates about response-system coherence (Brown et al., 2019; Petrova et al., 2021; Sommerfeldt et al., 2019) and underscore the need for more fundamental work in this spacework that affective scientists, with their deep familiarity with these debates, are uniquely well positioned to contribute.

A number of theoretically grounded algorithms for emotion recognition have also emerged in recent years (e.g., Han et al., 2024; Picard et all., 2001; Salgado & Clempner, 2018; 29 COMPUTATIONAL AFFECTIVE SCIENCE Vitale et al., 2014). For example, an algorithm inspired by the hypothesis that facial emotion recognition operates via simulation (whereby individuals simulate others' facial expressions to infer latent emotional states that gave rise to those expressions; Adolphs, 2006) offers a fertile ground for affective scientists to test mechanistic hypotheses about the potential roles of the sensory-motor system in emotion perception (Vitale et al., 2014). In another recent example, Han et al. (2024) drew on the Bayesian Cue Integration (BCI) framework in social affective cognition (Ong et al., 2015) to develop a context-based emotion recognition system. The findings show that a BCI-inspired algorithm not only mirrors human performance but outperforms alternative decontextualized algorithms, lending support to the claim that humans integrate contextual cues with facial information in an approximately Bayesian fashion. These are just two examples of fully specified, mechanistically distinct models-exactly the kind that complement existing theoretical work in affective science. Yet, because research in affective computing is largely driven by incentives to optimize performance, many of these models have not been explicitly pitted against one another to evaluate their theoretical commitments. This leaves a wealth of theoretical insights unexplored. Luckily, this is precisely the kind of opportunity that the emerging field of computational affective science will be well positioned to seize.

Another major goal in affective computing is to build affective agents that not only recognize but also express emotion in socially appropriate ways. Here too, some approaches are atheoretical pattern-learners, but many are grounded in emotion theory (for a review, see Ojha et al., 2021). For example, Barriga et al. (2012) draw inspiration from both theoretical and empirical work in affective (neuro)science to design a system capable of modulating its attention in response to emotionally-salient stimuli. Though the authors' main focus is on advancing applications in AI and human-computer interaction (HCI), the underlying model doubles as an COMPUTATIONAL AFFECTIVE SCIENCE excellent formal framework for studying the broaden-and-build effect of positive emotions on attention deployment (Fredrickson & Branigan, 2005).

Beyond their practical utility, affective agents can also serve as "model organisms" for affective scientists. Even if the internal mechanisms by which these agents generate affective outputs are not isomorphic to those of biological agents (i.e., the tightness of the link between the hypothesized process and the algorithmic form it takes is low), they can still serve as useful tools for basic science. This logic is familiar from other areas of computational science: artificial neural networks are not literal models of the brain, yet, as we saw earlier, lesioning them has yielded important insights into neuropsychological phenomena. Similarly, language models likely do not reason like humans do, yet recent years have seen a surge of work treating large language models (LLMs) as proxies for exploring a myriad of cognitive and linguistic phenomena (e.g., Binz & Schulz, 2023; Kosinski, 2024; McCoy et al., 2024; Prystawski et al., 2023; Ullman, 2023). The same logic can be extended to the study of affect and motivation: we can manipulate the "wiring" of artificial agents and compare their behavior to human data.

Discrepancies can point to mis-specified mechanisms in the artificial models or to unexamined assumptions in existing theories, inspiring new hypotheses amenable to novel empirical and computational work.

In closing this section, even a cursory overview of models in affective computing makes it clear that there is a growing need for more cross-talk among engineers, computer scientists, and affective scientists. Application-oriented models can almost certainly be improved with theoretical input and lessons from decades of empirical work. Likewise, affective scientists can benefit from leveraging existing applications as in-silico laboratories-probing their errors, stress-testing their generalization, and drawing inspiration for new formal frameworks.

Theoretical Models

The specific models and real-world applications surveyed in the preceding sections are the raw bricks of science, but bricks alone do not make a house. The third and final domain of computational affective science is also its most ambitious: it seeks to move beyond isolated phenomena and applications toward comprehensive, formal theories of affect-and, in doing so, to sharpen our answers to the most fundamental questions the field has been asking all along.

Over time, scientists integrate countless isolated empirical observations (and, increasingly, formal models) into broader theories that aim to explain affective life writ large. Verbal theories continue to do much of this heavy lifting in affective science. They have generated decades of testable predictions, organized vast swaths of empirical findings, and offered rich accounts of why people do what they do and feel what they feel. Translating these theories into explicit algorithms adds a layer of precision, sharpening the theories' predictions and placing competing accounts on equal footing.

What distinguishes theoretical work from the more targeted modeling reviewed above is the ambition of theoretical models to synthesize not only across phenomena, but across levels of analysis. The theories discussed next live mostly at Marr's computational and algorithmic levels (Marr, 1982; see also Griffiths et al., 2015), which describe, respectively, what problem a system is solving and what procedure it uses to solve it. Computational affective neuroscience then supplies the implementational level, which describes how the phenomenon under study is realized in the brain. Of the three broad domains in this review (Models of Specific Phenomena, Applied Models, and Theoretical Models), theoretical modeling has been the least cumulative.

The reason for this is not that the work that has been done is weaker, but that synthesizing across phenomena, disciplines, modeling approaches, and levels of analysis is genuinely hard. What follows is, therefore, best viewed as a map of promising avenues.

Computational and Algorithmic Level Models.

One of the deepest questions in affective science concerns why we feel the way we feel. Verbal theories have offered a range of possible answers that agree on many observations yet diverge on the mechanisms behind them.

More recently, computational work has begun to translate these answers into the language of equations and algorithms.

In 1884, William James famously asked, "What is an emotion?" (James, 1884 (James, /1948)-a question that has since animated almost a century and a half of debate. The 21st-century answer is not a single unified account, but an increasingly formalized landscape of complementary perspectives. At the verbal level, three families of theories set the conceptual stage. Basic-Emotions Theories posit a small set of evolutionarily conserved and biologically hard-wired emotions (e.g., anger, fear, joy; Ekman, 1992; see also Ortony, 2022). Appraisal Theories argue that emotions arise from evaluations (i.e., appraisals) of events along dimensions such as goalrelevance, goal-congruence, responsibility, and others (Lazarus, 1991; Scherer, 1999). Finally, Constructionist Theories contend that emotions are ad-hoc constructions built from the dimensions of core affect (e.g., valence, arousal) and conceptual knowledge shaped by the social and cultural environments we inhabit (Barrett & Lida, 2025; Mesquita & Parkinson, 2025).

Computational work has begun to formalize each of these theoretical perspectives, resulting in sharper questions and opening the door to more comparable models.

Within Basic-Emotions theories, several models have been proposed in which systemwide emotion "modes" are triggered by rule-based detectors (e.g., Sun et al., 2016). By contrast, COMPUTATIONAL AFFECTIVE SCIENCE other models have focused on embedding dedicated modules for specific emotions inside a distributed connectionist system (e.g., Barriga et al., 2012). Rather than switching between discrete modes, the network continuously modulates visual attention toward threat cues whenever activity exceeds a threshold. Comparing these models clarifies what "hard-wired" can mean in practice-from all-or-none global state transitions to implementations within a localized circuitry.

Appraisal theories disagree on a number of important questions, including the causal status of appraisal in emotion; the number of appraisal dimensions that exist; as well as the functional form by which those dimensions interact (i.e., additive, multiplicative, threshold, etc.; Moors, 2014). To see what is at stake, consider a simple appraisal rule in the spirit of Lazarus (1991): if an event is appraised as relevant to one's goals, as obstructing those goals, and as caused by another person who could have acted otherwise, then anger results. Written this way, the theory already resembles an algorithm, but it leaves open whether all three conditions must be met (a threshold rule), whether each contributes independently to the intensity of anger (an additive rule), or whether they amplify one another (a multiplicative rule). Each answer yields different predictions about when, and how strongly, people become angry. In addressing these disputes, many appraisal models adopt a rule-based approach, introducing if-then links between appraisals and discrete emotions. These rules can be encoded in symbolic architectures, where they operate deterministically (the condition "desirable and high agency" always triggers pride; Castellanos et al., 2019; Ortony et al., 1988) or probabilistically (i.e., returning a graded likelihood of "pride" given the input; Gluz & Jaques, 2017).

Going a step further, process-oriented models of appraisal shift the focus from "which appraisals trigger which emotions" to "how the triggering variables themselves are computed and updated over time." This can involve "zooming in" to model and empirically test how specific appraisals (e.g., goal-congruence, Hesp et al., 2021; progress, Voodla et al., 2024) can dynamically arise from perceptual data, or take a bird's-eye view to model the general processes through which appraisal dimensions interact to give rise to discrete emotions (e.g., Meuleman & Scherer, 2013). One longstanding puzzle in appraisal theory has been the seemingly everproliferating list of proposed dimensions needed to explain the complexity of human emotions (Yeo & Ong, 2024). Computational modeling helps reframe the issue by testing whether the perceived complexity of the appraisal space might stem from conflating distinct types of computations-such as reward-tracking vs. pleasantness-under the single umbrella of "appraisal" (Emanuel & Eldar, 2023). Disentangling these mechanisms in a principled way could shed light on the inner workings of emotion generation and open the door to more parsimonious models of appraisal.

Finally, constructionist views treat emotion as emerging from the same domain-general processes that support the rest of cognition, rather than from a dedicated, emotion-specific system-an appealing premise that raises the bar for computational modeling, requiring that a robust domain-general cognitive architecture be specified before emotions can "emerge." Several promising efforts have begun to meet this challenge. Some efforts directly embed affect into existing cognitive architectures (Bosse et al., 2008; Juvina et al., 2018). Context-sensitive approaches, inspired by the idea that meaning arises from situated inference (Barrett, 2022), simulate emotions as by-products of continuous interactions among perception, interoception, and environmental affordances (Cai et al., 2012 (Cai et al., , 2013)). Further pushing the socialconstructionist angle, affective Ising networks borrow a classic model from statistical physics to demonstrate how simple valence units can self-organize into stable higher-order emotions when COMPUTATIONAL AFFECTIVE SCIENCE constrained by existing knowledge (Loossens et al., 2020). By embedding affect within largerscale cognitive architectures, these and similar models are uniquely positioned to generate predictions about downstream cognitive processes, linking constructionist ideas to broader computational cognitive science. This point matters well beyond debates about the nature of emotion. If affect emerges from, and feeds back into, the same processes that support perception, attention, memory, and decision-making, then cognitive scientists cannot fully model those processes while leaving affect out.

Taken together, these efforts show how formalization can sharpen theoretical debates and help uncover areas where seeming disagreements may arise from a difference in emphasis or level of analysis. The following section takes up the question that these models leave largely unanswered: how are such computations realized at the neural level?

Implementational Level Models. While computational affective science aims to formalize the mechanisms of motivation and emotion at the computational and algorithmic levels, computational affective neuroscience seeks to ground these mechanisms in the brain. Its cross-level insights inform computational modeling in basic affective science, highlighting what is neurally plausible and which constructs are reflected in brain activity. Perhaps the most valuable contribution of neuroscience to computational affective science, then, is constraint: testing whether the mechanisms posited at the computational and algorithmic levels can be implemented in the brain, and understanding how interacting brain systems give rise to affect.

The dominant approach in affective neuroscience has traditionally relied on brain mapping: identifying which brain regions respond to which emotional states or stimuli. This work has been enormously productive in cataloguing where in the brain affective processes show their fingerprints. At the same time, several scholars have argued that brain mapping, while a powerful tool for localization, is less well suited to characterizing how the affective phenomena we observe at the computational and algorithmic levels are implemented in the brain (Niv, 2021; Uttal, 2001; Wixted & Mickes, 2013). Offering a complementary path forward, computational affective neuroscience adds a focus on brain modeling-asking what patterns of brain activity can tell us about the underlying mechanisms (Kragel & Wager, 2019). Specifically, predictive models of affect in the brain can offer useful insight into whether the constructs posited at higher levels of analysis (e.g., discrete emotions) are instantiated in distinct neural signatures. One example comes from work suggesting that subjective affective experience may be generated by a process distinct from that which represents value, opening the door to a novel computational model of affect-informed decision-making (Roberts et al., 2024). In contrast, a formalization of momentary happiness as a process that calculates the weighted sum of recent reward-prediction errors has been shown to track striatal activity, providing a unified bridge across levels of analysis (Rutledge et al., 2014).

A large part of computational modeling in affective neuroscience relies on powerful machine learning algorithms. Machine learning, which entails training algorithms to detect statistical regularities in large datasets, has often been criticized as a black-box approach that can achieve high predictive accuracy yet offer little mechanistic insight (Rudin, 2019). Yet recent advances in computational affective neuroscience show that machine learning can be usefully leveraged for scientific discovery and theory building. By pairing cross-validated encoding/decoding frameworks with tools that can advance interpretability (e.g., featureimportance mapping, representational similarity analysis), affective neuroscientists have begun to translate distributed activation patterns into concrete hypotheses. For example, the sharedrepresentations hypothesis proposes that witnessing another person's pain engages parts of the same circuitry as first-hand nociception, offering a compelling account of empathy (Preston & De Waal, 2002). Traditional brain-mapping studies, which consistently report partially overlapping activation in anterior insula and mid-cingulate cortex for both types of "pain," have been cited extensively as evidence for this view (Singer et al., 2004). However, multivariate predictive modeling reveals a more nuanced picture. A neurologic pain signature (NPS) derived from first-hand pain does not generalize to empathic pain, while a distinct vicarious pain signature (VPS) predicts others' suffering yet remains insensitive to direct nociception (Kragel & Wager, 2019; Krishnan et al., 2016; Wager et al., 2013). Importantly, the goal here was not to overturn the shared-representations hypothesis but to refine it; the functional dissociation of the two signatures sets a higher bar for future theoretical and computational models of empathy while preserving the empirical work that motivated the original account.

Not all computational affective neuroscience models are data-driven. A growing body of work also seeks to integrate psychological theory and neurobiology through more structured frameworks. For example, Bayesian models have been used to characterize how categoryspecific emotional brain responses vary across individuals and contexts (McVeigh et al., 2024; Wager et al., 2015). This is where computational affective science and neuroscience are poised to form a particularly virtuous cycle. Mathematically explicit psychological theories can supply the priors that would guide neural modeling, while carefully crafted and validated neural models can return with posterior estimates and boundary conditions for further refinement of the same theories.

All in all, progress in formalizing broad theories of affect and motivation has been gradual, in part because different modeling frameworks use different feature sets and evaluation metrics, making apples-to-apples comparisons rare. Framing computational models of emotional theories in a shared mathematical vocabulary would be a meaningful next step for computational affective science. Theory-agnostic frameworks (e.g., Suri & Gross, 2022; Zhang et al., 2023) have begun to emerge, offering a provisional way of treating differing theoretical perspectives as special cases within a richer computational landscape. But the deepest synthesis that theory can offer is across levels: binding computational and algorithmic accounts of what emotion and motivation are to implementational accounts of how the brain makes them. Realizing it will require more affective scientists fluent in formal modeling, and more modelers willing to engage deeply with emotion theory and neurobiology-the two-way fluency through which today's rich library of verbal theories might finally grow into an integrated science of affect and cognition.

An Agenda for the Emerging Field of Computational Affective Science

From Charles Darwin sketching the facial expressions of emotion in humans and animals to William James meticulously cataloguing bodily feelings, affective science began as an observational enterprise. Over the century and a half that followed, it matured into a rigorous discipline devoted to understanding the forces that energize and direct behavior. The argument of this paper is that advances in understanding motivated behavior will require a shared formal language that integrates insights from affective and cognitive science. As we saw from examples from meteorology and cognitive science, the scientific gains of such a shift are substantial.

Formal models can sharpen verbal theories, adjudicate among competing mechanisms, generate novel predictions, and build bridges across levels of analysis. They also provide a shared language that can help dissolve the longstanding divide between affect and cognition, allowing researchers who study emotion, motivation, learning, memory, and decision-making to jointly contribute to a more integrated science of behavior. But the case for embracing computational affective science goes far beyond the scientific arguments reviewed here. So, before considering how the field might go about taking this next step, it is worth zooming out to consider what computational affective science could make possible for the world beyond the ivory tower.

What Stands to be Gained?

Many of the gravest challenges of our time-intergroup conflict, political polarization, climate change-are, at their core, problems of human emotion and motivation. We have long known that explanatory depth is the gateway to real-world impact (Aristotle, ca. 350 B.C.E/1984; Machamer et al., 2000). Clearer causal models of affect can offer clearer understanding of why people act as they do and point to promising levers for designing more effective interventions. The stakes are equally high for technology. Today's artificial intelligence is extraordinarily capable, but only because we hand it its objectives; unlike humans and other animals, it has no intrinsic drive to explore and set goals of its own. Building agents that do has been called the next frontier on the road to artificial general intelligence (Colas et al., 2022). A formal account of how motivated behavior emerges and becomes elaborated into the complex architecture of emotions, values, and goals is exactly what the extant work on motivated AI lacks, and exactly what computational affective science could supply, along with the ethical guardrails any artificial motivated system will need. In each of these arenas, the road to deeper causal understanding runs through formalization.

Why Now?

Today, computational models are so commonplace in cognitive science that one can hardly open a journal without encountering one. Other branches of psychology, including developmental (Shultz & Sirois, 2008), social (Cushman, 2024), and clinical (Montague et al., 2012), are increasingly following suit. If the case for embracing computational affective science is so compelling, why has the field of affective science been slower to develop a cumulative computational tradition than its neighbors? One common explanation is that affect is simply harder to formalize than cognition-that it is too subjective, too difficult to measure, too volatile (see Neisser, 1963; Simon, 1967). I do not believe this to be the case. This intuition rests on the historical centrality of subjective experience in affective science. But one does not need to look too far to see that the study of what has traditionally been relegated to the realms of cognition is no less reliant on measuring subjective experience than the study of motivation and emotion is. Cognitive scientists routinely ask participants to reflect on their deeply held beliefs, rate subjective confidence in a decision, say whether a word feels familiar, and even describe their reasoning process out loud as it unfolds (Newell & Simon, 1961). These judgments have been the bedrock of formal theories for decades; and if such subjective data can support rigorous computational models of cognition, there is no principled reason why they cannot do the same for affective science.

Instead, the lag might be better understood as an artifact of how the two fields developed.

Affective science is comparatively young and unusually interdisciplinary, drawing on psychology, neuroscience, physiology, philosophy, anthropology, sociology, and clinical practice. Although this breadth is indisputably a source of richness, it has also likely contributed to the field's fragmentation. Much of modern affective science grew out of clinical psychology, where the immediate priorities were diagnosis and treatment rather than formal mechanistic explanation. Several developments are now changing this picture. Advances in artificial intelligence have made questions of motivation and emotion newly urgent well beyond psychology. Within cognitive science, too, affect is moving from the margins of models toward their core, with models treating it as both emerging from the system's computations and shaping its subsequent behavior (e.g., Cervantes et al., 2017; Dubey et al., 2022; Eldar et al., 2016; Juvina et al., 2018). Importantly, the evidence increasingly suggests that this move is not only elegant but necessary: building affect into cognitive models as a core component improves their predictions even in traditionally cognitive domains such as perception, learning, and memory (Stussi et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2016). Taken together, these developments make this an especially opportune moment. Amid what has been called the era of affectivism (Dukes et al., 2021), affective science now has both the rigorous foundations and the momentum it needs to take its own computational turn that will play a key role in shaping the next chapter in the science of the mind and behavior.

Where Do We Go From Here?

Across the areas reviewed in this paper, a common lesson emerges: translating hypotheses into computational terms accelerates both theoretical progress and practical application. Models of specific phenomena sharpen theories; theories guide applications; and applications generate new phenomena that demand explanation. Realizing the full potential of this virtuous cycle over the coming decade and beyond requires making progress on three fronts.

Training And Collaboration.

A basic fluency in formal and quantitative methods is broadly useful across the behavioral and cognitive sciences, which is why it is essential to cultivate it more deliberately. Statistical and computational coursework in graduate education often lags behind the demands of contemporary research (Howard, 2024), and even graduate students trained within the same department may receive strikingly different methodological training depending on whether they are socialized into affective, cognitive, clinical, or neuroscience traditions. Importantly, the goal is not for every affective scientist to become a mathematician. Computational modeling is, first and foremost, a way of thinking, and adopting COMPUTATIONAL AFFECTIVE SCIENCE that mindset can sharpen a verbal theory long before any code is written. General literacy in computational modeling methods-enough to read a model critically, grasp what it does and does not commit to, and collaborate productively with computational modelers from cognitive science, computer science, engineering, neuroscience, and economics-is a much more productive and attainable target. Above all, we need collaboration. Global climate models were not built by the hand of a lone meteorologist. Instead, the discipline leapt forward when physicists, mathematicians, and instrument-makers rallied around a shared problem. Affective scientists, cognitive scientists, and modelers across disciplines can, and should, do the same.

Evaluation And Benchmarking.

Whether modeling the weather or the mind, once a computational model is specified, two questions emerge: how do we know if the model is any good, and what can we learn from it? Philosophers of science have long noted that a model's clarity often comes at the steep cost of simplifications and idealizations, both of which are indispensable for understanding complex systems (Winsberg, 2010). The relevant question, therefore, is not whether a model is realistic in every respect, but whether it captures the features of a phenomenon that matter for making progress on the scientific question at hand. In practice, a model earns credibility through a combination of theoretical coherence, empirical adequacy, and its ability to generate accurate out-of-sample predictions across contexts (Yarkoni & Westfall, 2017). Meeting this standard as a field, rather than one lab at a time, will require shared infrastructure. As things currently stand, models of the same phenomena are often tested on different tasks, measures, and populations, which makes it nearly impossible to tell whether two models disagree because their mechanisms differ or because their data do. Adopting common, multimodal benchmark datasets and open modeling architectures would make head-to-head comparison of models from different traditions the norm rather than the exception (Carvalho & COMPUTATIONAL AFFECTIVE SCIENCE Lampinen, 2026). Investments of this kind have already paid off in computer vision, natural language processing, and climate science (e.g., Deng, 2009; Eyring et al., 2016). Computational affective science must follow suit.

Description And Measurement.

The final priority that I would like to emphasize is more careful description of the phenomena under study. In Marr's (1982) framework, the computational level is itself a description, a precise statement of the problem a system solves. A field becomes ripe for formal modeling only when its descriptive and theoretical foundations are sufficiently mature to support it. Darwin did not develop a theory of evolution in a vacuum; he stood on the shoulders of naturalists like Linnaeus, whose painstaking descriptive work came first. Computational affective science will require a similarly rigorous descriptive tradition. Importantly, by "description" I do not only mean rich qualitative accounts. Indeed, some of the most consequential descriptive work in cognitive science is rigorously quantitative. For example, the classic finding that people are reliably faster to verify that "a canary has wings" than that "a canary has skin" (Collins & Quillian, 1969) is, at its core, descriptive in nature. By describing regularities in response times, this work set the agenda for a generation of formal models of conceptual knowledge. In affective science, analogous descriptive work is similarly essential.

In closing, let us once again look to the sky for inspiration-only in this case, not just at the clouds above, but far, far beyond the Earth's atmosphere. In the 20th century, astrophysicists used a model to predict the shape and timing of gravitational waves decades before they were ever observed. Einstein hypothesized that events like collisions of two black holes should send ripples-known as gravitational waves-through the fabric of space (Einstein, 1916 (Einstein, /1996)). Following Einstein's prediction, physicists constructed a precise mathematical model of what those waves should look like if Einstein's theory were correct. Their prediction became a reality in 2015, when LIGO (Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory) detected a signal that matched the predicted waveform almost perfectly (Caltech/MIT/LIGO Lab, 2016), confirming both the existence of gravitational waves and, importantly, lending support to the theoretical model that had anticipated them (Abbott et al., 2016).

Affective science is, of course, not astrophysics. Its subject matter is closer to home, yet the broader lesson still holds. Affective scientists, who have already spent decades building rich conceptual accounts of emotion, are in a position to begin a similar dialogue-aiming, in time, for quantitative forecasts and data capable of confirming or refuting those predictions with LIGO-level precision. This effort will require more than any one discipline can provide. It will require cognitive scientists who can specify mechanisms of learning, attention, memory, and decision-making; neuroscientists who can connect those mechanisms to biological implementation; computer scientists and engineers who can build and test artificial affective systems; clinicians who understand how affect breaks down; and affective scientists who can keep the field anchored in the phenomena that matter most. Only by recognizing these efforts as part of the shared project of Computational Affective Science can we make their contributions add up to more than the sum of their parts: a truly cumulative science of behavior.
