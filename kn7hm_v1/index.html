How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

Anorexia nervosa (AN) is a serious eating disorder that can become chronic and potentially life-threatening. Hospitalizations for AN among American youth have increased, making pediatric medical settings an increasingly important part of the treatment continuum 1,2. When youth with AN are hospitalized, the immediate priority is their nutritional rehabilitation and medical stabilization-however, hospitalization also represents an important opportunity to support caregivers (including parents, guardians, and other custodial adults) as they navigate their child's next stages of treatment. Though caregivers play a crucial role in the treatment of children and adolescents with AN, many feel uncertain about how to respond effectively to the illness and doubt their ability to support their child's recovery. 3, 4 In particular, caregivers report difficulties during care transitions from higher to lower levels of care. 5 Though caregivers play a central role in supporting ongoing nutritional rehabilitation and recovery, structured approaches to preparing caregivers for these responsibilities are not routinely integrated into inpatient medical care 6, 7.

Transitions in care often require caregivers to take the lead in responding to their child's eating disorder behaviors and maintaining expectations for meals and exercise. Caregivers of young people with AN frequently report high levels of fear, emotional distress, and burden, which may make it more difficult to respond confidently and consistently to their child's eating disorder-driven behaviors and distress 8, 9 . In an understandable effort to reduce their child's symptoms, manage their own fear, or avoid escalating conflict, caregivers may accommodate the eating disorder by modifying meals, permitting avoidance of feared foods, participating in eating disorder rituals, or reorganizing family routines around symptoms 10 . Although accommodation may reduce distress in the short term, it may also reinforce avoidance and make it more difficult to maintain recovery-oriented behaviors 11.

Caregiver self-efficacy, defined as caregivers' confidence in their ability to support their child's recovery, may be particularly important in navigating these challenges 12 . Greater caregiver self-efficacy has been associated with improved weight-related outcomes in treatment, and as such, is a core target for the majority of multiple evidence-based child and adolescent outpatient eating disorder interventions [13] [14] [15] [16] . One study found that caregiver self-efficacy may remain relatively unchanged during brief medical admissions, highlighting hospitalization as a potential opportunity to strengthen caregivers' confidence before they transition to supporting continued recovery at home 17.

Single-session interventions (SSIs) offer a promising approach to addressing this gap.

SSIs are structured interventions designed to provide a meaningful therapeutic experience within a single encounter, regardless of what supports are accessed before or after 18, 19 . Brief, selfguided, web-based SSIs may be particularly well suited for delivery during medical hospitalization because of their relative low burden, which is crucial given the numerous competing demands during a child's medical crisis.

One such digital SSI is Project EMPOWER (available at https://www.leafbyleaf.org/empower), an approximately 30-minute, web-based, self-administered SSI for caregivers of youth with anxiety symptoms. The intervention teaches parents to recognize accommodating behaviors in their own daily routines, understand how those behaviors can inadvertently sustain their child's anxious avoidance, and guides them to build a personalized plan for reducing these behaviors.

Project EMPOWER has been evaluated in three randomized trials, demonstrating high acceptability, feasibility, and effectiveness among caregivers of diverse socioeconomic status 20- 22 . Project EMPOWER reduced accommodating behaviors (ds = 0.49-0.61) across three trials where this outcome was measured at follow-up, relative to an informational control.

Although Project EMPOWER was designed for caregivers of children struggling with anxiety, the process it targets-caregiver accommodation of their child's disorder-driven behaviors-appears across many forms of psychopathology 11 . Accommodation is especially salient in AN, which can look like buying only low-calorie foods, going along with rigid mealtime rituals, or repeatedly offering reassurance 10 . Reaching caregivers directly through a single-session, self-administered format could extend support to many caregivers around eliminating accommodation. As such, an adaptation of Project EMPOWER was developed for caregivers of young patients with AN, called Project EMPOWER-ED. The purpose of this paper is to describe the development, feasibility, and acceptability of Project EMPOWER-ED for a sample of caregivers of patients hospitalized for AN.

Method

Ethics and Research Transparency

Study procedures were approved by the Lurie Children's Hospital Institutional Review Board. Informed consent was obtained for all enrolled families. The data collection and analytic plan was pre-registered on Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/4amf7) and all deviations to the pre-registration are outlined in Supplement 1.

Recruitment and Procedures

Data collection occurred between January 13, 2025 and May 30, 2025. Participants were recruited at a large, urban pediatric hospital in the Midwest. The hospital sees patients ages 7 to 25 years old in need of medical stabilization due to AN, with approximately half of AN-related admissions are covered by Medicaid. Eligible participants were the legal guardians of patients who met the following criteria: (1) patient's primary admission diagnosis was AN, and (2) caregiver ability to read and write in English or Spanish. All study materials were available in both English and Spanish, and participants could decide which language they preferred.

Prior to discharge, caregivers completed a baseline survey and the SSI on either a hospital-provided iPad or via a secure online link. The baseline survey included pre-intervention questionnaires, the SSI, and post-intervention questionnaires. Taken together, the baseline survey and SSI took about an hour to complete. Participants who completed this portion received a $25 gift card.

Measures

Demographics: At pre-intervention, caregivers completed a demographic questionnaire assessing caregiver and patient race/ethnicity, caregiver gender identity, caregiver's relationship to the patient, caregiver's marital status and caretaking responsibilities, caregiver language, caregiver education, caregiver income, suspected family history of an eating disorder, patient's eating disorder treatment history, and patient's age. Additional demographic information was obtained electronic medical record review including patient's healthcare coverage, patient's cooccurring psychiatric conditions, patient's length of medical stabilization admission, and patient's body mass index (BMI) at admission. Feasibility: Feasibility was assessed through intervention enrollment and completion metrics, including the proportion of eligible caregivers who initiated and completed the intervention, completed pre-post assessments, and completed the qualitative feedback questions.

Intervention duration was also recorded.

Acceptability: Acceptability was assessed immediately post-intervention using the 7-item Program Feedback Scale (PFS) designed to evaluate acceptability brief, web-based, self-guided interventions 27 . The PFS was originally used with adolescents and was therefore modified for the current study to target caregivers' perceptions (e.g., replaced the word "kids" with "caregivers" in the item "I think the program would be helpful to other caregivers"), aligning with other research involving caregivers 20 . Items evaluated caregiver enjoyment of the program, understanding of the material, ease of use, engagement during the program, perceived helpfulness for other caregivers, willingness to recommend the program to others, and agreement with the intervention's message. Responses were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater acceptability. An overall acceptability score was calculated by averaging responses across items, with a mean overall score ≥3 indicating that the intervention is acceptable.

Qualitative Feedback: A total of nine open-ended questions were displayed to participants at post-intervention. Participants could respond to these questions about intervention aspects they found helpful, intervention aspects they would change, their perception of how engaging with the intervention may impact their responses to their child's eating disorder behavior, their opinion about optimal timing of the intervention (i.e., when during their child's treatment it would be most helpful), and their opinion about the digital self-guided format.

Caregiver Preparedness: Two investigator-developed items assessed caregiver preparedness at pre-intervention and immediately post-interventions. Caregivers rated their preparedness to (1) help their child manage eating disorder-related distress and (2) tolerate their child's distress related to eating disorder treatment. Responses were provided on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater preparedness.

Intervention

Project EMPOWER-ED was developed through an iterative, stakeholder-engaged adaptation process involving Project EMPOWER developers and three psychologists with expertise in pediatric eating disorder treatment. These stakeholders reviewed the original intervention and provided detailed feedback regarding its relevance and application to eating disorder care. The intervention developers then elicited additional input through collaborative discussion and iterative content revision, followed by further review and refinement by the clinical-research team. This process preserved the original intervention's focus on avoidance, accommodation, emotion-focused validation, and personalized action planning while adapting these principles to the specific demands of supporting a loved one's eating disorder recovery.

The version of Project EMPOWER-ED evaluated in this pilot study included: (1) psychoeducation about eating disorders, eating disorder-driven avoidance, and caregiver accommodation; (2) strategies for recognizing and responding to eating disorder-driven avoidance while validating distress and maintaining nutritional expectations; (3) interactive vignette to reinforce these concepts in which caregivers identified avoidance, accommodation, and caregiver vulnerabilities; (4) development of a personalized action plan to reduce accommodation and support consistent caregiver responses; and (5) links to optional resources addressing caregiver distress, social support, food insecurity, and access to specialized care.

Analytic Plan

Descriptive Statistics: We calculated demographic factors, as percentages of the total sample, for all applicable demographic indicators collected. We calculated means and standard deviations for patient' age, patients' length of medical stabilization admission, patients' BMI at admission, and caregivers' level of distress tolerance at baseline to further characterize the sample.

To assess feasibility, we calculated out of the total number of invited potential participants, the proportion of participants who (1) began the SSI, (2) completed the SSI, (3) completed the Program Feedback Scale, (4) completed all pre-intervention and postintervention questionnaires, and ( 5) completed the open-ended focus group questions. An additional pattern of intervention use, the mean and standard deviation of time spent completing the SSI, was derived using the interquartile range (IQR) method to exclude page-level outliers more than 1.5 times the IQR below the first quartile or above the third quartile.

To assess intervention acceptability, we calculated the means and standard deviations for the PFS sum score as well as for the individual PFS items. We plotted the PFS sum score of each participant to visualize the distribution of acceptability ratings. These explorations of acceptability were conducted for the overall sample as well as for subgroups stratified by language (English and Spanish), Insurance Coverage (Public Insurance and Private Insurance), and Education Level as dichotomized during analysis (More Education defined as caregivers who reported obtaining a college degree or above and Less Education defined as caregivers who reported educational attainment less than completing college).

While the small sample size and pilot nature of this study precludes claims about intervention effectiveness, we descriptively evaluated pre-to-post change in caregiver preparedness to (1) help their child manage eating disorder-related distress and (2) tolerate their child's distress related to eating disorder treatment. We calculated the mean and standard deviation for each item at pre-intervention and post-intervention and derived the difference scores.

Qualitative Analysis: Two coders conducted inducted content analysis on responses to nine post-SSI open-ended feedback questions 28 . Individually, coders familiarized themselves with the data and generated initial codebooks. The coders then met to compare codebooks and generate a combined codebook. The coders separately used the combined codebook to code each response.

Meetings were held for the coders to resolve discrepancies and refine the definitions included in the codebook. This process was repeated and the combined codebook updated until all responses were coded with consensus.

Results

Sample Characteristics

The caregivers enrolled in the study were primarily biological mothers (80.00%) and endorsed diverse racial and ethnic identities, with 48.00% identifying as white non-Hispanic, 44.00% identifying as Hispanic/Latinx/Latine, and 8.00% identifying as Asian. Participant Enrollment and Intervention Usage

A total of 28 caregivers were identified as eligible by the research team and invited to participate. After learning about the study, 25 (89.29%) consented to participate, initiated and completed the intervention, completed the Program Feedback Scale, and submitted the pre-and post-intervention survey. Most of these caregivers (n=18) completed open-ended feedback questions. Excluding observations more than 1.5 times the interquartile range below the first quartile or above the third quartile, the intervention itself took caregivers about a half hour to complete (M=33.18 minutes; SD=22.13 minutes).

Change in Caregiver Preparedness

On a 5-point scale, caregivers reported a mean 0.48-point (SD=1.01) increase in their preparedness to help their child manage eating disorder-related distress from pre-to-post intervention (pre-intervention M=3.84, SD=1.25; post-intervention M= 4.32, SD= 0.63).

Regarding preparedness to tolerate their child's distress related to eating disorder treatment, caregivers reported a 0.56-point (SD=0.87) increase from pre-to-post intervention (preintervention M= 3.88, SD=0.88; post-intervention M= 4.44, SD= 0.58), indicating that caregivers went from feeling between neutral and somewhat prepared to feeling between somewhat prepared and very prepared, on average.

Intervention Acceptability

Overall, caregivers found the intervention to be highly acceptable, with a total PFS score of 4.02/5 (SD=0.87). When grouped according to selected language, overall mean acceptability was 4.29 (SD=0.53) for the English version and 2.94 (SD=1.15) for the Spanish version.

Caregivers with higher educational attainment reported an overall mean acceptability score of 4.27 (SD=0.48) and caregivers with lower educational attainment reported an overall mean acceptability score of 3.77 (SD=1.16). Stratifying by healthcare insurance type, overall mean acceptability was 4.33 (SD=0.43) for families with private insurance and 3.74 (SD=1.07) for those with public insurance. Qualitative Analysis of Caregiver Feedback

Of the 25 enrolled caregivers in the study, 18 provided open-ended responses.

Theme 1: Facilitators of Positive Change

Caregivers identified several aspects of Project EMPOWER-ED that supported their understanding of their child's eating disorders and their sense of confidence in their ability to respond to their child's eating disorder behaviors.

1) Practical and Relevant Intervention Content.

Most caregivers described the intervention content as practical and relevant to the challenges their families were experiencing.

Caregivers particularly appreciated the scenarios, examples, and recommended language to use in various challenging situations. Caregivers noted that these components of the SSI helped translate treatment principles into concrete strategies they could use with their child.

One caregiver stated, "The examples were relatable and helped me better understand the cycle." Another highlighted the value of receiving "situational advice specific to what we may face." Caregivers also described learning to recognize their child's eating disorderdriven avoidance behaviors and their own accommodation responses, separating their child from the eating disorder, and strategies to maintain firm eating expectations despite eating disorder-related distress. One caregiver shared the program "reinforced that parents need to be strong against the eating disorder, not the child." Another reported that the intervention helped them understand that their child was "not in control" and therefore needed caregiver support in fighting the eating disorder.

2) Validation and Increased Caregiver Confidence. Several caregivers described that the intervention validated their experience, helped them feel more confident in their ability to support their child's treatment, normalized the difficulty of responding consistently when their child is distressed, and reinforced that maintaining nutrition-related expectations is an important form of caregiver support. As one caregiver explained, "It was helpful to learn that her fighting back is normal. That I'm not a bad parent by pushing her to eat." Another stated that the intervention "helped me find the right words to empathize but stay consistent."

Caregivers also noted the intervention reinforced and validated the strategies they were already implementing, with one noting, "It helped me realize that I was doing some of the right things, and I need to change a few things."

3) Digital and Self-Guided Delivery. Caregivers generally viewed the digital, self-guided format favorably. They described the intervention as clear, accessible, and easy to use. The ability to complete the program independently and at an individualized pace was also valued.

One caregiver stated, "I liked that I could do it at my own pace," while another described the presentation of information as "helpful" and "easy to use." Some caregivers indicated that the self-guided format was particularly helpful when combined with discussions and support from the treatment team. As one caregiver noted, "I liked it and combined with live conversations with therapists and psychologists, it's beneficial."

Theme 2: Barriers

Although feedback was generally favorable, caregivers identified challenges related to the intervention's structure, content, and implementation. 2) Content and Tone. Two caregivers identified concerns regarding the relevance or tone of the intervention. One caregiver felt the content was better suited to caregivers of younger children and did not fully reflect the experience of caring for a young adult. Another described the intervention as "a bit accusatory, which isn't necessarily helpful for parents questioning if they are to blame."

3) Timing and Mode of Delivery. Timing emerged as a prominent implementation consideration, with most caregivers who commented on timing indicating that Project EMPOWER-ED would have been more helpful earlier in their child's illness course or at the beginning (rather than the end) of the hospital admission, citing alternative times that the intervention would be helpful including at initial diagnosis, before admission, or shortly after admission. One caregiver recommended providing the intervention at "initial diagnosis because it's not easy or straightforward to identify and combat the behaviors right away." Another recommended receiving it "earlier, maybe when we first got here" so caregivers would understand that maintaining eating expectations was appropriate and supported by the treatment team.

Of note, these timing preferences were not uniform. For example, another caregiver valued completing the intervention close to discharge because it reinforced previously learned information. Others indicated that the program could be useful at multiple points, including both before and after hospitalization. Some caregivers also expressed a preference for receiving the information directly from a clinician or having an opportunity to discuss the content with a treatment provider.

Theme 3: Recommendations for Future Directions

Caregivers offered several recommendations for future iterations of Project EMPOWER-ED. Suggestions included dividing the intervention into shorter sections, simplifying survey measures or the wording, and clearly signaling the transition from the intervention to the postintervention assessment. Caregivers also requested additional scripts for language to use during difficult meals, examples of coping strategies, and practical guidance for navigating challenging meals after discharge. Several caregivers noted wanting continued access to the intervention content through downloadable notes, emailed resources, or a printed summary of key information. Additional recommendations included combining the intervention with opportunities for clinician discussion.

Discussion

This pilot study was an initial investigation into the feasibility and acceptability of a brief web-based caregiver SSI targeting accommodation of eating disorder behaviors during inpatient hospital admission for acute medical stabilization of patients with AN. Overall, our results suggested that Project EMPOWER-ED had high uptake and completion rates, high overall ratings of acceptability, and promising preliminary changes from pre-to post-intervention on measures of perceived preparedness.

A strong positive result of our pilot study is that feasibility of the SSI was high. With 89% of possible participants enrolling and completing the intervention, and of those, 72% completing the pre-and post-intervention measures, caregivers seemed to feel that the intervention was manageable and of interest to them. In addition, time to complete Project EMPOWER-ED was relatively brief with an average completion time of about 33 minutes.

Given the significant burden of caring for a child with an eating disorder, the fact this intervention had such high uptake and was completed efficiently is an important strength. In particular, embedding this intervention into a hospitalization was shown to be a practical way to provide support to caregivers in need of this information 29 . Taken together, this SSI delivered in this setting may be a highly feasible way to help address the provider shortages, waitlist concerns, and overall lack of evidence-based care in the community facing a particularly highrisk population.

Acceptability pilot data scores were also high. Qualitative feedback highlighted a number of likely contributors to generally high acceptability, including practical content, feeling validated, and the digital, self-guided format. It is worth noting, though, that acceptability may have varied between demographic groups, however, the sample size was small overall and there were differences in sample sizes between subgroups, precluding inferential analyses. Future research should robustly evaluate group differences in the acceptability and effectiveness of Project EMPOWER-ED given that the purpose of the intervention is to address access and equity issues within the eating disorder treatment landscape.

Qualitative data also highlighted some potential barriers to acceptability or areas for improvement. Despite a relatively short time to completion, some caregivers reported concerns about the length of time to complete the SSI and accompanying measures or the overall burden of completing it during an already overwhelming hospital stay. It should be noted that some of this feedback was specifically centered on the length of the pre-and post-intervention assessments, as compared to the SSI itself. Regardless, efforts to continue to work on maximizing brevity in the intervention should be considered, particularly given emerging research on SSIs in other contexts demonstrates that even briefer interventions with reduced text density and duration overall can produce meaningful impacts, raising the possibility that caregivers would find a shorter version of Project EMPOWER-ED helpful. [30] [31] [32] Further, Project EMPOWER-ED was designed to be relevant for caregivers of children through young adults, so future iterations will also ensure that the language, graphics, and vignettes are appropriate across a wider age range and, when possible, offer caregivers a choice of age-relevant scenarios.

Finally, caregivers gave important feedback on timing. Many felt that this intervention would have been most helpful at diagnosis or admission to the hospital rather than closer to discharge, which offers actionable guidance for future intervention iterations and trials. Collectively, these recommendations support retaining the intervention's practical and self-guided components while reducing completion burden, increasing flexibility in timing and delivery of the SSI, and improving the intervention's developmental relevance.

Despite areas for improvement, preliminary data suggests that the intervention could successfully impact important established mechanisms of change for young patients with eating disorders. Caregivers' assessment of their preparedness to manage their eating disorder-related distress as well as tolerate their child's distress both improved after completing the SSI. While this cannot be taken to suggest that the intervention is effective due to the small sample size and lack of control group, this is important and promising because these items conceptually align with research suggesting that distress tolerance reduces accommodation which can exacerbate harmful eating disorder behaviors 10 . In addition, the identified changes in caregiver preparedness are consistent with findings from the original anxiety-focused SSI on which Project EMPOWER-ED was based 20, 21 . This preliminary data is a promising step in the right direction toward improving caregivers' abilities in supporting their children's eating disorder recovery.

This study has several important strengths that merit discussion. First, its mixed-methods design allows for rich feedback alongside quantitative data, allowing caregiver perspectives to contextualize the numeric findings. In addition, this study achieved a high completion rate, despite recruiting from a clinically burdened inpatient population. This offers an important perspective on how we can support a very acute and high-risk population during a particularly sensitive time. Finally, the collaboration amongst a multidisciplinary group of creators that included both the original intervention developers as well as pediatric psychologists with expertise in eating disorders strengthened both the SSI's clinical relevance and fidelity to evidence-based principles.

Despite these strengths, this study also has limitations. Most importantly, this was a small pilot study without a control or randomization, limiting our ability to draw inferences about clinical effectiveness. This was a narrow sample in that it was limited to a single site and specific to caregivers of youth with anorexia in one hospital. We currently lack data about whether this SSI might help a broader population of caregivers, possibly at different stages of care. Given caregiver feedback on timing, it would be particularly beneficial to explore for whom and when during treatment it would be most helpful to offer this intervention 33 . Additionally, while our multidisciplinary team of developers brought strong clinical expertise, it did not include community stakeholders or individuals with lived experience. Key next steps for Project EMPOWER-ED include larger, multi-site trials with more diverse samples, more rigorous efficacy testing with stronger experimental controls, incorporation of community stakeholder input into intervention refinement, and evaluation of the SSI's impact across different stages of the recovery journey.

Taken together, these findings suggest that a brief, web-based SSI can be feasibly and acceptably delivered to caregivers during a uniquely stressful window of their child's medical hospitalization for anorexia nervosa. Preliminary improvements in caregiver ability to manage and tolerate distress, alongside caregivers' own reports of increased confidence and validation, offer an encouraging early signal that this low-cost, scalable approach may help address a persistent gap in caregiver support during eating disorder treatment. A multi-site trial, informed by community stakeholder input and designed to test timing and setting directly, represents the next step toward determining not just whether Project EMPOWER-ED works, but for whom and when it can be most useful.
