Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Abstract

Introduction: Accurate identification of developmental language disorder (DLD) requires assessment measures validated for their intended populations. This study examined the psychometric properties and diagnostic accuracy of preliminary Spanish grammatical measures from the Bilingual English-Spanish Assessment (BESA) in monolingual Mexican children.

Method: Participants included 395 monolingual Spanish-speaking children aged 3;0-5;5 years (295 typically developing, 100 with DLD). Twenty-two grammatical cloze items and 53 sentence repetition targets were examined using regression, classical item analyses, item response theory, receiver operating characteristic analyses, and repeated cross-validation.

Results: Children with DLD performed significantly below typically developing children on both tasks, while scores increased with age. Clitic pronouns and subjunctive constructions demonstrated strong item-level discrimination, and two-parameter item response models fit better than one-parameter models. The 21-item Morphosyntax score yielded an area under the curve of .872, compared with .847 for the 29-target Sentence Repetition score. Combining both tasks yielded .885, although the improvement in discrimination was not statistically significant.

Discussion: Findings provide promising evidence of the diagnostic utility of preliminary BESA Spanish grammatical measures in monolingual Mexican children and highlight the importance of item-level evaluation and population-specific validity evidence. Further research is needed to evaluate the complete published assessment, establish clinical cutoffs, and compare item performance across monolingual and bilingual populations.

1. Introduction

Accurate language assessment depends on instruments that are linguistically appropriate, psychometrically sound, and capable of distinguishing typical variation in language development from clinically significant difficulties. Although standardized assessments provide systematic approaches to evaluating children's linguistic abilities, their utility depends on the populations for which they were developed and validated. Instruments developed within one linguistic context may not demonstrate comparable performance when administered to children with different language experiences or sociocultural backgrounds. Research examining the application of monolingual assessment instruments to bilingual populations has demonstrated that using population-inappropriate reference standards can compromise diagnostic accuracy (Altman et al., 2022). Consequently, evaluating assessment instruments across linguistic populations is important for establishing the validity of their intended interpretations and expanding access to appropriate assessment practices.

These considerations are relevant to Spanish language assessment. Although Spanish is spoken across diverse linguistic communities, assessment instruments with population-specific validity evidence remain unevenly available. Instruments developed for Spanish-English bilingual children in the United States may target grammatical structures relevant to Spanish more broadly, but shared linguistic content does not necessarily imply equivalent item functioning or diagnostic performance across bilingual and monolingual populations. Differences in language experience and developmental patterns may influence performance on particular structures, requiring empirical investigation of their psychometric properties in other populations. Such investigations can also clarify which linguistic structures maintain diagnostic relevance across contexts and which require population-specific consideration.

In Mexico, the availability of language assessment instruments developed or validated specifically for monolingual Spanish-speaking children remains limited (Auza et al., 2024). This creates an opportunity to investigate whether existing instruments developed for other Spanish-speaking populations can contribute to assessment practices in Mexico, provided that their diagnostic performance is established locally. One such instrument is the Bilingual English-Spanish Assessment (BESA; Peña et al., 2018), developed to support the identification of developmental language disorder (DLD) in Spanish-English bilingual children. Its Spanish Morphosyntax subtest incorporates grammatical cloze and sentence repetition tasks that assess grammatical abilities relevant to Spanish language development, making these components candidates for evaluation in monolingual populations rather than measures whose validity can be assumed to transfer directly. Examining the item-level psychometric properties and diagnostic accuracy of these Spanish grammatical measures in monolingual Mexican children can provide evidence regarding their potential clinical utility while contributing to broader discussions of assessment across monolingual and multilingual contexts.

1.1 Language Assessment and the Identification of DLD in Mexico

Developmental language disorder (DLD), previously referred to as specific language impairment (SLI), is characterized by persistent language difficulties that affect everyday communication and functioning without a known biomedical cause (Bishop et al., 2017).

Although DLD affects approximately 7-8% of children (Norbury et al., 2016; Tomblin et al., 1997), its identification remains challenging because linguistic manifestations, diagnostic criteria, and assessment practices vary across populations. Recent efforts to establish consensus across Spanish-speaking countries have emphasized the need for consistent diagnostic guidelines and linguistically appropriate assessment practices. In a two-round Delphi study involving 51 specialists from 13 Spanish-speaking countries, Andreu Barrachina et al. (2026) established 25 consensus statements addressing terminology, classification, and diagnosis. The panel recommended multidimensional assessment incorporating standardized measures, language samples, clinical observations, and caregiver reports, while emphasizing the importance of instruments with adequate reliability, validity, and linguistic and cultural appropriateness.

In Mexico, these challenges are compounded by limited access to validated assessment instruments and systematic identification procedures. Auza et al. (2024) reported an estimated DLD prevalence of 8.5% among 543 monolingual Spanish-speaking children, while identifying gaps in screening practices and diagnostic resources. Recent research has begun addressing these limitations through complementary approaches to language assessment. The Tamiz de Problemas del Lenguaje (TPL; Auza et al., 2018a) provides a screening measure targeting grammatical difficulties, while Auza et al. (2023) demonstrated the utility of parental questionnaires alongside language screening for identifying children at risk for DLD. More recently, Auza et al. (2026) provided age-referenced values for narrative measures, including mean length of utterance, total number of words, percentage of grammatical utterances, and lexical diversity, offering developmental reference points for interpreting language performance among Mexican children. Emerging computational approaches, including speech graph analysis (Cabrera Pérez et al., 2025), may further complement conventional assessment methods by characterizing the structural organization of children's language production. Together, these research initiatives contribute to a growing evidence base for language assessment in Mexican Spanish-speaking populations. Nevertheless, additional research is needed to establish the psychometric properties and diagnostic accuracy of existing instruments, particularly those developed for bilingual populations, when applied to monolingual Mexican children.

1.2 Morphosyntax and Sentence Repetition in Spanish Language Assessment

Morphosyntactic abilities are important indicators of language development and have received considerable attention in the assessment of Spanish-speaking children with DLD.

Morphosyntactic difficulties have been documented in Spanish-speaking children with DLD across both monolingual and bilingual populations, although their manifestations may vary according to language experience and the grammatical structures examined. Research has documented difficulties with articles, clitic pronouns, verb morphology, and the subjunctive mood (Bedore & Leonard, 2001 , 2005; Castilla-Earls et al., 2020). Studies examining nonfinite verb forms and tense marking have further highlighted the potential diagnostic relevance of Spanish verbal morphology (Grinstead et al., 2009 (Grinstead et al., , 2013)). Additional research has examined difficulties with prepositions, including polysemic forms (Auza & Morgan, 2013; Chaf Gallardo et al., 2023), and derivational morphology (Morgan et al., 2009). Together, these studies illustrate the range of grammatical structures affected in Spanish-speaking children with DLD, although their diagnostic informativeness may differ.

The diagnostic relevance of specific morphosyntactic structures has also been investigated across monolingual and bilingual populations. In a study of 50 monolingual Spanish-speaking children in Mexico, Castilla-Earls et al. (2020) found that clitic pronouns and verb morphology were informative for diagnostic classification. Similarly, Dickinson et al. (2023) demonstrated that subject pronoun use in switch-reference contexts contributed to distinguishing Mexican children with and without DLD. Research with Spanish-English bilingual children has also identified morphosyntactic differences associated with DLD, although performance varies with age and language experience (Castilla-Earls et al., 2023). These findings support the diagnostic relevance of Spanish morphosyntax while emphasizing that individual grammatical structures may differ in their sensitivity to language difficulties across populations. This variability underscores the importance of examining item-level psychometric properties to identify which grammatical targets provide the most informative measures of language difficulties within specific linguistic populations.

Sentence repetition provides another approach to evaluating grammatical abilities. Unlike elicited morphosyntactic production, which targets specific grammatical forms, sentence repetition requires children to reproduce sentences containing structures of varying complexity, drawing on linguistic knowledge, verbal memory, and processing abilities (Komeili & Marshall, 2013). Previous research has demonstrated the clinical utility of sentence repetition for identifying language difficulties in monolingual and bilingual populations (Armon-Lotem & Meir, 2016; Gutiérrez-Clellen & Simón-Cereijido, 2007; Li'el et al., 2019). Recent evidence supports sentence repetition as a robust clinical marker of DLD in bilinguals (Pratt et al., 2021). Systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and longitudinal studies have found substantial differences between children with DLD and typically developing peers across languages and task designs (Albudoor et al., 2026; Ang et al., 2026; Ward et al., 2024). Additional findings further support its diagnostic potential while highlighting that sentence repetition should be used alongside other language measures, rather than as a stand-alone diagnostic tool (Ward et al., 2026). Nevertheless, differences in linguistic experience and the grammatical properties of individual sentences may influence performance, underscoring the importance of evaluating these tasks within the intended population. Together, morphosyntactic elicitation and sentence repetition provide complementary approaches to examining grammatical abilities, although their relative and combined contributions to diagnostic classification require empirical investigation.

1.3 Evaluating the Spanish BESA Across Linguistic Populations

The Bilingual English-Spanish Assessment (BESA; Peña et al., 2018) was developed to identify DLD in Spanish-English bilingual children between 4 and 6 years of age. The instrument includes standardized measures of morphosyntax, semantics, and phonology in both languages, alongside procedures for documenting language exposure, use, and caregiver and teacher concerns. Its development was informed by research on grammatical structures associated with language difficulties in both languages, and the need to distinguish differences related to bilingual development from language disorder. The Morphosyntax subtest incorporates grammatical cloze and sentence repetition tasks targeting language-specific grammatical structures, including articles, clitics, verb forms, and subjunctive constructions in Spanish. The BESA was standardized using a sample of 756 bilingual children with varying levels of Spanish and English proficiency, and its manual reports evidence concerning reliability, validity, and diagnostic classification (Peña et al., 2018).

Subsequent research has further examined the psychometric properties of BESA components. Fitton et al. (2019) evaluated the Spanish and English Sentence Repetition tasks in 291 Spanish-English bilingual children and found evidence supporting the interpretation of overall task scores, while identifying variation associated with specific linguistic components.

Similarly, Peña et al. (2020) demonstrated the diagnostic utility of combining grammatical, semantic, and narrative measures in bilingual school-age children. These investigations provide evidence supporting assessment approaches developed for bilingual populations, but their findings cannot automatically be generalized to monolingual children. More broadly, research on the application of assessment instruments across linguistic populations has demonstrated that diagnostic accuracy may be compromised when measures are used without population-appropriate reference standards (Altman et al., 2022).

Its original standardization and validation focused on Spanish-English bilingual children in the United States. Evaluating Morphosyntax and Sentence Repetition independently and in combination may also determine whether both tasks provide additional diagnostic information. Such evidence is relevant in Mexico, where expanding the empirical foundation for existing assessment instruments may support more linguistically appropriate identification practices. Establishing the diagnostic accuracy of the Spanish BESA components in monolingual Mexican children may support their use as additional tools for classifying children with and without DLD within a comprehensive language assessment framework.

1.4 The Present Study

The present study examined the psychometric properties and diagnostic accuracy of the Spanish BESA Morphosyntax and Sentence Repetition tasks in 395 monolingual Spanish-speaking Mexican. Specifically, the study investigated (a) diagnostic-group and age-related differences in performance, (b) item difficulty and discrimination using classical item analyses and one-and two-parameter item response theory models, and (c) diagnostic classification accuracy of both tasks independently and in combination. Receiver operating characteristic analyses and repeated stratified cross-validation were used to evaluate the stability and accuracy of diagnostic classification. Through this investigation, the study seeks to extend the psychometric evidence for the Spanish BESA beyond its original bilingual population and evaluate its potential utility for identifying DLD in monolingual Mexican children who live in a different linguistic context. RQ2. 2.2 Eligibility and Diagnostic Classification

Eligibility criteria were applied to both diagnostic groups. 2.3 Measures

2.3.1 Spanish BESA Morphosyntax and Sentence Repetition

The Bilingual English-Spanish Assessment (BESA; Peña et al., 2018) is a standardized language assessment developed for Spanish-English bilingual children aged 4-6 years. The instrument includes measures of phonology, morphosyntax, and semantics in Spanish and English. The present study focused exclusively on the Spanish Morphosyntax subtest, which comprises two components: Grammatical Cloze and Sentence Repetition. These components assess grammatical production and sentence-level language abilities and are combined to derive the overall Morphosyntax score in the published assessment.

2.3.2 Preliminary BESA protocol

Data were collected using preliminary versions of the Spanish BESA Morphosyntax protocol administered before the instrument's final publication. The available item pool included 22 Grammatical Cloze items and 53 scored targets distributed across eight Sentence Repetition sentences. Consequently, the dataset permits examination of a broader set of preliminary items than those identified as retained in the published version.

The Grammatical Cloze task required children to complete elicited sentences using targeted grammatical forms. The preliminary pool included seven article items, two preterite items, eight clitic-pronoun items, and five subjunctive items. Sentence Repetition required children to reproduce orally presented sentences, with accuracy evaluated for specified words and phrases rather than every word in each sentence. Across the eight sentences represented in the dataset, 53 targets were available for analysis. Responses were scored dichotomously as correct (1) or incorrect (0).

2.3.3 Comparison with the published BESA

The published Spanish Morphosyntax subtest retains the Grammatical Cloze and Sentence Repetition format but differs from the preliminary materials in item composition. Using the item correspondence records available for the present study, we identified preliminary targets marked as retained in the final instrument (see Table 2). This distinction allowed the preliminary item pool to be examined while also evaluating subsets corresponding to the published measure.

2.4 Analytic Approach

Analyses were conducted at the task, item, and diagnostic-classification levels to examine group and age differences, item-level psychometric properties, and diagnostic accuracy. All analyses were performed in R version 4.4.2 (R Core Team, 2024). Data preparation and descriptive analyses used the tidyverse (Wickham et al., 2019). Psychometric models were estimated using TAM (Robitzsch et al., 2025), with emmeans (Lenth & Piaskowski, 2026) and (Ben-Shachar, et al., 2020) used for adjusted group comparisons and standardized effects. Diagnostic accuracy and cross-validation analyses used pROC (Robin et al., 2011) and rsample (Frick et al., 2026), respectively. Statistical significance was evaluated at α = .05, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported where applicable.

2.4.1 Group and Age Differences in Task Performance

Morphosyntax and Sentence Repetition scores were calculated as the proportion of correct responses across available items and expressed as percentages. Separate ordinary least-squares multiple linear regression models examined diagnostic-group differences in each task, adjusting for age in years and sex. Age was mean-centered before analysis. To examine whether age-related associations differed between diagnostic groups, Diagnosis × Age interaction terms were added to the models. Models with and without the interaction were compared using nested-model F tests. Regression coefficients were interpreted as percentage-point differences in task performance, and unadjusted standardized group differences were quantified using Cohen's d.

2.4.2 Item-Level Psychometric Analyses

Item-level analyses examined accuracy, discrimination, and model fit across the 22 Morphosyntax and 53 Sentence Repetition preliminary items or scored targets. Accuracy was calculated overall and by diagnostic group, with diagnostic separation expressed as the percentage-point difference between TD and DLD accuracy. Corrected item-total correlations assessed each item's association with overall task performance, excluding the target item from the total score.

Separate Rasch models were fitted to each task to estimate item difficulty in logits and evaluate infit and outfit statistics. Items were flagged for review when infit or outfit values fell outside 0.50-1.50, corrected item-total correlations were below .30, or TD-DLD accuracy differences were below 10 percentage points. Items meeting none of these exploratory criteria were classified as performing well, items meeting one criterion as monitor, and items meeting two or more criteria as review. These categories were used for descriptive screening rather than as validated clinical item-selection criteria. Classifications were compared with the items retained in the published BESA, with published-item status determined independently of the empirical screening results. This comparison was limited to preliminary items that could be matched to the published version and did not constitute an evaluation of every item in the finalized instrument.

To further evaluate item discrimination, one-parameter logistic (1PL; Rasch) and two-parameter logistic (2PL) models were estimated using the complete preliminary item pools.

The 1PL model constrained item discrimination to a common value, whereas the 2PL model estimated item-specific discrimination and difficulty parameters. Model fit was compared using log-likelihoods, Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and likelihood-ratio tests. Expected a posteriori reliability estimates were also summarized for each model. Item difficulty estimates and item characteristic curves were examined to compare model predictions across latent ability levels. In the 2PL model, discrimination reflected how sharply an item differentiated individuals by latent ability, whereas difficulty represented the ability level associated with a 50% probability of a correct response. For Sentence Repetition, item-level analyses used available responses for each target. Because participants received different preliminary test versions, some targets were not administered to all children; these were treated as structurally missing rather than incorrect responses.

3.2 RQ2: Item-Level Psychometric Performance and Morphosyntactic Structures

To identify the grammatical items and structures most informative for distinguishing children with and without DLD, the second research question examined item accuracy, corrected item-total correlations, diagnostic-group differences, and item response theory parameters across the preliminary Spanish BESA Morphosyntax and Sentence Repetition subtests. The analyses also examined how psychometric performance corresponded to items retained in the published BESA.

3.2.1 Exploratory Item Screening

Of the 22 preliminary Morphosyntax items, 19 met all three exploratory performance criteria, whereas two were classified as monitor and one as review. Among the 19 items meeting all criteria, eight corresponded to items retained in the published BESA and 11 were not.

Similarly, 42 of the 53 preliminary Sentence Repetition components met all three criteria, whereas six were classified as monitor and five as review. Of the 42 components meeting all criteria, 24 corresponded to targets retained in the published BESA, whereas 18 did not. These categories were used to summarize patterns across the psychometric indicators rather than establish validated clinical item-selection thresholds.

3.2.2 Morphosyntax

As shown in Figure 1, most Morphosyntax items demonstrated corrected item-total correlations above .30 and TD-DLD accuracy differences greater than 10 percentage points. The largest diagnostic-group differences were observed for subjunctive_3 (56.1 percentage points), subjunctive_4 (53.0), clitic_8 (50.3), clitic_7 (47.6), and clitic_2 (47.3). These findings identified clitic pronouns and subjunctive constructions as informative grammatical targets for distinguishing children with and without DLD. In contrast, article_3, an item retained in the published BESA, demonstrated no TD-DLD separation, with 22% accuracy in both diagnostic groups, and a weak corrected item-total correlation (r = .06). Together, these findings indicated limited diagnostic differentiation for this item in the present sample. Complete item-level results are reported in Supplemental Table S1. Rasch/1PL analyses further revealed considerable variation in item difficulty. Based on the exploratory item-quality estimates, article_3 was the most difficult item (b = 1.69), followed by subjunctive_2 (b = 0.64), whereas preterite_2 (b = -2.40), article_7 (b = -2.25), and article_2 (b = -2.15) were among the easiest. Most items demonstrated acceptable fit according to the exploratory infit/outfit criterion, although article_3 showed elevated outfit (2.20). Detailed screening and Rasch estimates are reported in Supplemental Table S1 and Supplemental Figures S1-S2.

The 2PL analysis also demonstrated substantial variation in item discrimination, with estimates ranging from 0.18 to 2.74. The highest discrimination parameters were observed for clitic_8 (a = 2.74), clitic_7 (a = 2.51), subjunctive_4 (a = 2.50), clitic_6 (a = 2.15), subjunctive_3 (a = 2.12), and clitic_2 (a = 2.09). These results were consistent with the classical item analyses, providing converging evidence that several clitic and subjunctive items exhibited particularly strong psychometric properties. By contrast, article_3 had the lowest discrimination parameter (a = 0.18) and the highest difficulty under the 2PL model (b = 1.27). Its low discrimination was consistent with its weak item-total correlation, elevated Rasch outfit, and absence of diagnostic-group separation. 3.2.4 Comparison of Rasch/1PL and 2PL Models

To determine whether allowing item discrimination parameters to vary improved model fit, the Rasch/1PL and 2PL models were compared separately for Morphosyntax and Sentence Repetition (Table 3). For Morphosyntax, the 2PL model provided significantly better fit than the Rasch/1PL model, likelihood-ratio χ²(22) = 275.16, p < .001. AIC decreased from 8,899.31 to 8,668.14, and BIC decreased from 8,986.84 to 8,843.22. EAP reliability increased slightly from .833 for the 1PL model to .851 for the 2PL model.

A similar pattern emerged for Sentence Repetition, with the 2PL model providing significantly better fit, likelihood-ratio χ²(53) = 687.84, p < .001. AIC decreased from 17,292.06 to 16,710.22, and BIC decreased from 17,502.94 to 17,131.98. EAP reliability was similar across the two models (.937 and .938, respectively). For both tasks, these results favored the model allowing item discrimination to vary rather than imposing a common discrimination parameter (see Supplemental Figure S5).

3.2.5 Summary of Item-Level Findings

Across classical item analyses and Rasch/1PL and 2PL models, most items demonstrated favorable psychometric properties, although their discriminative strength varied. Within Morphosyntax, several clitic and subjunctive items consistently showed strong diagnostic-group separation and 2PL discrimination. Sentence Repetition targets likewise differed in their psychometric properties, with some demonstrating substantially stronger discrimination than others.

Correspondence between item performance and retention in the published BESA was incomplete. Several preliminary items not retained in the published measure demonstrated favorable properties, whereas article_3, an item retained in the published BESA, exhibited weak item-total association, negligible TD-DLD separation, elevated Rasch outfit, and low 2PL discrimination. These findings identify population-and sample-specific variation in item performance and support further investigation of which grammatical targets are most informative for assessing monolingual Mexican children. They do not establish that the preliminary items would necessarily perform better in other samples or linguistic contexts.

3.3 RQ3: Diagnostic Classification

The third research question examined how accurately the Spanish BESA Morphosyntax and Sentence Repetition tasks differentiated monolingual Mexican children with and without DLD, both independently and in combination. Diagnostic accuracy was evaluated using full-sample receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses and repeated stratified cross-validation.

3.3.1 Full-Sample Diagnostic Accuracy

Full-sample ROC analyses indicated good diagnostic discrimination for both tasks.

The reduced 21-item Morphosyntax score excluding article_3 yielded an AUC of .872, 95% CI [.833, .912]. The 29-target Sentence Repetition score corresponding to items retained in the published BESA yielded an AUC of .847, 95% CI [.805, .888], whereas the complete 53-target preliminary Sentence Repetition score yielded an AUC of .852, 95% CI [.812, .892]. The difference between the 29-and 53-target Sentence Repetition scores was not statistically significant (p = .193), suggesting that the additional preliminary targets did not produce a detectable improvement in overall diagnostic discrimination in this sample. Combining Morphosyntax and Sentence Repetition yielded the highest full-sample discrimination. The model combining the 21-item Morphosyntax score with the 29-target Sentence Repetition score yielded an AUC of .885, 95% CI [.848, .921]. Adding Sentence

Repetition significantly improved model fit relative to Morphosyntax alone, Δχ²(1) = 6.30, p = .012; however, the corresponding increase in AUC did not reach statistical significance (p = .059). Thus, although Sentence Repetition contributed additional information to model fit, the improvement in overall diagnostic discrimination was modest.

3.3.2 Effect of Removing article_3

Given the weak item-level psychometric properties observed for article_3 in RQ2, additional ROC analyses examined whether excluding this item improved diagnostic discrimination. The complete 22-item preliminary Morphosyntax score yielded an AUC of .870, whereas the 21-item score excluding article_3 yielded an AUC of .872. This difference was not statistically significant (p = .230). Similarly, the 10 Morphosyntax items corresponding to the published BESA yielded an AUC of .848, whereas the nine-item subset excluding article_3 yielded an AUC of .856; this difference was also not statistically significant (p = .105). Thus, although article_3 demonstrated weak item-level psychometric properties, its removal resulted in only modest changes in overall diagnostic discrimination. These findings suggest that weak performance by an individual item does not necessarily translate into a substantial reduction in task-level diagnostic accuracy.

3.3.3 Diagnostic Classification After Accounting for Age

Because diagnostic groups differed slightly in age, supplementary logistic regression analyses examined whether age contributed to diagnostic classification beyond Morphosyntax performance. Each 10-percentage-point increase in Morphosyntax accuracy was associated with approximately 50% lower odds of DLD classification. Age was not independently significant (b = 0.556, SE = 0.302, z = 1.841, p = .066), and adding age did not significantly improve model fit, Δχ²(1) = 3.515, p = .061.

3.4 Supplementary Psychometric Analyses

Additional analyses examined convergent performance, internal consistency, and diagnostic differentiation using composite scores derived from the complete preliminary item pools.

Morphosyntax and Sentence Repetition scores were strongly and positively associated in the full sample, r(392) = .779, 95% CI [.737, .815]. 4. Discussion

The present study examined the psychometric properties and diagnostic accuracy of the Spanish BESA Morphosyntax and Sentence Repetition tasks in monolingual Spanish-speaking Mexican children. The findings extend the evidence for these tasks to children assessed in Mexico and provide population-specific information about the performance of preliminary grammatical items originally developed within the BESA framework. Three principal findings emerged. First, children with DLD demonstrated substantially lower performance on both tasks, even after accounting for age and sex. Second, individual grammatical structures differed in their psychometric properties, with clitic pronouns and subjunctive constructions demonstrating strong discrimination. Third, both tasks showed good diagnostic accuracy, although combining them produced only modest improvements in overall discrimination. Together, these results show the potential value of Spanish grammatical measures for identifying language difficulties while underscoring the importance of evaluating assessment performance within the population of intended use.

Regarding RQ1, children with DLD obtained significantly lower Morphosyntax and Sentence Repetition scores than their TD peers, with large group differences observed across both tasks. Age was positively associated with performance, consistent with previous research documenting age-related variation in Spanish morphosyntactic development (Castilla-Earls et al., 2023). However, the absence of significant Diagnosis × Age interactions provided no evidence that these cross-sectional age associations differed between diagnostic groups. Morphosyntax accuracy also contributed substantially to diagnostic classification, whereas age alone showed limited discrimination and did not significantly improve classification beyond grammatical performance. These findings underscore the value of directly assessing grammatical abilities when identifying children with DLD. Importantly, age-related differences in performance should not be interpreted as longitudinal developmental change, and the lack of significant interactions does not establish equivalent developmental trajectories. These findings suggest that chronological age and grammatical performance provide distinct information, with direct measures of grammatical ability contributing more strongly to diagnostic differentiation in the present sample.

Regarding RQ2, the item-level analyses revealed variation in the psychometric properties of individual grammatical items. Within Morphosyntax, clitic and subjunctive items showed particularly strong differentiation between children with and without DLD, together with high discrimination in the two-parameter logistic model. These findings are consistent with earlier studies documenting difficulties with direct object clitics and other grammatical morphemes in Spanish-speaking children with language impairment (Bedore & Leonard, 2001 , 2005; Jacobson & Schwartz, 2002). Research on Spanish verb morphology has also shown that tense-related measures can distinguish children with and without DLD (Grinstead et al., 2013), while work on the subjunctive has identified this mood as a potentially informative grammatical target in Spanish-English bilingual children (Castilla-Earls et al., 2018). The present findings extend this literature by demonstrating the strong item-level discrimination of clitic and subjunctive targets within the Spanish BESA Morphosyntax task in monolingual Mexican children. The present findings extend this literature by demonstrating converging evidence from diagnostic-group accuracy differences, corrected item-total correlations, and item response theory discrimination parameters in a relatively large sample of monolingual Mexican children.

The superior fit of the two-parameter models further indicated that grammatical items varied in their capacity to differentiate children along the underlying ability continuum. This variability suggests that items targeting the same broad grammatical domain may differ substantially in the information they contribute to overall assessment performance. In Sentence Repetition, individual linguistic targets also differed in their psychometric characteristics, reinforcing the importance of examining the grammatical components of repetition tasks rather than relying exclusively on overall scores. Notably, strong discrimination on one indicator did not always correspond to favorable performance across all indices, illustrating the value of considering multiple psychometric criteria when evaluating individual items.

At the same time, several preliminary items not retained in the published BESA showed favorable properties, whereas article_3, an item retained in the published measure, showed weak item-total association, little diagnostic-group separation, elevated Rasch outfit, and low discrimination in the two-parameter model. Both diagnostic groups demonstrated approximately 22% accuracy on this item, suggesting that it was difficult for children regardless of diagnostic status and contributed little to distinguishing the groups in this sample. Its performance may reflect characteristics of the targeted structure, task demands, or the ages and linguistic experiences of the children assessed; the present analyses cannot distinguish among these explanations. Importantly, the performance of article_3 illustrates that item difficulty and diagnostic informativeness are not interchangeable. An item may be challenging for both groups without meaningfully distinguishing children with and without DLD. However, favorable performance among preliminary items does not establish that they would improve the published instrument, nor does the weak performance of article_3 establish that it should be removed universally. Rather, these findings identify candidate items for further investigation and support an empirical approach to item selection across assessment populations.

Regarding RQ3, both Morphosyntax and Sentence Repetition showed good diagnostic discrimination in the present sample. Morphosyntax yielded an area under the curve of .872, whereas Sentence Repetition yielded an area under the curve of .847, indicating that both tasks differentiated children with and without DLD, with numerically higher discrimination for Morphosyntax. The absence of a significant improvement when additional preliminary Sentence Repetition targets were included suggests that the larger item pool did not provide a detectable advantage in diagnostic discrimination. However, this finding does not establish equivalence between the shorter and longer item sets. Morphosyntax performance contributed meaningfully to diagnostic classification, whereas age alone showed limited discrimination and did not significantly improve the model.

The cross-validation findings further support this interpretation. Across the evaluated models, mean areas under the curve ranged from .846 to .875, with considerable overlap in their distributions. Although the combined model incorporating nine Morphosyntax items and 29

Sentence Repetition targets demonstrated the highest mean area under the curve, its advantage over the other models was small. Similarly, removing article_3 resulted in only modest improvements in overall discrimination, despite its weak item-level performance. These findings illustrate that individual-item discrimination and overall diagnostic classification provide related but distinct forms of evidence. An item may demonstrate limited diagnostic value without substantially reducing the accuracy of a composite score, particularly when other items capture overlapping linguistic information. Consequently, additional studies are needed to determine whether particular item combinations consistently improve diagnostic performance in independent samples.

The high internal consistency of both preliminary item pools provides additional evidence that the items functioned coherently within this sample. Nevertheless, internal consistency should not be equated with diagnostic validity or taken as evidence that all items measure a single underlying ability. The strong association between Morphosyntax and Sentence Repetition, including after accounting for diagnosis, age, and sex, suggests that the tasks capture related dimensions of language performance without establishing that they are psychometrically interchangeable. Sentence repetition involves the interaction of grammatical knowledge with lexical and phonological representations and the ability to recall and reproduce linguistic structures (Komeili & Marshall, 2013; Ward et al., 2024). This interpretation is consistent with previous research supporting sentence repetition as an informative measure of language difficulties while emphasizing the multiple linguistic and processing abilities involved in task performance (Armon-Lotem & Meir, 2016; Pratt et al., 2021). Similarly, although the global composite differentiated TD children from children with DLD, its diagnostic accuracy was similar to that of the other combined models, and these analyses did not establish a clear advantage for combining the full preliminary item pools.

More broadly, these findings contribute to understanding how grammatical measures perform across populations and assessment contexts. Although the BESA was developed primarily within a Spanish-English bilingual assessment framework, its manual also describes use with Spanish-speaking monolingual and bilingual children and reports diagnostic classification evidence that includes functionally Spanish-monolingual children in the United

States (Peña et al., 2018). The contribution of the present study therefore lies in providing population-specific evidence from children recruited and assessed in Mexico, rather than establishing the first application of BESA materials to monolingual children. Previous research has emphasized that applying assessment instruments across linguistic populations without appropriate validity evidence can compromise diagnostic interpretation (Altman et al., 2022). By examining both item-level properties and overall classification, the present study demonstrates an approach for evaluating the performance of grammatical measures in populations beyond those represented in their original development and standardization. However, direct comparisons between monolingual and bilingual children would be required to establish whether the same grammatical items demonstrate comparable measurement properties across these groups.

4.1 Clinical Implications

The present findings have implications for assessing Spanish-speaking children with suspected DLD, particularly in settings where locally validated instruments remain limited. The diagnostic performance of Morphosyntax and Sentence Repetition suggests that both tasks may provide useful information about grammatical abilities in monolingual Mexican children.

However, these measures should be considered within a comprehensive assessment that incorporates language samples, caregiver reports, developmental history, and other relevant clinical information, rather than as stand-alone diagnostic tools (Peña et al., 2018).

The item-level findings also highlight the importance of examining specific grammatical structures alongside overall assessment scores. Clitic pronouns and subjunctive constructions demonstrated particularly strong diagnostic differentiation, suggesting that these structures may be informative when evaluating Spanish grammatical abilities. Nevertheless, individual items should not be used independently to identify DLD. Instead, patterns of grammatical performance may guide further assessment and help clinicians identify areas requiring closer examination (Peña et al., 2018).

The findings also have implications for assessment efficiency and the application of existing instruments across linguistic populations. The absence of a significant difference in diagnostic accuracy between the 29-and 53-target Sentence Repetition scores suggests that additional scored targets may not necessarily improve classification. This is relevant to efforts to develop efficient Spanish language screening procedures (Auza et al., 2018b). However, shorter item sets require independent validation, and fewer scored targets do not necessarily reduce administration time. More broadly, the promising performance of these preliminary BESA materials underscores the importance of establishing population-specific evidence before applying assessment norms or diagnostic thresholds across linguistic contexts (Altman et al., 2022). Open science practices that promote transparency, accessibility, and collaboration may further support the validation of multilingual assessment measures and advance more equitable research and practice in special education (Cabrera Pérez et al., 2026).

4.2 Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the analyses were based on preliminary BESA materials rather than the complete published instrument. Although some items corresponded to those retained in the published version, the resulting scores were not equivalent to standardized BESA scores, and the findings cannot establish the diagnostic validity of the complete published assessment. Additionally, the sample included children aged 3;0-5;5 years, whereas the BESA manual specifies an age range of 4;0-6;11 (Peña et al., 2018). Further validation is needed for children younger than four years.

Second, diagnostic classification was established using multiple clinical and linguistic indicators, some of which assessed abilities similar to those targeted by the BESA tasks. This overlap may have contributed to the observed diagnostic accuracy. Furthermore, differences across preliminary Sentence Repetition versions resulted in unequal item-level sample sizes, and scoring multiple linguistic targets within individual sentences may have introduced dependencies among responses, potentially affecting item-level psychometric estimates. Finally, the item subsets and classification thresholds were evaluated within the same sample. Although repeated cross-validation provided evidence of internal performance, sample-informed item selection may have produced optimistic estimates of diagnostic accuracy. The proportion of children with DLD was also higher than expected in the general population, limiting the generalizability of predictive values. Future research should prioritize independent validation of item subsets and diagnostic thresholds, examine performance across age groups and regional varieties of Spanish, and directly compare identical grammatical targets in monolingual and bilingual children. Such investigations would help establish which grammatical structures provide consistent diagnostic information across linguistic populations and which require population-specific interpretation.

5. Conclusion

The present study provides population-specific psychometric and diagnostic evidence for preliminary Spanish grammatical measures developed within the BESA assessment framework.

Across the three research questions, children with DLD demonstrated lower Morphosyntax and Sentence Repetition performance, clitic and subjunctive items emerged as particularly informative grammatical targets, and both tasks showed good diagnostic discrimination. These findings demonstrate the importance of examining both individual-item properties and overall diagnostic performance when evaluating existing language assessment measures in new populations. Although combining the tasks offered modest additional information, the broader contribution lies in identifying promising grammatical measures for further validation in monolingual Mexican children. This work provides a foundation for continued research toward more evidence-based, efficient, and culturally and linguistically responsive approaches to identifying DLD.

3.3.4 Cross-Validated Diagnostic Accuracy
