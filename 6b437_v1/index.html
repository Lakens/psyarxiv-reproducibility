How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Handling between-study differences in effect sizes, or heterogeneity, is a key challenge in meta-analysis. The common assumption that heterogeneity is normally distributed implies an absence of systematic sources of heterogeneity, or known and measured sources of heterogeneity that can be controlled using moderator analysis. These assumptions fall short when effect sizes originate from multiple unknown subpopulations -for example, due to different research traditions, unmeasured moderators, publication bias, and outliers. It has been argued that finite mixture models -also known as latent class analysis (LCA) -can capture these unobserved subgroups, but there is currently no generalized framework for estimating such models. We introduce open source software for estimating random-effects mixture, or "remix" meta-analyses. Implemented in OpenMx, these structural equation models can be fully customized and further extended. We conducted a simulation study to evaluate the remix algorithm's convergence, bias and confidence interval coverage, and the performance of several class enumeration metrics: the AIC, BIC, and ICL information criteria, predictive model comparison (PMC), and cross-validation. Results show that the method is fast and has low bias, but confidence interval coverage is below nominal levels, and class enumeration remains challenging -which are known limitations of LCA. Nevertheless, remix meta-analysis is the appropriate method when effect sizes originate from a mixture of unobserved subpopulations; a scenario which is arguably commonplace in meta-analysis.

[div-01]

When One Random Effect Is Not Enough: Mixture Model Meta-Analysis When synthesizing effect sizes originating from different studies, a key question is how to properly account for between-study differences. The choice of an appropriate statistical model depends on theoretical assumptions about the sources of heterogeneity. For example, the fixed-effect model assumes no differences in true effect size. If effect sizes result from close replications of a particular experiment, it is sensible to assume one true population effect, which implies that any differences between observed effect sizes must be due to sampling error. If effect sizes instead originate from a wide range of different studies, conducted in different labs with unique designs and distinct measures -then we might expert there to be variance in true effect sizes. If we assume that there are no systematic differences, then the random effects model can estimate the variance of true effect sizes, τ² . If we know that there are systematic sources of heterogeneity, for example, because some studies used a stronger manipulation than others, and we have coded these sources as moderator variables, then we can control for these differences using meta-regression. And finally, if we suspect that there are systematic sources of heterogeneity, but we don't know which moderator variables are relevant, machine learning methods meta-regression can be used to perform variable selection, retaining only the relevant moderators (Van Lissa, 2020; Van Lissa, van Erp, & Clapper, 2023). These methods address situations where there is no heterogeneity, random heterogeneity, or heterogeneity due to known and measured moderators.

A scenario that has been less commonly discussed occurs when there is systematic heterogeneity in effect sizes, but its causes are unmeasured, or not uniquely identifiable.

When this occurs, observed effect sizes do not follow a single normal distribution, but rather, a mixture of multiple normal distributions, as illustrated in Figure 1. The family of methods known by the umbrella term "Latent Class Analysis" (Vermunt et al., 2004) disentangles the underlying constituent distributions. The finite normal mixture model -or latent profile analysis -assumes that each class is best described by a normal distribution, which renders it suitable for the analysis of continuous observed indicators, such as effect sizes in meta-analysis. A mixture model assumes a known number of unobserved subgroups, called classes. Like individual normal distributions, each class is characterized by two parameters: a mean and variance. These can also be assumed to be equal across classes. The model jointly estimates these class parameters, as well as the mixing proportions of the classes (how prevalent each class is).

The logic behind mixture modeling can be straightforwardly applied to meta-analysis.

One important difference from primary data analysis is that each observed datapoint is more or less informative about the mixture distributions, depending on its precision, which is determined by its sampling variance. Studies with smaller sampling variance (typically those with larger samples) provide more accurate information about the (mixture) distribution of true effects. For this reason, models for meta-analysis typically include a form of weightingeither explicitly, or implicitly via the model's likelihood -that accounts study-specific sampling variance.

The present study introduces a random-effects mixture -or "remix" -model for meta-analysis and evaluates its performance in a comprehensive simulation study. The algorithm is implemented in the metamixture package, written in the free open source language R (R Core Team, 2026). It builds upon the structural equation modeling framework of OpenMx (Boker et al., 2011). Consequently, the resulting remix models are fully customizable, extensible, and can make use of the ecosystem of packages for generating, running, and reporting OpenMx models, like tidySEM (Van Lissa, Garnier-Villarreal, & Anadria, 2024). To illustrate the relevance of the remix model for meta-analysis, we first present several scenarios where the model would be an appropriate choice.

When is a Mixture Model Appropriate for Meta-Analysis?

The random-effects model is most commonly used for meta-analysis (Hedges & Vevea, 1998). It assumes that between-study differences are normally distributed. Let's consider several scenarios that would violate this assumption. First, even if true effects are normally distributed in the population, the studies included in a meta-analysis may not constitute a representative sample of that population. New studies are not designed to randomly probe the full range of conditions under which an effect could occur, but instead, they follow in the footsteps of work that has been done before. Consequently, it may be more reasonable to think of published studies as a "snowball sample" from the population of possible studies, arising from a genetic process: each published study has a probability of inspiring further research, and this probability may be higher for studies with large effects than studies with small effects. This follow-up research inherits will inherit many of the characteristics of the preceding work, and thus, new effect sizes are correlated with prior effect sizes, as are the values of any moderator variables that encode between-study differences. The published effect sizes may thus contain an over-representation of specific populations, interventions, or measures.

It is easy to see how such a process would give rise to systematic deviations from normality in the distribution of published effect sizes. For example, consider a field that is dominated by two strong "research traditions": A and B. Both favor a particular research design (e.g., experience sampling versus lab experiments), methodology (e.g., random prompts sent to participants' mobile devices, versus a series of computerized tasks), set of measures (questionnaire versus behavioral measures), and population (general public versus undergraduate students). Within each tradition, there can be minor variations in all of these characteristics. Previous Applications of Mixture Models in Meta-Analysis.

The present study is emphatically not the first to introduce mixture models in meta-analysis. Prior work has demonstrated their usefulness for addressing several methodological challenges. One such application is the use of mixture models for moderator analysis. Different approaches have been taken; for example, some authors have used mixture models to dichotomize the moderator matrix, and use predicted class membership in meta-regression (Graefe, 2025).

This way, several study quality characteristics could be reduced to a single binary study quality indicator (high versus low), which could increase power to detect a moderator effect.

In this context, moderators are assumed to be measured without sampling error, and thus, the regular mixture model can be used. Other authors have taken the inverse approach, performing mixture modeling on the effect sizes and then predicting class membership, rather than effect size value, in moderator analysis to increase statistical power (Zhang, Wang, & Xu, 2022). Like the present paper, these authors appear to use a random-effects mixture model, because the sampling variances are mentioned (p. 378). However, their focus is on moderator analysis, and little detail is provided on the mixture model implementation ("we treat the mixture decomposition algorithm as a black box", p. 378). Compared to this prior work, the present paper introduces a more general random-effects mixture meta-analysis framework, which can also be extended to moderator analysis that properly accounts for classification error via the weighted BCH approach (Bolck, Croon, & Hagenaars, 2004), two-step method (Bakk & Kuha, 2018), or pseudo-class draws (Wang, Hendricks Brown, & Bandeen-Roche, 2005), all implemented in tidySEM (Van Lissa et al., 2024).

Another application of mixture modeling to meta-analysis focused on robustness to outliers (Beath, 2014). This approach is suitable for scenarios like Figure 1B, where outliers inflate the tails of the observed data distribution. It estimates two latent classes with a common mean and free variances. Thus, one class can have a large variance and capture the outliers, while the other class offers less biased estimation of residual heterogeneity and more powerful inference for the pooled effect. This method is implemented in the metaplus R-package. As it is limited to 2-class models with equal means and free variances, it is a special case of the more general framework introduced in this paper.

Other applications of mixture models to meta-analysis have been developed in the context of diagnostic tests, to summarize tests' sensitivity and specificity across studies, and construct aggregate receiver operating characteristic curves (Eusebi, Reitsma, & Vermunt, 2014; Schlattmann, Verba, Dewey, & Walther, 2015). One of these approaches models these test characteristics as discrete random effects, taking into account sampling variance but not residual heterogeneity within classes (Schlattmann et al., 2015). While this method is implemented in an R-package, it has been removed from CRAN, and we were not able to compile an archived version to compare its performance to our method. The other approach assumes binomial sampling distributions, which precludes its application to normal mixture models Eusebi et al. (2014)]. Moreover, it is only implemented in closed-source commercial software.

An important factor that distinguished these methods from our approach is that they are all designed for bivariate diagnostic accuracy outcomes rather than for conventional univariate meta-analysis of continuous effect-size estimates with known sampling variances.

The research discussed here lays the statistical foundations for the present work, but differs in its intended applications, distributional assumptions, and generality. We introduce an open-source implementation of univariate random-effects mixture meta-analysis that accommodates class-specific or constrained mean effects and residual heterogeneity, while accounting for study-specific sampling variances. It is implemented in the remix() function in the metamixture R-package. Building on OpenMx, this implementation allows further model customization and extension, auxiliary analysis of predictors and moderators of class membership, and use of existing tools for reporting and visualization, like tidySEM. We evaluate the performance of these models in a simulation study, focusing on parameter recovery, confidence interval (CI) coverage, and the identification of the number of latent classes.

Statistical Model.

Finite mixture models represent the observed data distribution as a mixture of multiple underlying distributions. Each observation has a probability of belonging to one of k latent classes; the term "finite" implies that the number of components is known or fixed. Each class, in turn, is defined by several parameters. For a single observed continuous variable, these are often means µ 1...k and variances σ 2 1...k . The overall distribution is a mixture of the class distributions, and each class contributes to the extent determined by the mixing proportion π k . A class with a high mixing proportion contributes more to the overall distribution than one with a low mixing proportion.

Unlike mixture models for individual participant data, random-effects mixture models must account for sampling variance. In the conventional random effects model, the observed sampling variance is treated as known to construct a weighted likelihood. This model describes a single normal distribution of true effects, with its two parameters µ, the overall mean effect, and τ² , the between-study variance or heterogeneity. Estimation. Estimation follows a three-stage procedure, based on the approach taken in the tidySEM R-package (Van Lissa et al., 2024). Theoretically, this method efficiently converges to global solutions while actively avoiding local solutions. First, the sample is divided into K clusters by applying k-means clustering to the observed effect sizes (Hartigan & Wong, 1979) 1 . Starting values for the class-specific mean effect sizes and residual heterogeneity variances are computed for each cluster using the DL-estimator (DerSimonian & Laird, 1986). Initial class proportions are set to the cluster proportions.

Next, simulated annealing is used to explore the likelihood surface and search for a global optimum (Pervez & Lee, 2015). Unlike numerical optimization, which greedily approaches the closest maximum from the starting values, simulated annealing occasionally accepts parameter changes that worsen model fit. This is useful, because mixture models' likelihood tends to have many local optima where a greedy algorithm could get stuck. Class Enumeration.

When the number of classes is known on theoretical grounds, it is sufficient to estimate the K-class model, and optionally, to test the hypotheses that K-classes suffice (Van Lissa et al., 2024). However, mixture modeling is also used for exploratory analyses of heterogeneity. In these cases, K is determined in a data-driven manner. Two well-established approaches to class enumeration are information theoretical methods and likelihood ratio tests. Information theoretical methods select the model with the lowest value on a statistic that balances model fit and complexity, by adding a complexity penalty to the minus two log-likelihood. Examples include the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC, Akaike, 1974), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC, Schwarz, 1978), or Integrated Completed Likelihood (Biernacki, Celeux, & Govaert, 2000), where the latter is unique in penalizing poor class separation as well. A limitation of these methods is that they do not offer significance tests. Likelihood ratio tests do, but are generally problematic for mixture models because when comparing a K -1 and K-class model, the K -1 model's parameters for one class are set to zero, which is on the boundary of the parameter space of the K-class model. The Bootstrapped Likelihood Ratio Test is an exception (BLRT, McLachlan, 1987).

However, its major limitation is that it re-estimates all models on all bootstrap samples (i.e., A recently introduced approach is Predictive Model Comparison (PMC). Based on the "lazy bootstrap", PMC repeatedly simulates data from each fitted model and evaluates how closely the simulated data distributions resemble the observed data (Kollenburg, Mulder, & Vermunt, 2018). For univariate data like effect sizes, one could use a metric that captures how "surprising" the observed data is in the context of the model-implied data distribution (Shannon, 1948). The surprisal statistic is given as the sum of negative log probability densities of the observed data in the (estimated) density distribution of a simulated dataset.

Lower values indicate that the the observed data have greater probability density in the simulated data distribution, which means it is a good approximation of the observed data distribution. Repeating the simulation yields a distribution of surprisal statistics, from which confidence intervals for differences in mean surprisal between models can be constructed. A model with K classes is preferred over a model with K -1 classes when the confidence interval indicates a reduction in expected surprisal.

The present study introduces another potential approach, based on cross-validation (Hastie, Tibshirani, & Friedman, 2009). In cross-validation, observed effect sizes are randomly split into multiple subsets. Each candidate model is fitted on effect sizes from all subsets except one, and the fitted model's predictive performance is evaluated on the one held-out subset. Whereas cross-validation normally compares predicted scores to observed scores, in mixture modeling, this is not possible because true class membership is unknown.

To circumvent this problem, we instead used the aforementioned surprisal statistic to evaluate how surprising the held-out effect sizes are in the estimated mixture distribution. A key distinction is that the likelihood of the held-out effect sizes can be directly evaluated with respect to the cross-validated model's parameters, rather then the simulated data density. Surprisal scores are summed across subsets. The model with the lowest value has the best out-of-sample predictive performance, and is selected. While this method also requires re-fitting models, it only estimates each model a few times, compared to BLRT's 100s or 1000s of times. At present, the best-performing class enumeration methods for individual participant data appear to be the BIC and BLRT (see Van Lissa et al., 2024).

However, their performance for random-effects mixture meta-analysis is unknown.

The Present Study.

We introduce and validate a method for estimating random-effects mixture meta-analysis models that accommodates known, study-specific sampling variances. In a comprehensive simulation study, we examine the method's parameter bias, CI coverage, and the performance of different approaches to class enumeration. To assess CI coverage, we used information-based standard errors to compute confidence intervals for class means and mixing proportions. For residual heterogeneity, we used profile likelihood confidence intervals, as these were found to have better coverage in preliminary analyses.

Discussion

423

Across both simulation studies, random-effects mixture models showed reasonable Class enumeration proved particularly challenging. None of the methods under consideration performed well, mostly because they underestimated the number of classes.

Given a trade-off between overfitting and underfitting, the latter is preferable for mixture models, whose parameter counts rapidly increases with the number of classes, which can lead to empirical under-identification when samples are small (Van Lissa et al., 2024).

Nevertheless, here, parsimony clearly comes at the cost of inaccuracy. AIC and BIC performed best, followed by PMC and CV. ICL performed at near-chance levels. Compared to AIC and BIC, PMC and CV have the advantage of offering class enumeration tests, but this comes at the expense of a loss of performance. These findings suggest that, even when mixture parameters can be estimated with reasonable accuracy, reliably identifying the underlying number of latent classes remains difficult.

Study 2 demonstrated that remix offers a computationally efficient and competitive alternative to the existing metaplus package. Both algorithms mostly found the same solutions; when their solutions differed, remix most often found the better solution.

Importantly, while the algorithms had comparable performance in terms of parameter estimation accuracy, remix was much faster to run (22 times). CI coverage of both methods was comparable for the pooled effect sizes, but could not be compared for residual heterogeneity and mixing proportions because metaplus did not provide confidence intervals for these parameters.

Taken together, results from these two studies indicate that remix reliably estimates the parameters of random-effects mixture meta-analysis. Moreover, remix performs similarly to metaplus for special cases covered by both implementations. However, statistical inference and reliable class enumeration remain challenging, particularly with small samples and poor class separation.

Strengths.

A strength of the present study is that it introduces the remix method for estimating random-effects mixture models, whose class means and heterogeneity parameters can be freely estimated, constrained to be equal, or fixed at specific values. This model is more general than those considered in previous research. We implemented this method in the remix() function of the metamixture package: a user-friendly wrapper with similar interface to existing functions like metafor::rma(yi, vi) for random-effects meta-analysis (Viechtbauer, 2010) and tidySEM::mx_profiles() (Van Lissa et al., 2024) for mixture models. The backend in OpenMx ensures efficient and fast estimation.

Furthermore, it is a general-purpose structural equation modeling framework, which means models can be fully customized (e.g., with equality constraints, starting values, auxiliary variable analyses) within its broader structural equation modeling framework.

Information-based standard errors and profile-likelihood confidence intervals are available out of the box in OpenMx, and the estimated models interface with many other packages.

Prior research has discussed mixture meta-analysis in the context of moderator analysis (Graefe, 2025; Zhang et al., 2022). With the tidySEM R-package, it is straightforward to conduct such moderator analyses with class as a predictor, or to predict class membership as an outcome, or even to conduct auxiliary analyses with a structural equation model of arbitrary complexity with class as a grouping variable. When examining class as a predictor of distal outcomes or moderator of auxiliary models, one option is to use the BCH approach (Bolck et al., 2004). It estimates a multi-group structural equation model using assigned class as grouping variable, accounting for classification error by weighting individual cases' contributions. A downside of BCH is that its standard errors are not valid.

When valid standard errors are required, the two-step approach is a suitable alternative (two_step()). This method first estimates the latent class model, then freezes its parameters and adds the auxiliary structural equation model so that the latter doesn't influence the estimation of the class solution (Bakk & Kuha, 2018).

In principle, both BCH and the two-step method can be used to identify significant predictors of class membership, but only in the univariate case. To this end, the candidate predictor is specified as an auxiliary variable. Based on an assumption of causal directionality, a significant omnibus test for between-class differences in the predictor is interpreted as evidence that it predicts class membership. A more flexible approach that can explicitly treat class membership as either a predictor an outcome is the pseudo-class method (Wang et al., 2005). It repeatedly draws random samples of class membership based on the individual posterior class probabilities, estimates the auxiliary model for each random sample, and then integrates the results across samples to account for classification uncertainty. All of these functions are implemented in tidySEM, along with functions for plotting and tabulating results (Van Lissa et al., 2024).

Limitations.

A key limitation of the method is that its CI coverage is low. This is a known limitation of mixture models, particularly with small sample sizes and model misspecification (Nylund, Asparouhov, & Muthén, 2007). Small samples are common in meta-analysis, and indeed, most sample sizes in our simulation study were below the minimum size that has been considered in studies on regular mixture models. Poor class separability may be a contributing cause of poor CI coverage in mixture models in general.

For mixture meta-analysis in particular, the additional heterogeneity introduced by sampling variance might further diminish coverage. This interpretation is bolstered by prior work that reported poor CI coverage for (different versions of) mixture meta-analysis (Beath, 2014; Negeri, 2025). Further research into the causes of poor CI coverage in mixture models is needed.

Another limitation is the poor performance of all class enumeration methods. This lack of power for class enumeration has been reported in previous work, especially for imperfect class separation (Tein, Coxe, & Cham, 2013). While we investigated conditions ranging from poor to very good class separation, performance of all class enumeration methods was subpar across conditions. While it is possible that some methods would perform better when classes are perfectly separated, we focused on investigating more realistic scenarios where class membership is somewhat ambiguous. Further research specifically focused on class enumeration methods could investigate conditions under which different class enumeration methods perform well.

One further limitation is that our simulation study focused on normal population distributions. When true effects follow skewed, heavy-tailed, or other non-normal distributions, class performance may deteriorate, as classes attempt to capture distributional idiosyncrasies (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2016). Future research could examine alternative within-class distributions.

The models presented here assume independent effect sizes, but commonly, multiple effect sizes are extracted from each paper, yielding a nested data structure. Future approaches might examine extensions of the method presented here to dependent effect sizes or multilevel data structures. The most straightforward approach would be to include all effect sizes in model estimation, ignoring the dependent structure, and then computing consistent standard errors for inference, using the sandwich estimator (Yuan, 2016). This is implemented as an ad-hoc solution in sandwich_remix(), but further research on its performance is needed. An alternative approach is to perform clustered bootstrapping, which is trivial to implement using R's replicate() function, but computationally expensive.

Recommendations for applied researchers. For general recommendations regarding mixture modeling, we refer readers to Van Lissa et al. (2024). Based on the present study, we provide several additional recommendations regarding random-effects mixture models in particular. These models can be reliably estimated, even with relatively small samples; they have low parameter bias on average and reasonably good CI coverage, albeit below nominal levels.

Because all class enumeration methods performed poorly, random-effects mixture meta-analysis is not very suitable for exploratory analysis, where the number of classes is unknown (Van Lissa et al., 2024). As all class enumeration methods tended to underfit, there is little risk of inadvertently selecting an overly complex solution. However, failure to identify additional classes should not be interpreted as evidence of absence of distinct subpopulations.

The primary use case for random-effects mixture meta-analysis is confirmatory analysis, where distinct subpopulations of true effects are expected based on a theoretical rationale. As explained in the introduction, such subpopulations could arise from different research traditions, intervention types, or study populations. Practices like p-hacking and publication bias, or the presence of outliers, may also produce distinct components in the observed effect-size distribution. Mixture models can capture these distinct sources of heterogeneity that would otherwise be obscured in a conventional random-effects model.

Researchers should be aware of the limitations associated with small samples, which are prevalent in meta-analyses. There is no rule of thumb for minimum sample size, as the information available for estimating a mixture model also depends on class proportions, separation, and within-class heterogeneity. In our simulation studies, the remix method performed acceptably with as few as 20 observations and moderate class separation. In general, small samples cause greater parameter bias, lower CI coverage, and poorer class enumeration performance. Pragmatically, researchers might favor parsimonious models (e.g., 2 classes), because small samples might not provide enough information to reliably estimate a higher number of classes. When drawing conclusions, researchers should exercise caution in generalizing from the sample to the population of all possible effect sizes. The safest approach is to consider the results of small sample meta-analysis as descriptive of the published effect sizes in a field, but not necessarily representative of its population distribution.

Researchers should always evaluate model convergence and the stability of the estimated solution. Mixture likelihoods can contain multiple local optima, and successful numerical optimization does not guarantee that the global maximum has been identified.

Although remix() uses simulated annealing and falls back on multiple random starts when the model fails to converge, researchers should inspect convergence diagnostics using tidySEM::mx_convergence(), and consider using random_starts() with a high number of nstarts to assess whether a better global optimum exists. Particular caution is warranted when one class has a very small proportion, when heterogeneity estimates approach their boundaries (e.g., due to a single outlier), or when parameter estimates change substantially across optimization attempts.

When performing auxiliary analyses (predicting class membership or testing for class differences in some outcome), always account for classification uncertainty. Simply assigning studies to their most likely class and treating these assignments as known biases these auxiliary analyses. The tidySEM package offers several approaches that account for classification uncertainty (BCH(), two_step(), and pseudo_class()). The two-step approach is particularly useful for predicting distal outcomes (or auxiliary models) from class membership, as it produces corrected standard errors. The pseudo-class method is most flexible when class membership can be either a predictor or an outcome.

Conclusion

Conventional random-effects models fail to account for discrete sources of heterogeneity.

There are many common scenarios in meta-analysis where heterogeneity is expected, for example, as a result of different research traditions, questionable research practices, or publication bias. In such cases, random-effects mixture meta-analysis is a useful technique to investigate unobserved sources of heterogeneity. The present study introduced and evaluated a generalized approach to random-effects mixture modeling that accounts for known sampling variances while allowing class means and heterogeneity parameters to be either freely estimated, constrained across classes, or fixed to known values. The remix() implementation, with a back-end in OpenMx, facilitates user-friendly model specification and customization, offers out-of-the box efficient estimation and information-and likelihood-based inference. The estimated models are compatible with other R-packages in the OpenMx ecosystem, like tidySEM, which can be used for valid analysis of predictors and outcomes of class membership, as well as reporting results in a publication-ready manner.

Two simulation studies showed that remix() performs well, and on par with existing solutions. Parameter recovery was unbiased on average and CI coverage was reasonable but below nominal levels. However, class enumeration methods performed poorly. These findings suggest that random-effects mixture meta-analysis is a useful method for explaining heterogeneity in effect sizes, especially when the number of classes is known based on theoretical or substantive considerations.
