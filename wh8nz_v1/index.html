What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Internally-displaced children often lack schooling and reside in highly multilingual contexts. In Nigeria, children must learn to read in a local language (e.g., Hausa), and English; here, language skills can transfer across languages to support biliteracy. Importantly, the strength of transfer is constrained by proficiency in language skills. Formal schooling and early life exposure are crucial for acquisition of language skills. In our sample of 1595 Nigerian internally-displaced children, we investigated how pre-displacement school enrollment and early life language language exposure (i.e., at home) predict language skills, and how language skills transfer across languages to support literacy. Pre-displacement school enrollment particularly supported phonological awareness, and early life language exposure particularly supported vocabulary. Phonological awareness was stronger in Hausa relative to English, resulting in greater transfer from Hausa to English. IDP context allows the investigation of cognition and environment to enrich theoretical models of biliteracy, and inform pedagogical practices.

Graphical Abstract Introduction

Education is a powerful tool for improving children's academic, social, and economic prospects. However, global access to schooling remains deeply unequal, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa, where 98.4 million children are out of school (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?5Lgc9j. Nigeria has one of the world's largest out-of-school populations, with nearly 19.7 million children not enrolled in school (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?HuqBYK. As a result, among the 105 million children in Nigeria, only 27% of 7-to 14-year-olds demonstrate literacy skills beyond the basic level, such as understanding simple sentences (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=ef8Y9k. Several factors contribute to challenges in access to education in Nigeria, including poverty, inaccessible infrastructure (e.g., geographic distance to schools), and gender disparities (2015 Nigeria National Education Data Survey (NEDS) In addition to structural and conflict-related barriers to learning among internally displaced people (IDPs), Nigeria is a highly multilingual country with more than 520 languages spoken, and nearly all children are bilingual or multilingual and face the challenge of learning in a language they may not speak at home (Bamgbose, 2005). Increasingly, mobility and migration from outside, and within, the country's borders is a contributor to linguistic diversity in Nigeria (Mgbemena, 2020). Resource-constrained, conflict-afflicted, multilingual contexts are rarely considered in the literature on literacy acquisition. Here, we investigate how children living in IDP camps learn to read in two languages.

Literacy Development

Biliteracy in IDP contexts can be examined through the Component Model of Reading, which posits that the acquisition of literacy skills is supported by cognitive, ecological (i.e., context-dependent), and psychological factors (Aaron et al., 2008). Here, we focus on cognitive and ecological predictors of letter-and word-level reading skills (i.e., emergent reading skills).

Learning to read involves mapping graphemes to phonemes and combining these phonemes into words to access meaning (Hulme, 2002) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=1EPP7z i.e., the orthography -phonologysemantics pathway, as described in the Triangle Model of reading (Harm & Seidenberg, 2004; Snow, 2006). Emergent reading is highly predicted by phonological awareness (PA), that is, the ability to recognize and manipulate sound structures at different levels of granularity (e.g., phonemic vs syllabic) in a language (Wagner & Torgesen, 1987). As word reading becomes more automatichttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=S3q9T3 readers can also access a word's meaning directly from its orthographic form and with less reliance on decoding (Harm & Seidenberg, 2004) , a process known as direct lexical access (i.e., orthography-semantics pathway). This type of word recognition is typically more robustly predicted by vocabulary than it is by PA, as experienced readers can access semantic information more automatically from a word's orthographic form (Anderson & Freebody, 1983; Berends & Reitsma, 2006; Harm & Seidenberg, 2004). These language skills (PA, vocabulary) have consistently been identified as predictors of reading outcomes across languages, diverse language scripts, and levels of orthographic transparency and granularity (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?6Jo4g2 Gottardo, 2002; Harm & Seidenberg, 2004; Greek: Protopapas et al., 2012; Spanish & Chinese: Ramirez et al., 2011; Spanish: Rinaldi & Páez, 2008; Lithuanian: Silinskas et al., 2024; Chinese: Song et al., 2015).

Biliteracy Development

Language skills also support reading skills cross-linguistically (Chung et al., 2019; Durgunoğlu et al., 1993; Geva & Ryan, 1993; Haigh et al., 2011; Keung & Ho, 2009; Wang et al., 2006). Cross-linguistic transfer is the ability of language skills in one language to facilitate the acquisition of literacy in the other language(s) (Odlin, 1989). Importantly, there is significantly less evidence for transfer of vocabulary relative to evidence for transfer of PA.

Transfer of vocabulary skills is often limited to etymologically similar languages (i.e., through cognate awareness) and is often task-dependent (Chung et al., 2019a; Lindsey et al., 2003; Ordóñez et al., 2002; Raudszus et al., 2018). Comparatively, robust evidence exists for the transfer of PA across both typologically similar and distant languages, and regardless of differences in tasks (Chung et al., 2019a; Dixon et al., 2025; Gottardo et al., 2023; Jasińska et al., 2019; Ordóñez et al., 2002; Veii & Everatt, 2005). For example, Dixon et al. (2025) found that children's PA skills in L1 (Hausa) predicted decoding skills in L2 (English), despite differences in orthographic transparency (i.e., English is opaque whereas Hausa is transparent), and less than 50% overlap in their phonemic inventories (Muhammad-Gombe, 2019).

Cross-linguistic transfer direction and strength are highly dependent on ecological factors that contribute to proficiency in the language of transfer (Chung et al., 2019a). Transfer typically occurs from the more to the less proficient language (e.g., Wang, Cheng, et al., 2006; Wang, Park, et al., 2006; Zhang et al., 2014). For instance, where L2 proficiency is greater, transfer is often observed from L2 to L1 (e.g., Chung et al., 2018; Pasquarella et al., 2011); and where L1 and L2 proficiency is similar, bidirectional transfer is observed (e.g., Deacon et al., 2007; Deacon et al., 2009). For example, in a sample of Syrian refugees in Canada, early life displacement experiences that were related to stronger L1 (i.e., Arabic) language proficiency (e.g., interim displacement in an L1-speaking country) also resulted in more transfer of L1 PA to L2 (e.g., English) literacy skills (Akkad & Jasińska, 2026). Students exposed to the language of literacy early in life (i.e., at home) demonstrate better overall language proficiency, which can support greater language transfer (Altman et al., 2014; Deacon et al., 2007; Flores et al., 2017; Huang & Choi, 2026; Jasińska & Petitto, 2018; Kim & Piper, 2019; Pham & Tipton, 2018; Rojas et al., 2016; Rydland & Grøver, 2021; Sorenson Duncan & Paradis, 2020).

The development of literacy-related language skills is further supported by formal schooling and literacy instruction. PA and literacy are reciprocally linked; children's PA improves with greater exposure to literacy instruction and mastery of the associations between letters and sounds (Bentin & Leshem, 1993; Eme, 2011; Jasińska et al., 2024; Morais & Kolinsky, 2019). Therefore, in-school children generally show greater PA relative to out-of-school children (e.g. see evidence from rural Tanzania; Alcock et al., 2018). Moreover, timely enrollment in formal schooling is associated with higher language and literacy proficiency over time; children who enroll in school late show poorer learning outcomes (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=RFjWnc Alcock et al., 2018; Birdsong & Molis, 2001; Burgess et al., 2002; Chansa-Kabali et al., 2014; Paradis, 2019; van den Bosch et al., 2019; Whitehead et al., 2024). Considering that displacement further delays school start for IDPs in Nigeria, with many displaced children instead attending temporary and makeshift educational programs, if any at all, IDPs are likely to experience difficulties in acquiring literacy-related language skills.

In the context of displacementhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=yuwL3l mismatches between the child's first language (i.e., spoken at home; L1) and the language they read in (i.e., here referred to as the language of literacy), and a lack of formal schooling are experiences that may reduce proficiency in language-related literacy skills (Bitetti & Hammer, 2016; Scarpino et al., 2019; Deacon et al., 2013; Hogan et al., 2005; Nagy et al., 1985) , thereby limiting cross-linguistic transfer for biliteracy acquisition.

The majority of research on biliteracy in displaced children includes children who resettled in middle-and high-income countries (i.e., externally-displaced refugees). Although these populations similarly show lags in literacy outcomes compared to age-matched peers (Al-Janaideh et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?loLy6M 2023; Kaplan et al., 2016) , the contexts in which many of these resettled refugees acquire literacy differ substantially from those experienced by internally displaced children who reside in majority-world settings. For instance, externally-displaced refugees who resettle in European or North-American countries experience relatively stable learning environments (i.e., with little or no more disruptions to schooling) and reside in predominantly monolingual settings (i.e., with a single language of instruction). Importantly, this body of research does not reflect the global distribution of displaced populations: the majority of refugees are internally displaced in low-income countries, where resources are often constrained, and education systems face greater instability (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=xKwF7Q. Jasińska & Guei, 2022; UNHCR, n.d.-b). The contextual differences of internally displaced populations' learning experiences are critical to consider; indeed, recent research suggests that unique displacement-related experiences differentially predict literacy acquisition (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=ilorOC Akkad & Jasińska, 2026; Gottardo et al., 2023).

The Present Study

The present study aimed to provide insight into predictors of literacy-related language skills and cross-linguistic transfer in children residing in IDP camps in Northeast Nigeria who were speakers of Hausa or Kanuri as well as English. We assume context-driven models of literacy and cross-linguistic transfer -i.e., the Component Model of Reading (Aaron et al., 2008a) and the Interactive Transfer Framework (Chung et al., 2019a) and focus on cognitive and ecological predictors of word-level reading skills (i.e., emergent reading skills) in both of a bilingual child's two languages. The consideration of ecological predictors of biliteracy is especially important in low-literacy and low-resource contexts, where cognitive predictors of literacy (e.g., PA and vocabulary) may themselves show floor effects (Nag et al., 2024; Whitehead et al., 2024).

We investigated the following research question: How are children's learning experiences in IDP camp contexts (pre-displacement school enrollment, language experiences) related to language skills, and how do these skills predict literacy within-and across-languages (e.g., Hausa → English and English → Hausa)? In line with the evidence in the literature, we hypothesized that ecological factors including pre-displacement school enrollment and home language experience were related to language and literacy skills. We predicted that children who received some schooling before displacement and who were exposed to the language of literacy at home would show better PA and vocabulary in that language. We also hypothesized that the direction (e.g., Hausa → English vs English → Hausa) and strength of cross-linguistic transfer will rely on relative language proficiency. Considering robust evidence showing that biliteracy acquisition is context-dependent (as motivated by theoretical models of literacy; Aaron et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?lJKfaN 2008; Chung et al., 2019) , it is crucial to examine how the unique and underexplored learning experiences of IDPs (i.e., learning in highly multilingual and low-resource environments) determine predictors of language and literacy skills to inform pedagogical practice and education policy.

Methods

Participants 1595 child participants (male = 627; female = 968) aged 5-15 years and their caregivers (N=1038) participated in the study. All families resided in IDP camps in Borno, Nigeria. Data for this study were collected as part of a one-year impact evaluation of the FastTrack intervention, a technology-driven educational intervention for foundational numeracy and literacy skills that ran between April 2025 and April 2026 (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?xVRv4X Jasińska et al., 2026). Only baseline data (i.e., before treatment assignment) were used in our analyses.

Procedure

The study obtained ethical approval from the University of Toronto and the University of Pennsylvania research ethics boards, as well as the Nigerian Ahmadu Bello University Teaching Hospital Shika Health Research Ethics Committee (#ABUTHZ/HREC/G55/2026). Participant oral consent (both informed consent from the caregiver and assent from the child) was obtained by trained staff members in Hausa or Kanuri, and trained AREAi enumerators administered all assessments to participants individually.

All child participants completed language and literacy measures in both the first language of literacy (i.e., either Hausa or Kanuri) and the second language of literacy (i.e., English), as well as language environment questions. Caregivers completed migration-and education-history questions about their child(ren).

In this sample, 642 participants reported speaking Hausa as their L1, and 879 reported speaking Kanuri as their L1. However, only 180 participants completed the language and literacy measures in Kanuri, whereas the remaining participants (N = 1415) completed the measures in Hausa. Of note, many of the participants whose L1 was Kanuri preferred to be assessed in Hausa. This may be attributed to the fact that Hausa is the lingua franca in Maidaguri, Borno State and children who received any schooling would likely have had Hause as the language of instruction; therefore, native Kanuri speakers' preference for Hausa interaction and assessment with our research team likely reflected a general preference for reading in Hausa, even among those who identified Kanuri as their L1.

Importantly, 50% of Hausa readers spoke Kanuri at home, and 5% spoke another language at home (i.e., Shuwa, Fulani, Margi, Kotoko, Gambai, Ngoza, Babur, or Marghi).

Comparatively, only 4% of Kanuri readers spoke another language (i.e., Hausa) at home.

Considering many participants evidently spoke another L1 than the one they read in; i.e., in which case Hausa/Kanuri is the L2 and English is the L3, we instead refer to Hausa and Kanuri as the first language of literacy, and English as the second language of literacy.

Measures

English PA was assessed using items from the International Development and Early Learning Assessment (IDELA; https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=AdsYfz.) and from Bruce (1964). English vocabulary depth was assessed using the https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=ZKkpWL Johnson 3rd Edition. The Hausa and Kanuri versions of the PA and vocabulary measures were developed by the research team according to the structure and content of the parallel English measures. The items in the Hausa and Kanuri PA and vocabulary measures were not always direct translations from English; they were sometimes adapted to include more contextually relevant words (e.g., Gwanjo, 'fairly used clothes; imported from elsewhere and sold'), and words that equal in linguistic difficulty (e.g., in terms of phonological structure and familiarity). See supplementary materials for the full list of items in the Hausa/Kanuri PA and vocabulary measures, their IPA transcriptions, and their translations. English, Hausa, and Kanuri letter-sound identification and word-level reading skills (i.e., familiar word reading and nonword reading) were assessed using the Early Grade Reading Assessment (EGRA; https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=jrIAll. We calculated the internal consistency of the newly developed language measures to ensure their reliability in this context (see Table 1 in supplementary materials). Considering that Cronbach's alpha underestimates reliability when data have high floor effects, ordinal alpha was used as a measure of internal consistency for our data.

Calculations involved estimating correlations between latent continuous variables underlying the binary response categories and then calculating alpha from the correlation matrix. Initial phoneme ID and initial phoneme deletion are components of the PA composite, and synonym and antonym are components of the vocabulary depth composite. An ordinal ɑ coefficient greater than 0.7 was considered acceptable; all English, Hausa, and Kanuri language measures showed good to excellent internal consistency (0.84 < ɑ < 0.98).

Phonological Awareness

PA was assessed with initial phoneme identification (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=R1f5w0 Pisani et al., 2018) and deletion (Bruce, 1964) tasks. In the initial phoneme identification task, children were asked to listen to a sequence of words and then identify the word that began with a particular sound.

For example, in English, children heard the /k/ sound then chose the word that started with /k/ among "knee", "fish", and "cow". See supplementary materials for examples of Hausa and Kanuri items.

In the initial phoneme deletion task, a word was read aloud and children were asked to repeat this while omitting its initial sound. For example, in English, "tower" without /t/ is "our".

Children skipped the initial phoneme deletion task if they failed to provide a single correct response on the initial phoneme identification task. A composite PA score was computed by summing the scores of the two tasks. See supplementary materials for examples of Hausa and Kanuri items.

Vocabulary Depth (Synonyms/Antonyms)

Vocabulary was measured using synonym and antonym generation tasks (McGrew & Woodcock, 2001). Children were presented with five words and asked to produce a word with the same meaning (e.g., in English, "jump" for "hop"); and then were presented with five words and asked to produce a word with the opposite meaning (e.g., in English, "no" for "yes"). A composite vocabulary score was computed by summing the scores of the two tasks. See supplementary materials for examples of Hausa and Kanuri items.

Letter/Sound Identification

Children were shown a list of letters and asked to provide either the letter's name or sound. After three practice items with feedback, children read a list of 100 items. The task ended after one minute or if the child failed to provide a single correct response within the first ten items.

Word Reading

Word reading was assessed with 50 high-frequency words (e.g., in English, "you", "he", "flight"; in Hausa, "ke" (you),"ta" (of), "ina" (I am); in Kanuri, "ti" (body), "balo" (scar), "kora" (big)). After three practice items, children read aloud as many of these words as they could. The task ended after one minute or if the child did not provide a single correct response on the first five words.

Non-Word Reading

Non-word reading was assessed using 50 monosyllabic pseudo-words (e.g., in English, "nad", "lus", "dit"; in Hausa, "soto", "lam", "tita"; in Kanuri, "lo", "peke", "kosi") that conformed to the phonotactic rules of each language. After three practice items with feedback, children read aloud as many of these words as they could. The task ended after one minute or if the child did not provide a single correct response on the first five words.

Language and Education Background Questionnaires

Self-reported information about education history and the home language environment was obtained from a background questionnaire adapted from the Alberta Language Environment Questionnaire, 4th edition (ALEQ-4; Paradis, 2011). Children reported on early life exposure to Hausa, Kanuri, and English (i.e., whether they spoke the language with parents at home), and parents reported on whether the child had received any schooling prior to displacement (i.e., before moving to the IDP camps).

Multidimensional Poverty Index

The Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) was used to obtain a comprehensive measure of family poverty status that is well suited to contexts of displacement and crisis where poverty extends beyond income to include four dimensions that have equally nested weights: Health, Education, Standard of Living, and Assets (Admasu et al., 2021; Alkire & Foster, 2011). This approach has been applied in comparable low-resource settings, including rural Côte d'Ivoire (Wolf et al., 2024). See supplementary materials for the specific indicators used for each dimension of poverty and their respective weighting.

We calculated the MPI score if a household had valid data on two or more dimensions, and missing or ineligible responses were treated as nondeprived (i.e., 1 instead of 0). The deprivation score per household was calculated by taking the weighted sum of the number of deprivations and thus the score for each household lies between 0 and 1; with higher scores indicating greater poverty. We standardized MPI scores for further analysis.

2.4 Analysis

To address our research question, we first examined whether pre-displacement school enrollment and early life language exposure (i.e., whether the child spoke the language at home) predicted PA and vocabulary depth in Hausa or Kanuri, and in English. We then examined how PA and vocabulary predicted within-language reading skills in Hausa, English, and Kanuri; and across-language reading skills in the Hausa-English language pair (see below for discussion of analytic limitation for Kanuri-English). We examined how PA and vocabulary depth predicted literacy skills as a composite of letter-sound identification, familiar word reading, and nonword reading. To create the literacy composite, we summed participants' raw scores across letter-sound identification, familiar word reading, and nonword reading (/100 for letter-sound identification; /50 for familiar word and nonword reading). Summing raw scores instead of averaging or standardizing scores was done to preserve zero-inflated and integer composite values, necessary characteristics of outcome variables in zero-inflated models.

Literacy scores were positively skewed and zero-inflated, as such, zero-inflated regression models were conducted. When the outcome variable was also significantly overdispersed (as indicated by theta values), zero-inflated negative binomial (NEGBIN) regression models were used; if not significantly overdispersed, zero-inflated Poisson regression models were used, instead. For an additional check of model choice, the Akaike Information Criteria (AIC) fit index was calculated for both Poisson and NEGBIN versions of each model; the model with the lowest AIC index was selected. The results of both the count model (i.e., including only non-zero scores) and the zero-inflated model (i.e., including also zero-count scores) were included and interpreted. Age, MPI, and gender were included in all models as covariates.

For descriptive statistics, participants' scores on PA, vocabulary depth, letter/sound ID, familiar word reading, and nonword reading were divided by the total number of items in each task to derive standard accuracy scores (i.e., 0 to 1) for easier comparisons. We controlled for the false discovery rate (FDR) across multiple hypothesis testing by adjusting raw p-values using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (i.e., by extracting q-values). A q-value threshold of (q < 0.05) was considered statistically significant.

A power analysis was run using the pwr package in R (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=h4oDHI) to determine the required sample size to answer the research questions. Assuming α = 0.05, f² = 0.20 (between small [0.15] and medium [0.35] effect sizes), power (1-b) = 0.8, and 7 predictors in the model (including 3 covariates), a sample size of 71 was considered adequate. In the Kanuri readers group, however, only 20 participants scored above a zero on at least one of the literacy tasks in Kanuri, and only 55 participants scored above a zero on at least one of the literacy tasks in English (see supplementary materials Figure 1). Therefore, we were underpowered to detect effects of within-and across-language predictors of Kanuri literacy outcomes and English literacy outcomes in the Kanuri readers group, and excluded this sample in analyses of literacy outcomes. Comparatively, in the Hausa readers group, 570 participants scored above a zero on at least one of the literacy tasks in Hausa, and 659 participants scored above a zero on at least one of the literacy tasks in English (see supplementary materials Figure 1). Therefore, we had enough power to detect effects of within-and across-language predictors of Hausa literacy outcomes and English literacy outcomes in the Hausa readers group.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?BRuY27. See supplementary materials for functions used from external R packages; all remaining data cleaning and analyses functions belong to base R.

Validity of Language and Literacy Measures

Kendall tau-b correlations, as an indicator of construct validity, were calculated between the language and literacy measures in Hausa or Kanuri and English (see Figure 2). Kendall's tau-b is a rank-based measure that accounts for non-normal data and tied ranks; making it a more suitable metric for positively skewed and zero-inflated data where many observations share the same value (i.e., zero) relative to Pearon, Spearman, or Kendall tau-a correlations. All language and literacy data showed significant positive associations within-and across-languages, attesting to the convergent validity of these measures.

Discussion

This study investigated how pre-displacement school enrollment and early life language exposure relate to literacy-related language skills (i.e., PA and vocabulary depth) and cross-linguistic transfer in children residing in IDP camps in Northeast Nigeria. Overall, our samples of Hausa-English readers and Kanuri-English bilinguals showed very poor language and literacy skills; most participants could not read in either their languages.

Pre-displacement school enrollment and early life language exposure supported literacy-related language skills (i.e., PA and vocabulary depth) in low-literacy and multilingual settings. We also found that stronger cross-linguistic transfer of PA to emergent literacy skills was observed in the Hausa → English direction relative to the English → Hausa direction, and that Hausa-English readers performed significantly better on Hausa PA relative to English PA.

The Paucity of Schooling is Nigeria is Related to Poor Language Skills

In line with the general consensus in the literature, Hausa-English readers who had some schooling prior to displacement were advantaged in their literacy-related language skills, particularly in PA. In our sample of Hausa-English readers, only 24% reported having some schooling prior to displacement. Nigeria has faced protracted and compounded issues resulting in inaccessible schooling. Classrooms often do not have enough educational resources and are significantly overcrowded, resulting in ineffective instruction (Adekola, 2007; Bolaji et al., 2019; Boulton et al., 2009). Schooling accessibility is further compromised by health riskshttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?DkpDhE such as poor water and sanitation (Bakari, 2009; UNDP Nigeria, 2010) , and lack of nutritional support (ActionAid, 2011; UNICEF, 2009 UNICEF, , 2012)). In more rural communities, compulsory household duties (e.g., having to look over younger siblings) and child labour further increase rates of absenteeism (Bakari, 2009; British Council, 2012; National Population Commission (Nigeria) & RTI International, 2011; Robson, 2004; UNICEF, 2012).

Relatedly, only 6% of our sample of Kanuri-English bilinguals had attended school at some point prior to their displacement. Research on the availability of resources for Kanuri-L1 speakers in the Northeast highlights a paucity in Kanuri education; schools that teach children to read in Kanuri are significantly more limited in academic resources relative to schools that teach children to read in Hausa (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?PlcvHP likely resulting in lower rates of school attendance for Kanuri-L1 speakers. Likely a direct result of lower rates of school attendance, our sample of Kanuri-English bilinguals performed significantly worse on almost all Kanuri and English language measures relative to our sample of Hausa-English readers's performance on Hausa and English language measures.

Late Exposure to the Language of Literacy is Related to Poor Language Skills

In line with the general evidence in the literature, Hausa-English readers who had early life exposure to Hausa (i.e., spoke it at home) were advantaged in their Hausa literacy-related language skills, particularly their Hausa vocabulary depth (Daskalaki et al., 2020; Gagarina & Klassert, 2018; Jia et al., 2014; Jia & Paradis, 2020). Home language exposure is typically more colloquial and reflective of everyday language use, supporting language-specific vocabulary (Zhang et al., 2025). Importantly, early life language exposure was also positively related to PA in Hausa-English readers attesting to the relevance of early life language exposure in preparing children for literacy skills acquisition.

Exposure to the language of literacy only at an older age is characteristic of other low-resource and highly multilingual contexts where children can experience delayed school starts. For example, in Côte d'Ivoire, many children start school at a later age (Gulematova et al., 2016), delaying their exposure to the language of literacy, and impacting the acquisition of literacy-related language skills (Brice et al., 2024; Whitehead et al., 2024; Jasińska et al., 2024; Hannon et al., 2025). With the exception of similar work done in Côte d'Ivoire, contexts where (1) children speak one language at home (e.g., Kanuri, Shuwa, or Fulani) but (2) attend school and learn to read in an entirely new language (e.g., Hausa and English) (3) without prior literacy experience (i.e., due to disrupted schooling) have not been considered in research on biliteracy acquisition.

Stronger Relation Between PA and Literacy in Hausa Relative to English

Hausa PA, but not Hausa vocabulary depth, was associated with Hausa literacy, and both English PA and English vocabulary depth were associated with English literacy. Of note, the strength of association between Hausa PA and Hausa literacy was stronger than that of English PA and English literacy. Relatedly, children scored significantly better on PA in Hausa relative to English. These results attest to the role of PA as a foundational skill in literacy acquisition, with a particular relevance in early literacy achievement (Bentin & Leshem, 1993; Calfee et al., 1973; Ehri et al., 200; Eme, 2011; Jasińska et al., 2024; Lonigan et al., 2000; Morais & Kolinsky, 2019; Torgesen et al., 1994; Wagner et al., 1997).

The observed lower English PA scores are likely explained by a lack of pre-displacement school enrollment (where children would be exposed to the English language), compounded with the paucity of literacy instruction that children who were enrolled in school were exposed to. Indeed, a Phonological Awareness and Early Grade Reading Instruction survey administered in an IDP primary school in Northern Nigeria revealed that literacy instruction was limited to teaching the alphabet, as teachers lacked the training to teach students PA skills (e.g., rhyming, blending, segmentation) (Egbe et al., 2026). Relatedly, the observed effects of English vocabulary depth on English literacy are likely a proxy for overall language abilities, exposure to the English language, and potentially other demographic characteristics (e.g., higher socio-economic status) (Mascheretti et al., 2018; Villa et al., 2025).

Cross-Linguistic Transfer Observed in Typologically Distant Languages

Cross-linguistic support was also associated with stronger literacy skills across Hausa and English, particularly for the transfer of PA to literacy skills. In our sample of Hausa-English readers, Hausa PA was positively related with English literacy, and English PA was positively related with Hausa literacy. These results provide support for the role of cross-linguistic support in biliteracy acquisition, regardless of differences in orthographic transparency and phonetic inventories in the language pair (Akkad & Jasińska, 2026; Chung et al., 2019a; Deacon et al., 2007b; Durgunoğlu et al., 1993).

In our sample of Hausa-English readers, children were exposed to Hausa in the Roman Script, i.e., similar to English's script. However, unlike English, Hausa is an orthographically transparent language, with the exception of distinguishing vowel length, and tone, which necessitate diacritics (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=KxS5KV. When learning to read in Hausa, students are exposed to Hausa without the diacritics, resulting in some orthographic opacity; but Hausa remains more orthographically transparent relative to English. Additionally, many differences exist between Hausa (30 consonants; 12 vowels) and English (24 consonants; 20 vowels) phonetic inventories, with less than 50% overlap between their consonant and vowel inventories. The observed transfer of PA across unique phonological systems (i.e., regarding transparency of grapheme-to-phoneme correspondences, and number/type of recognized phonemes) presents evidence that cross-linguistic transfer is based on linguistic processes fundamental to reading in all languages (Geva & Siegel, 2000). (UNESCO, 2025; Ball et al., 2022). In 2022, Nigeria introduced a dual-language education policy which mandates that children learn in one of three recognized languages of instruction (i.e., Hausa, Igbo, and Yoruba) as well as in English (Obiakor, 2024). This approach is motivated by evidence that (1) when the language of instruction is the child's L1, literacy performance is higher, and (2) the acquisition of L2 literacy is facilitated by the automatic transfer of strong foundational literacy skills in the child's L1 (i.e., cross-linguistic transfer) (Dutcher, 2001; Lavoie, 2008; Muskin, 1999; Obiakor, 2024; Takam & Fassé, 2020). Ultimately, however, the dual-language policy of Nigeria was reversed in 2025 when research reflected poor academic performance in schools (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=8R58ji attesting to the complexity of bilingual education in highly multilingual contexts (August & Shanahan, 2017; Ball et al., 2022; Hoff, 2013; Slavin & Cheung, 2005). Indeed, in our sample of participants, nearly half of participants read and were assessed in a language that was not their L1 (i.e., that they did not speak at home).

Moreover, studying the educational experiences of displaced children provides us with unique opportunities to advance the evidence base of models of reading and cross-linguistic transfer. Specifically, the educational experiences of displaced children provided evidence into the role of contextual variables (e.g., lack of schooling and early life language exposure) into the acquisition of literacy-related language skills, as suggested by the Component Model of Reading.

Additionally, investigating a context where children learn to read in two languages and in a linguistically diverse setting provides insight into the dynamics of cross-linguistic transfer (i.e., its direction and strength) and how these dynamics rely on relative proficiency in the language pair, as suggested by the Interactive Transfer Framework (Chung et al., 2019a).

By the end of 2025, more than 68 million people were internally displaced persons, constituting the majority of the world's forcibly displaced population (58%) (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ikKplY Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR, n.d.). For IDPs, protracted absences from school pre-displacement and during-displacement means that students urgently need to be integrated into education programs. In such cases of educational loss, international guidance emphasizes learning recovery rather than access alone, such as prioritizing foundational literacy skills (e.g., teaching PA and vocabulary depth), meeting learners of different ages at their current level of literacy (i.e., emergent), and adapting teaching to learners' linguistic and contextual needs (e.g., disproportionately little resources for large groups of children learning to read in Kanuri) (UNICEF, 2022; Mendenhall & Hough, 2024). Critically, beyond supporting the acquisition of literacy-related language skills, schooling and education programs protects children in the Northeast from exposure to violence, exploitation, child labour, and recruitment by armed groups (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=fQG8n7 https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?broken=nQv7oj.

Limitations and Future Directions

Much of the previous research on educational outcomes of children in similar low-literacy and high-poverty contexts accounted for potential differences at demographic levels such as location (i.e., village or camp) and family (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?iAWxRE Ball et al., in preparation; Brice et al., 2024, under review, in preparation; Wortsman et al., 2024). This is because heterogeneity in learning experiences is expected when individuals live in highly unstable environments.

Considering our sample did not show enough variance in their language and literacy scores, as evident in the highly zero-inflated distributions, our models could not account for additional variance contributed by IDP camp name or family identification. Future research looking into post-intervention outcomes (which are likely to have less zero-inflated distributions) could investigate whether these contexts further distinguish children in their educational outcomes.

Additionally, although our analysis of internal consistency showed good overall reliability for the English, Hausa, and Kanuri language measures, this high internal consistency might be due to many participants scoring zero on most items in all tasks at baseline. In other words, zero inflation is likely to reduce variability in the participants' scores, resulting in no directionality in associations between measures as well as high correlations in item statistics. Future research should continue to examine internal consistency of measures in samples with wider skill ranges.

Moreover, the majority of biliteracy and cross-linguistic transfer research comes from samples where children are receiving instruction in the language-pair or in at least one of the languages at the time of data collection (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?irfksq Akkad & Jasińska, 2026; Ball et al., 2022; Dixon et al., 2025; K. K. Jasińska et al., 2019). However, considering the learning context in IDP camps in Northeast Nigeria, most participants in our sample were out of school or had no prior instruction. Future research with post-intervention data could inform whether predictors of biliteracy skills and directions of cross-linguistic transfer change as readers are exposed to more regular instruction.

Conclusion

This study explored how unique learning experiences of internally displaced children relate to language skills, and how these language skills support emergent literacy skills withinand across-languages. Schooling and early life language exposure to the languages that children have to read in are crucial for the acquisition of language and literacy skills, but are not common experiences for children in IDP camps. With limited language skills, children in IDP camps show high rates of illiteracy and low literacy skills. Despite overall poor language skills, children show cross-linguistic support for literacy, attesting to the role of cross-linguistic support for biliteracy acquisition. In Hausa-English readers, within-and across-language skills supported literacy performance in both Hausa and English, but the relative strength of the contributions of each of the predictors was different (i.e., as indicated by their standardized beta values).

Regarding predictors of Hausa literacy, both Hausa and English PA were positively associated with Hausa literacy, but Hausa PA compared to English PA was more strongly associated with Hausa literacy (see Table 3 and Figure 3); both in the count and zero-inflated models. Comparatively, regarding predictors of English literacy, both English PA and Hausa PA were positively associated with English literacy. Hausa PA compared to English PA was more strongly associated with English literacy in the count model, whereas English PA compared to Hausa PA was more strongly associated with English literacy in the zero-inflated model (see Table 3 and Figure 3).

Overall, we see greater transfer of Hausa PA to English literacy (i.e., Hausa → English direction) than we do English PA to Hausa literacy (i.e., English → Hausa direction). Of note, children also had stronger Hausa PA relative to English PA (see Figure 2), in alignment with the greater support offered by Hausa PA for both Hausa and English literacy. While children's vocabulary depth was also stronger in Hausa relative to English (see Figure 2), only English vocabulary depth predicted Hausa and English literacy.

The Relative Strength and Direction of Transfer Are Proficiency Dependent

Importantly, however, cross-linguistic transfer of PA in our sample of Hausa-English readers was overall stronger in the Hausa → English direction relative to the English → Hausa direction. In line with this finding, Hausa-English readers had overall greater proficiency in PA in Hausa relative to English. These findings align with the Interactive Transfer Framework's directionality hypothesis (Chung et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?pXi2D7 2019) , and provide evidence for this hypothesis in the Hausa-English language pair. Of note, this sample's stronger relative proficiency in Hausa relative to English contrasts with previous research findings from bilinguals in Nigeria who are enrolled in school (Jegede, 2025). Students exposed to instruction in both a local language and English in Nigeria typically show either similar performance in literacy outcomes in each language, or greater performance in English outcomes; likely as a result of differences in quality of instruction in each language, the availability of resources in each language, and challenges in accommodating differences between the language the child is exposed to at home and the language the child is learning to read in (Jegede, 2025; Ball et al., 2024). If children's English literacy skills improve with exposure to formal schooling, cross-linguistic transfer from English to Hausa could be observed. Deacon et al., 2007 provide evidence for such dynamics of cross-linguistic transfer: first-to-third grade English-L1 students in a French immersion program only showed transfer from English to French earlier on (i.e., before receiving formal instruction in French); with time and increased French language skills, children instead showed transfer from French to English.

Implications and Theoretical Significance

More than 40% of children globally lack access to education in the language they speak at home and understand fluently, more frequently in low-resource and highly multilingual settings their less proficient language. Our findings provide novel insight into biliteracy development in low-resource and highly-multilingual contexts, and inform the relatively scarce evidence-base of the Interactive Transfer Framework. Finally, our study motivates the need for additional investigation into how underexplored, but widespread learning experiences relate to children's education outcomes in similar contexts and beyond.
