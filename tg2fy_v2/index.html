Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

This study is a secondary analysis of publicly available data from the Center for Research and Innovation in Translation and Translation Technology (CRITT) Translation Process Research Database (TPR-DB; Carl et al., 2016), hosted by CRITT at Kent State University.

The two corpora analysed, BML12 (Carl & Hill, 2012) and SG12 (Carl et al., 2015; Nitzke, 2019), were collected by their original investigators, and ethical oversight of that data collection, including participant consent, rested with those investigators and their institutions. No new data were collected, and no additional ethical approval was sought.

1 Introduction 1.Metacognition

Metacognition describes cognition directed at one's own cognitive processes, and involves the ability to evaluate perceptual states, memories, or decisions (Flavell, 1979; Fleming, 2017). Metacognition includes both monitoring processes, which track information about the state of a cognitive process, and control processes, which use that information to regulate behaviour. Metacognitive control allows for seeking relevant information, correcting errors and choosing a strategy.

Metacognitive knowledge concerns beliefs about learning, strategies, and conditions a! ecting performance, while metacognitive skill concerns monitoring confidence and applying an appropriate strategy (Dutemple et al., 2024).

In the context of decision-making during perceptual tasks, metacognitive judgements constitute confidence about decisions. Importantly levels of confidence alone do not correspond with metacognitive ability. Rather, metacognitive sensitivity refers to the extent to which confidence discriminates between correct and incorrect decisions (Fleming & Lau, 2014), and reflects the precision of underlying internal representations on which decisions are based (Fleming & Daw, 2017). Confidence can incorporate evidence supporting a choice, information accumulated after commitment, prior expectations, and beliefs about the decision-making system itself. For this process to be truly metacognitive, second order judgements formed on first order responses must be based on a meta-level representation of the object-level decision (Fleming, 2024). In contrast, second order confidence judgements based on stimulus information or object-level representations may not require metacognition and can be attained through 'strength-of-evidence' heuristics.

Such models are called 'direct translation' models (Kepecs & Mainen, 2012; Fleming et al., 2012). Alternatively, when additional processing occurs after a decision, the model is post-decisional (Fleming et al., 2012; Yeung & Summerfield, 2012). In the second order or dual-pathway model proposed by Fleming and Daw (2017), decision and confidence are correlated hidden states. In this framework, as direct access to first-order states is unavailable to confidence calculations, performed actions may reveal additional information for self-evaluation through reflection. Both this model and the post-decisional model could describe a metacognitive process. Subjective confidence is thought to be computed by the mismatch between top-down expectations and bottom-up sensory signals. Distinctions are also made between prospective and retrospective metacognitive judgements. In the same perceptual discrimination paradigm, retrospective judgements were strongly influenced by the speed and accuracy of the preceding decision, whereas prospective predictions were influenced by confidence over a longer preceding time window. Global confidence levels were correlated across the prospective and retrospective judgements, suggesting a shared contribution of a stable trait-like metacognitive e! ciency and distinct local information (Fleming et al., 2016).

1.2 Signal Detection Theory

The Standard psychophysical approach to studying metacognition is Signal Detection Theory (SDT) (Green & Swets, 1966). SDT experiments for studying metacognition involve perceptual decisions (Type-1 task) and confidence reports on them (Type-2 task) to derive measures of performance and metacognition. In visual discrimination paradigms, a Type-1 task usually involves deciding whether a stimulus is present or not. After this perceptual decision, the participant estimates whether that choice was correct. Type-2 judgements are therefore retrospective evaluations of the Type-1 decision (Galvin et al., 2003; Massoni et al., 2014).

A participant's performance is measured through sensitivity to the stimulus d → and is the standardised distance between the means of the two Type-1 distributions. Response bias is represented by a decision threshold or criterion, c which determines the amount of evidence needed to give a certain response. When c is halfway between the means of the two distributions, the participant is unbiased. Similar Type-2 measures can be obtained to quantify metacognitive abilities where in this case the two distributions represent judgements of incorrect and correct performance of Type-1 tasks. When the conditional probabilities of confidence given correct or incorrect performance are cumulated and plotted as a Receiving Operator Characteristic (ROC) curve, a model-free measure of metacognitive sensitivity can be obtained by calculating the area under the curve (AUROC2). This measure gives the probability that a randomly selected correct trial receives higher confidence than a randomly selected incorrect trial (Massoni et al., 2014). This measure avoids equal-variance Gaussian assumptions and is una"ected by a tendency to lean towards one response, giving it advantages over correlational measures when response bias is a concern. To eliminate the influence of Type-1 sensitivity and criterion on Type-2 sensitivity, a di"erent measure -meta-d → assumes a single type 2 ROC is completely determined by an equal-variance Gaussian SDT model (Maniscalco & Lau, 2012). Meta-d → gives the value of Type-1 performance that an ideal observer would need to generate the observed Type-2 data. If meta-d → equals d → , confidence is based on as much evidence as predicted for an SDT-optimal observer; a lower value implies loss of decision-relevant evidence in confidence (Fleming 2017; Maniscalco & Lau, 2012). The ratio of meta-d → /d → gives the metacognitive e! ciency, M-ratio (Maniscalco & Lau, 2012). An M-ratio of 1 represents the ideal observer case in which meta-d → equals d → . An M-ratio of 0.7 means that meta-d → is 70% of d → , meaning 30% of the evidence available to the Type-1 decision was lost when confidence was formed (Fleming & Lau, 2014). AUROC2 values are bounded between 0.5 and 1.00 for chance to perfect discrimination. Another SDT-derived measure, m-distance measures propensity to report high or low confidence independent of decision bias and task performance (Sherman et al., 2018).

Expected values of meta-d → or AUROC2 can vary across studies and depend on factors including task design, sample, and model assumptions. In one perceptual-confidence experiment involving a dot-comparison task (Massoni et al., 2014) mean AUROC2 was approximately .67 to .66 across subtasks. In another graded-confidence visual experiment, age di"erences in AUROC2 were found where values ranged from 0.74 for 5-6 year-olds to 0.79 for adults in one task and 0.73 (age 5-6) to 0.75 (adults) in another task (Watanabe et al., 2025).

1.3 Implicit and explicit measures of metacognition

An explicit Type-2 judgement involves an expressed evaluation of one's own performance (typically a confidence rating). On the other hand, implicit measures do not require a direct confidence report. Examples include response time, opting-out decisions, options to request additional information, and post-decision wagering (Dutemple et al., 2024; Fleming & Lau, 2014).

In a preschool visual-identification experiment, explicit confidence reports and implicit clue requests were both positively correlated to each other and school readiness (based on literacy, numeracy, and visual perception). (Dutemple et al., 2024). Other work has shown that children seek further information (Call & Carpenter, 2001) or opt out (Balcomb & Gerken, 2008) more when uncertain. However, both measures may not be formed of the same internal information: A study comparing explicit confidence reports with implicit wagers found that patients with frontoinsular stroke (FIS) and behavioural variant frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD) showed impaired wagering but accurate confidence reports, whereas Alzheimer's disease patients were more likely to su"er impairment in both (García-Cordero et al., 2019). Explicit and implicit measures may therefore distinguish inaccurate self-evaluation from failure to use available evaluative information to control action. Moreover, Nicholson and colleagues (2021) suggest that explicit metacognitive tasks involving reflection and verbal reports on internal states draw on metarepresentational resources, whereas implicit task gambles may not require this, corresponding with first order models of metacognition (Fleming & Daw, 2017).

In one post-decision wagering task, (Moreira et al., 2018) certainty readouts about being incorrect were associated with meta-d → exceeding d → , meaning confidence discriminated correct from incorrect decisions better than accuracy alone would allow, suggesting that confidence computation draws on information beyond the decision variable itself. Other implicit indicators, such as response time, information seeking and clue selection, must first be translated to ordinal second-order choices based on object-level accuracy to obtain measures of metacognitive sensitivity.

1.4 Dynamic metacognitive monitoring

A standard metacognitive SDT experiment involves independent trials consisting of a Type-1 and Type-2 tasks with the objective of disentangling confidence bias, sensitivity and e! ciency. Most everyday tasks involving metacognition involve decisions and confidence as processes that unfold over time as evidence accumulates. Evidence accumulation models have described confidence as the di"erence between maximal evidence and a decision bound (Pereira et al., 2021). Drift-di"usion simulations and binary perceptual task data show that M-ratio changes with response caution, both with and without instructions to prioritise speed or accuracy. A proposed dynamic measure, v-ratio, did not show this speed-accuracy e"ect (Desender et al., 2022).

1.5 Translation Process Research and Metarepresentation

The process of human translation, being inherently metalinguistic and context-dependent makes it a valuable domain for studying metacognition: translators assess implicit and explicit knowledge about semantics, syntax, context-derived relevance and make cross-linguistic judgements about semantic content, genre, register, and usage (Halverson, 2018) in order to make production decisions.

In the monitor model (Tirkkonen-Condit, 2005; Carl & Dragsted, 2012) translation is initially guided by automated routines, while monitoring becomes more prominent when the target text presents a challenge. Bursts of production, pauses, gaze patterns, edits, and revision behaviour can be tracked with keystroke and eye-tracking data, along with timestamps, allowing for mappings between observed reading and typing behaviour and source and target text token positions (Carl, 2009; Carl & Jakobsen, 2009) This data does not give direct access to internal cognitive processes but temporal resolution means that monitoring can be examined as a process that unfolds with time through the gradual knowledge accumulation of a source text.

Translation Process Research (TPR) currently uses mixed-method approaches such as qualitative meta-analysis (Xie & Wang, 2025) annotated translation (Costa & da Silva, 2023) and triangulation (Alves, 2003). for studying metacognition. Xie and Wang (2025) conducted a meta-analysis on 23 studies finding a positive association between metacognition and translation competence. However, this study aggregated existing measures, including questionnaire and interview-based measures, rather than measures of metacognitive sensitivity or e! ciency.

Alves, (2005) presents a framework where meta-reflection bridges procedural skill (automatized translation performance) and declarative and explanatory articulation (knowing what and why translation decisions were made) by allowing procedural knowledge to become increasingly consciously accessible to the translator as expertise increases. However, this framework provides no quantitative measure of metareflection. Furthermore, Costa & da Silva (2023) argue for concomitant annotation during translation as a pedagogical approach instead of retrospective annotation as the latter does not require the monitoring processes that occur during translation. They mention that empirical evidence is needed to support this claim. Halverson (2018) expresses the need for sharper distinctions between metalinguistic knowledge, awareness, and ability, speculating that metalinguistic knowledge precedes awareness and ability. Gutt (2004) suggests that metarepresentational ability is an important aspect of translation competence as it involves managing mismatches between di"erent cognitive environments (Sperber & Wilson, 1995) and audiences. Another conceptualisation of translator competence (Alves & Gonçalves, 2007) highlights the importance of metacognition in synthesizing task-specific and general competence. In line with the domain-general nature of metacognition, Whyatt and Naranowicz (2020) suggest that planning, self-monitoring, and self-revision can transfer from translation to intralingual paraphrasing once a threshold level of expertise has been reached. Pinet and Nozari (2020), applied a signal detection framework to error correction in typing. In a single-word typing-to-dictation task, participants heard a word and typed it under a time limit. An error was defined objectively, as a keystroke sequence that di"ered from the intended word. Monitoring was identified based on whether the typist attempted a correction, (pressing the backspace key), and an explicit report on whether the participant believed they had made an error. They found systematic evidence of correction attempts that were not explicitly reported. They explain such repairs as largely automatic and occurring without attentional control.

1.6 Translation styles

Translation styles are distinct, relatively stable procedural patterns in how translators allocate attention through reading and producing text, drafting a target text, pausing between production, and making revisions. Translation progression graphs integrate fixations on source and target tokens with keystroke data across a duration of time. Carl et al. (2011) have identified distinct styles of orientation, drafting and revision. For example, head-starters begin translation immediately whereas large-context-planners read far ahead into the source text before beginning production. Similarly, Chen et al. (2025) used clustering to identify three distinct preparatory-reading patterns: Fast Surface-level, Systematic Deep-level, and Extended Iterative.

A standardised, quantitative measure of metacognitive sensitivity during translation has not yet been demonstrated for translation. This paper addresses this by applying SDT to translation process data from multiple translators and translation sessions from the Translation Process Research Database (TPR-DB) and calculates AUROC2 values for individual translators across 2 language pairs.

2 Methodology

2.1 Participants, Materials and Data Sources

This analysis draws on two studies from the TPR-DB (Carl et al., 2016). Data acquisition software Translog-II was used to record keystrokes with timestamps to the millisecond, insertions and deletions, and cursor o"set. Gaze data was recorded via a Tobii T120 eye tracker with tracking fixations and timestamps on a source or target window. Post-hoc alignment (Carl, 2009; Carl & Jakobsen, 2009) between keystrokes, fixations and source and target tokens, as well as temporal and positional information are accessible through tables in the TPR-DB allowing for detection and analysis of behavioural events without access to the original recordings (Carl, 2012).

The study used 2 corpora which were part of the TPR-DB MultiLingual series: BML12, an English-to-Spanish study (Carl & Hill, 2012), and SG12, an English-to-German study (Carl et al., 2015; Nitzke, 2019). Both use a common set of six English source texts (110-160 words per text -four obtained from news sources and two from a sociological encyclopaedia with 847 words in total) which were translated into several target languages under a shared experimental design. Although both corpora contain recordings of several task types, only from-scratch translation sessions (of the form P20 T4) were used in this study. Each session involves only one participant and there were no time limits placed on translation sessions.

The two studies were selected on three grounds: 1.

Shared materials, recording apparatus and experimental design to control for text di! culty, measurement and task structure.

2. Same source language so that di"erences in reading behaviour are not a result of di"erences in source-text syntactical structure.

3. Typologically distinct target languages so that cross-linguistic replication can be scrutinised.

Overview of corpora: BML12: Consists of 32 participants and 60 sessions, 9,938 target tokens, 411 target segments and 10.34 hours of production. Each translation session was approximately 20 minutes long, with some running up to over an hour. Participants were a mixture of students and experienced translators, but the exact split is unknown (Carl & Hill, 2012; Carl & Schae"er, 2017). 28 of 32 participants contributed 2 sessions each.

SG12: Consists of 24 participants (12 professional, 12 student) and 47 sessions, 6,777 target tokens, 305 target segments, 12.46 hours of production (Carl & Schae"er, 2017). 23 of 24 participants contributed two sessions each.

Translation sessions are algorithmically divided into three phases (Jakobsen, 2002; Carl et al., 2011): Orientation, where source text is initially read, Drafting, where the target text is initially produced, and Revision, where the produced text is reviewed and amended. Phases are included in session tables in TPR-DB. By definition, no keystrokes occur during Orientation and Drafting is identified by the sequentiality of successive keystrokes and is required to account for at least half of a session's keystrokes. Its endpoint is based on the production of the last word of the target text rather than the translation of the last source word. The remaining Revision phase typically involves both insertions and deletions.

2.2 Procedure

A SDT experimental paradigm must include a decision (Type-1), involving a binary choice and a response (Type-2) consisting of a confidence report. Each trial is formed of one Type-1 task and its corresponding Type-2 task.

The TPR-DB contains unit tables that aggregate keystrokes and fixations into behaviourally interpretable segments (Carl, 2014; Carl et al., 2016) such as activity units (AU -6 types based on whether reading and/or typing occurred and whether reading occurred on the source or target text) and translation units (TU -formed by combining a stretch of production with the immediately preceding fixations). This study uses production units (PU): bursts of typing, separated by pauses (production unit breaks -PUB). A PUB is a pause whose duration is treated as reflective rather than automated processing, and is located wherever an inter-keystroke interval (IKI -the pause between a non-alphanumerical keystroke and a successive alphanumerical keystroke) exceeds a text-specific quantile threshold on the IKI distribution. Following Bandaru, Carl & Ren (2026), the threshold is set at the quantile 1/(c → k), where c is the average characters per word for a given text and k the chunk length in number of words (language based) (with c ↑ 6 and k = 3 for English placing it at 5.5% of IKIs), A similar measure (Muñoz Martín & Apfelthaler, 2022), the 'Task Segment Pause' (TSP) is a pause exceeding a threshold of 3↓ median between-word inter-keystroke interval. According to Muñoz Martín & Apfelthaler (2022), a TSP is an intentional typing break, requiring high-level cognitive functions for planning and assessment (cf. Angelone, 2010). A PU boundary also occurs at target 'segment' (sections of text within one text) boundaries regardless of pause length (Schae"er et al., 2016). Median inter-unit intervals are 1,891 ms in BML12 (IQR 1,094-3,797) and 3,604 ms in SG12 (IQR 1,997-8,132). This study used only keystroke data and PUs as the foundational task units.

Each PU is the basis for either a Type-1 decision or Type-2 response. Trials constructed as such are not independent in the sense assumed by classical signal detection theory because adjacent spans of source text are semantically correlated. This dependence is impossible to eliminate when applying SDT to a complex, naturalistic, continuous cognitive task without interruption. However, using PUs as task units provides adequate mitigation and they are selected as base units for the following reasons: 1.

PUs are delimited by pauses thought to indicate cognition on the span of text they surround (TPR-DB fixation tables). This allows for a time gap between tasks within a trial so that decision and response are separated.

2. PUs are small, cognitive units corresponding to deliberate decisions the translator makes.

3. Recorded information in the TPR-DB tables gives us access to exactly what was typed and deleted within a PU, and the target-token identifiers map production to the final target text.

4. PUs are not fixed-time units and vary in length and di! culty. However, as the source texts are of similar di! culty, participant-level behaviour is largely una"ected by individual PU di"erences within the large number of PUs in each session.

Each trial consists of:

A Type-1 decision: A PU containing an initial target production which is either correct (1) or incorrect (0), based on perception of the corresponding source text as the stimulus.

Correctness of the initial production is measured against the corresponding text in the final translation post-revision (discussed ahead).

A Type-2 response: A subsequent decision where it is decided whether or not reformulation is needed. Type-2 task = 2 (high confidence) if no reformulation occurs during drafting and Type-2 task = 1 (low confidence) if reformulation of an already produced span of text occurs in a later PU during Drafting. The PU where reformulation occurs can be anywhere between (and including) the PUB immediately following the corresponding Type-1 PU and the final PU of the drafting phase within a session. This range supplies the lower and upper bounds of the response window.

Pairing was performed strictly within participant and session, so the final unit of one session was never paired with the first unit of another. Response units do not overlap with decision units; a Type-1 trial records a span of target text the translator committed to initially. Subsequent behaviour, on which a Type-2 task response is derived, either lets it stand or reformulates it.

Neither study collected confidence ratings; participants translated without interruption. The Type-2 response is therefore an implicit proxy measure, inferred from the available behavioural data. Importantly, a Type-2 task labelled as a reformulation need not reformulate the entire span of text produced during the initial PU but it must be a correction of at least 1 word from the initial span that amounts to more than correcting a motor slip (discussed further). This framework assumes that not reformulating a Type-1 production during Drafting means the translator chose not to reformulate that span because they considered it unnecessary. This allows for the translation process to proceed in a naturalistic way. Despite the 'incorrect' and 'correct' Type-1 labels, reformulation may replace a span of text because a better formulation became available regardless of whether the initial production was objectively incorrect. Such trials are still coded as Type-1 task = 0.

Furthermore, all PUs were drawn from the Drafting phase because an initial production choice is, by definition, made during the first production, and a reformulation signals monitoring only if it is made while that production is still current. On the other hand, changes made during Revision are the product of systematic later review: this phase does not reflect a purely metacognitive process since the translator reads their produced target text to verify its accuracy rather than reflecting on their own past behaviour from when the span of text was initially produced. All Revision-phase PUs are excluded from analysis accordingly.

Excluding Revision-phase PUs does not preclude the e"ect of Revision on the final text. A Type-1 task is correct if it survived in its recognisable form, into the participant's own final translation. A production that the translator leaves alone during Drafting but later replaces during Revision is recorded as incorrect (at the time of the trial) because the translator only caught the problem later on in review. Stated clearly, Since we assume that each translator has a working knowledge of the language, 'Revision' is treated as a phase during which, all trials are complete and Type-1 accuracy is evaluated based on the translators' own judgement. The use of the final text as the criterion is justified because: 1.

It provides a translator-calibrated measure of accuracy. Unlike the standard signal detection approach, translation, a complex and partly subjective task, admits no external ground truth of comparable status: a rendering may be more or less appropriate. Whether a participant's evaluation at the moment of initial production anticipates their own later, considered judgement is the metacognitive question being investigated in this study by keeping both the Type-1 outcome and the Type-2 judgement within a single participant's frame of reference. External verification would measure whether confidence tracks someone else's standard of accuracy, which conflates metacognitive sensitivity with whether the translator and verifier share the same notion of "correct".

2. The final translation, can be regarded as 'correct' provided all 'errors' were detected during Revision. Since there was no time limit placed on producing the final translation (Nitzke, 2019; Carl & Hill, 2012), we take the final translation as what the translator deemed as correct and most appropriate, based on the assumption that the participant picks up on all Drafting 'errors'.

Since both Type-1 and 2 task definitions involve whether a span of text was changed, AUROC2 values can only be viewed as useful or accurate if the following assumptions hold enough to eliminate the confound between Type-1 and 2: 1.

A semantically substantive drafting reformulation reflects a deliberate monitoring decision concerning the corresponding initial production.

2. Revision is assumed to identify and retain a suitably acceptable translation, such that the final translation provides the criterion against which the validity of the initial production can be evaluated.

3. When translators detect that an initial production is incorrect, they prefer to replace it with a correct formulation during Drafting rather than deferring the correction to Revision.

These three assumptions are consistent with the structure of the task and with previous research in the field. Assumption 1 is supported by the method used to assign Type-2 reformulations (Section 2.3). This approach is not infallible but it is designed to exclude motor slips so that reformulations are the product of a more evaluative act. Assumption 2 is supported by the participant pool and experimental design: both student and professional translators were of su! cient competence and sessions were not constrained by a time limit. Consequently, final translations can be expected to represent considered renderings and the translators' final judgement. Assumption 3 is what allows Drafting reformulation to serve as the locus of online monitoring. According to the Monitor Model, problematic renderings are addressed as they arise (Tirkkonen-Condit, 2005). This could be explained by the demands of working memory: a detected problem that is left unresolved incurs an additional cognitive load that must be carried for as long as production continues without making the correction (Levelt, 1983). However, the Type-1/Type-2 confound cannot be entirely eliminated within a self-defined accuracy framework. AUROC2 remains informative nevertheless; It is computed identically across two distinct language corpora such that the reliability and reproducibility of the results can be scrutinised.

2.3 Task Derivation Process

Task 1: In CRITT edit notation, a bracketed span records deleted characters, so hoka[ak]la resolves to hola. To assess task 1 accuracy, the typed text in each PU was recovered and compared against the span of the final target text at the same token positions, using a partialratio 'fuzzy match' scaled from 0 to 100. PUs scoring at or above 75 were coded Type-1 = 1 (correct); those below were coded Type-1 = 0. The value of 75 was chosen to permit orthographic variation and the propagation of neighbouring edits into a span's boundary, while excluding cases where the final span is a substantively di"erent rendering. Two considerations support this: 1.

The fuzzy score is a noisy index of the latent binary state, discriminating between correct and incorrect production. A superficial change such as an added accent, or a shifted clitic due to a neighbouring edit should still code as correct.

2. A sparse region exists around the chosen value. Figure 1 shows the distribution for both corpora. In BML12, the interval from 70 to 75 contains 0.7% of trials and that from 75 to 80 contains 1.3%. SG12 shows a similar pattern with 1.2% from 70 to 75 and 1.8% from 75 to 80. Very few trials sit close enough to the dense region for small changes to recategorise them (as shown in Figure 1). A sensitivity analysis (Section 3.4.2) confirms that the classification does not strongly depend on this threshold. Where the span of final text at the relevant positions was empty, whitespace-only or punctuation-only or where the text was shorter than two characters, those PUs were excluded from Type-1 grading due to insu! cient material for comparison.

Task 2: To detect reformulations and distinguish typing noise due to motor slips from evaluative judgements, a trial was coded Type-2 task = 1 (low confidence) only if three conditions held jointly: 1. the PU was of 'Type' Deletion or Combined (deletion and insertion) -it removed material rather than only appending.

2. The PU target tokens overlapped the corresponding Type-1 target tokens.

3. The deleted material satisfied a content condition, distinguishing reformulation from motor slips: A deletion is reformulation if it contains at least two letters and crosses a word boundary, or at least five letters within a single word.

The third condition captures withdrawals of formulation while largely excluding the repair of slips and was evaluated over the bracketed deletion spans of the edit string. Letters were counted with a Unicode-aware pattern treating accented characters as letters and excluding digits, punctuation and the underscore used to mark spaces. A deletion under these constraints, implies the removal of material spanning a word boundary or that a word was rewritten rather than repaired.

2.4 Defining a Response Window

A translator who registers a problem with the text may not correct it immediately, and may instead, complete the current clause, and return to the span several PUs later. Carl et al. (2010) document such revisions spanning considerable distances within the Drafting phase. To admit an appropriate time span for the Type-2 judgement, both a lower and an upper bound are reported for the same quantity.

Lower bound: Type-2 = 1 is assigned only if the immediately following production unit reformulates the preceding unit's tokens. This is the strictest reading of online monitoring where detection and action are contiguous. This necessarily underestimates sensitivity.

Upper bound: Type-2 = 1 is assigned if any subsequent production unit within the drafting phase reformulates the preceding unit's tokens. This admits deferred action while maintaining the requirement that the reformulation occurs during the Drafting phase.

The two bounds are informative in di"erent ways: The lower bound characterises immediately expressed monitoring (occurs after just a PUB); the upper bound characterises monitoring that manifests at any point within the same Drafting episode. The problem could be detected immediately after the Type-1 task or at any point before the reformulation is made. A larger distance between the upper and lower bound could identify a translator who registers a problem but defers acting on it or registers it later. Conversely, a small gap implies a case where detection occurs early and is more tightly coupled with action. Because the upper bound admits a larger set of reformulation events within drafting, it gives a more complete estimate of metacognitive sensitivity, while the lower bound serves as a floor.

2.5 Quantifying Metacognitive Sensitivity

Metacognitive sensitivity is quantified as the area under the Type-2 receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC2; Galvin et al., 2003; Fleming & Lau, 2014). With a binary confidence report, the Type-2 ROC has a single empirical operating point and the area under the curve is: where H2 = P (Type-2 = 2 | Type-1 = 1) is the proportion of correct productions left unreformulated in Drafting and F2 = P (Type-2 = 2 | Type-1 = 0) is the proportion of incorrect productions left unreformulated in Drafting (false-alarm).

Under this convention, high confidence is expressed by leaving a production unreformulated, while low confidence is expressed as a reformulation decision. AUROC2 = 0.5 implies chance discrimination, suggesting that reformulation behaviour during Drafting is independent of production accuracy. Values above 0.5 indicate that the decision to return and reformulate discriminates the translator's own correct from their own incorrect productions. Read as a ranking statistic, AUROC2 gives the probability that a randomly chosen incorrect production receives lower implicit confidence than a randomly chosen correct production. The four possible trial cases existing under this design are: (unreformulated | correct): the translator leaves an initially correct production alone during Drafting (characterised as a 'hit') (unreformulated | incorrect): the translator does not reformulate a production that is later deemed incorrect during Revision (false alarm).

(reformulated | correct): a correct production is reformulated during Drafting but the translator later reverts to the initial choice during Revision.

(reformulated | incorrect): the translator catches an incorrect production and reformulates during Drafting.

AUROC2 was found to be preferential to Meta-d → (Maniscalco & Lau, 2012) and M-Ratio: At the aggregate level, the contingency tables are heavily unbalanced in both margins. Across corpora, correct productions account for 77-78% of trials, and unreformulated trials account for 94% under the lower bound and 82-86% under the upper bound. Of the 4 possible combinations, reformulated incorrect productions form the smallest group, containing 74 trials in SG12, and reformulated correct productions form the smallest group (containing 85 trials) in BML12, under the lower bound. Meta-d → requires fitting an equal-variance Gaussian model to the contingency table by maximum-likelihood. With unbalanced populations, parametric extrapolation depends heavily on the sparse case. AUROC2 is computed directly from the observed conditional proportions and involves no such extrapolation (Fleming and Lau, 2014). Per-participant cell counts are too small to support a stable parametric fit in places. Under both bounds sparsity is substantial: under the lower bound 21 of 32 BML12 participants and 19 of 24 SG12 participants have fewer than five trials in the incorrect-and-reformulated cell. Under the upper bound this is ten and nine respectively.

All quantities are computed within participant and then aggregated across participants so the translator is the standard unit of statistical inference. Group-level intervals are obtained by resampling participants rather than trials, preserving existing dependence within each participant's data. Estimates should therefore, be read as characterising individual translators. The Drafting phase accounts for approximately nine tenths of PUs in both corpora, suggesting most productive activity occurred during Drafting. The trials analysed here are drawn exclusively from this phase and capture the majority of each participant's production. the Revision phase, by contrast, contributes a small minority of units. Per-participant median Drafting proportions were 0.91 for BML12 (range 0.45-1.00) and 0.96 for SG12 (range 0.67-0.99).

3.4.3 Mechanical Dependency

As discussed in Section 2.2, under the assumption that revision identifies all correct and incorrect drafting translations, the confound between type-1 and 2 is su! ciently mitigated: a reformulation can drive a PU to be incorrect, independently of whether that PU was destined for a di"erent rendering post-revision. Additionally, under a self-assessed accuracy metric, the assumption that a translator will only replace a span of text if they deem another rendering preferable, and that errors detected in Drafting are reformulated in Drafting, the dependence is at most partial: 60.8% of Type-2 = 1 trials in BML12 and 47.7% in SG12 are coded Type-1 = 0 under the lower bound, against roughly 100% were the link entirely mechanical. Nevertheless, a tipping-point sensitivity analysis was undertaken to establish how large the mechanical component would need to be to wholly account for the result. Let t denote the proportion of incorrect-reformulated trials that are incorrect solely due to reformulation and that would otherwise have been correct. Reassigning that fraction to the correct row and recomputing gives AUROC2 as a function of t, with t = 0 the primary analysis and t = 1, the case where no reformulated PU would have been replaced on its own. The estimate declines smoothly and reaches 0.5 at t = 0.68 in BML12 and t = 0.53 in SG12 under the lower bound, and at t = 0.69 and t = 0.69 under the upper. Combining the cohorts yields t = 0.63 under the lower bound and t = 0.69 under the upper bound (Figure 5). Taking the lower limit of the bootstrap interval rather than the mean moves these values to 0.63, 0.44, 0.64 and 0.61. In every case but one, more than half of all drafting reformulations that produced an incorrect PU would have to be altering the final text for reasons unconnected to the PUs accuracy before the deviation from chance were eliminated. The exception is SG12 under the lower bound at t = 0.44. Moreover, given that the detection of reformulations involves filtering out motor slips, this level of contamination is implausible as surviving Type-2 = 1 events are mostly considered deliberate withdrawals of formulation rather than incidental deletions. The finding is therefore robust to moderate mechanical contamination and AUROC2 values should be read conservatively as upper limits on metacognitive sensitivity.

3.4.4 Self-Repair Leaks

Although motor slips are filtered out of reformulations, the filter does not evaluate position. Under the lower bound, all three Type-2 = 1 conditions (Section 2.3) may be satisfied if a given PU has a following PU where new material is typed and backspaced through its own characters, touching the first PU without altering its production at all. This means that a trial can be coded as reformulated without actually being altered and 'leaks' into the reformulated category. Target-token overlap is established by a single shared token at the unit boundary. Because CRITT notation records a bracketed span as the characters removed, this can be identified directly: a deletion reaches into a preceding PU only where its length exceeds the running total of characters typed within the current PU, and a leak is a deletion that stayed within that unit's own typing. Deletions landing exactly on the boundary are ambiguous. Therefore, both a strict and an inclusive criterion are reported, where the strict criterion yields the higher leak estimate. Between 9.7% (inclusive) and 18.4% (strict) of reformulated PUs in BML12, and between 15.5% (inclusive) and 21.9% (strict) in SG12, are confined to the PU's own typing. This leak is concentrated among correct-reformulated trials: under the strict criterion, 95.5% of deletions in incorrect-reformulated trials reach the preceding production in BML12 and 89.2% in SG12, compared to only 60.0% and 67.9% in correct-reformulated trials. Yet recoding the confined cases to Type-2 = 2 leaves AUROC2 almost unchanged: BML12 moves from 0.567 to 0.569 under the strict criterion and remains at 0.567 under the inclusive, and SG12 from 0.539 to 0.539 (strict) and 0.537 (inclusive), because only a small number of trials are recoded (40 and 34 under the strict criterion, 21 and 24 under the inclusive). The changes are at most 0.002 in either direction. In conclusion, contamination exists but is close to neutral for the discrimination measure. The diagnostic cannot be applied to the upper bound, where the reformulating unit may fall several units later and its edit string is not retained in the trial table; the lower-bound result is reported as the conservative case.

4 Discussion

4.1 Overview of results

This study uses translation as a task to demonstrate a novel SDT design for complex, naturalistic tasks, where confidence can be obtained without probing to allow for uninterrupted task completion and where monitoring may be continuous, allowing for evidence accumulation throughout the task. This was done by using implicit behavioural proxies as decision (initial production of a span of text being correct or incorrect) and response variables (low or high confidence based on whether or not reformulation occurred during drafting), and the response window was delimited by a lower and upper bound during which a type-2 response can be made. Trials are not fully independent but are treated as such by using pre-existing behavioural units called production units. The method was applied on behavioural data collected during previous studies.

AUROC2 was calculated to measure how well Drafting reformulation was able to discriminate between self-assessed correct and incorrect productions, where the type-1 task was the production of a span of text and the type-2 task was 1 or 2 depending on whether or not reformulation of that span of text later took place during the Drafting phase. The method defined a window during which any reformulation (type-2 response) on any already completed type-1 decision (produced PU) could be made, by defining a window beginning immediately after initial production and ending at the end of the Drafting phase. AUROC2 was chosen as a more suitable metric than meta-d → due to sparsely distributed conditionals. The measure used the final translation post-Revision as a self-assessed accuracy metric against which to judge type-1 accuracy. This choice was made to accurately quantify whether confidence tracks a translator's own standard of accuracy.

Two distinct language corpora were used (English-Spanish, BML12 and English-German, SG12). The Group AUROC2 was 0.567 and 0.539 under the lower bound, 0.618 and 0.625 under the upper for BML12 and SG12 respectively, with every bootstrap interval excluding 0.5 and P (> 0.5) = 1.000 throughout. A small participant range between lower and upper bounds implies more immediate reformulation. By design, the upper bound captured a larger set of type-2 tasks and thus produced higher AUROC2 estimates. These higher estimates are expected to be more in line with true metacognitive sensitivity. Under the upper bound, 33.6% (BML12) and 43.0% (SG12) of eventually-altered productions are reformulated during drafting rather than left to revision; the remainder (66.4% and 57.0%) are false alarms of online monitoring. Under the lower bound, 16.6% (BML12) and 13.4% (SG12) of eventually-altered productions are reformulated during drafting.

Two typologically distinct language pairs converge, and converge more closely under the more complete measure: the cross-corpus gap narrows from 0.028 to 0.007 when the upper bound replaces the lower.

Sensitivity is broadly distributed rather than concentrated: 31 of 32 and 24 of 24 participants exceed 0.5 under the upper bound. Individual variation is substantial and stable: the perparticipant range widens from 0.173 to 0.338 in BML12 and 0.120 to 0.297 in SG12 as the bound admits deferred reformulation, and 47 of 56 participants gain. The gap distinguishes translators whose detection and action are tightly coupled from those who register problems later or register problems and defer acting. There was no reliable association between sensitivity and accuracy. 9.7-18.4% (BML12) and 15.5-21.9% (SG12) of lower-bound type-2 = 1 events constitute potential self-repair leaks. However, recoding them moves AUROC2 by at most 0.002. A no-monitoring model suggests t = 0.69 for both groups under the upper bound. These results largely mitigate the logical confound that would undermine an interpretation of AUROC2 due to the following assumptions: 1.

A semantically substantive drafting reformulation reflects a deliberate monitoring decision concerning the corresponding initial production.

2. Revision identifies and retains a suitably acceptable translation, such that the final translation provides the criterion against which the validity of the initial production can be evaluated 3. When translators detect that an initial production is incorrect, they replace it with a correct formulation during drafting rather than deferring the correction to revision.

Over 90% of the between-participant variance in AUROC2 is driven by the P(reformulated | incorrect) group. Despite this, the measure provides value over the use of the fraction of total corrections occurring during drafting for three reasons: 1.

The AUROC2 values can be situated within the broader set of metacognition literature.

2. Future metacognition studies on translation may use di"erent behavioural measures for trials, explicit confidence ratings, and externally assessed metrics for accuracy and this may change how much variance comes from P(reformulated | incorrect).

3. Future samples may exhibit very di"erent behaviour e.g. more reversions (which aren't captured by the fraction)

4.2 Interpretation

The Monitor Model (Tirkkonen-Condit 2005) describes translation as a two-part process: a default, low cost, largely automatic rendering procedure which renders the target text to closely mirror the source text's lexical and syntactic structure, and a supervisory monitor that intervenes when it has detected that a literal translation is inadequate, triggering an e"ortful strategic process. In agreement with this notion, Tirkkonen-Condit et al. (2008) found that when typingerror revisions were excluded, 42.3% of revisions were corrections of literal translations. Carl and Dragsted (2012) further propose that comprehension and production occur as parallel processes rather than sequentially. This is consistent with their finding that long-distance revisions, show up as often during Drafting, as Revision because the monitor is active throughout production. Continual monitoring during production also implies that error correction of a past production can occur at any point during the window in which monitoring is taking place. How well a translator is able to notice that a previous action was incorrect is what is meant by metacognitive sensitivity. This type of protracted metacognitive process di"ers from the standard visual discrimination tasks in several ways and crucially, error-detection may ensue through di"erent mechanisms: new source-text information provides additional evidence which allows the translator to retroactively reveal an earlier target-text span as incorrect. This is evident through reformulation behaviour that occurs several PUs after the initial production (when additional context has concluded that an earlier literal translation was inadequate).

Post-decisional: a translator produces a span of text before 1 st order sensory evidence has fully accumulated. Once this encoded sensory evidence reveals an inadequacy in the original production, a reformulation occurs. Importantly, this type of sensory evidence accumulation is endogenous and has been found to decay faster over time than high-level task evidence, which conversely, benefits from additional time (Balsdon et al., 2024). These types of corrections may reflect more immediate corrections such as those contributing to the lower bound of the response window in this framework.

2 nd order evidence: the translator uses sensory information about the text as well as their experience during an initial production to decide whether they are confident about it or not. For example, a translator may find a span of text di! cult to translate and use the felt uncertainty at the time of production, as well as the text itself to form a confidence judgement. Since this type of evidence does not rapidly decay over time (Balsdon et al., 2024), it is possible that such corrections guide the translator to return to a span of text much later in Drafting or the Revision phase. This type of metarepresentation is not linguistic or pragmatic.

We cannot be sure whether any reformulation in the text was a result of either of the three cases outlined, and AUROC2 does not make a distinction. Moreover, it is important to clarify the distinction between linguistic and cognitive metarepresentation: Compositional semantics builds increasingly complex representations but it may still be first-order content, regardless of its linguistically hierarchical structure. According to Shreve (2009) and Angelone (2010), monitoring a text and controlling it through modifications and corrections, is a metacognitive skill central to translation expertise but this is not the same as metacognition under Fleming & Daw's (2017) definition who specifically refer to a representation whose content is another mental state.

4.3 Limitations and Comparison

The experimental design, di"ers from a standard visual metacognitive discrimination task in several structural ways and has several limitations: Proxy confidence measure: The current paradigm did not use explicit or implicit probes to measure confidence. It used an implicit confidence proxy to preserve the underlying mechanism driving error-detection (Nicholson et al., 2019). Had explicit confidence probes been used to interrupt during Drafting, the underlying mechanism and AUROC2 may be di"erent.

The temporal decoupling of decision and response: In a typical visual task, the type-1 decision and the type-2 confidence judgment are yoked together, milliseconds to a couple of seconds apart, which limits the ways confidence-generating evidence accumulates. The protracted response window used in this study introduces new sources of noise and evidence.

Dependence between trials and heterogeneity: In translation, segments of varying di! culty are linked through discourse coherence and working memory. SDT assumes trial-by-trial independence of the confidence/accuracy pairing. Applying SDT to translation data stretches the model's assumption whenever cross-unit dependencies drive detection.

Memory dependence: the requirement of holding a span of text in memory adds an extra source of noise as signal decay is also a factor.

Signal distortion: Fleming and Dolan (2010) showed implicit measures are systematically distorted by factors unrelated to the underlying certainty signal (like loss aversion and wager size). In translation, revising a span many words behind could cost more than fixing a recent error so translators may leave these cases for Revision even if they were detected during Drafting.

Binary confidence outcome: Studies tend to use 4-6 confidence bins to build a full ROC curve. This study used a single operating point (2-bin) which mechanically compresses AUROC2 toward 0.5 relative to a graded bin design.

For these reasons, comparison to values from previous research provides limited value. However, visual, retrospective, binary classification/detection tasks have produced AUROC2 in the range of 0.55-0.75 (Fleming et al., 2010; Mazor et al., 2020; Zizlsperger et al., 2016; Fleming et al., 2016; Sherman & Seth, 2024), typically clustering in the 0.6-0.7 range. Broadly, the type-2 ROC approach (Fleming & Lau, 2014) treats values meaningfully above 0.5 as evidence of metacognitive sensitivity, with well above 0.5 but below 1.0 being typical of healthy perceptual discrimination. In the present study, the obtained upper bound values (0.618 and 0.625 for Spanish and German respectively) sit comfortably within the lower region of the expected range for reasons that are partly genuine and partly methodological (as discussed). Furthermore, a slightly lower estimate may come from a high type-1 di! culty, which can lower AUROC2 despite constant endogenous metacognitive e! ciency (Fleming & Lau, 2014).

4.4 Future Work

The present analysis would benefit from replication with a larger participant pool and the inclusion of a third language pair, which would allow language-general patterns to be distinguished from pair-specific artifacts. It should also be acknowledged that the dataset analysed here was not originally designed to test the metacognitive questions raised in this study; it was assembled for other purposes within the CRITT TPR-DB, and a purpose-built study, with a controlled confidence-elicitation protocol and balanced item di! culty, would allow more direct tests of claims. A next step would be to apply the same tools used to ask how metacognitive sensitivity varies systematically with translator expertise and task di! culty. A further avenue is to extend Nicholson et al.'s (2019; 2021) dissociation between explicit and implicit uncertainty monitoring, and its link to mentalising ability, into the translation domain. Their work shows that explicit metacognitive judgments recruit the same resources as mentalising, while implicit uncertainty-monitoring tasks do not. Translation is a strong test case for this dissociation, because Theory of Mind is integral to resolving ambiguity in a source text, tracking authorial intent, and rendering pragmatic nuance. Moreover, using additional data could be useful: Incorporating additional implicit behavioral proxies for confidence, such as pause duration, typing rhythm, or gaze patterns would move the implicit measure from a single-point signal towards a graded confidence report, addressing the AUROC2-computability limitation discussed.

A di"erent question which could be asked is what the process of self-monitoring looks like as it unfolds in time. A Drift Di"usion Model (DDM) or related evidence-accumulation model could be used to ask whether deferred reformulations show di"erent accumulation dynamics from reformulations made immediately upon production. Specifically, one could test whether the proximate/deferred distinction is better explained by a di"erence in decision boundary (deferred reformulations could require more accumulated evidence before a revision is triggered) or by a di"erence in drift rate (deferred reformulations could reflect a slower-building signal), and whether the pause preceding a reformulation carries a measurable evidence-accumulation signature. Such a project would constitute a substantial modelling undertaking and is proposed here as a direction for future research.
