Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Infant research has often treated early neural and behavioral expression as a privileged window into the initial state of the mind, with early-emerging capacities sometimes uncritically taken as broad evidence of innate knowledge. This paper evaluates the strength of such inferences through four intersecting lenses: genetics and epigenetics, cultural evolution, definitional precision, and historical and contemporary standards of scientific explanation and falsifiability. It distinguishes among several non-equivalent forms of innateness, including claims about representational content, architectural or processing biases, developmental canalization, and maturational timing. In place of broad or underspecified appeals to innateness, it advances greater specificity in accounts of co-determination as a framework for theorybuilding that encourages delineation of biological constraints, experiential inputs, cultural ecologies, developmental timing, and interactive mechanisms proposed to produce a given outcome, as well as patterns of variation that would support or challenge those claims. The central aim is to provide a more tractable and testable basis for theorizing about the initial state of the infant mind.

Specifying Innateness in Infant Development: A Framework for Theory-Building Based on Co-Determination

Infant research has long treated early behavior as a privileged window into the initial state of the mind, inviting strong claims that capacities observed in the first months of life reflect innate representational knowledge (e.g., Bloom, 2010; Carey, 2009; Chomsky, 1980; Spelke, 2000; Spelke & Kinzler, 2007; Spelke, 2024a Spelke, , 2024b)). Highly impactful programs in core knowledge, language acquisition, conceptual development, natural pedagogy, numeracy, and morality have often taken early emerging behavioral patterns as evidence for pre-specified knowledge structures that scaffold later learning and development (e.g., Bloom, 2010; Carey, 2009; Feigenson et al., 2004; Gergely & Csibra, 2013; Gleitman, 2020; Spelke & Kinzler, 2007).

At the same time, developmental science has cultivated influential alternatives, including work on ontogenetic niches (West & King, 1987), experiential canalization (Gottlieb, 1991), probabilistic epigenesis (Gottlieb, 2004), connectionism (Elman et al., 1996), and extended brain-body-behavior networks (Byrge et al., 2014). It is likely that most infant theorists would agree that both biologically grounded constraints and developmental experience contribute to the emergence of knowledge. The aim of this paper is therefore neither to defend nor to reject innateness as an explanatory principle, but instead to examine whether innatist claims are sufficiently integrated with evidence on gene-culture interaction, cultural evolution, contextually limited sampling, and standards of definitional precision and falsifiability.

On this basis, the paper argues for a more explicit approach to theory-building grounded in clearly specified principles of co-determination.

Innatist accounts of development are varied, diverse, and non-equivalent. For example, a claim that infants possess innately specified conceptual content that is manifest early in development is not equivalent to a claim that learning is shaped by biologically grounded biases in attention and perception.

Nor are these claims identical to the notion that a developmental outcome is canalized across a range of environments, or that it emerges according to a relatively robust maturational timetable. In addition, evidence that challenges one level of innateness does not necessarily challenge others. For example, evidence of prenatal speech learning may qualify any inference that newborn language preferences reflect experience-independent representational content, but it does not, by itself, rule out biologically prepared auditory biases, experience-expectant tuning mechanisms, or innate architectural constraints that shape language learning. The argument developed here is directed primarily at strong representational nativism and at under-specified innatist mechanisms in more moderate accounts (for an example, see Bai et al., 2025, discussed below), rather than at the broader claim that biological constraints and biases contribute to developmental change.

Strong claims about core knowledge, innate morality, and related early emerging capacities, attributing rich innate structure to infants, have had outsized influence on how infant findings are used to theorize about the origins of human cognition. Their influence has been tremendously generative for the field. However, they have also encouraged a recurring inferential pattern in which infant behavioral expression is treated as evidence for a richly endowed initial state without an equally detailed account of developmental mechanisms, sufficient connection with socioecological diversity, engagement with interactive developmental conditions, and perhaps most critically, without a precise articulation of the specific conditions under which the claim could be falsified.

Innateness itself is variously defined as a concept (see Table 1 for a summary). As Mameli and Bateson (2011) argue, "innateness" has been synonymized with genetic specification, evolutionary shaping, the absence of learning, developmental robustness, and modularity, definitions that are often treated interchangeably or, at the very least, seldom explicitly distinguished. Consistent with the goal of this paper to encourage a more explicit elaboration of how innate contributions to development are defined and to clarify the kinds of evidence required to adequately test those claims, the paper draws on genetics and epigenetics, cultural evolution, sampling diversity, and theories of scientific determinism.

The aim is not simply to replace a broad claim of innateness with a similarly broad claim of codetermination. Rather, the article argues for more precisely specified and measurable approaches to codetermination as a source of insight into developmental mechanism in the form of theories and/or models that identify which biological biases, input structures, developmental timetables, and feedback processes impact development; how they interact; and under what conditions particular outcomes are projected to vary or remain stable. This is not the first attempt to question the basis for strong innatist accounts of development.

Empiricist, constructivist, neuroconstructivist, connectionist, dynamic-systems, and developmental robotics approaches have repeatedly challenged strong innateness interpretations by showing how domain-relevant structure can emerge from interactions among bias, input, embodiment, task demands, and developmental timing. This work spans language, object cognition, social cognition, theory of mind, causal reasoning, and categorization, and has shown that apparently early or abstract competencies can sometimes be generated by domain-general learning mechanisms operating over structured input (Benton, 2025; Berthiaume et al., 2013; Christiansen & Curtin, 2005; Cohen et al., 2002; Elman, 1993; Mareschal et al., 1999; McClelland & Thompson, 2007; Morton & Munakata, 2002; Munakata et al., 1997; Rakison & Lupyan, 2008; Schlesinger & Casey, 2003; Van Overwalle, 2010; Westermann et al., 2007). For example, Benton (2025) demonstrated that infants' apparently early expectations about the causal properties of animate agents and inanimate objects need not be interpreted as evidence for core knowledge specified in advance. Across simulations of four classic infancy studies, a domain-general associative-learning model was sufficient to reproduce key patterns of infant looking behavior, suggesting that sensitivity to causal action can emerge from learned regularities among surface features, motion patterns, contact relations, and outcomes. Similarly, Berthiaume et al. (2013) demonstrated, in the context of false-belief tasks, how developmental transitions often treated as evidence for conceptual reorganization can also be modeled through constructivist connectionist learning.

A different kind of alternative is offered by Bai et al.'s recent "core perception" account (Bai et al., 2025). Rather than denying that infants possess rich early representations of objects, number, geometry, and social agents, this account relocates many of those representations from conceptual reasoning (or more broadly, nonperceptual processes) to perception. On this view, infant findings traditionally interpreted as evidence of precocious conceptual reasoning may instead reflect perceptual processes that are fast, automatic, and sensitive to particular features of sensory input. This proposal poses an important challenge to the inference that sophisticated infant behavior necessarily reflects conceptual knowledge. At the same time, it illustrates a central theoretical distinction relevant to the present discussion: recharacterizing a capacity as perceptual rather than conceptual does not resolve questions about its developmental origins or innateness. Indeed, the core-perception account appears to retain, or at least does not explicitly reject, that these capacities emerge early, are species-typical and universal, and are rooted in human biology. Thus, while the account challenges claims about the nature and source of infants' knowledge, it leaves questions about innateness largely unresolved.

Like much of the literature Bai et al. (2025) seek to reinterpret, the account is principally concerned with the nature of internal processes rather than with the range of developmental ecologies in which those processes emerge. It therefore leaves largely unaddressed how variation in prenatal environments, early experience, gene-culture interactions, cultural practices, and historically narrow sampling might shape or constrain the development of these perceptual systems, or what evidence would distinguish species-typical perceptual organization from perceptual organization that is canalized through common developmental environments. A co-determination framework would instead ask which components of these perceptual systems are biologically constrained, how they are calibrated through prenatal and postnatal experience, how they vary across developmental contexts, and what empirical evidence would distinguish pre-specified perceptual organization from rapidly constructed perceptual organization.

Thus, the empirical challenge in characterizing innateness is not simply to ask whether a behavior appears early or consistently in infants, but to specify the conditions and dynamics that would be sufficient-or insufficient-to produce it. An important charge for the field is to articulate scientific frameworks that specify how co-determination can be articulated with specificity and precision, and how hypothesis emerging from these frameworks can be subjected to robust empirical scrutiny. In the following section, the interactive roles of genetics, epigenetics, and culture are discussed as joint factors that would inform such a framework.

Genetics, epigenetics, and culture

Innateness is often posited in infant research on the grounds that infants are thought to lack cultural experience. Yet developmental theory has long emphasized that organisms develop within organized developmental niches and thus, genes, bodies, and environments co-act across space and time (Gottlieb, 1991; Moore & Lickliter, 2023; West & King, 1987). Contemporary work in cultural evolution, developmental science, and behavioral genetics similarly demonstrates that genes and culture interact so extensively that disentangling their separate contributions is often difficult (Henrich, 2016; Laland et al., 2015; Muthukrishna et al., 2021). Infants encounter variation in linguistic input, caregiving routines, nutrition, physical and social affordances, and stress exposure all of which are structured by family life, institutions, and broader cultural practices (Keller, 2018). Developmental processes thus may reflect gene-culture co-development responsive to this variation rather than experience-independent representational specification.

Culture shapes the environments in which genes act from the earliest moments of life. This does not begin at birth: culture organizes pregnancy through norms around work, nutrition, stress exposure, social support, medical care, household structure, and shared expectations about caregiving. These patterned conditions shape the prenatal environment within which gene expression and fetal development unfold. Maternal stress during pregnancy and its impact on the developing infant provides a clear illustration of this. Crucially, prenatal stress is culturally patterned through work conditions, social support, discrimination, nutrition, access to care, household structure, and locally shared expectations about pregnancy and caregiving (Buffa et al., 2018; Coussons-Read, 2013). For example, in their scoping review of prenatal stress research in low-and middle-income countries, Buffa et al. (2018) note that much of the existing evidence has come from high-income countries, even though low-and middle-income countries often face greater maternal and infant health burdens, including higher rates of low birthweight and prematurity. Across studies from 24 countries, prenatal stress was assessed through measures such as perceived stress, stressful life events, anxiety, depression, common mental disorders, occupational stress, trauma, and cortisol, and was examined in relation to gestational age, preterm birth, birthweight, smallfor-gestational-age status, temperament, motor development, behavior, physical growth, and child health.

The conclusions of this study underscore that prenatal stress is frequently embedded in local cultural ecologies of health care access, poverty, nutrition, gendered labor, social support, political instability, and exposure to violence or disaster, making it an especially clear example of how structural conditions, intertwined with geography and culture, can permeate developmental pathways before birth.

These broader structural conditions are also evident within high-income countries, where racialized stress and unequal access to protective resources have been implicated in persistent disparities in birth outcomes, including higher rates of low birth weight, infant mortality, and small-for-gestationalage births among African American women compared with White women (Coussons-Read, 2013).

Moreover, although some prenatal risk factors, such as smoking and hypertension, predict low birth weight across racial groups, stress exposure and low social support appear to have especially pronounced associations with adverse birth outcomes among African-American women, suggesting that sociocultural factors associated with minority status can intensify the developmental consequences of prenatal stress.

The developmental significance of these disparities lies in the fact that contextual exposure can become biologically embedded before birth. In terms of mechanisms, elevated maternal cortisol and stress-related hormones cross the placenta and alter the fetal environment, influencing infant stress regulation and neurodevelopment. For example, newborns whose mothers experienced depression and anxiety during pregnancy showed increased DNA methylation of the NR3C1 glucocorticoid receptor promoter in newborn cord blood; this methylation pattern predicted heightened cortisol reactivity in infancy, suggesting that prenatal stress had already shaped infant stress responsivity before birth (Oberlander et al., 2008). Similarly, there is converging evidence that prenatal maternal distress is associated with altered expression and epigenetic regulation of genes related to stress-response pathways, which in turn predict differences in infant temperament, affect regulation, and later risk for internalizing problems (Monk et al., 2012). These findings provide a mechanism for how cultural and structural conditions can become biologically embedded before birth. The same patterned ecologies often continue after birth, creating continuity between prenatal exposure and the early postnatal environments in which regulation, attention, and learning unfold.

Epigenetic mechanisms, including DNA methylation, histone modification, and alterations in chromatin accessibility, provide a biological pathway through which early caregiving, stress, nutrition, and sensory environments shape neural and behavioral development. For example, Meaney and colleagues' rodent studies demonstrated that variation in maternal licking and grooming led to stable methylation changes at the glucocorticoid receptor (NR3C1) promoter, producing long-term differences in stress reactivity (Meaney, 2010; see also Weaver et al., 2004). Parallel findings in humans show that infants exposed to parental stress and anxiety during pregnancy are associated with altered methylation patterns in stress-regulatory genes in the placenta, and that these methylation patterns are in turn associated with newborn neurobehavior, including stress regulation, muscle tone, and lethargy (Conradt et al., 2013). These findings are directly relevant to culture because prenatal stress and caregiving consistency are not randomly distributed across individuals; they are shaped by culturally organized expectations, social support systems, family structures, economic insecurity, racialized stress, migration histories, and institutional access to care (Coussons-Read, 2013; Dunkel Schetter & Tanner, 2012; National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2020). Thus, environments beginning in utero do not simply activate innate programs; instead, they configure the molecular landscape within which development unfolds.

Human "natural experiments" also suggest that environmental influences on development begin well before birth. One of the most studied cases is the Dutch Hunger Winter, a famine that occurred in the Netherlands during the winter of 1944-1945, when pregnant women experienced severe caloric restriction during different windows of gestation. Six decades later, individuals exposed to the famine around the time of conception showed lower DNA methylation of the imprinted IGF2 gene than their unexposed same-sex siblings, whereas those exposed only late in gestation did not (e.g., Heijmans et al., 2008). Early prenatal famine exposure has also been linked to later health outcomes such as coronary heart disease and schizophrenia, although clear causal pathways have yet to be established. Nonetheless, the Dutch Hunger Winter illustrates that what might later be treated as innate individual differences may partly reflect prenatal environmental exposure.

Taken together, these findings have four key implications for innateness. First, infants are never truly "acultural": prior to birth they are already embedded in patterned developmental niches. Second, genes do not act apart from cultural variation, but in continuous interaction with it. Third, epigenetic mechanisms provide biological pathways through which early experience shapes later behavior. Fourth, early appearance or cross-population recurrence do not by themselves establish experience-independence.

Species-typical outcomes can emerge from species-typical niches just as readily as from pre-specified representational content (Gottlieb, 1991; West & King, 1987). Because gene-culture interactions operate not only within development but also within and across generations within a cultural context, their implications for innateness extend directly into the domain of cultural evolution.

Innateness and cultural evolution

Cultural inheritance can offer a wider and faster route to adaptation than genetic inheritance because ideas, norms, and practices can spread and change far more quickly than genes. Whereas genetic change in humans is tied to the long interval between generations, cultural change might happen within moments, across years, or over lifetimes. In many animal species, and especially in humans, localized rituals, learned traditions and social practices shape how individuals behave, survive, and reproduce, making culture a potent evolutionary influence. Over long periods, these culturally driven behaviors appear to have fed back onto human biology, contributing to shifts (e.g. in speech anatomy, the mental capacities that support learning from others, and internalized social norms) (Waring & Wood, 2021).

The expansion and increasing complexity of human societies, alongside our rise as a dominant species, suggest that cultural inheritance is a major driver of evolutionary change on a global scale. Some have argued that culture acts as a second inheritance system that co-evolves with genetic evolution, producing traits that emerge from long histories of gene-culture co-adaptation rather than from purely genetic mechanisms (Muthukrishna et al., 2021). This means that behaviors observed in infants today reflect developmental outcomes shaped by evolved cultural practices that have exerted selection pressures over generations. Thus, what might look like an innate behavior could instead be products of cultural evolution, both independent from and interdependent with genetic transmission.

A further complication arises from the covariance of genes and culture. Parents transmit both genes and culturally patterned practices to their children, making it difficult to assign developmental outcomes cleanly to one source or the other (Scarr & McCartney, 1983). Because these influences are inherently entangled, developmental pathways cannot be neatly partitioned into biological and cultural origins. This has important consequences for how perceived universality (i.e. sameness across respondents) is interpreted as evidence of innateness. Universality is a descriptive claim about the recurrence of a pattern across groups, whereas innateness is an arguably stronger explanatory claim about why that pattern emerges. Even genuinely widespread outcomes may reflect recurring developmental environments and similar cultural affordances rather than biological pre-specification.

Moreover, universality itself may be mis-inferred when the evidentiary base is narrow. Patterns observed repeatedly in relatively homogeneous samples can appear species-general simply because relevant cultural, linguistic, socioeconomic, and ecological variation has not been sampled (Singh et al., 2023; Singh & Rajendra, 2024). As these survey studies demonstrate, many infant studies do not report basic information about participants' ancestry, community of descent, socioeconomic circumstances, or even the site of testing, making it difficult to establish the true breadth of the evidence. Studies that do report such information reveal a pronounced and enduring concentration of White participants from a limited range of Euro-American contexts. Claims of universality-and the use of apparent universality as evidence for particular innateness claims-are therefore often advanced on the basis of evidence drawn from a restricted and often incompletely characterized set of populations. A behavior may appear widespread or species-typical within these contexts because humans within like contexts can inherit and inhabit a common set of cultural and ecological affordances. This does not mean that behavior is prespecified or invariant. The field's history of relying on narrow and underspecified samples therefore limits universalist interpretations of infant behavior that often motivate innatist theory.

The problem is compounded when the populations under study share similar histories of cumulative cultural evolution. Standardized schooling, similar family structures, shared institutions, and other culturally evolved practices can produce developmental environments that are more alike than they initially appear (Uchiyama et al., 2022). As a result, variation in culturally relevant experiences may be compressed in the literature, making behaviors appear more universal within the sampled populations than they would across the broader range of human developmental contexts and populations. The Scarr-Rowe effect offers a concrete illustration of this: in some U.S. samples, the heritability of general intelligence is substantially higher among high-SES than low-SES families. Samples from Europe and Australia show far more uniform heritability of general intelligence scores across SES strata (D. C. Rowe et al., 1999; Scarr-Salapatek, 1971; Tucker-Drob & Bates, 2016). This pattern implies that what looks like a genetic contribution to intelligence may be in part contingent on the structure of socioeconomic inequality within the broader environment.

Applied to infant research, this means that when researchers draw on similarly situated samples to make claims about human cognitive architecture, they are often generalizing from a relatively small region of the much wider backdrop of human developmental environments. Without sampling a wider range of gene-culture configurations, it is difficult to know whether an observed pattern reflects a species-typical developmental process or one that depends on recurrent environmental conditions. These empirical challenges also expose a conceptual challenge. Because genes, culture, and development are so deeply intertwined, the evidence reviewed above does not simply complicate empirical tests of innateness; it also complicates what researchers mean when they invoke the term. Whether observations are taken to support or challenge innateness depends in part on whether innateness is taken to mean representational content, developmental robustness, architectural constraints, maturational timing, or some other property. Greater semantic precision is therefore a prerequisite for interpreting empirical findings and for evaluating competing developmental explanations.

Definitional uncertainty of innateness

A major complication of invoking innateness is that it is irregularly defined. Griffiths (2002) points out that scientists and philosophers use innateness to convey a range of meanings: present at birth, not learned, genetically specified, developmentally canalized, species-typical, environmentally invariant, amongst others. Mameli and Bateson (2006) similarly offer many candidate definitions of innateness; from this, they conclude that none gives a satisfactory, unified scientific notion, and that researchers often use them interchangeably, implying conceptual equivalence across different definitions (see Table 1).

One way forward is to disaggregate at least five separable claims that are often aggregated: (1) innate content is present early; (2) architectural or processing biases constrain learning; (3) privileged learning biases or preparedness make some inputs easier to acquire than others; (4) developmental outcomes are canalized across a range of environments; and (5) maturation follows a relatively robust timetable. These are distinct and non-substitutable conceptual claims, and evidence that challenges one does not automatically challenge the others (Elman et al., 1996; Mameli & Bateson, 2011). As noted, the empirical base for many innateness claims is narrow, further complicating any of these claims. But the problems of limited diversity compounds when innateness is not clearly defined.

Much infant work implicitly equates availability at birth with innateness. Availability at birth means only that a capacity can be observed in newborns under the conditions they have experienced. On one hand, this does not, by itself, establish that the capacity emerged independently of experience. On the other hand, evidence of learning, including prenatal learning, should not be treated as a blanket refutation of innateness claims. Many theoretical frameworks allow that biologically prepared systems require input to be expressed, tuned, or calibrated; in this sense, learning and innateness are not mutually exclusive. Prenatal sensitivity to speech, for example, is not evidence against the possibility of innate languagerelevant structure per se. Rather, it challenges the narrower inference that language-related preferences observed at birth are entirely experience-independent. In such cases, it is critical to clearly articulate whether the available input was informative for the capacity in question, and what prior biases, architectures, or constraints would still be needed to explain the observed behavior. For example, auditory, visual, social, and motor capacities differ in the kinds of input available before and immediately after birth and thus differ in the kinds of developmental explanations that are plausible. This point is important for cases such as early visual preferences. Some early visual preferences may ultimately be best explained by low-level perceptual biases, orienting mechanisms, maturational constraints, or rapid postnatal tuning; others may prove to require stronger biological constraints. Face preferences in newborns provide an important example of why early emergence should not be equated with fully innate representational content. Newborns show reported attentional biases toward faces and face-like configurations, including preferences for upright face-like stimuli, open eyes, direct gaze, attractive faces, and the mother's face. However, these findings do not by themselves establish that newborns possess an innate representation of faces. As Slater et al. (2010) note, some accounts interpret newborn face preferences as arising from general properties of the immature visual system, including sensitivity to stimuli with more elements in the upper visual field, top-heavy structure, or congruent arrangements of visual elements. On this view, what appears to be a preference for "faces" may initially reflect sensitivity to visual configurations that overlap with faces along a range of dimensions, rather than a pre-specified representation of faces as a social category. Indeed a recent systematic review and metaanalysis found only limited and inconsistent support for a face preference in newborns tested before postnatal face exposure, with most evidence coming from newborns whose preferences may already reflect this experience (Wilkinson et al., 2026).

A developmental-systems interpretation further obscures the inferential leap from newborn preference to innate capacity. Reynolds and Roth (2018) argue that early attentional biases for faces are best understood as emerging from interactions among organismic constraints, stimulus properties, prenatal and postnatal sensory experience, arousal, and the developing attention system. Newborns are not experience-free organisms: they arrive with prenatal auditory experience, including familiarity with the mother's voice and native-language rhythm, and this prior experience may shape postnatal visual attention in multimodal social contexts. Similarly, findings that only a few hours of postnatal interaction can support preference for the mother's face indicate that early face preferences may be calibrated by experience. Relatedly, Scott and Arcaro (2023) propose a domain-relevant developmental framework in which face specialization emerges through experience operating over general neurobiological mechanisms, providing a mechanistic account of how domain-selective organization can develop without requiring fully pre-specified face representations.

The developmental trajectory of face processing also argues against treating newborn preferences as definitive evidence for a fully specified innate face system. Slater et al. (2010) describe the newborn "face space" as broadly rather than narrowly tuned. Newborns around the world do not yet show the same race-, gender-, or species-specific tuning that emerges over the first year of life. With repeated exposure, infants develop preferences for familiar classes of faces and become more accurate at recognizing ownrace and own-species faces. However, three months after birth, infants raised in multiracial contexts demonstrate an early other-race preference (Singh et al. 2022) whereas those raised in monoracial contexts demonstrate an early own-race preference (Kelly et al. 2005). Within these contexts, infants with personalized multiracial interaction demonstrate a more pronounced other-race preference with age.

These varied findings are consistent with experiential canalization: early biases may direct attention toward socially relevant input, but that input then may shape the systems that processes visual information including faces. This distinction is relevant to innateness claims more generally. If a newborn preference can be explained by low-level perceptual salience, prenatal sensory integration, rapid postnatal learning, or broad attentional biases that become specialized through experience, then the stronger and more useful claim must specify what is proposed to be innate, how it operates mechanistically, how experience modifies it, and what evidence would falsify the proposed mechanism. The central claim of this paper is therefore not that developmental science should replace all biological explanation with learning, but that theories should specify the level of explanation at issue and relevant sources of input for the particular domain and target behaviors under study.

These examples also reflect that immediately after birth, infants are embedded in active social and cultural environments that vary significantly along multiple axes. This view is consistent with developmental-systems accounts that characterize infant learning as both embodied and embedded, emerging through recurrent feedback among infant action, caregiver response, and structured environmental contexts (Tamis-LeMonda & Masek, 2023). Such variation supplies the very input through which perception, attention, and social expectations are calibrated in the earliest weeks and months.

Together, these considerations underscore that even the earliest observable capacities are already the products of structured developmental histories.

The appeal of innateness

Despite the challenges outlined above, innateness remains an appealing explanatory framework for infant researchers. This appeal may partly reflect the field's intellectual history: much of infant research developed around innatist questions and theoretical frameworks when methodological tools and experimental paradigms were less sophisticated, infants' capacities for learning were less well understood, and the implications of restricted sampling for developmental explanation were less widely acknowledged. Although tools, paradigms, and knowledge have become increasingly sophisticated, the refinement of innateness as an explanatory principle has not always developed in step with these advances. The field often continues to cast the infant as an instrument with which to examine psychological organization independent of experience. For example, Geraci et al. (2025) argue that evidence from human newborns, who have "extremely limited experience with sociomoral phenomena" relative to older infants, suggests that selective responses to aspects of the social world may emerge independently of experience. Similarly, Geraci et al. (2026) refer to newborn visual sensitivities as an innate capacity to detect communicative intent. Other recent work has made related appeals to newborn or very early sensitivities as bearing on innate empathy, multimodal understanding, or number knowledge (Davidov et al., 2025; Lorenzi et al., 2025; Streri & de Hevia, 2023). Core knowledge remains an active contemporary theory rather than a historical position. It continues to be developed in contemporary work (e.g., Spelke, 2024a Spelke, , 2024b) ) and is placed in focus in theoretical challenges, including those raised by Bai et al. (2025). The mere fact that core knowledge theory remains both actively advanced and contested underscores its continuing influence in current developmental science. In these traditions, infants are presumed valuable precisely because they are presumptively closer to the biological starting point than older children and adults. Many foundational theories and experiments in the field are therefore implicitly or explicitly organized around these particular accounts of innateness.

These traditions also interact with methodological limitations. In the absence of detailed, mechanistic models of how fetal learning, early perceptual biases, caregiver input, and cultural practices could jointly generate early abilities, it becomes easy to default to the idea that the structure must already be present in some form. Against the messy reality of gene-culture co-development, innatist narratives may seem tractable, not because alternative accounts are impossible in principle, but because current developmental models are often too underspecified to compete with them. This is reinforced by a deeply ingrained cultural image of the infant as an unconditioned or "pure" organism, pre-social, pre-cultural, and largely unimpacted by learning.

Even though there is now strong evidence for fetal learning and for dense social and cultural input from birth onward, much theorizing still leans on the intuition that very young infants have not yet had enough experience for cultural explanations to be plausible. That intuition is reinforced by a broader rationalist tradition in philosophy and cognitive science in which early competence is treated as evidence for inborn structure, as well as by behavioral-genetic findings showing substantial heritability for some psychological traits. For example, Plomin and von Stumm's (2018) review of the genetics of intelligence argues that individual differences in intelligence are substantially heritable and increasingly predictable from genome-wide polygenic scores. Such findings are important, but they are often taken to carry more developmental significance than heritability estimates can support. Heritability estimates describe sources of variation within particular populations and environments; they often do not specify the developmental mechanisms through which traits emerge, nor do they establish that particular abilities are innate, experience-independent, or unmalleable across environmental and cultural contexts.

Taken together, these forces make innateness an attractive explanatory position. It is built into the foundational theories, fills explanatory gaps where detailed developmental accounts are lacking, resonates with traditional images of infants as culturally unconditioned, and appears to harmonize with some approaches to heritability. This history underscores the value of more explicitly specifying what is claimed to be innate, the developmental mechanisms proposed to produce it, and the evidence that would distinguish competing accounts.

Innateness and theory-building

There is no doubt that most if not all aspects of infant development must reflect biological constraint, and some version of an innateness-based account is necessary. Specific concerns arise, however, when claims are made of a "representational infant" with complex conceptual systems that are posited to exist prior to, and largely independent of, experience.

Those stronger claims are often inferred from experimental effects rather than from mechanistic accounts of how such structures could arise through development. A more productive approach may be to ask what, exactly, is being claimed about observed competencies. It then becomes easier to evaluate what evidence would support it, what evidence would challenge it, and what sort of developmental model would be required.

In this way, claims about innateness evoke classic debates about falsifiability. Scientific claims must expose themselves to the risk of refutation: they should rule out possible observations that would show them to be wrong (Popper, 1959). Claims should not be so broadly framed that an unbounded set of outcomes can be retroactively reinterpreted as consistent with the claim, leaving no conceivable pattern of data that could disconfirm it. In infant research, some innateness claims are framed so broadly that almost any developmental outcome can be reinterpreted as "expression" of an innate structure. Kuhn's subsequent analysis provides insight into the impact of falsifiability: whether claims are treated as falsifiable in practice depends on their role within a paradigm (Kuhn, 1962). Within any given dominant research tradition, causal accounts (e.g., appeals to innateness) can function as a partial explanation; variation in findings (e.g., null findings in infants; non-replication) may be absorbed by appealing to methodological variation or adding auxiliary explanations, rather than prompting rejection of the dominant framework.

In their strongest and least specified forms, some claims about innateness in the infant literature fall short on both counts. By Popper's account, many claims of innateness may not satisfy basic definitions of hypotheses at all, because they are framed so broadly that no realistic pattern of developmental data could falsify them. They instead function as default explanations, justified by the presumption that infants are socially and culturally unconditioned, rather than by having survived serious attempts at refutation. In Kuhnian terms, innateness often functions less as a testable hypothesis and more as part of the protected core of a long-accepted paradigm, insulated from challenges by its historical entrenchment and the inertia of existing theory-building practices.

By contrast, a more disciplined innateness hypothesis might try to specify what is supposedly built-in, when it should be observable, and what developmental factors would fail to surface the expression of innate capacities. Innateness is not automatically unfalsifiable, but it becomes scientifically interesting when embedded in theories that generate clear and potentially disconfirmable predictions about development, variation, and plasticity, rather than operating as an explanation that derives from a range of equivalently possible interpretations of data.

Future directions

Moving beyond the limitations of innateness claims does not mean relinquishing the guiding questions that have advanced infant research over decades. It means creating clearer definitions of innateness, aligning inferences about innateness with methods and findings, and considering putative innate structure within a broader multiplex of factors that co-determine development. In the present usage, codetermination does not simply mean "everything matters," nor is it intended to function as a formal theory on its own. It refers to a framework in which outcomes are explained by specifying interacting classes of factors: (a) organismic constraints, predispositions, and architectures; (b) the form, timing, and distribution of input available to the infant; (c) caregiving and cultural ecologies; (d) gene-culture dynamics; (e) the mechanisms through which these factors interact to produce change; and (f) the kinds of variation expected within and across ecologies. This distinction between a framework and a theory is important: frameworks can organize attention toward relevant concepts, processes, timescales, and methods, but their scientific value depends on how explicitly they are translated into testable theoretical claims (Stallworthy et al., 2026). The same explanatory standard should apply to co-determination. Evidence that experience influences development is no more sufficient as an explanation than evidence that a behavior appears early is sufficient to establish innateness. A well-specified co-determination account must therefore identify experience that is informationally relevant to the target behavior, specify the mechanism through which that experience acts together with organismic constraints, generate predictions about developmental or contextual variation, and identify evidence that would count against the proposed account. In this sense, co-determination is not a complete theory in itself, but a constraining framework for constructing and comparing developmental theories on common empirical ground.

The requirement for symmetrical explanatory standards can be illustrated by recent evidence that newborns preferentially attend to prosocial over antisocial interactions. Geraci et al. (2025), for example, reported that newborns looked longer at prosocial than antisocial events, including helping relative to hindering. This presents a demanding test for a co-determination account because demonstrating that newborns have extensive prenatal developmental histories, relevant to this particular outcome, is not sufficient to explain this particular response (but see Lourenco, 2026, for alternative explanations).

Prenatal stress, maternal physiology, auditory learning, or epigenetic modification do not themselves provide information about helping and hindering, and the visual relations instantiated in these displays are unavailable prenatally. A co-determination account would therefore have to identify developmentally available information relevant to the distinction, such as structure in newborns' early postnatal experience of agents, goals, contingent action, or approach and withdrawal and specify how learning operating over that input, together with any proposed perceptual or attentional biases, could generate differential responding to the displays. It should further predict how the response would vary with relevant experience or ecology and identify evidence that would falsify this explanation. If the preference proved robust despite meaningful variation in relevant postnatal experience, and models constrained by infants' approximated input could not generate the effect without additional built-in structure, then knowable and documented evidence of experience would be insufficient to explain the phenomenon. Such a finding would strengthen the case for biologically prepared constraints, while leaving open the further question of what those constraints consist of, for example, representational content, attentional or perceptual biases, learning preparedness, or architectural organization. The point is therefore not to privilege experiential explanation, but to require competing developmental accounts to meet the same standards of specificity and falsification.

A second and related future direction concerns the empirical base of the field. A stronger basis for claims about the origins of human cognition and innate structure requires data from infants in a wide range of cultural, linguistic, socioeconomic, and ecological contexts. Agreement across multiple crosscultural settings is not, by itself, confirmation of universality or innateness. A concerning response to calls for greater sampling diversity has been the tendency to test relatively small samples of infants in one high-resource and one low-resource setting, often using tasks developed and implemented by researchers from the high-resource context, and to infer universality from similar patterns of responding across the two groups. Thus, diversification is not simply a matter of adding research sites. Thoughtful diversification includes building infrastructures for global developmental research, supporting cross-site collaborations, partnering with researchers and communities in underrepresented settings from conception onward, and adopting transparent standards for reporting sociodemographic and cultural information. In this respect, enterprises such as ManyBabies have immense value as we wrestle with sampling diversity (Frank et al., 2017). It also includes harmonizing measures across laboratories while preserving contextual relevance, reporting cultural and ecological variables in theoretically meaningful ways, and creating incentive structures that reward collaborative, interdisciplinary, and community-engaged work. A lack of diversity in infant samples means that what appears to be innate and thus universal features of development may instead reflect the boundaries of the widely studied populations, settings, and methods used to map the infant mind rather than boundaries of the infant mind itself (Singh, 2026). Diversification is thus a necessary condition for claims of universality and of innateness.

Third, the assumption that newborns are effectively experience-free is difficult to sustain. By extension, newborns cannot be treated as uniquely privileged "test beds" for innateness simply because they have had little postnatal experience. Birth is not the beginning of development, and the presence of a behavior at birth does not, on its own, establish that the behavior emerged independently of experience.

As discussed, newborns arrive with developmental histories, and in at least some domains, those histories are already consequential. Clear evidence comes from perceptual and sensory development and its later expression. In the auditory domain, fetuses hear filtered versions of maternal speech, learn about the rhythm and prosody of the ambient language, and show postnatal preferences for the mother's voice, familiar speech passages, and language rhythms to which they were exposed prenatally (DeCasper & Fifer, 1980; DeCasper & Spence, 1986; Mehler et al., 1988; Partanen et al., 2013). Related evidence comes from chemosensory learning. Flavors from the maternal diet can be transmitted through amniotic fluid, and prenatal exposure can shape infants' later acceptance of those flavors; newborns also show attraction to odors associated with amniotic fluid, suggesting that olfactory experience before birth can guide early orientation and feeding-related behavior (Mennella et al., 2001; Varendi et al., 1996).

These examples make clear that early-emerging preferences should not be treated as evidence of experience-independent structure simply because they are present at birth. As noted above, they also should not prohibit innateness claims. Prenatal exposure to speech, for example, bears directly on claims about newborn language preferences, voice recognition, rhythm, and prosody because auditory and linguistic information is available before birth. By contrast, the same evidence does not by itself explain early-emerging visual, object-based, or social sensitivities, for which the relevant sources of input may be absent, degraded, or available only after birth. Those capacities require their own domain-specific analyses of what input was available, whether that input contained the structure needed to support the observed behavior, and what prior biases, architectures, or maturational constraints would still need to be posited. Thus, the argument is not that early experience broadly refutes innateness, but that claims about innate structure should be evaluated against the specific input histories, learning opportunities, and mechanistic demands of the target domain.

Fourth, a co-determination perspective also encourages resisting the partitioning of infant development into seemingly independent constructs such as genetics, language exposure, caregiving, or culture. Instead, it favors models that treat these as interacting sources of constraint and change. For instance, when infants classified as bilingual or monolingual are compared on developmental outcomes, a host of intertwined factors-migration histories, socioeconomic conditions, caregiving practices, community networks, and educational resources-shape both groups in ways that make it impossible to interpret differences as the product of language exposure alone. In other words, the measured variable is often one element of a wider constellation of ecological and child-internal influences. This approach parallels calls in language development to integrate accounts of learning mechanisms with the nested social and cultural contexts in which language input is encountered (M. L. Rowe & Weisleder, 2020). A co-determination model should therefore specify which of these factors are treated as relevant to the mechanism of interest, how they are expected to interact, and where the model predicts convergence versus divergence across contexts.

Fifth, human infants develop in culturally organized niches that have themselves been shaped by long histories of cultural evolution. Virtually every aspect of infants' experience is part of the developmental process, not merely a backdrop against which genes independently do their work. Accordingly, most single-laboratory infant experiments support local, situated interpretations and do not straightforwardly license innatist or universal claims. It then becomes natural to ask how the cultural environments within which infants learn are themselves intertwined with underlying patterns of genetic variation. The idea that cultural and genetic variation can become tightly intertwined over time has gained increasing traction in human population research. For example, when cultural communities maintain stable, distinctive practices, these cultural boundaries can shape social networks and patterns of assortative mating. Over generations, such processes can lead to fine-scale genetic structure variation that is aligned with cultural and linguistic boundaries, even within a single country and a single language. A recent study by Pichkar and Creanza (2023) illustrates this dynamic by showing that English dialect boundaries co-localize with fine-grained genetic clusters across the UK, suggesting that dialect communities have historically constrained who interacts with whom and who has children with whom.

Extrapolating this to infant learners, this implies that early language learners in different regions are likely to inherit both region-specific linguistic input and genetic ancestry associated with language use in these regions. The extent to which such region-based genetic variation shapes infant language learning processes or outcomes remains an open empirical question and will require designs that can query interactions of cultural experience and genetic variation.

Seen in this light, evaluating innateness is an ambitious proposition that is not the province of any single researcher, laboratory, method, or domain area. It is unrealistic to expect that a single lab, working with a narrow range of paradigms in a limited set of populations, could establish innateness or universality on its own. The average infant scientist simply does not have access to the breadth of samples, methodological diversity, or analytical toolkit needed to do so. These considerations point toward a more consilient and collaborative science of infancy that explicitly integrates evidence across disciplines. The most informative accounts of infant development will likely be those that link genetic predispositions, learning processes, and the structure of cultural input within a single probabilistic framework, drawing on insights from the psychological sciences, cultural evolution, developmental biology, behavioral epigenetics, cognitive anthropology, sociology, and related disciplines. This integrated framework should also attend to developmental timing: experience may have different effects depending on whether it occurs during prenatal development, early postnatal calibration, sensitive periods, or later periods of increasing specialization (Nelson & Gabard-Durnam, 2020). Although the field may not yet be fully equipped-conceptually, methodologically, or infrastructurally-to evaluate such complex multi-level models, particularly given its current tendency toward "solo science," greater cross-disciplinary integration provides a useful direction for future work.

Finally, alongside these conceptual and empirical shifts, we advocate a stronger normative commitment to parsimony in theorizing and in parallel, for greater intellectual humility in communicating claims. When confronted with striking early abilities, it is tantalizing to posit rich interpretation (a practice, implicitly encouraged by many high-impact outlets); a more conservative strategy is to first ask whether a simpler account, grounded in known perceptual biases, fetal and postnatal experience, and task demands, could plausibly produce the observed behavior. Work on scientific writing shows that seemingly small stylistic choices can lead readers to treat findings as broader and more general than the underlying, often narrow, samples warrant (DeJesus et al., 2019). In parallel, metascientific work on intellectual humility argues that credible science depends on foregrounding uncertainty and limitations, and on resisting incentive structures that reward overconfidence in exposition (Hoekstra & Vazire, 2021).

For claims about innateness, this suggests that modest, tightly constrained, data-derived interpretations should be treated as a strength rather than a weakness, and that stronger claims about universality or inborn structure should be explicitly marked as long-term hypotheses that often require convergent evidence across methods, models, and populations.

Machine learning as a source of insight into innateness

We are now seeing rapid advances in machine learning, developmental robotics, and computational modeling that shed unprecedented light on the origins of learning in both artificial and biological systems (Cangelosi & Schlesinger, 2015; Orhan & Lake, 2024; Oudeyer & Smith, 2016). Combined with finegrained behavioral and neural data, these approaches give us tools for tracing how induction, memory systems, embodiment, and regularities in the input can generate surprisingly sophisticated behavior over time, sometimes mimicking human behaviors often described as innate. We can now query within an input-dependent learner how knowledge arises, how learning stabilizes, and what kinds of priors are actually required for generalization. Contemporary machine learning also offers a way to make innateness claims about infant learning more precise by decomposing them into assumptions about inductive bias, input dependency, and learnability. This logic has clear precedents in earlier connectionist and developmental modeling traditions (Elman et al., 1996).

In computational terms, "innate structure" corresponds to architectural and prior constraints, while the environment is instantiated as distributions of visual, linguistic, or social input. Learning is implemented through optimization over that input (Lake et al., 2017; Stojnić et al., 2023). Models trained on this logic have produced mixed but informative results. Some show that fairly generic architectures can learn useful visual representations from naturalistic, child-directed input (Orhan & Lake, 2024).

Others suggest that some built-in structure may still be needed: a model of intuitive physics, for example, learned key physical expectations only when it represented the world in terms of objects (Piloto et al., 2022) and current models still fall short of infants on tests of commonsense psychology (Stojnić et al., 2023; for a review of computational approaches to theory of mind, see Langley et al., 2022). These models cannot tell us directly what infants' brains represent or how they work; however, they can help clarify which constraints must be built in, which regularities can be learned from structured input, and offer some predictions that would distinguish among competing innateness claims.

Conclusion

Taken together, the evidence reviewed in this paper suggests that innateness, in its broad and underspecified form, is not a sufficiently robust explanatory principle for understanding infant development. This conclusion is directed most strongly at claims that infer rich representational content from early behavior while leaving the developmental route to that behavior largely unspecified and to more moderate accounts that invoke innateness without clear specification of mechanisms. Earlyemerging patterns arise from developmental processes in which culturally organized experiences can already have begun to shape development in interaction with biological mechanisms. Philosophical analyses of falsifiability further suggest that many strong innateness claims remain insulated from refutation when they are framed too broadly or when the relevant level of explanation is left ambiguous.

A developmental science that seeks to explain the origins of infant capacities should therefore adopt a more explicit framework of co-determination. Such a framework treats infants' abilities as emerging from dynamic, historically situated interactions among biological biases, prenatal and early postnatal experience, caregiving ecologies, cultural practices, and longer-run gene-culture dynamics. It also encourages closer integration with research on cultural evolution, behavioral genetics, epigenetics, developmental theory, and computational modeling. Advancing expository practices such as parsimony in interpretation, specificity in writing, and humility in inference-making is likely to benefit the future of the field as well. None of this represents a retreat from the central questions that animated innateness debates; instead, it is a proposal for addressing them with defined concepts, robust developmental models, and more disciplined modes of inference.
