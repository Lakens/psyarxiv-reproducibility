To improve Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 4 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Despite being hailed as a promising avenue for preventing increasingly prevalent adolescent mental health problems, digital parenting interventions to strengthen adolescent mental well-being remain scarce. How such interventions are developed and experienced by end-users provides key insights for their later implementation and contextualizes information on their effectiveness, yet little systemic evidence is available.

Objective: To inform optimal development and aid careful evaluation of digital parenting interventions, this preregistered multimethod study served three aims. First, to identify theoretical, functional, and user-derived lessons for digital parenting interventions for adolescent mental well-being, distilled from the challenges of developing a novel digital parenting intervention. Second, to examine user experience in terms of feasibility, adoption, acceptability, and appropriateness as early markers of implementation success. Third, to understand whether user experience is linked to characteristics of the intervention (i.e., universal versus personalized delivery), parents (e.g., openness to advice), or adolescents (e.g., depressive symptoms).

Methods: Development of the intervention was based on Self-Determination Theory and followed an iterative process, including focus groups with parents and adolescents, a pilot study among n = 12 families and subsequent exit-interviews, and a brainstorm session with experts (n = 6). The intervention was evaluated among parents (N = 199, Mage = 47.6, SDage = 4.7), resulting in administrative and daily diary data of user experience (feasibility, adoption, acceptability, and appropriateness). Baseline data were used to test whether intervention, parent, and adolescent characteristics related to user experience.

Results: Findings suggest that adolescent perspectives align with the tenets of selfdetermination theory as a framework that can be translated to concrete advice for parents, that the frequency and delivery of advice should fit family routines, and parental agency and choice were important user needs. Feasibility, adoption, acceptability, and appropriateness were sufficient according to our preregistered criteria, but there was substantial heterogeneity between parents and within parents (across days). User experience did not differ between a personalized and universal version of the intervention. Parents who were generally more open to advice, and parents of boys, younger adolescents, and less depressed adolescents evaluated the intervention more favorably.

Conclusions: Findings suggest that the fit between our intervention and parents differed substantially across parents, and from tip tot tip, highlighting the need for further refinement of our digital parenting interventions before implementation, and the consideration of user experience throughout development.

Introduction

Adolescents increasingly suffer from mental health problems, such as feelings of depression and anxiety [1] [2] [3] [4]. There has been a strong call, therefore, for preventive interventions [2, 3], whose effectiveness may be strengthened by tailoring to specific populations or distributing prevention at a large scale [5]. By delivering preventive interventions digitally, both avenues can be supported, facilitating personalization [6], and scalability ( [7]. As a critical window for the emergence of mental health problems, adolescence is a prime target for preventive interventions [8]. Indeed, digital mental health interventions are increasingly used to improve adolescents' mental well-being and they have shown generally favorable effects: a recent review identified 103 distinct eHealth interventions, all directed at helping adolescents improve their own mental well-being [9]. In addition to interventions targeting individual resources, strengthening adolescents' social support systems is also crucial for resilience [10] [11] [12]. Even though parenting interventions in particular have been identified as a promising avenue for improving adolescent mental wellbeing [17], we identified only two digital interventions specifically targeting parents to support adolescents' mental well-being [18, 19].

Although digital interventions for parents of adolescents demonstrated promising results in term of strengthened parenting [18, 20, 21], their development process and implementation have received relatively little attention despite being crucial to their success in the "real world" [22] [23] [24]. This preregistered multimethod study therefore (1) aims to identify transferable lessons from the development process of a novel digital parenting intervention for youth mental health (the PARADOx intervention) in terms of theoretical, functional, and user-derived insights which may promote satisfaction and usage of digital parenting interventions. Among almost 200 families, we (2) evaluated four indicators of how this digital parenting intervention was experienced by parents as early markers for implementation success (feasibility, adoption, acceptability, and appropriateness; [23]), and (3) examined whether intervention, parent, and adolescent characteristics related to user experience.

Digital Parenting Interventions and Adolescent Mental Well-Being

Parents are uniquely positioned to support adolescents' mental well-being because they maintain a critical role in their child's development throughout adolescence [13] [14] [15], despite changes in adolescents' social lives, such as an increasing orientation towards peers [16]. Many parents seek parenting support via a range of resources [26], which may aid parents in helping their children navigate adolescence. Professionally guided interventions, such as family therapy or coaching sessions with individual professionals, typically have a solid scientific foundation and allow for tailoring to individual families' needs, especially now that they can be combined with data-driven dashboards (e.g., [27]). However, such interventions are generally costly and less scalable. By contrast, self-help and informal advice, such as seeking information online or from other parents, can be scalable and inexpensive and might align with parents' unique demands, but these resources are not always strongly evidence-informed or scientifically sound. More static advice, such as books by experts, may be more scientifically grounded (e.g., [28]), but offer less scalable options for personalization. Digitally supported parenting interventions can uniquely assist in delivering theory-driven and empirically validated advice to parents at a large scale, with low costs [7], and with opportunities for personalization (e.g., through data or algorithms; [6]).

Despite their potential to deliver scientifically grounded, tailored advice to parents at scale, surprisingly few digital parenting interventions exist to promote youth mental health. A recent meta-analysis of 31 studies on digital parenting interventions identified only a single intervention specifically targeting adolescent internalizing mental health problems, described in three studies [25]. The Partners-in-Parenting (PiP) program [19] [20] [21] aims to prevent adolescent anxiety and depression by strengthening parenting through a personalized feedback report and up to nine interactive online modules (15-25 minutes). An evaluation of the PiP-program using a 12-month follow-up showed favorable effects on parent-reported parenting [21], although not for adolescent-reported mental health problems. Another recently reported program [18] aimed to promote parents' supportive practices in the context of their children's transition to secondary school. This program successfully strengthened maternal autonomy support and improved children's perceived control, competence and school motivation via nine 20-minute online modules.

The PARADOx Intervention

To address the paucity of digital parenting interventions to support adolescent mental well-being [25, 29], we developed and evaluated a novel preventive digital parenting intervention aimed at strengthening supportive parenting [30, 31]. The content of the PARADOx intervention is grounded in applications of Self-Determination Theory (SDT; [32, 33] in the domain of parenting [15, 34, 35]. This theory forwards three dimensions of parenting that are assumed to feed into adolescents' basic psychological needs for autonomy, relatedness, and competence, namely autonomy support (i.e., promoting volitional functioning), support (e.g., warmth and responsiveness), and structure (e.g., clear boundaries and expectations, process-oriented feedback). Through fulfilment of adolescents' basic psychological needs, these dimensions of need-supportive parenting are assumed to promote adolescent mental well-being [36, 37], an assumption that has received systematic empirical support (e.g., [38] [39] [40]).

The PARADOx intervention is fully digitally delivered, without professional blended care, and co-designed with researchers, parents and adolescents, which may strengthen theoretical grounding and ecological fit (i.e., meeting the end-users needs; [41]). The intervention includes two elements: a one-time data-driven parent feedback report followed by brief, digital parenting advice formulated as text-based 5-minute challenges and activities.

Challenges were distributed three times per week for six weeks (18 in total; total time investment approximately 1.5 hours). Two different versions of the intervention were developed that differ in terms of delivery. In a personalized condition, participants received feedback on their own data and they could choose between several challenges. In a universal condition, parents received a report based on "the average" parent in the sample and expertselected tips. Compared to current digital parenting interventions which aim to support adolescent mental well-being, the PARADOx intervention requires a lower time commitment per module, which enhances its scalability. It also allows comparing personalization in terms of parental agency in choosing between tips with universal advice, and comprehensively examines user experience among a large sample. For an overview of intervention and study characteristics, see Appendix 1.

Development Process of Digital Parenting Interventions

Intervention development often remains somewhat of a "black box", potentially leading researchers to continually encounter similar conceptual and practical challenges, rather than building upon a cumulative body of knowledge. Despite the existence of several frameworks for coding and guiding the development and evaluation of interventions (e.g., [42] [43] [44]), the development process of interventions or lessons which may be derived from it, are still rarely reported [45]. One exception is the Partners-in-Parenting program [19], for which the development principles, activities, and suggested use cases have been described explicitly.

To further aid the development and careful evaluation of digital parenting interventions, we draw transferable lessons from the development process of the PARADOx intervention. To integrate and understand the unique interplay between scientific theory and a validated evidence-base and design-related considerations, we developed the intervention in co-creation with end-users during iterative phases, following good practices of the Bloombox Toolkit (https://bloombox.tools/; [46]). By iteratively combining insights from scientific theory and evidence-base, parents and adolescents (the direct and indirect end-users), and theoretical experts, we identified three broad lessons derived from challenges encountered during development: (1) theoretical lessons, especially concerning the translation of theoretical principles into concrete intervention content that could be applied in daily life, (2) user-based lessons, which may shape parents' satisfaction and usage of the intervention, including agency and motivation, and (3) functional lessons: technical and practical needs for intervention delivery. We report parent-, adolescent-, and expert-insights obtained throughout development within these lessons.

User Experience of a Digital Parenting Intervention

A theoretically sound, iteratively designed intervention may still fail however, when intended end users do not (want to) use it in practice [23]. Even when an intervention is distributed at large scale it may yield null results, either because the intervention itself failed to produce such effects, or because users were unsatisfied with how an intervention was delivered and did not apply provided insights. Without understanding how end-users experienced an intervention, it is therefore hard to grasp and interpret any study on the effectiveness of interventions. The scarce studies on digital parenting interventions for adolescent mental well-being already provide the very first insights into parents' user experience, focusing mainly on intervention completion [18, 20], or general satisfaction [18].

While parental satisfaction was generally high (4.29/5; [18]) and feasibility, in terms of program completion, was reasonably strong [18, 21], it remains unknown which specific aspects of the interventions were evaluated more or less favorably, or how they were experienced by parents at all.

In this study we draw on the Taxonomy of Implementation Outcomes [23, 24] to structurally investigate parents' user experience in an early phase as preconditions for successful implementation, focusing on: (1) feasibility, (2) adoption, (3) acceptability, and (4) appropriateness, as each of these steps are essential to guarantee implementation success at large. Feasibility concerns how well an intervention could be applied in its intended setting and population. We include a single marker of feasibility in terms of compliance, operationalized as the number of tips viewed by parents. Adoption concerns the (intended) application of insights (i.e., whether parents (intended to) apply provided tips in their daily life). Acceptability refers to participants' satisfaction with various aspects of an innovation (i.e., perceived helpfulness of tips). Finally, appropriateness refers to how well an intervention fits, for example, its recipients (i.e., how well parents felt that tips matched their, or their child's needs; [23, 24]).

Predictors of Successful User Experience

Because parenting interventions are known to yield heterogeneous effects (e.g., [47]), it is important to identify predictors of user experiences in digital parenting interventions.

Because parenting is theorized (e.g., [48, 49]) and empirically established (e.g., [50]) to be highly heterogenous, a parenting intervention will likely be experienced very differently by individual parents. Tailoring intervention content to individual users' needs is assumed to enhance relevance and effectiveness [6]. Hence, we examine the potential of a personalized version of our intervention compared to a one-size-fits-all version. Based on previous intervention-based research documenting the benefits of a tailored approach (e.g., [21, 51]), we expect the personalized intervention to be experienced more favorably compared to the universal intervention (H1).

Unfortunately, due to the absence of systematic research on the implementation of digital parenting interventions, it is still unknown for whom interventions are more feasible, which parents are more likely to apply insights, or who might find certain insights most helpful. Identifying parent and adolescent characteristics which are related to how an intervention was experienced is important both to explain heterogeneity in the effectiveness of parenting interventions and to aid personalization efforts. Based on scientific literature, we hypothesize that two parent-characteristics in particular are related to the appraisal of a digital parenting intervention: parental openness to advice, and parental perfectionism. We further explore a range of parent-(e.g., sex, age, baseline parenting practices, parent well-being) and adolescent-characteristics (e.g., sex, age, adolescent well-being).

Parents likely differ in their motivation to engage in an intervention [52]. Parenting advice may also be perceived as normative [53], or even judgmental [54], resulting in perceived parental pressure to "solve" or prevent their children's problems [53]. The way parents perceive advice is key to its application, and even parents' own well-being [55].

Hence, we assess whether openness to advice relates to more favorable user experience outcomes (H2).

Parental perfectionism may be another characteristic affecting how advice is experienced. This orientation involves setting exceptionally high standards for one's parenting or the belief that such standards are imposed by society [56]. Such disproportionate standards may lead parents to adhere to intervention protocols more strictly but to simultaneously appraise the quality of an intervention more poorly, reflecting increased worrying and rumination [57]. Longitudinal research indeed indicates that certain forms of parenting perfectionism predict lower parental self-efficacy [58] and increased parental burnout [59]. Hence, we assess whether parents with higher reported parenting perfectionism generally report higher feasibility and adoption, but lower acceptability and appropriateness (H3).

Current Study

To address several gaps in research on digital parenting interventions for youth mental health, and to inform the development and implementation of future digital parenting interventions, the current preregistered study (https://tinyurl.com/mrvyn6sf) has three aims.

First, to structurally describe transferable lessons learned from the development process of the PARADOx intervention. Second, to describe feasibility and parents' adoption, acceptability and appropriateness as early markers of implementation success. Third, to understand how these user experience outcomes may depend on intervention, adolescent and parent characteristics, including H1) personalization, H2) parents openness to advice, and H3) parenting perfectionism.

Between August 27th and October 7th 2024, 541 parents of adolescents participated in the broader PARADOx study. To participate, parents had to live together with their participating child three or more days per week, speak Dutch, and own a smartphone (iOS or Android). The full study protocol and trial have been preregistered separately (https://osf.io/zfcmd/). Parents filled out an online baseline survey (ca. 45 minutes) via Qualtrics to obtain demographic characteristics. Afterwards, participants received 100 daily surveys (ca. 3-5 minutes) via the Avicenna application on parenting practices and well-being.

To evaluate the intervention, a subgroup of 217 parents received the digital parenting intervention. Parents received 18 parenting tips over six weeks, embedded within the daily diaries. User experience outcomes were assessed the day after receiving a tip.

Analytical Sample

The analytical sample included 199 parents who contributed at least one valid evaluation of a tip. Data were considered valid if a parent viewed a tip, and subsequently evaluated this tip the following day, resulting in 1963 observations. Parents (age 36-61 years, Mage = 47.6, SDage = 4.7) were primarily Dutch (95%, 2% European non-Dutch, 3% non-European), biological mothers (82%, 18% biological fathers), and theoretically educated (78% college or university degree, 18% applied education, 4% high-school), living in an urban environment (60%). Most parents were together with the other biological parent (78%), and either shared parenting responsibilities equally (65%), or considered themselves the primary caregiver (34%). Mothers were overrepresented, and education level was substantially above the national average [60].

Measures User Experience -Feasibility, Adoption, Acceptability, and Appropriateness.

Feasibility of daily parenting tips was operationalized using administrative data, in terms of the number of daily tips (out of 18) viewed by parents over the course of six weeks.

In daily diaries, parents' evaluation of the adoption, acceptability, and appropriateness was administered through self-reports, via agreement with statements (visual analogue scale from 0 ("Not at all") to 100 ("Very much"), which referred to the tip provided the previous day ("Yesterday, you received a tip. To what extent do you agree with the following statements about this tip?"). Adoption was measured with a single item ("I applied the tip in my own parenting or intend to do so."). Acceptability was operationalized with a single item ("I found this tip helpful."). Appropriateness was operationalized as the average on two items ("The tip suited me well as a parent." and "The tip suited my child well."), which correlated highly with each other across all tips (r = .93). To allow for qualitative context, parents could share additional thoughts about provided tips ("Would you like to share anything else about the tip?"), which we include to illustrate quantitative findings.

Openness to Advice (H2)

Parents reported at baseline whether they were "open to receiving parenting advice as a parent" on a visual analogue scale of 0 ("Not at all") to 100 ("Very much").

Aim 1. Intervention Development

Aim 1. Intervention Development

Theoretical Lessons

To build the intervention on a solid theoretical fundament, and to translate a broad theoretical framework into specific parenting practices as intervention targets, we conducted focus groups with adolescents and parents, and iteratively designed and revised parenting tips with theoretical experts and parents. We identified parenting as a promising context for promoting adolescent mental well-being, identified concrete parenting practices to target, and constructed parenting tips.

"The Home Environment" is Key to Well-Being. The underlying theoretical assumption that the home environment, parenting specifically, may be a promising target for intervention, was confirmed by adolescents. A majority (62%) of adolescents in the focus group sessions indicated the home environment, largely because of their parents, as the most important context for their mental well-being, above school, peers, the online domain, or "self" (e.g., personal resilience). One adolescent (girl, 12 years) illustrated this by highlighting the stable role parents play in their children's lives, when asked whether she felt having arguments with one's parents was "worse" than an argument with friends: "Parents, because you can't get a new parent, but you can get new friends."

Parenting Practices Align with Theory.

To move from broad theoretical dimensions (Self Determination Theory; [32, 33]) to concrete parenting practices for intervention, we qualitatively investigated whether parenting resonated with parents and adolescents as a solution to adolescent mental health problems. Adolescents also identified specific parenting practices which they felt were particularly salient to their mental well-being, which aligned with the theoretical needs for parents to promote autonomy (e.g., allowing adolescents room to explore an activity, stepping in with advice afterwards), structure (e.g., establishing clear responsibilities regarding household chores), and relatedness (e.g., taking the time to ask about concrete activities). For autonomy support, adolescents emphasized that parents should genuinely listen to their perspectives, involve them in decisions affecting them or at least clarify the reasoning behind those decisions, and emphasized the freedom to make mistakes and learn from them. One adolescent girl (14 years) illustrated the latter: "I think it's good if you also have freedom. So that your parents are strict, but that at a certain moment they let you discover things for yourself and that they then [afterwards] say, maybe this can be done differently next time and maybe next time we should set our boundaries better, you know like that." Concerning structure, adolescents considered rules and boundaries as sometimes unpleasant in the immediate moment but important to their well-being in the long term. One girl (13 years) highlighted the value of predictability that clear rules and boundaries provide: "I find it really convenient if I just know where I stand and what I need to do.

And when someone is going to be home or something like that, so that it's just clear, that nothing ever comes really unexpected or something. I just know when I have a chore to do, or not."

Finally, concerning relational support, adolescents underlined spending quality time with parents. One girl (14 years) illustrated this within the context of everyday activities when discussing how parents could best display affection: "Show that they know what you have been doing so then for example ask like 'how did that project end up' or something like that, and then for example with listening just say, like, shall we drink a cup of tea then you can tell me about it', you know, so really making some time, that's nice."

Designing Tips with Experts and Parents. Throughout development, we constructed the intervention content iteratively with experts, adolescents and parents to optimize the interplay between theoretical validity and ecological fit. The final tips were rated highly by experts (on average 8.3/10 on theoretical alignment, and 7.7/10 on practicality) and parents (M = 8.5/10 on perceived quality, M = 8.6/10 on practicality). Whether these scores were equally high in a larger study was tested (see Aim 2) in terms of adoption, acceptability, and appropriateness.

Functional Lessons

Functional lessons encompass technical or practical insights that may support the delivery or application of parenting interventions and were distilled from (1) the brainstorm session with experts, and (2) the technical pilot study and related semi-structured interviews with parents and adolescents. We learned that interventions that need to be applied in real life, also need to align with family routines. That is, delivered at the right frequency (a couple of times per week but not daily), in a format that stimulates activation, combined with attractive and simple data-visualization.

Key to the functional delivery of tips according to parent interviews was that it should align with families' routines. That is, advice should be delivered in the evenings, outside of the busy daily routine, to aid visibility and application of tips. Parents also advised that distributing tips daily was considered too much to be able to apply. Experts mentioned that the form should match the type of provided advice. For example, if aiming to strengthen a specific skill, advice should promote interactivity with that skill rather than passively presenting theory. Concerning the intended data-driven personalized feedback report, experts highlighted that visualizations of such complex data should be simple and attractive, which can be facilitated by involving parents throughout the development process. A second element of our intervention was to apply parenting tips embedded in daily surveys via the Avicenna smartphone application. Daily surveys in general, collected via the smartphone app during the pilot test, were feasible, with adolescents completing 71.3% of daily surveys during the pilot test, and parents 92.2%. The feasibility of the study was tested further at larger scale (N = 217, see below).

User-derived Lessons

Throughout development, we identified two important user-derived lessons which may promote satisfaction and usage of digital parenting interventions, namely parental agency, and openness to advice. Insights were obtained through (1) a brainstorm session with experts, and (2) semi-structured interviews with parents and adolescents following the technical pilot study.

Parental Agency and Personalization.

Experts emphasized in a brainstorm session that parents should retain agency by deciding whether they want advice, which advice fits their family, and what advice they choose to apply. Parents and adolescents likewise indicated such a need for personalization in exit interviews following the pilot study, in that advice should be related to their own parenting, but did highlight an important condition for personalization: privacy. Parents and adolescents should not have insight into each other's data. The hypothesis that personalization would lead to better uptake was subsequently tested in the evaluation study (H1).

Openness to Advice.

Parenting advice was generally supported by both parents and adolescents in semi-structured interviews: parents indicated an interest in insight into their family functioning, openness to receiving parenting advice, and positively appraised reflecting on interactions with their child, but some indicated that they would personally experience advice as confronting. Adolescents were also generally positive about their parents receiving parenting advice, but highlighted that parents should be willing to receive such advice. The hypothesis that openness is indeed key to user experience was tested in the evaluation study (see Aim 3, H2).

Aim 3. Which Characteristics Explain User Experience?

Our third aim was to investigate parental, adolescent, and intervention characteristics that might explain differences in user experience. Three hypotheses were investigated. First (H1), we expected parents who received the personalized intervention (n = 110), and were offered more choice and agency, to experience the intervention more favorably compared to the universal intervention group (n = 106). However, no statistically significant differences were identified in feasibility (number of tips viewed; t(206.75) = .31, p = .758), adoption (t(194.25) = .38, p = .708), acceptability (t(189.55) = -.61, p = .541), or family appropriateness (t(192.43) = -1.00, p = .317; see Table 2). Hence, personalization did not result in meaningfully different appraisals of intervention content, rejecting H1.

Second, confirming H2, parents who indicated a greater openness to receiving parenting advice at baseline generally experienced parenting tips more favorably across outcomes, which was also identified as a lesson from the development process. While unrelated to the number of tips viewed (feasibility), openness to advice at baseline was modestly correlated with adoption (r(193) = .27, p < .001), acceptability (r(193) = .20, p = .006), and appropriateness for parent and adolescent (r(193) = .21, p = .004; see Table 4).

Third, the theoretically informed hypothesis that parenting perfectionism would be related to higher feasibility and adoption but lower acceptability and appropriateness of daily tips (H3), was rejected. Parenting perfectionism was not associated with user experience (see Table 4).

Subsequently, we conducted (preregistered) exploratory analyses without explicit hypotheses to assess whether several parent and adolescent characteristics were associated with user experience (see Table 4). Concerning parental characteristics, older parents generally viewed slightly more tips (i.e., higher feasibility; r(185) = .15, p = .038). Baseline parenting practices were also modestly correlated with user experience. Specifically, more autonomy supportive parents were more likely to (be willing) to apply tips in their own family (r(199) = .14, p = .042), and parents who reported more structure and autonomy support rated the appropriateness of tips for their family higher (respectively: r(199) = .16, p = .028 and r(199) = .16, p = .020). Parental well-being, self-efficacy, personality, support, and overprotection were not significantly related to user experience.

Adolescent characteristics were unrelated to feasibility, but user experience was generally poorer among parents of older adolescents (adoption: r(199) = -.26, p < .001; acceptability: r(199) = -.18, p = .010; appropriateness: r(199) = -.23, p = .001), and youth with depressive symptoms (acceptability: r(199) = -.14, p = .047; appropriateness: r(199) = -.15, p = .030), and lower for parents of girls (acceptability: r(199) = -.15, p = .031; appropriateness: r(199) = -.14, p = .049). For instance, one mother (age 51, practical education) of a 14-year old girl indicated about a tip promoting enjoyment of household tasks such as cleaning the dishes: "Tips seem intended for younger children, I did this when they were little but they won't fall for this anymore."

Discussion

Parenting interventions are being hailed as a promising answer to the rising tide of adolescent mental health problems [2, 3, 17]. Yet despite the established potential of eHealth in other domains, such as to support adolescents' own mental well-being [9], or address children's behavioral problems [25, 29], there are remarkably few scientifically sound digital parenting interventions which directly target parenting to improve adolescents' mental wellbeing [25, 29]. Although the first evaluations of such interventions in terms of participant engagement and broad indicators of program satisfaction are promising [18, 20, 21], more systematic research programs are needed to guide the optimal development of such interventions and to better understand factors that may facilitate or hinder adequate implementation in practice.

Therefore, this study on a novel digital parenting intervention (PARADOx) for promoting adolescent mental well-being served three purposes. First, to describe transferable lessons learned of the development of digital parenting interventions. From our iterative development process, Self-Determination Theory proved a valuable theoretical framework which could be translated into modifiable parenting targets. Parental openness to receiving advice, and agency, choice, and privacy in applying advice were key user needs. Functionally, delivery of advice should align with parents' routines and be unambiguous to promote feasibility. Second, mapping user experience, parents (N = 225) generally evaluated the intervention sufficiently (scoring ≥ 50/100 on adoption, acceptability, and appropriateness across tips), but not ideal. There were important individual differences between and within parents in their evaluation of the tips. Third, we explored parent, adolescent, and intervention characteristics related to parents' user experience. Personalized advice was not evaluated more favorably compared to universal advice. Parents who reported a higher openness to advice, and higher autonomy support and structure at baseline generally evaluated tips more favorably, as did parents of boys, parents of younger adolescents, and parents of adolescents with less depressive symptoms.

Transferable Lessons from Intervention Development

The development process of our intervention yielded several findings, from which we draw three broad transferable insights concerning future development of digital parenting interventions. First, a theoretical framework is an important foundation for intervention development, but should be complemented with insights from intended end-users to translate theory into actionable content (see for example Bloombox; https://bloombox.tools/; [46]).

Self-Determination Theory proved a particularly useful framework for the development of our digital parenting intervention given its theoretical depth and extensive application to the domain of parenting. This allowed us to draw from a range of well-described and empirically supported constructs. However, parent-ratings of the tips during development highlighted that despite a solid theoretical foundation and expert backing, certain tips were found to be poorly applicable in daily life. This is in line with literature emphasizing the need to include enduser perspectives in the development of parenting programs to improve ecological fit [41].

Second, the success of an intervention depends not solely on the quality of its content: how it is delivered to end-users is equally important, yet a key challenge remains balancing user needs with practical feasibility.

Mapping the needs and preferences of parents can promote their eventual engagement with an intervention providing advice [75]. In our qualitative sessions, participants mentioned several needs to improve the end-user experience.

Specifically, advice should be unambiguous, delivered with the right frequency, and the timing of parenting advice should follow family routines. Crucially, however, such insights do not automatically translate into evident design solutions. Family routines are not uniform for example, and the optimal timing of advice may differ per family, and even from moment to moment. Hence, accommodating every preference fully may not be feasible. The development process of an intervention should extend beyond mapping user needs, also defining which needs can actually be accommodated, to which extent, and how needs are handled that cannot be accommodated.

Third, we suggest a careful balance between providing personalized interventions, and maintaining privacy and agency. While parents and adolescents felt that advice should meet their personal needs, privacy was also an important condition raised by families; parents and adolescents should not have insight into each other's data. Parents and adolescents often place great importance om maintaining control over which health related data is shared between them for a variety of reasons [76], and disagreement on such privacy rules may induce conflict [77]. This raises an important consideration about how personalization is applied, which is particularly salient to interventions which concern data from multiple sources. Information which may be of particular use to personalizing advice for one user, may be sensitive information to another user and affect whether a participant feels comfortable engaging with an intervention. Hence, we suggest that privacy should be viewed not just as a technical or lawful requirement, but as a key condition which may affect how users experience and engage with an intervention. Given that parents, adolescents, and experts indicated that parental agency in terms of deciding if and how they apply parenting advice was crucial, one solution may be to provide families control over the extent to which their data is used for personalization.

To Personalize or Not to Personalize?

One of the key debates in parenting literature is whether parenting affects adolescent well-being in entirely unique and very diverse ways across families, or whether certain parenting practices are fundamentally beneficial or harmful for all adolescents [78]. However, even the route towards universally beneficial parenting practices may vary from family to family, and from moment to moment [78]. Hence, even universally beneficial intervention targets should benefit from personalization by addressing the pathways through which such universal effects are achieved. Indeed, while parents and adolescents in our study provided various examples of important parenting practices in line with Self-Determination Theory, the exact practices highlighted differed per participant. Parents and adolescents also indicated that advice should match their own family, and when applied to a larger sample, user experience of tips varied notably between and within families and tips. That is, most parents evaluated some tips favorably and others poorly, and most tips were rated poorly by some parents, and positively by others. This would suggest that personalization is needed to align with families' unique circumstances and preferences.

Given the potential benefits of personalization in intervention research [6, 75], and its relevance inferred from our findings, it is surprising that personalization did not result in significantly different appraisals of daily tips in the current study. This may reflect the rudimentary nature of personalization in our study. Personalization included parental agency in selecting between different tips within the same theoretical domain, but parents could not choose the domain itself on which they received advice (e.g., advice on providing structure versus autonomy support), and received advice at set times. This personalization may have been too superficial to really create a sense of agency and ownership among parents.

Personalization may need to be more profound at a content level to account for the uniqueness of parental pathways to universally beneficial practices. Future studies might investigate other more comprehensive forms of personalization such as allowing parents to choose the very domain on which they receive advice and explicitly exploring different levels of agency, or tailoring advice based on data-driven daily parenting dynamics (i.e., JITAI's; [79]). More profound tailoring of content has shown potential in strengthening parenting practices [21], but how it affects parents experiences with an intervention remains unknown.

Another reason why personalization may be needed is that the degree to which parents engage in need-supportive parenting fluctuates from day to day [36], depends on their own need-satisfaction [80], and can be driven by adolescents as active agents in the parentchild relationship [50, 81]. These findings suggest that personalization should concern not just what content is delivered to an individual family, but also when such advice is delivered, and how often it is delivered. Data-driven algorithms, as used in Just-In-Time Adaptive Interventions (JITAIs; [79]), where the content is adapted to an individual's current (changes in) condition and context, may offer a promising direction for parenting interventions.

Which Characteristics Explain User Experience?

It is well-established that parents differ in their openness to be engaged in an intervention, with engagement being critical to effective implementation [83, 84]. Because the current intervention was developed for a broad target group, we explored characteristics salient to user experience, which may prove vital to further strengthening the intervention before implementation. We identified three parent characteristics at baseline that were generally associated with more positive user experience: more openness to advice, more structure, and more autonomy support.

The finding that parents who already engage more in the targeted practices or who are more open to advice provided more favorable evaluations, is not inherently problematic. This pattern does warrant attention however, if poorer evaluation also translates into lower engagement among parents for whom such parenting interventions might be particularly relevant, as the public health benefits of universal prevention rest upon its ability to reach a large population [85]. One way to remedy this problem may be to include explicit motivational strategies, which has shown promise in promoting parental engagement in formal professional interventions [86]. Engagement may also be improved by further lowering barriers to entry or increasing perceived relevance, for instance by more comprehensively including parents in the design of a program [75], personalization of intervention features [87], or by tailoring to relevant family characteristics such as cultural needs up front [88]. To increase intervention relevance across the board, it may be particularly salient to include those families in its development who are initially less open or motivated to engage in such an intervention. By only including highly motivated parents in development efforts, researchers may sustain distance to those who might benefit most.

How an intervention is experienced may depend not only on parents' characteristics, but also on characteristics of their adolescent. This SDT-based preventive intervention appears to be best experienced by parents of younger adolescents, boys, and parents of adolescents with fewer depressive symptoms. Limited suitability for parents of older adolescents may reflect a mismatch between intervention content and those families' needs.

An intervention aiming to support parents across adolescence may require substantively different content based on the adolescents' age to align with developmental changes [89], shifting family interactions [90], and resulting needs for advice. Early adolescence may also constitute a time where parents are particularly receptive to advice in general. Marking the onset of renegotiations of the parent-adolescent relationship, early adolescence is often characterized by increased conflict [91], potentially stemming from discrepancies between parents' and adolescents views of parenting practices [92]. Because early adolescence in particular may represent a time of profound change in family dynamics, parents may seek additional support and appraise it more positively.

The poorer user experience by parents of adolescents with higher symptoms of depression could be explained through the strain such mental health problems place upon a family. The Family Adjustment and Adaptation Response (FAAR) Model [93] may (partially) clarify this. Normally, families operate in an equilibrium, with demands upon the family system (e.g., stressors or daily hassles) being balanced out by the family's capabilities (e.g., coping behaviors, resources) to deal with such demands. However, adolescent symptoms of depression, especially if they persist over an extended period, may constitute an excessive demand upon a family system. During such a period of disequilibrium, parents may be hampered in their ability to apply parenting advice or even absorb new information.

Alternatively, a family dealing with (increased) symptoms of adolescent depression may simply require more aid than provided via light and universal parenting advice. Such families may require considerably different help and may benefit more from blended care for example, where digital advice is combined with professionally-guided help (e.g., [27].

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Directions

The current study aimed to contribute to better and more systematic procedures to developing digital parenting interventions to support adolescent mental well-being, and provides insight into how parents engage with, and experience such advice. By extensively investigating parents' user experience with a digital parenting intervention, we provide a systematic overview of factors which may be salient to the development and tailoring of such interventions. Nevertheless, a number of limitations warrant attention. First, the generalizability of the current study across countries and socio-economic groups may be limited. Participants were Dutch families, and parents were primarily highly-educated mothers. Given that evaluations of parenting interventions may differ depending on cultural considerations [88] and socio-economic factors [87], replication in diverse, cross-national studies is essential.

Second, our intervention was evaluated in a very early stage and thus, we assessed user experience as early pre-implementation indicators (i.e., feasibility, acceptability, adoption, and appropriateness), which should not be confounded with a comprehensive evaluation of a ready-to-implement intervention. To translate this rudimentary intervention into a real-world setting, requires further iterative development to improve the match between tips and parents' needs [22], and a much more elaborate evaluation (e.g., costs, fidelity, sustainability; [23, 24].

Conclusion

The current study aimed to identify theoretical, functional, and user-based lessons which may inform the development of digital parenting interventions, and explored how such interventions are evaluated. Our challenge-based digital parenting intervention was generally evaluated sufficiently well but not ideal, and heterogeneity was considerable: individual parents evaluated different tips very differently, and individual tips were in turn rated differently across parents. The intervention was evaluated more favorably by parents of younger adolescents and boys, and more poorly for adolescents who reported elevated symptoms of depression. These findings suggest that the fit between our intervention and parents differs substantially. To better align intervention content with the needs and preferences of individual families, more comprehensive personalization of intervention (delivery) may be a valuable target for future research.

Establishing Intervention Needs

Focus Groups with Adolescents and Parents. We conducted focus groups with adolescents and parents to identify parenting behaviors which they felt were most beneficial to adolescent well-being. Sessions consisted of several steps: after a brief introduction on the topic of the session, participants were asked to freely contribute things they felt affected adolescent mental well-being, which were then grouped into the domain in which they occurred (home, school, friends, self, or online & other). Adolescents mostly lived in an urban (73%) environment. Participants were recruited via two Dutch high schools offering a variety of educational tracks. Sessions were conducted by the first author at the participants' school and were audio-and video-recorded and transcribed.

Designing the Intervention

The first element of the intervention, the parenting tips, were developed iteratively.

Based on Self-Determination Theory, tips were aimed at strengthening parental autonomy support, support or structure and were subdivided into 18 dimensions (6 each for structure, autonomy support, and support), each relating to a theoretically important aspect of needsupportive parenting. Focus groups and expert brainstorms helped the research team (n = 5, Parenting experts) to develop an initial list of 75 tips within this theoretical foundation and were constructed as 5-minute challenges intended to motivate parents to apply new skills or reflect on their parenting.

The initial 75 parenting tips were further refined in three iterative stages between July and August 2024. First, tips were rated (1-10) by an international panel of theoretical experts (N = 5) on Self-Determination Theory and parenting research, and by parents (N = 4). Experts rated tips on theoretical quality (How well-supported is this tip by theory on SDT and parenting?), and practicality (How easily might parents implement this in their daily parenting?). Parents evaluated tips on perceived usefulness (Would a specific tip benefit parents?) and practicality (Can parents implement this in their daily practice?). Experts and parents could provide open-ended feedback, and experts were free to suggest alternative tips.

Second, we removed tips of insufficient quality (rated < 5.5 by parents or experts, aligning with the Dutch grading system generally qualifying a 5.5 out of 10 as the benchmark for a "pass"). Six tips scored < 5.5 on either quality or practicality. For the 69 remaining tips, the three highest rated tips (scoring ≥ 7 on average) were selected per parenting dimension, but if tips overlapped significantly in content, selection was based on diversity of content.

Finally, the research team refined the tips on tone (active, clear call to action, nonjudgmental), and language (b1-level appropriate). Hence, the final tips (1) aligned adequately with Self-Determination Theory, (2) were rated ≥ 7 by experts and by parents on quality and applicability in daily life, and (3) contained appropriate (b1-level) language and tone. The final version consisted of 53 tips: 18 tips each relating to structure and autonomy support, and 17 tips relating to support (only two tips were maintained for one dimension relating to support).
