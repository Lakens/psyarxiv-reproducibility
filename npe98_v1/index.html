Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Growing up involves learning about ourselves: Who am I, what can I do, and how can I improve? Although social feedback from knowledgeable others (e.g., parents, teachers) serves as a critical source of information for learning about the self, little work has examined children's curiosity about this information. Using both naturalistic information-seeking behaviors and a binary choice measure, the current work investigates whether and when preschool-aged children seek information about what others think about them. We find that children are more curious about how an adult evaluated their own drawing compared to a drawing made by an unknown peer, while ruling out possible alternative explanations (Experiments 1-3). Critically, this curiosity was modulated by children's uncertainty about their performance (Experiment 4), suggesting that children's preference for the self reflects genuine information-seeking. These findings provide evidence for an early-emerging curiosity about what others think about the self.

1 Introduction

Decades of research in cognitive development has painted a picture of young children as active, curious learners who construct a rich understanding of the world around them (Gopnik, 2012; Gweon, 2021; L. Schulz, 2012). Rather than being passive spectators, they engage in active informationseeking in a number of ways, ranging from visual attention (e.g., Cao, Raz, Saxe, & Frank, 2023; Colombo & Mitchell, 2009; Kidd, Piantadosi, & Aslin, 2012) and exploration (e.g., Cook, Goodman, & Schulz, 2011; Lapidow & Bonawitz, 2023; L. E. Schulz & Bonawitz, 2007; Sim & Xu, 2017; Stahl & Feigenson, 2015), to social bids for information such as help-seeking and questionasking (e.g., Chouinard, 2007; Goupil, Romand-Monnier, & Kouider, 2016; Ronfard, Zambrana, Hermansen, & Kelemen, 2018). Taken together, this large body of empirical work on early learning has primarily characterized curiosity as an intrinsic motivation to learn about the world: the physical and social environment that surrounds the learner (Gottlieb, Oudeyer, Lopes, & Baranes, 2013; Jirout, Evans, & Son, 2024; Kidd & Hayden, 2015; Loewenstein, 1994).

Yet, growing up means far more than learning about the external world; it also means learning about ourselves. From our strengths and weaknesses to our identity and personality, building a coherent and integrated understanding of the self is a key challenge throughout the lifespan. As adults, we can easily recall a time when we were curious about ourselves and actively sought out information: We may have attempted a challenge just to see if we could do it, directly asked others for feedback on our work, or even eavesdropped on a conversation if we heard our name. Such a drive to learn about the self may be particularly important in early childhood, as children tackle new tasks and interact with new people. While psychology as a field has long recognized that the self may also be a target of learning and reasoning (Bem, 1972; Epstein, 1973; Nisbett & Wilson, 1977), the question of whether young children actively seek information about themselves still remains open.

Curiosity and information-seeking about the self may manifest in many ways. Just as children learn about the external world through their own exploration or from others, they might learn about the self through trying something out themselves (e.g., observed successes and failures) or through feedback provided by others. But especially for young learners, who often lack the expertise to evaluate their performance on their own, social feedback from others can be a particularly powerful source of information about the self. Specifically for tasks without clear, objective markers for success (e.g., drawing), children often look toward adults for feedback (e.g., "How did I do?"). Yet, these anecdotal behaviors do not provide clear evidence for curiosity about the self.

Given that children, especially in Western cultures, often receive (and may even expect) positive responses from adults (Brummelman, Nelemans, Thomaes, & Orobio de Castro, 2017; Brummelman, Thomaes, Orobio de Castro, Overbeek, & Bushman, 2014), such solicitation may simply reflect children's desire for socially rewarding responses.

Based on prior work, we may entertain at least two possible hypotheses about children's curiosity about what others think about the self. First, given that praise can serve as positive social reward (Gaines, Duvall, Webster, & Smith, 2005; Webster, Duvall, Gaines, & Smith, 2003), children may only seek such feedback to "feel good" rather than as a source of information; if this is the case, then children's tendency to seek self-relevant feedback may reflect a blanket drive to attain rewarding input. Second, it is possible that children treat such feedback as not just positive reward, but also genuinely informative about the self. If so, this may not only lead children to seek such feedback, but critically, to do so strategically depending on the context. The current studies aim to adjudicate between these possibilities.

In order for children to be curious about what someone thinks of them and to use it as information to learn about the self, two key cognitive prerequisites must be present. First, children should be able to represent other agents as capable of evaluating them in the first place. Indeed, prior work has shown that young children are sensitive to what others think about the self and try to manage such beliefs. Preschool-aged children can be more likely to cheat at a game if they are praised or told they have a reputation for being "smart" or "good" (e.g., Zhao, Heyman, Chen, & Lee, 2017 , 2018), and they adjust their behaviors when they believe others may be watching them (e.g., to appear more prosocial, fair, or mature; Engelmann, Herrmann, & Tomasello, 2012; Leimgruber, Shaw, Santos, & Olson, 2012; Ma, Zeng, Xu, Compton, & Heyman, 2020). By age 4, children even strategically communicate about their competence to revise others' outdated beliefs about them (e.g., Asaba & Gweon, 2022), suggesting that they can represent, and care about, others' beliefs about the self.

Second, to use others' feedback as a signal for learning, children must be able to consider how and when others' evaluations can be informative about the self. A body of prior work on mindset development (e.g., Dweck, 2013) has shown that children interpret adults' social feedback as a nuanced signal about the self. For instance, praising school-aged children's intelligence, rather than effort, can decrease their motivation when they encounter difficulty (Henderlong & Lepper, 2002; Mueller & Dweck, 1998), suggesting that children are sensitive to how adults' praise bears on different aspects of the self. In addition, recent work has shown that preschool-aged children are able to infer the informativeness of an adult's praise based on the adult's past history of being a selective or indiscriminate praiser (Asaba, Stegall, Hembacher, Frank, & Gweon, 2025), suggesting that children also know when particular feedback may be more or less informative about the self (see also Brummelman et al., 2014).

Taken together, this body of work has shown that the ability to represent and reason about others' evaluative feedback is present even after just a few years of life. However, these studies often involve situations where children are given explicit feedback or can readily infer what others think about them, leaving open questions about whether children are genuinely curious to learn about themselves and actively seek out self-relevant information from others. To address this question, we start by gauging children's curiosity about the self against their curiosity about another child: All else being equal, would children be more motivated to find out how an adult evaluated their own performance than a peer's performance? This contrast allows us to experimentally assess the degree of children's self-curiosity with a well-matched alternative. Given that the cognitive prerequisites to such self-relevant curiosity may be present by around 3 to 5 years of age (e.g., Asaba & Gweon, 2022; Engelmann et al., 2012; Silver & Shaw, 2018), we focus our investigation on this age group. Children were randomly assigned to either the Self condition or the Other condition. The procedure made it clear to children that the experimenter was evaluating each drawing independently rather than directly comparing the two. Finally, the experimenter returned to the table and offered to show the participant if the folders contained star stickers, suggesting that they look at one of the two drawings first: either the child's own drawing (in the Self condition) or Jordan's drawing (in the Other condition). The experimenter then returned to the room and looked inside both folders with the participant.

In this experimental context, children in either condition were given little incentive to peek; the experimenter clearly intended to open both folders with the child, and left after saying they would be right back, signaling the prospect of looking inside both folders and an implicit agree-ment to wait. Thus, peeking-a form of information-seeking-would emerge only if the child's curiosity about the experimenter's evaluation was stronger than their desire to wait. This behavior offered two distinct but complementary dependent measures: (1) whether children peeked inside the folderfoot_0 and (2) if they did, how quickly they did so (peek latency). If children are more curious about the experimenter's evaluation about themselves than about Jordan, they should be more likely to peek and be faster to do so in the Self than in the Other condition.

Results

As predicted, children were more likely to peek inside the folder in the Self condition (n = 13/20, 65%) than in the Other condition (n = 5/20, 25%; Fisher's exact test: p = .025, OR = 5.31, 95% CI: [1.19, 27.62]; see Fig. 2). To analyze peek latency, we conducted a survival analysis in order to account for the potential of heavily right-censored data (i.e., children who never peek and thus would not have a peek latency). The survival analysis yielded a significant effect of condition (Mantel-Cox log rank test: p = .015). We found no evidence of an age effect in either measure (see Materials and Methods). These findings provide preliminary support for our hypothesis. Despite the clear prospect of looking inside the folders and the implicit signal to wait for the experimenter to do so,foot_1 children in the Self condition were more likely to peek inside the folder and were faster to do so than children in the Other condition.

However, it is possible that children in this study treated the star sticker as a form of material reward. If so, children in the Self condition may have been simply drawn to "their" sticker or become curious about whether they received one, not as a form of positive evaluation but as a tangible, marshmallow-like reward-even though the folders were explicitly described as the experimenter's and there was no indication that children could take the sticker with them (see Materials and Methods). Experiment 2 was designed to address this possibility.

Experiment 2 (n = 73, 3-and 4-year-olds, preregistered at https://aspredicted.org/f6q2-wm3z.pdf) aimed to provide a replication of Experiment 1 with a younger, narrower age range, while also addressing the concern that Experiment 1 results could be explained simply by the appeal of the gold star sticker. Given the absence of an age effect in Experiment 1, we constrained the age range to just 3-and 4-year-old children.

The procedure of Experiment 2 largely followed that of Experiment 1, with two main changes.

First, the gold star stickers were replaced with an arbitrary symbol: square pieces of paper with black printed circles on them. These black circle slips were unlikely to have any intrinsic value to children or prior association with a reward. During the Introduction Phase, children were shown the circle slips and told that the experimenter would place one inside the folder if they thought a drawing was "really good." Second, children completed a tracing task, rather than a free-form drawing task, which we implemented in order to standardize and reduce the time children spent making their drawings.

Results

Overall, we found that children were more likely to peek in the Self condition than in the Other condition (Self vs. Other: 56% (20/36) vs. 30% (11/37), Fisher's exact test: p = .034, OR = 2.91, 95% CI: [1.02, 8.70]). 3 Exploratory analyses on the latency to peek also showed that children were faster to peek in the Self condition than in the Other condition (Mantel-Cox log rank test: p = .024; see Fig. 2). Mirroring Experiment 1, we did not find an effect of age on either measure (see Materials and Methods).

Overall, we found a pattern of results in Experiment 2 that largely mirrored those in Experi-ment 1. However, the difference in peeking rates between conditions was attenuated (OR = 5.31 in Experiment 1 vs. OR = 2.91 in Experiment 2; see Materials and Methods for additional analyses) and the effect was relatively fragile (see Footnote 3). While this may simply reflect random variability across experiments, it is also possible that the key change in Experiment 2 (i.e., replacing the star sticker with a black circle slip) yielded a smaller effect size. For instance, the meaning of getting a black circle slip-compared to getting a gold star sticker, a symbol often used to indicate successful or positive performance-may have been too arbitrary and confusing, especially given the younger age range in this experiment. Another possibility, however, is that some children in Experiment 1 did treat the sticker as a simple tangible reward, and replacing it with the circle slip correspondingly reduced the effect across conditions. These possibilities are not mutually exclusive, and both would weaken children's desire to peek in the Self condition in Experiment 2 compared to Experiment 1.

Thus, in Experiment 3, we sought to conceptually replicate this effect in 4-and 5-year-olds by using the circle slips again, but with a different dependent measure: a binary choice to peek at their own vs. Jordan's folder. While spontaneous peeking provides a naturalistic way to reveal children's desire for self-relevant information, converging evidence using a direct comparison between one's own and Jordan's folder would provide stronger support for our hypothesis.

4 Experiment 3

Experiment 3 was a single-condition experiment (n = 36, 4-and 5-year-olds, preregistered at https://aspredicted.org/fz47y8.pdf)foot_3 . The procedure was largely similar to that of Experiment 2, including the use of circle slips. The critical difference occurred at the end of the Evaluation Phase: once the experimenter evaluated the tracings and returned to the child, children were told they could look in any folder they wanted, but had to choose only one. Both folders were then placed in front of the child, and they could indicate their choice by pointing to or grabbing one of the folders.

Results

As predicted, children in Experiment 3 showed a strong preference to look in their own folder (n = 27/36, 75%, Bayesian Single Proportion Test: BF10 = 20.56; Exact Binomial Test: p = .004), providing converging evidence that complements children's peeking behaviors in Experiments 1 and 2. Notably, children continued to exhibit this preference even with the arbitrary marker of positive evaluation (black circle slip), suggesting that the prospect of material reward inside the folders cannot alone explain our results.

Beyond finding converging evidence across different dependent measures, Experiment 3 also helps rule out another alternative explanation for Experiments 1 and 2: Given prior research on children's understanding of ownership and property rights (Nancekivell, Friedman, & Gelman, 2019; Rossano, Rakoczy, & Tomasello, 2011; Shaw, Li, & Olson, 2012), children may have avoided peeking at Jordan's folder simply because they believed they shouldn't peek at someone else's work and its evaluation. Given that children in Experiment 3 were given explicit permission to peek at either folder, concerns about ownership cannot alone explain children's preference to look at their own folder over Jordan's.

Collectively, Experiments 1 through 3 show that children preferentially seek self-relevant information about their performance. These findings cannot be explained by a simple desire for material reward or a resistance to peeking at other people's information. However, these studies still leave open at least two alternative explanations. First, the results are consistent with the possibility that children have a blanket preference for self-relevant information regardless of context.

Second, given that children in our sample routinely receive praise from adults, it is possible that children's tendency to peek at their own folder was driven by their desire for a positive social reward (e.g., feeling good from receiving praise) rather than information about themselves.

A hallmark of curiosity as a drive for information-seeking and learning is that it reflects the learner's uncertainty and expected information gain (Gottlieb et al., 2013; Loewenstein, 1994); indeed, prior work suggests that children exhibit more information-seeking behaviors when there is more to be learned about the world (e.g., Kidd et al., 2012; Lapidow & Bonawitz, 2023; L. E. Schulz & Bonawitz, 2007; Stahl & Feigenson, 2015). If children's responses in our studies reflect genuine curiosity to learn about the self, they should also be able to modulate their information-seeking behaviors accordingly. Experiment 4 aims to test this possibility.

5 Experiment 4

In Experiment 4 (n = 72, 4-and 5-year-olds, preregistered at https://aspredicted.org/j845kc.pdf), we sought to test whether children's choice to peek at their own folder is modulated by their uncertainty: If children's preference for peeking inside their own folder reflects genuine information-seeking, they should be more likely to do so when they have performed a difficult task (and are therefore uncertain about their performance) rather than an easy task that they had already completed and received positive feedback on in the past (and are therefore more certain about their performance).

To test this hypothesis, Experiment 4 used the same binary choice measure as in Experiment 3 with a few modifications. First, before being introduced to Jordan and working on the tracing to be placed inside their folder, children completed two identical tracings that were very simple (dotted line) and received a positive evaluation from the experimenter. Critically, the third tracing differed by condition: Children in the High Uncertainty condition were asked to trace a bird (i.e., a new, difficult task) whereas those in the Low Uncertainty condition were asked to trace the same dotted line they had already completed twice. The rest of the procedure was similar to that of Experiment 3: The experimenter introduced Jordan, told children that their third tracing and Jordan's tracing would be evaluated, and then gave them a forced choice to look inside their own or Jordan's folder.

Second, given the strong preference for the self in Experiment 3, some changes were made to minimize ceiling effects by increasing the appeal of choosing Jordan's folder: Children never saw the tracing that Jordan made before their final choice, and the experimenter nudged children to choose Jordan's folder by saying, "Jordan said it is okay for other kids to look." By making both options-peeking at their own vs. Jordan's folder-reasonably appealing, we could test whether children's choice would differ depending on their third tracing. Finally, to indicate positive evaluation, we used a paper slip with a star (rather than a black circle) to minimize children's confusion about the arbitrary symbol while retaining the benefit of using a marker of evaluation that is not as intrinsically rewarding as stickers. Additionally, unlike the previous studies, we also excluded participants who did not remember the meaning of the paper slips (preregistered exclusion criterion, see Materials and Methods).

This design allowed us to test our hypothesis against several alternative explanations. For instance, if children simply had a blanket preference for self-relevant information, then children would choose their own folder regardless of condition. Alternatively, if children simply wanted to maximize expected reward and the possibility of "feeling good," then they would be more likely to choose their own folder in the Low Uncertainty condition than in the High Uncertainty condition.

However, if prior results reflect genuine curiosity about the self, children would be more likely to choose their own folder in the High Uncertainty condition than in the Low Uncertainty condition.

Results

Overall, we found a credible effect of condition on folder choice-children were more likely to choose their own folder in the High Uncertainty (bird) condition (n = 21/36, 58%) than in the Low Uncertainty (line) condition (n = 11/36, 31%; Bayesian Logistic Regression: BF10 = 1.76, β = 1.19, 95% CrI: [0.21, 2.18]; Fisher's exact test: p = .032; Fig. 3), 5 suggesting that children's information-seeking was modulated by whether they completed an easy, familiar task with a predictable evaluation (i.e., low uncertainty) or a novel, difficult task with an unknown evaluation (i.e., high uncertainty). Unlike Experiments 1-3, which did not show any effects of age, further exploratory analyses revealed that this effect was primarily driven by older children. While 4-yearolds (n = 36) showed no difference in folder choice across conditions (BF10 = 0.16, β = 0.15, 95% CrI: [-1.46, 1.58]; Fisher's exact test: p > .99), 5-year-olds (n = 36) showed a robust effect of condition on folder choice (BF10 = 19.16, β = 2.38, 95% CrI: [0.84, 4.12]; Fisher's exact test: p = .005).

Together, the results of Experiment 4 suggest that children's curiosity about the self-at least by age 5-is more than a blanket preference for the self, or a desire to attain praise. The fact that the condition difference increased with age provides evidence against simple low-level preferences for the more novel and complex bird tracing. Rather, this pattern is consistent with the possibility that older (but not younger) children strategically decided whether to seek information about the self depending on their uncertainty.

6 General Discussion

Scholars have long argued that a central goal of development is to learn who we are (e.g., Bowlby, 2010; Erikson, 1963; Piaget, 1954). From this perspective, the idea that children learn about themselves may seem like a truism, especially given how often children ask "How did I do?" or "What do you think?"-questions that appear to reflect a drive to learn about the self. Yet, this drive has received surprisingly little scientific attention. Schulz, 2012) Using spontaneous peeking as a naturalistic measure of information-seeking, we found that children have a stronger drive to seek information about their own performance than that of a peer (Experiment 1), and that these results are not explained by a simple desire for material reward (Experiment 2). Using binary choice, we again found a preference for the self, ruling out concerns about another child's ownership or privacy (Experiment 3). Critically, 5-year-old children flexibly chose to seek information about the self versus another child depending on the uncertainty about their performance, suggesting that children's responses reflect genuine curiosity rather than a blanket preference for self-relevant information or a desire to "feel good" by maximizing praise (Experiment 4). Taken together, these studies provide evidence for an early-emerging curiosity about the self.

While we did not find evidence for an age-related change in Experiments 1-3, exploratory analyses in Experiment 4 revealed a potential developmental change: Children's tendency to choose their own vs. others' folders depending on their uncertainty was clear in 5-year-olds but not in 4-year-olds. What might explain the difference across age? It is possible that younger children were more susceptible to the experimenter's nudge to choose the peer's folder, which suppressed their preference for the self in the High Uncertainty condition. Alternatively, it is also possible that younger children start out with an undifferentiated representation of self-relevant information as a learning signal, and later come to understand that more novel, difficult tasks carry more diagnostic (and thus valuable) information about one's abilities. These possibilities are not mutually exclusive, and may reflect the development of metacognitive and social reasoning. More generally, how children's curiosity about the self leads to actual learning gains-the development of selfknowledge and the construction of an abstract understanding of oneself-remains an important goal for future work.

Our experimental tasks were optimized for a particular sociocultural context (Western, relatively high-SES), leveraging the fact that children in our sample were generally familiar with drawing/tracing and with receiving spontaneous feedback from adults on their work. However, given that there may be substantial cultural differences in the degree to which children anticipate and receive such feedback in their daily lives (e.g., Ng, Pomerantz, & Lam, 2007; Rogoff, Paradise, Arauz, Correa-Chávez, & Angelillo, 2003), children's curiosity about the self may also manifest differently depending on culture. While the experimental task we used to index self-curiosity may be culturally specific, what our findings show-an early-emerging drive to learn about the selfmay be a fundamental property of the human mind that transcends cultural boundaries (Dweck, 2017). From this perspective, our work highlights the need for studies investigating such learning across cultures, as well as for a unified theory of learning that incorporates a drive to learn not only about the world but also about the self.

Relatedly, our tasks constrained children's curiosity about the self to a particular source (i.e., social feedback), target (i.e., abilities and performance), and measure (i.e., peeking). However, the drive to learn about one's abilities goes far beyond the simple scenarios presented here. First, even though the current work used peeking as a way to index information-seeking, children may also learn about themselves in a number of ways, including passive observation (e.g., observing their own successes and failures, or recognizing how others behave toward them) and active informationseeking (e.g., trying out a new challenging task, or directly asking for feedback). Second, while we focus on a domain where others' feedback is critically helpful, self-generated data may play a more central role in other domains. For instance, prior work in early motor development has studied infants' physical exploration as a function of locomotor skill (e.g., Adolph, Bertenthal, Boker, Goldfield, & Gibson, 1997; Adolph, Eppler, & Gibson, 1993), which some have characterized as a form of attaining information about the self from one's own actions (i.e., one's motor abilities; Gibson, 1988; Rochat, 1998). Finally, even though social feedback is a useful source of information to learn about the self, simply knowing how others view us can serve important goals of its own. For instance, even after substantial learning about our own traits, abilities, moral character, and physical appearance, we may still care about how others judge us in these domains. Such "social curiosity" about the self-wanting to know what others think of us-may have been present in the current work, and may play an even larger role as children begin to see themselves as a part of a larger social network.

Lastly, our findings also present key challenges for computational theories of curiosity and learning. Past and current computational efforts to model intrinsic curiosity and motivation (e.g., curiosity-based reinforcement learning) have primarily focused on methods to push agents to explore and learn about their external environment (e.g., Haber, Mrowca, Fei-Fei, & Yamins, 2018; Kim, Sano, De Freitas, Haber, & Yamins, 2020; Oudeyer, Kaplan, & Hafner, 2007). Attempts to model self-directed learning have primarily focused on unsupervised agents whose goal is to discover new skills, or learn to use an unknown or changing set of actions in a fixed environment (Baranes & Oudeyer, 2013; Eysenbach, Gupta, Ibarz, & Levine, 2018; Zahavy, Haroush, Merlis, Mankowitz, & Mannor, 2018). However, our studies point toward a more complex kind of learning about the self: Rather than simply knowing whether we can produce a drawing at all, we want to know whether we are good at doing so, what others think about our abilities, and how we compare to others around us. These cases of representationally rich, socially laden curiosity about oneself will be a key challenge in the attempt to provide a unified description of curiosity that spans both the world and the self.

7.1.1 Participants

Forty 3-to 5-year-olds (M age = 4.75, SD age = 0.57, Range: [3.52, 5.97]; 20 girls) were randomly assigned to the Self (n = 20) or to the Other condition (n = 20). An additional seven children were excluded for experimenter error (n = 5) and for not completing the study (n = 2). All children were run at a local nursery school.

7.1.2 Stimuli

Children completed their drawings on a blank sheet of paper by using a set of colored pencils presented in a plastic container. Two standard-sized folders (green and yellow) were used to put the drawings into; a roll of star stickers was also used, and was either placed on the drawing or on the folder. Two photos of "Jordan" were used (one boy, one girl); the photo shown was gender-matched to the participant. Jordan made a tree or a bird (whichever the child did not).

7.1.3 Procedure

A description of the overall study procedure can be found in the main text. Children were randomly assigned to either the Self condition or the Other condition. In the Introduction Phase, children were shown a stack of blank white paper and colored pencils. The experimenter told children that they were going to play a drawing game. After children completed their drawings, the experimenter took their drawing and casually moved it to one side of the table (on the left side of the table in the Self condition, and the right side of the table in the Other condition). Children were then shown a photo of "Jordan," who was described as "a kid, just like you!" The experimenter said that Jordan also made a drawing and showed it to the child. Jordan's drawing was selected from several premade drawings made to look like it was drawn by a child. To avoid direct comparison, Jordan's drawing always depicted something the child did not draw (e.g., a tree if the child drew a bird).

Then, the experimenter told participants that the drawings would be evaluated and placed into two different colored folders (yellow or green), adding that participants' drawings would be placed in one folder and Jordan's drawing in the other. The experimenter told children that the folders belonged to the experimenter ("This is my [green/yellow] folder!"), such that children did not believe that they could take the folder home, and placed each folder on top of the drawing.

The experimenter then said: "Okay, first, I am going to take a real close look at [your/Jordan's] drawing, and if I think [your/Jordan's] drawing is really good, I'm going to put a star sticker on the inside of my folder. But if I think this drawing is just okay, I won't put a star sticker." The experimenter then repeated the same line for the other folder. The yellow folder was always placed on the left side of the table and referred to first, and the green folder was placed on the right side of the table and referred to second (i.e., the participant's drawing was in the yellow folder in the Self condition, and Jordan's drawing was in the yellow folder in the Other condition).

In the Evaluation Phase, the experimenter then went to the back of the room out of sight, and pretended to evaluate the drawings ("Hmm. . . what did I think of Jordan's drawing? Okay! Hmm. . . what did I think of [participant]'s drawing? Okay!" ; evaluation order reversed in the Other condition). This procedure made it clear that the experimenter was evaluating each drawing independently, rather than giving one sticker to the drawing that was better. Unbeknownst to the participant, the experimenter placed a star sticker in both folders.

In the Wait Phase, the experimenter returned to the table with the folders and reminded the participant which drawing was inside each folder. The experimenter then offered to show the participant whether the drawings had a star sticker. The drawing the experimenter chose to show first differed by condition: either the child's own drawing (Self condition) or Jordan's drawing (Other condition); the folder shown first was always yellow. The experimenter placed the folder down on the table in front of the child as if to open it, but then received a phone call and had to step outside. The experimenter said, "I'll be right outside if you need me, I'll be back!" and took the other folder with them as they left the room. Thus, the participant was left alone with their own folder in the Self condition, and with Jordan's folder in the Other condition. After the end of the Wait Phase, the experimenter returned to the room and showed children that they received a star sticker.

A few minor modifications to the procedure were introduced midway through data collection (after the first 16 included participants). First, many children asked to draw something other than a tree or a bird; therefore the task was changed so they could draw anything they wanted. Second, the camera was placed behind a one-way mirror, rather than in the testing room, so that children would not feel "watched" while alone. Finally, to avoid implying that the star sticker was theirs to take, it was placed onto the folder (which was explicitly described as the experimenter's folder) rather than on the drawing.

7.1.4 Analyses

To analyze the effect of condition on children's peek rates (binary), we conducted a Fisher's exact test. In order to analyze peek latency, we conducted a Mantel-Cox log rank test using the survival and survminer packages in R, right-censoring children's peek times at 180 seconds (the maximum we allowed children to sit in the room before the experimenter returned).

7.1.5 Additional Results

As mentioned above, a few minor procedural changes were introduced partway through data collection-in the main text, we present results collapsed across these changes. These results did not differ descriptively (5/8 peeking in the Self condition vs. 2/8 peeking in the Other condition in the first version that involved drawing a tree or bird, and 8/12 peeking in the Self condition vs.

3/12 peeking in the Other condition in the second version in which children could draw whatever they wanted).

In order to assess whether age, or an age by condition interaction, predicted peeking, we ran a generalized linear model predicting peek rates from condition, age (mean-centered), and their interaction. Effects were estimated using the emtrends and emmeans commands from the emmeans package in R (Lenth, 2023), averaging across other factors at the mean age. 7.4.2 Stimuli

Stimuli used were identical to those of Experiment 2.

7.4.3 Procedure

A description of the overall of the study procedure can be found in the main text. In addition, we counterbalanced whether children were assigned to trace a tree or a bird, as well as whether their drawing was evaluated first (and placed into a yellow folder) or second (and placed into a green folder).

Experiment 3 utilized a Bayesian sequential sampling procedure (e.g., Mani et al., 2021; Schönbrodt et al., 2017); this procedure involved collecting an initial sample of participants, then iteratively evaluating the effect of interest until a predetermined evidentiary cutoff was met or until a maximum n. This procedure was preregistered. Our sequential sampling procedure involved testing an initial sample of n = 20 children, then evaluating the Bayes factor (BF) using the proportionBF command from the BayesFactor package (Morey & Rouder, 2026) in R. We utilized the package default priors: a logistic prior on the log-odds of the proportion centered at the null with a default rscale value of r = 1/2. The BF was evaluated every four participants, and predetermined BF cutoffs were set at BF10 = 10 (strong evidence in favor of the predicted hypothesis), BF01 = 5 (moderate evidence in favor of the null), 6 or a maximum n of 36. This sampling procedure yielded a final n of 36, the maximum sample size set.

Prior sensitivity analyses (using the package rscale values of "wide" [r = √ 2/2] and "ultrawide" [r = 1]) showed that the Bayes factor appeared robust to choice of prior (wide: BF10 = 21.68, ultrawide: BF10 = 19.73).

7.4.4 Additional Results

We also ran an exact binomial test to complement the Bayesian analysis with a frequentist test, as in Experiments 1 and 2 (preregistered). We found that children's folder choice differed significantly from chance (p = .004).

As an exploratory analysis to assess whether there was a credible effect of age on children's folder choice, we ran a Bayesian generalized linear model predicting folder choice from age. We did not find an effect of age (β = 0.02, 95% CrI: [-1.23, 1.27]).

7.5 Experiment 4 7.5.1 Participants Seventy-two 4-and 5-year-olds (M age = 4.94, SD age = 0.54, Range: [4.04, 5.95]; 34 girls, 36 boys, 2 gender unspecified) were randomly assigned to the Low Uncertainty (n = 36) or the High Uncertainty condition (n = 36 ).

An additional 16 participants were excluded for preregistered exclusion criteria: not completing the study (n = 7), not passing a single comprehension check (n = 7), or tech error (n = 2). Children were run at a combination of a local nursery school and a local children's museum.

7.5.2 Stimuli

Line tracings were presented on 8.5 × 11 in. paper; the bird tracing used in Experiment 4 was identical to the one used in Experiments 2 and 3. We replaced the circle slips with "star slips": printed 2 × 2 in. slips with a star icon on them.

7.5.3 Procedure

A description of the overall of the study procedure can be found in the main text. In each of the first two tracings (line tracing), the procedure followed that of Experiment 3 except that the only tracing being evaluated was the participant's own. Before moving on to the next tracing, the experimenter spontaneously offered to open the folder with the child ("I'm going to put your tracing away, but first, do you want to take a look inside to see if I gave you a star slip?").

Unlike previous experiments, however, comprehension questions were administered four times throughout the experiment: three instances when the participant opened up a folder ("I put a star slip! Do you remember what that means?"), and once before the final evaluation phase ("If I put a star slip, do you remember what that means?"). Answers were coded as "correct" if participants indicated anything similar to the star denoting positive quality (e.g., "That means the drawing was really good" or "That means I did a good job."), and participants were excluded if they failed to answer a comprehension question correctly at least one of the four checks. In addition, we did not counterbalance the order of evaluation or the left/right position of the folder across conditions (the participant's tracing was always evaluated first and placed on the left), but did counterbalance folder color (yellow vs. green).

Like Experiment 3, Experiment 4 utilized a preregistered Bayesian sequential sampling procedure. BFs were computed by running Bayesian generalized linear models in the rstanarm package in R (Goodrich, Gabry, Ali, & Brilleman, 2025). We utilized the package default of weakly informative priors (normal distributions on coefficients with SD = 2.5, scaled to predictor magnitudes).

To generate the BFs, we utilized the bridgesampling package in R (Gronau, Singmann, & Wagenmakers, 2020), comparing a model predicting folder choice from condition versus one without (i.e., intercept only). We utilized the same sequential procedure and BF cutoffs as Experiment 3: testing an initial sample of n = 20 per condition, then iteratively evaluating the BF every four participants (two per condition) until strong evidence in favor of an effect of condition (BF10 = 10), moderate evidence in favor of the null (BF01 = 5), or a maximum n of 36 per condition.

This sequential sampling procedure yielded a final n of 36 per condition, the maximum sample size set.

7.5.4 Additional Results

In the main text, we report an age analysis binning participants into 4-and 5-year-olds. A Bayesian generalized linear model predicting folder choice from condition, age (mean-centered), and their interaction yielded a credible effect of condition (β = 1.26, 95% CrI: [0.23, 2.32]), and of age (β = 1.53, 95% CrI: [0.38, 2.63]), but not their interaction (β = 1.79, 95% CrI: [-0.35, 3.98]).

Descriptively, the effect of age was credible only in the High Uncertainty condition (β = 2.41, 95% CrI: [0.61, 4.25]) but not the Low Uncertainty condition (β = 0.64, 95% CrI: [-0.62, 1.93]).

At the observed effect in our data, our sample was likely underpowered to detect the interaction effect. Thus, future work should robustly replicate this age effect with a larger sample size.

In addition, we ran Fisher's exact tests to complement the Bayesian analysis with a frequentist test (preregistered), as in Experiments 1 and 2. Overall, we found a significant effect of condition (OR = 3.13, 95% CI: [1.09, 9.44], p = .032).

Our preregistration specified the default prior in the rstanarm package in R, which was a Gaussian centered at 0 with a standard deviation of 2.5 scaled to predictor magnitudes; this yielded a Gaussian centered at 0 with standard deviation of 5. After data collection, we realized that this prior may have been overly conservative. Thus, we evaluated the robustness of the BF across a range of prior SDs (0.25, 0.5, 1, 2.5, 5, 7.5, and 10) with a sensitivity analysis. See Fig. S1. Note that at extremely wide, uninformative priors (e.g., standard deviation of 10), the BF dips below 1, as H 1 's prior weight over large and implausible effect sizes poorly explains the data. Finally, a 2 × 2 contingency table BF command (the contingencyTableBF command) from the BayesFactor package yielded a BF10 of 4.55.

Growing up means learning about ourselves: Who am I, what can I do, and what do you think of me? Just as much as the field of cognitive development has shown children as motivated learners of the external world around them, here, we show that children are also motivated to learn about themselves, and in particular, what others think about them. By demonstrating the early presence of curiosity about the self, the current work opens up a new area of inquiry to better understand its modulating factors, developmental trajectory, and downstream consequences on learning. Beyond serving as the engine for the development of self-concept, such self-oriented curiosity can guide how we navigate our social world and build relationships with others.
