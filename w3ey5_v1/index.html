Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

This scoping review surveys quantitative online dating research from a media-market perspective. We conceptualize online dating as online relationship search, which refers to twosided matching activities that capitalize features of online dating platforms as both media and markets. Accordingly, we reviewed findings about the links between search strategies and outcomes, particularly covering a stream of strategies aimed to unleash the potential of online dating as media-markets: endorsing online dating as a venue for relationship search, expanding access to market options, optimizing matching procedures, and enhancing mutual understanding via interaction. A total of 134 studies were included via manual and computerized screening.

First, pre-2010s research tended to find meeting a partner via an online dating site associated with higher relationship quality whereas post-2010s research often showed dating apps associated with less favorable search outcomes. Second, both excessive and inadequate access to options were found to be counterproductive. Third, meta-cognition about matching procedures were found to enhance matching outcomes. Fourth, daters were found to leverage different communication modalities/affordances for achieving different goals (garnering attention vs. facilitating long-term development). Findings suggest that successful searches can be facilitated by strategies that capitalize media market potential at different stages of search. We discuss how the media-market perspective may contribute to future work.

Online Relationship Search: A Computerized Review from A Media-Market Perspective

Online dating has intrigued communication scholars: it goes beyond being merely a topic of research but provokes impactful thoughts and theories about how mediation communication shapes relationship development. Specifically, scholars have theorized relationship development in online dating by studying the effect of endorsing online dating as a meeting venue (e.g., Hu et al., 2024; Sharabi & Dorrance-Hall, 2024), increased choice size (e.g., D'Angelo & Toma, 2017; M. F. Thomas et al., 2026), matching processes (e.g., Sharabi, 2021), and initial interaction (e.g., Gibbs et al., 2011; Hancock & Toma, 2009; Ramirez et al., 2015; Sharabi, 2024; Walther, 1996), among other perspectives. These perspectives, corresponding to the questions about how entry, access, matching, and communication (EAMC) in online dating have relational impacts as proposed in Finkel et al.'s framework (2012), offer rich insights into the dater-media, dater-dater, or dyad-media interaction.

The goal of the current scoping review is to ( 1) understand what the field has discovered in the chain process of EAMC and (2) how findings may be integrated to guide better media design that helps daters enhance their search. Thus, we organize the review from a media-market perspective (Hu & Markowitz, 2025; Roth, 2015), which we argue helps to integrate findings in different steps of relationship initiation in online dating from a market design perspective, with caveats informed by media psychology. The process and effect of relational interaction are profoundly shaped by the social structure that the interaction is situated in (Bourdieu & Wacquant, 1992; Granovetter, 1973; Monge, 1973). Drawing upon market economics (Oyer, 2014; Roth, 2015), we argue that online dating can be conceptualized (among many other alternatives) as a two-sided matching market where stakeholders choose each other. Thus, online dating can be understood as an online relationship search problem where the central mechanism to generate successful relationships is to capitalize the characteristics of the media-market of online dating, that is, a form of social structure where mutual choice is mediated and marketized.

Though prior work has shown a certain degree of sensitivity to this market conceptualization, the insights of market economics and market design (e.g., How can market be thicker without creating match congestion? How signals work to help market function efficiently; Roth, 2015) are rarely seriously integrated into interpreting empirical findings. We will fill this gap: whereas the EAMC framework helps to categorize different findings, the media-market perspective offers a lens to coherently interpret and integrate EAMC-relevant findings to design better platforms that help daters strategize and strengthen their search.

In this scoping review, we as social scientists will survey existing studies that directly, quantitatively examined the links between relationship search strategies that are relevant to endorsing online dating as a meeting venue, expanding market access, optimizing matching, and enhancing interaction and relationship outcomes of such search. In reviewing them, we aim to describe the area, understand what was found for each media-market-relevant strategies, and inform theoretical advancements. We used both manual and computerized screening to achieve objective judgments. Specific analysis of included papers remained fully manual. Below, we first introduce the media-market perspective, briefly define components of online relationship search, and state the task of this work.

A Media-Market Perspective

We define online dating (platforms) as media-markets. By media-market, we mean a form of social structure where two-sided matching market activities are mediated by communication technology (Hu & Markowitz, 2026; Oyer, 2014; Roth, 2015); relational communication operates within such structure-that is, communication is shaped by the affordances and constraints of such structure (Bourdieu & Wacquant, 1992; Monge, 1973). 1 There is duality in online dating: it serves both as media and markets. First, online dating offers markets for two-sided matching where people with heterogeneous traits and preferences make mutual choices in hopes to find a compatible match (Hu & Markowitz, 2026; Oyer, 2014; Roth, 2015). 2 By endorsing online dating as a venue to meet a mate, daters could gain expanded access to mate options 3 , automated-though not always optimized-matching procedures, and interact-or bluntly, promote oneself-in strategic ways (Finkel et al., 2012). Indeed, Finkel et al. (2012) in their review, mapped the relational impact of online dating in access, matching, and communication-which we will call EAMC. This chain-classification of strategies also resonates with the Searching-Matching-Interacting Framework (Adelman & Ahuvia, 1991; Coduto & Fox, 2024). Therefore, we will organize our review following such typology, in addition to the outcome of endorsing online dating as a meeting venue-which represents entry to mediamarkets.

For us to treat online dating as markets is inadequate, for it ignores the media nature of online dating. Online daters are not merely market participants, but media users who strategize 1 By social structure, we mean enduring forces that are largely agnostic to individual willpower, which shape individuals' cognition, emotion, and behavior; for example, for merchants' economic decisions, a nation's economic policy constitutes one form of such structure (Bourdieu & Wacquant, 1992). 2 The market metaphor dates back to at least Heino et al.'s (2010) study, which conceptualized online dating as onesided market where people shop for relationships. Inspired by their work, we further refine online dating as twosided matching markets. 3 The literature distinguishes between option and choice: people can have multiple persons to choose a mate from; the candidates are options, and the preferred mate is their choice (Hu & Markowitz, 2026).

their relationship-search by leveraging the features and circumventing the constraints of platforms (Coduto & Fox, 2024; Hu & Thomas, 2026; Sin & Chan, 2026). While markets are abstract, rule-based structure that governs relationship search, media are tangible infrastructure that is closer to subjective experience, which is the direct object of daters' attempts to strategize their search (i.e., to gain better media use experiences). Thus, we will not only organize this review under EMAC, but also pay close attention to strategies that people leverage to transform their media use experiences.

Together, it stands to reason that the media-market conceptualization informs a more structural understanding of online relationship search. The conceptualization helps to reveal overarching structural forces while preserving possibilities of change via media use strategies; it anchors around such phenomenologically sensible strategies while situate such strategies within structural affordances and constrains. Indeed, it would be what Bourdieu and Wacquant (1992) called, a structural constructivist view. This view forms a beneficial complement to existing perspectives (e.g., deception, choice, social information processing, modality switching, as mentioned in this paper). Next, we begin to define online relationship search strategies under EAMC to understand specific areas that this review covers.

Endorsing Online Dating as A Meeting Venue

The first component in online relationship search is naturally entering media-markets: endorsing online dating as a way of meeting others. Does endorsing online dating as a meeting venue improve or worsen relationship search outcomes? This question pertains to the general function of online dating as media-markets. Two competing predictions can be found for the link between online dating endorsement (e.g., meeting a partner in online dating vs. offline, or using online dating more vs. less) and relationship search outcomes. One prediction carries a positive tone and suggests that online dating is associated with more satisfying relationships (e.g., Cacioppo et al., 2013). A predominating rationale is that online dating can reduce demographic and geographic constrains in partner selection; for readers familiar with market economics, elimination of matching restrictions is theorized to improve market outcomes (Roth, 2015). Sharabi (2024) called this the selection effect.

By contrast, a competing prediction suggests that online dating endorsement will be associated with poorer relationships (e.g., Hu et al., 2024; Sharabi & Dorrance-Hall, 2024). One rationale is that two people who are fixed by algorithms may have fewer shared network members who support their relationship development; it was called the (network) marginalization effect (Sharabi & Dorrance-Hall, 2024). Furthermore, having excessive dating options can make it harder to choose a satisfying relationship, often described as the paradox of choice (Chernev et al., 2015; D'Angelo & Toma, 2017; M. F. Thomas et al., 2026). These explanations are not exhaustive, but they lead to a critical point: whether online dating endorsement is beneficial or detrimental to relationship search appears highly contested in the literature. Each prediction comes with good reasoning. Thus, it will be valuable to review what the empirical evidence from different research traditions or approaches has said about the effect of online dating endorsement. We ask: RQ1: How is online dating endorsement related to relationship search outcomes?

Access

In addition to probing the effect of entering the media-market of online dating, it helps to examine effects of specific, downstream strategies. The next group of strategies are about expanding access to dating options, commonly studied in research of partner choice (e.g., "Does having more mate options benefit search outcomes?"). Though choice is used to name a research area, it helps to distinguish choice as an outcome (i.e., the person that one chooses to date from a pool of persons) from options (i.e., a pool of persons that are available for selection). Market economists favor thick markets (markets with abundant options) over thin markets (markets that are under-optioned) because thick markets host more diverse option and searchers with different preferences and traits are more likely to find a compatible option (Roth, 2015).

By contrast, consumer and media psychologists are skeptical of the market economics prediction. They suggest that having too many options can cause choice overload, which can lead to poorer relationship choices; this is because human brains often find it hard to properly process excessive mate options afforded in online dating (Chernev et al., 2015; D'Angelo & Toma, 2017; M. F. Thomas et al., 2026). Thus, it helps to know what evidence in different strands of research has said about each view. We propose: RQ2: How are strategies related to gaining access to dating options related to relationship search outcomes?

Matching

Increased mate options can crowd the media-markets of online dating, create market congestion, and necessitate efficient matching (Roth, 2015). Matching is defined as the pairing of two people with certain traits and preferences. Online dating-like other sorts of romantic pairing problems (Gale & Shapley, 1962)-is a two-sided matching market where each person is a chooser and a choosee at the same time; success is defined as a stable match where one cannot improve satisfaction by trading their match for another match, that is, optimal match satisfaction (Hu & Markowitz, 2026; Oyer, 2014). This definition of success is mathematical and ideal-in reality, a hard market boundary is difficult to define. A more practical definition of success can be finding a compatible relationship where two people exist in harmony (vs. one where one person or both persons want to terminate; Sprecher, 2011).

Effects of matching in online dating is likely more nuanced than other contexts like student-school matching or patient-donor matching (Roth, personal communication). First, matching preferences are inferred and explored (vs. e.g., organ donation, where seekers and doners have explicit preferences or offers; Roth, 2015). Second, the process is highly automated by algorithms, meaning two people have little process information (how they are matched) and shared context (e.g., a shared friend who introduces them; Hu & Wang, 2024). Also, the rules of matching via algorithms are often vague, making effects of matching via algorithms a myth (Hu, 2024a; Hu & Wang, 2023). Additionally, the mate evaluation theory (MET; Eastwick et al., 2023) questions whether matching (vs. not matching) in traits and preferences dictates relationship quality. Therefore, the relationship between uses of matching strategies and relationship search outcomes is likely complicated. There are merits in knowing how empirical work has found out about this association. We propose: RQ3: How are matching strategies related to relationship search outcomes?

Communication

The last component in online relationship search is to foster mutual understanding via strategic communication in media-markets. Communication here means verbal or nonverbal interaction between two online daters, ranging from viewing content in someone's profile to talking in an offline date (Sharabi, 2024). Communication is central to uncertainty reduction and building a compatible relationship (Berger, 1979; Eastwick & Joel, 2025). Though computermediated communication allows people to selectively present themselves, by writing a witty biography in one's profile, choosing a filtered photo, or sending humorous text messages, using any strategy alone may not guarantee a successful relationship search; for example, attraction often drops when daters switch from online to face-to-face (FtF) communication (e.g., Hancock & Toma, 2009; Ramirez et al., 2015; Sumner et al., 2025). Given the volume and centrality of research examining communication strategies in online dating, it is beneficial to understand roles of these strategies in online relationship search. We propose: RQ4: How are communication strategies related to relationship search outcomes?

Overview of This Review

Taken together, the media-market perspective of online dating leads to four questions that help to draw insights from existing perspectives and findings about online relationship search.

Each question pertains to one step in the chain process. We present this process and key concerns conceptually in Figure 1. The media-market perspective, again, is aimed to offer an underexplored, structural constructivist view that can derive practical strategies within mediamarket constraints and guide future work.

Anchoring around the specific link between media-market search strategies and search outcomes, this scoping review surveys studies that generate direct findings regarding such links (thus not being aimed to include every impactful study in online dating). These findings will be reviewed to generate directions for future work.

Non-strategic predictors that represent stable traits fall beyond our focus. Given traits like how people look, their socio-economic status, race, biological sex, attachment styles cannot be easily altered in a short time, or at all; findings about these traits are not about strategies per se.

By contrast, strategies are approaches to online dating that people can adjust in a short time (e.g., spending more time in online dating, migrating to a platform with more mate options or a different matching procedure, communicating in a certain way). Therefore, though trait-like findings are valuable, the media-market perspective led us to only focus on studies that have examined predictors translatable to strategies (though strategies may not be the only concern in these studies).

To understand the reviewed studies, we will also describe their topic distribution and methodology. Thus, we propose the fifth research question, which will be answered first given the way we present our results: RQ5: How are the associations between media-market strategies and online relationship search outcomes studied?

Method

This scoping review follows the preferred reporting items for systematic review and meta-analyses (PRISMA 2020; Page et al., 2021) and is not pre-registered. The procedures of searching and screening are visualized in Figure 2.

Search Strategy

Following existing reviews of online dating (e.g., Konings et al., 2022; Sharabi et al., 2026), we gathered 2187 English research articles in major databases for communication (Communication & Mass Media Complete, n=195), psychology (PsycInfo, PsycArticle, n=581), sociology (Sociological Index, n=478), which were supplemented by an interdisciplinary database (Web of Science, n=933). Searched articles need to include at least one keyword from both keyword sets in their title or abstract. That is, an article needed to have one keyword from the set of online dating keywords (e.g., dating app*, online dating) and one keyword from the set of relationship keywords (e.g., relation*, intima*) to be included. The asterisk allows variations of keywords sharing the same stem (e.g., dating app, dating application). See Table 1 for full keywords. We also manually checked for missing papers by screening ten most cited papers in each database's search results to see whether eligible papers in their reference and citation list were included. This procedure added another six papers. Removing duplicate articles, we found 1413 papers remained for title and abstract screening.

RQ1: Online Dating Endorsement and Relationship Search Outcomes

Examining the links between strategies and outcomes, RQ1 was concerned with the relationship between online dating endorsement and relationship outcomes. First, meeting a partner in online dating (vs. offline dating) was associated with less satisfying, stable, or successful relationships (Bleize et al., 2023; Blunt-Vinti et al., 2016; Hu et al., 2024; Kowal et al., 2025; Langlais & Lee, 2025; Sharabi & Dorrance-Hall, 2024). A candidate presented on a dating app was also seen less trustworthy than a candidate presented on a non-dating social app (e.g., social networking sites; Silva et al., 2019). Meeting a partner in online dating was also associated with more perceived societal marginalization and lower social network support (Sharabi & Dorrance-Hall, 2024). By contrast, less evidence suggests a positive association between online dating and more high-quality relationships: one study was conducted prior to the mobile dating era (Cacioppo et al., 2013) and other studies measured people's anticipated effect on relationships (Fullwood & Attrill-Smith, 2018; Lane et al., 2020; Lane & Joslyn, 2019; Langlais et al., 2024; Sharabi & Timmermans, 2021). As an exception, one study found a positive association between Tinder exposure and relationship stability (S. Jung & Lusher, 2026).

Online dating was indeed associated with more relationship experiences (Lapsley et al., 2023; Mohan, 2023) and more first-dates offline (Mohan, 2023). Higher compulsive dating app use was likewise associated with more subjective online success (Gao et al., 2024) but lower firstdate satisfaction (Vera Cruz et al., 2023) or commitment (Hu, 2024a).

Second, online dating was associated with more opportunities to form a relationship with people who are similar or different. Two people in a relationship that began in online dating were more likely to have a similar education background (Potarca, 2017 (Potarca, , 2021;; Qian & Hu, 2025).

Mixed findings were found for the associations between online dating and homogamy in other demographics (e.g., age, religion, race; Kornrich & Robbins, 2024; Potarca, 2017; Qian & Hu, 2025; R. J. Thomas, 2020). Two mechanisms were raised in these studies: selection, by which online dating allows people to select a mate of a certain demographic, and constraint, by which online dating frees people from being stuck with mates of a certain demographic only. However, it is worth pointing out that most studies examining the relational correlates of online dating endorsement were cross-sectional. This limitation prevents drawing causal conclusions and may have introduced selection bias and confounds. It is possible that people who chose to find a relationship via online dating faced greater romantic constraints prior to online dating than people who chose to meet someone via offline dating; these traits are rarely controlled in a cross-sectional study commonly relying on large-scale survey data. Longitudinal studies, particularly ones that employed stricter causal inference approaches (propensity score matching, difference-in-difference) were rarer. Together, a negative association between endorsing online dating and quality of searched relationship seems to have emerged, but it is too soon to conclude that online dating makes poorer relationships. Having reviewed findings of online dating endorsement, we turn to reporting findings about specific online dating strategies.

RQ2: Access-gaining Strategies and Relationship Search Outcomes

RQ2 was concerned with access to dating options. There was no universal choice effect.

Some evidence suggests that having more dating options was associated with or led to lower match participation (Fong, 2024) and lower relational pursuit (D'Angelo & Toma, 2017, delayed effect). Some evidence suggests that online daters preferred to have more options (Lenton et al., 2008, Study 1), and that having more options led to more option-searching activities (Wu & Chiou, 2009), higher match compatibility and similarity (Hu & Markowitz, 2026), higher relational pursuit (Hu & Markowitz, 2026), and higher commitment readiness (Hu, 2024a; Hu & Markowitz, 2026). Studies also reported a non-significant association between the number of dating options and relationship outcomes (D'Angelo & Toma, 2017: immediate effect; Lenton et al., 2008, Study 2; Lenton & Stewart, 2008; Pronk & Denissen, 2020; Sculley et al., 2021).

Contrary to a common expectation, having more options was not consistently associated with being more selective (Hu & Markowitz, 2026; Shi et al., 2025; Wu & Chiou, 2009). To explain the inconsistencies, some studies commented that boundary conditions may exist, such as evaluation timing (immediate vs. delayed; (D'Angelo & Toma, 2017), evaluation focus (anticipated vs. actual; Lenton et al., 2008), and clarity in preferences (Hu & Markowitz, 2026).

Evidence also suggests that people like having all options presented simultaneously (which is a more dated design in online dating) rather than sequentially (which is a more contemporary design), which was indicated by feeling more satisfied and less regretful with the person they chose (Bischof et al., 2025). Indeed, one paper reported that people increased their rejection as they went through a sequence of choice (Pronk & Denissen, 2020).

The studies examining choice effects were often experimental, thus offering causal evidence. However, context differed between studies: options were operationalized as an imagined number of options, vignette-style profiles, or profiles on real-time online dating platforms; evaluations were made immediately after choice was made, some time after the choice was made, or even without making a choice; the variety of options also differed, with available social information being lean in some studies and rich in others. Such variation may explain why effects differed across studies.

RQ3: Matching strategies and Relationship Search Outcomes

With expanded choice sets, examining efficient matching procedures can be important (RQ3). Though matching involves complicated computational procedures, studies showed that being mindful of the matching process and a dating candidate's availability are often associated with better matching outcomes. First, higher trust in dating algorithms was associated with more favorable relationship appraisal and higher relational pursuit online (Cho & Sundar, 2022; Hu & Oh, 2025; Sharabi, 2021a; Tong et al., 2016) and after the first date (Sharabi, 2021a); higher trust in algorithms was also associated with a higher likelihood of being in a relationship found in online dating among past online daters (Hu, 2024b). Higher awareness or knowledge of algorithms was associated with lower difficulty in searching for a mate in online dating (Hu & Zhan, 2024).

Second, the use of different matching procedures can make a difference too. Online daters experienced higher matching success when availability of a potential partner (in terms of the ability or probability of accepting one's matching request or replying to their message) was considered in the design. Specifically, this was achieved through highlighting partner availability to users (N. Huang et al., 2022; J. Jung et al., 2022). Interestingly, highlighting users' availability or commitment to a potential partner (e.g., by allowing users to propose a match with a rose, which is a scarce resource) can also enhance matching success (Bapna et al., 2016 (Bapna et al., , 2023;; Lee et al., 2015). Alternatively, availability information could be used through having algorithms considered daters' availability when recommending matches (Bojd & Yoganarasimhan, 2022; Celdir et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2023; Kleinerman et al., 2021; Krzywicki et al., 2015). These studies suggest that availability information functions as an important signal that improves matching success.

However, there is a disconnection between small-scale studies that examined placebo effects of matching-related cognition (e.g., trust in algorithms) and large-scale studies that tested mechanical effects of particular matching procedures on real or simulated online dating platforms. It is highly plausible that both play a role in online relationship search, yet few studies examined both. Social theories that explain what happens between input and output in matching seem particularly lacking in the latter studies, though the end goal of studies examining matching is to decode what is hidden in the black box of matching in online dating.

RQ4: Communication Strategies and Relationship Search Outcomes

Not less important than gaining access to dating options or being matched with compatible options, online daters need to communicate with a chosen match to build relationships (RQ4). Communication is defined as verbal and nonverbal interaction, including crafting one's profile and browsing others' profiles, exchanging messages online, and communicating via other modalities (e.g., video chat or FtF; Frost et al., 2008; Sharabi & Caughlin, 2017; Sharabi & Dykstra-DeVette, 2019).

First, studies showed that strategic self-presentation can help daters garner more attention and opportunities for initial interaction (e.g., Wotipka & High, 2016). Higher attraction and romantic interest were often predicted by showing desirable traits (e.g., warmth, ambition, responsiveness: Buckner et al., 2024; de Vries, 2010; Hefner & Kahn, 2014; Jin & Martin, 2015; et al., 2025; Sumner et al., 2024). To add to the point that strategic self-presentation does not guarantee satisfying relationships in the long term, deception or manipulated self-presentation can be frowned upon (Campbell & Parker, 2022). Studies found that deception and negative expectancy violation were associated with reduced mate evaluation and romantic opportunities (DelGreco & Denes, 2020; Sharabi & Caughlin, 2019; Zhao & Yan, 2022). Rather, proactive, truthful, honest, and in-depth self-disclosure was associated with reduced uncertainty, more favorable mate evaluation, and higher relational pursuit (Freyth & Jonason, 2025; Ghoshal & Holme, 2006; Gibbs et al., 2006 Gibbs et al., , 2011;; S. A. Huang & Hancock, 2022; Kotlyar & Ariely, 2013; Nayar & Koul, 2021; Rosen et al., 2008; Sharabi & Dykstra-DeVette, 2019; Strugo & Muise, 2019). Even when rejection was necessary, daters who disclosed more were treated with a more sensitive approach of rejection (Halversen et al., 2022). Consistent with this finding, more time spent chatting online before seeing each other's face was associated with more favorable mate evaluation and higher future romantic pursuit (Ramirez et al., 2015; Sumner et al., 2025; Sumner et al., 2024) though in one study the effect leveled off after reaching a peak (with the x-axis being the amount of online interaction prior to modality switching; Ramirez et al., 2015).

Overall, the rich literature focusing on communication strategies generated diverse insights. From a media-market perspective, these insights can be understood as about how social information can be strategically distorted and restored to fit certain needs in relationship development. For example, many findings reveal the effect of strategically presenting one's desirable traits on garnering further interaction; other findings explain how the revelation of previously under-represented social information impact mate evaluation over the course of relationship initiation. Ultimately, communication strategies are often aimed at (creating opportunities for) fostering understanding between market players, with a common goal of producing a stable match. As we mentioned, communication (e.g., self-disclosure) helps people construct mutual understanding, thus creating perception of a stable match. Notwithstanding this possibility, the media-market perspective will revise what is considered "successful communication". Contrary to the common assumption that a positive outcome means two people like each other after interaction, the media-market perspective will suggest that disillusion (e.g., losing interest as one learns more about another) can be a favorable outcome for finding a stable match too. Market transactions are all-about timely gauging of mutual interest and moving on from mismatches quickly, where communication strategies play important roles.

Discussion

From a novel, media-market perspective, this scoping review addresses the connection between media-market strategies and online relationship search outcomes. Findings suggest merits in understanding online dating from a media-market perspective, where daters strategize media use strategies to capitalize potentials of media-markets (i.e., online dating platforms) and optimize outcomes of relationship search. Below, we briefly discuss how the media-market perspective helps to make sense of the reviewed findings and propose four emerging questions in online dating research, each correspondent to one type of strategy. We will then review how the media-market perspective contributes to advancing online dating research and broader field of mediated interpersonal communication.

Online Dating Endorsement: Toward Informed Technology Acceptance

We found that more studies point to a negative connection between endorsing online dating as a way of mate selection and relationship quality, particularly in recent years. This contrasts earlier findings about online dating websites. From a media-market perspective, this suggests that media-markets fail to function as expected, or that features of media-markets failed to be capitalized in daters' favor, such that market entry was associated with low or negative utility. In market design theories, market failure is attributed to poor market designs-or in our case, poor media-market designs, which block access to options (e.g., hiding matches behind the paywall), distort signals (e.g., encouraging people to lie about their traits and preferences), and congest market (e.g., prioritizing popular daters over maximizing overall matching rates), among other malfunctions (Roth, 2015).

What would a media-market perspective recommend for research about online dating endorsement? Technologies emerge every few years: social networking sites, online dating, chatbots, and so on. A common scenario is that despite doubts these technologies get integrated into everyday lives eventually. A media-market perspective would encourage research that promotes informed acceptance of these technologies, including online dating platforms, along with their latest features like AI-matchmaking, with questions like "Do users enter a new mediamarket by endorsing this new technology or feature?" and "Are new media-markets functioning well-that is, is entry of such media-markets associated with higher utility for relationship search?" To analyze how media-markets may be best designed for strategies to be useful, we turn to discussing specific strategies post media-market entry.

Access: What Qualifies Choice Effects?

Our review gathers mixed findings about whether having more dating options propels or postpones relationship progression. One salient feature of online dating as media-markets is that it can gives many more mate options that other venues for couples to meet; however, the number of mate options vary by platform design (Oyer, 2014). The reviewed studies suggest that effects of having more options (i.e., choice effects) may not be universal but contingent upon conceptualization of option and research design. This tension further shows the value of a media-market perspective (vs. a pure media or a pure market perspective). A pure media perspective often suggests that human brains have not evolved to process excessive information that technology affords, thus predicting a negative choice effect on relationship development (e.g., D'Angelo & Toma, 2017; M. F. Thomas et al., 2026). This perspective is more focused on phenomenological experiences. A pure market perspective would predict that more options mathematically host more diversity and afford higher match compatibility, thus predicting a positive choice effect (e.g., Oyer, 2014; Roth, 2015). This view is more structural, which "puts agents on vacation" (Bourdieu & Wacquant, 1992). The mixed findings in our work shows that a reconciliation between both views can be beneficial, and we suggest that the media-market perspective can help achieve that.

Matching: How Do Mechanical and Placebo Effects Interact?

This review also shows that certain matching strategies make daters more engaged in the relationship initiation process. Trust in algorithms seems to be beneficial for promoting a more pro-relationship approach in online dating (e.g., thinking and behaving in ways that do not help develop a relationship), which is suggested as a placebo effect in the literature (e.g., Hu & Oh, 2025). Additionally, considering daters' availability seems to contribute to a higher matching efficiency: algorithm or interface design that considers such information (rather than merely how popular a user is) in recommendation processes can lead to more matching success. We think this is a mechanical effect where algorithmic procedures are altered by different data input (vs. placebo effects).

Once again, there seems to be effects that are within control of individuals (trust in algorithms) and effects that are structural and agonistic to individuals' will (how algorithms match people). Therefore, a media-market perspective can help strike a balance between accounting for individuals' experiences while situating those experiences in bigger market designs and structures. It follows for future research to examine how individuals' cognition and behavior (e.g., trust in algorithm, mate evaluation) and market structure (circulation of available mates, matching mechanisms) interact to produce compatible matches.

Communication: Communication as Media-Market Behaviors

Furthermore, communication is central to developing a satisfying relationship (Finkel et al., 2012), and this review suggests that choosing the right communication strategies for the right purpose is helpful. Strategic presentation seems rewarding to attracting attention while truthful disclosure seems beneficial for long-term development.

Where Do Diverse Perspectives Meet? A Media-Market Perspective

To sum up, the media-market perspective helps leverage the strengths of diverse existing perspectives by situating strategies to find a compatible match online within media-market designs. Therefore, the media-market perspective to online dating is what Bourdieu and Wacquant (1992) suggest a "structural constructivist" view. We have accordingly proposed four questions that help produce future research that examine online relationship research considering platforms as both media and markets, the process as capitalizing their designs using certain strategies, the success of such attempts as constrained by both individuals' capacity and structural conductivity.

Limitations and Future Directions

We refrain from offering pragmatic implications. This scoping review makes it clear that findings are very contingent upon boundary conditions that are not yet clarified. More importantly, we follow Wacquant's (2026) principles for social theorists to separate scientific discovery from moralist judgment (i.e., assigning responsibilities to persons and institutions). We will leave the latter task to roles that are more fitting than social scientists.

Instead, we conclude this paper with limitations and future research directions. While it is common for reviews to suggest that causal evidence is lacking in a field, this scoping review shows that online dating is advantageously full of experimental research. The main limitation lies in an over-reliance on White, educated, industrial, rich, and democratic (WEIRD) samples, which are not uncommon in social sciences. A more theoretical limitation, or rather, a future direction, is as we argue to integrate more individualist views with more structural views. The forwarded media-market perspective has suggested a few potential directions for research production. Hopefully, these directions will help capitalize the rich causal evidence in separate views through the integration of these views.
