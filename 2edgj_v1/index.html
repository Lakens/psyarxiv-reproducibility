Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Speech Monitoring During Self-Comprehension

Spontaneous speech is full of false starts, rephrasing, and speech errors. How do we recognize the need to clarify or reword our own utterances, and how do we know when we make errors? Many theories assume that some pre-articulatory representation, production state, or derivative prediction-originating in the production process-provides information against which the utterance can be monitored (e.g., Hickok, 2012 Hickok, , 2014;; Levelt, 1989; Levelt et al., 1999; Pickering & Garrod, 2013). But if speech is a fundamentally interactive endeavor, in which our utterances and their meanings are inherently contextually anchored and emerge as speech unfolds, there is reason to question whether pre-articulatory information can provide a sufficiently specified basis for evaluating our own speech. This paper develops a theory of verbal self-monitoring that is grounded in a framework we propose for addressing the wider question of how we as speakers dynamically determine what we ourselves are saying as speech and interaction unfold.

The assumption that sufficiently specified information about what we are going to say is available before articulation-as instantiated in notions such as speech intentions, prelinguistic messages, or production commands-raises questions about the ontology of such pre-articulatory states, and their content is challenging to study empirically. Dennett (1991) warned against the (often tacit and unknowing) positing of central executives-or overly powerful homunculi-to explain complex cognitive processes. In relation to speech production, Dennett (1991) criticized what he called the illusion of the Central Meaner, or the idea that all the intricate processes that result in words being spoken can be reduced to a single point of origin. Focusing particularly on Levelt's (1989) Conceptualizer as an example of this problem, Dennett (1991) noted that it has access to all the speaker's knowledge, memory, and plans, and produces pre-verbal messages that constrain the meaning and subsequent formulation of the overt utterance. Moreover, for Levelt, the Conceptualizer is also the seat of the monitor, and the original message ultimately provides a criterion against which the appropriateness of the resulting utterance can be evaluated. While Levelt explicitly acknowledges that the Conceptualizer is a functional abstraction encompassing disparate cognitive processes that ultimately need to be unpacked, he leaves that unpacking outside the scope of his theory. Dennett's (1991) concern is that this abstraction is assigned precisely the capacity for message generation and monitoring that requires explanation.

Many theories of verbal self-monitoring similarly treat message generation as abstract-or outside the explanatory scope-while nevertheless making monitoring dependent on information generated through the production process. Although the information made available to monitoring differs between theories-e.g., conflict during lexical or phonological selection in Nozari et al. (2011), sensory targets and predictions in Hickok (2012 Hickok ( , 2014)), and predictions generated from an efference copy of a production command in Pickering and Garrod (2013)-monitoring is consequently conceived as downstream of and tightly coupled with the production process.

A related challenge to assumptions about the pre-articulatory specification of upcoming speech comes from Linell (e.g., 1979 Linell (e.g., , 1998 Linell (e.g., , 2009)), who emphasizes the intrinsically interactional nature of speech and criticizes the view of the speaker as a fully autonomous subject. Instead, Linell proposes that speakers are interdependent with others, that cognition and communication are inseparably connected, and that, consequently, an utterance's broader meaning and significance do not originate solely in the speaker's mind, but emerge through interaction between the speaker, interlocutors, and the unique communicative context. Therefore, the speaker cannot be assumed to know the full meaning and significance of their own utterance prior to speaking. Instead, meaning is progressively specified-for the speaker-as the utterance unfolds and they hear their own voice and perceive both their own reactions and those of their interlocutors. As Dennett (1991) put it: "… the speaker gets no preview; he and his audience learn what the speaker's utterance is at the same time" (p. 238).

We have previously investigated the idea that speakers use sensory feedback from their own speech to comprehend and make sense of what they themselves are saying. In Lind et al. (2014a), we created a mismatch between what speakers said and the auditory feedback they received, thereby also creating a putative mismatch between the feedback and whatever pre-articulatory information had guided the utterance. Speaking into specially constructed sound-isolating headsets while performing a Stroop test, participants' utterances (e.g., green) were sometimes surreptitiously recorded. Using a precise sound trigger mechanism, the recordings were later inserted into speakers' auditory feedback while feedback from what they said (e.g., gray) was blocked. Participants often believed that they had uttered the inserted word, demonstrating that auditory feedback can substantially influence speakers' judgments about what they themselves have said (see Meekings et al., 2015, and Lind et al., 2015, for discussion). Based on these findings, we proposed the selfcomprehension hypothesis: that speakers use sensory feedback from their own speech as a source of information about what they themselves are saying (Lind et al., 2014b). We further proposed that verbal error monitoring has a strong cue-integrative 1 character: rather than determining if what one has said was correct with reference to privileged pre-articulatory representations, judgments about appropriateness and correctness are instead shaped by multiple cues from sensory and contextual information (Lind et al., 2014a (Lind et al., , 2014b)).

In the present paper, we develop our account of how speech errors and other problems can be detected within the self-comprehension model. 2 Sensory feedback is a natural candidate for playing a prominent role in this account because it is clearly available to the speaker, even if no other assumptions are made about the speech production system.

Speaking generates several forms of sensory feedback. Self-produced sound reaches the auditory system through both air and bone conduction, while somatosensory signals convey information about the configuration and movement of the articulators as well as contacts between oral surfaces (e.g., Lackner & Tuller, 1979; Postma, 2000; Kent, 2024; Lind & 1 In Lind et al. The self-comprehension model does not depend on a particular account of how errors are generated, and it does not require the processes responsible for generating an error to also provide the mechanism by which that error is detected. Rather, it concerns how information in the unfolding utterance can allow them to be detected. Although we use conventionally transcribed speech errors as examples throughout the paper, such transcriptions should not be taken to imply that errors necessarily involve categorical substitutions or transpositions of discrete speech units. Articulatory and acoustic studies have shown that apparent errors can involve gradient, overlapping, or co-produced articulatory patterns (e.g., Boucher, 1994; Goldstein et al., 2007; Mowrey & MacKay, 1990; Pouplier, 2007; Pouplier & Goldstein, 2010), with gradient phonetic effects also observed in spontaneous speech errors (Alderete et al., 2021). First, if self-monitoring does not rely on access to sufficiently specified pre-articulatory information, and if speakers determine what they themselves are saying through self-comprehension as the utterance unfolds, how can they distinguish feedback indicating a speech error or communicative problem from feedback indicating an appropriate utterance? Second, assumptions about substantial processing delays in the use of sensory feedback have led many accounts of speech monitoring to conclude that sensory feedback cannot explain the many error interruptions that are made faster than those delays would allow. Consequently, different monitoring mechanisms relying on information from speech planning processes-including internally perceived phonological representations (e.g., Levelt et al., 1999; Wheeldon & Levelt, 1995), predictions (e.g., Hickok, 2012 Hickok, , 2014;; Pickering & Garrod, 2013), and conflict within lexical or phonological selection processes (Nozari et al., 2011)-have been proposed to account for such swift interruptions. The present paper therefore centers on two questions: How can speakers determine that their unfolding speech is erroneous or otherwise problematic without requiring privileged monitoring access to pre-articulatory production representations? And how can sensory feedback become informative rapidly enough to contribute to the detection of even very quickly interrupted errors?

We suggest that verbal self-monitoring is a natural sub-function of selfcomprehension. We propose no mechanism developed specifically for self-monitoring; rather, speakers sometimes detect errors and other problems in the course of comprehending their own speech. Like speech comprehension more generally, selfcomprehension is a multilevel, cue-integrative process in which available contextual, linguistic, and sensory information can jointly constrain the interpretation of unfolding speech. Importantly, these sources of information need not operate independently. Through speech production and learning, systematic relationships develop among speech representations and the auditory, somatosensory, and motor states associated with speaking (e.g., Franken et al., 2022; Guenther & Vladusich, 2012; Ito et al., 2016; Lametti et al., 2012; Nasir & Ostry, 2009). We propose that these learned relationships provide a basis for interactions between speech and sensory information during self-comprehension. 3 Within this cue-integrative system, we propose that two sources of anticipatory information are particularly important for self-comprehension and self-monitoring. The first is multi-scale pattern completion (MSPC), proposed by Falandays et al. (2021) as a mechanism capable of producing prediction-like anticipatory activation during language comprehension without requiring explicitly predictive representations, and here extended to self-comprehension. The second is anticipatory sensory information generated by the speaker's own unfolding articulation, particularly information arising from auditory and somatosensory coarticulation. Auditory coarticulatory cues are well established as a source of anticipatory information during other-comprehension (e.g., Beddor et al., 2013; Gow & McMurray, 2007; Salverda et al., 2014; Schreiber & McMurray, 2019), and recent work has proposed that speakers may use early sensory consequences of anticipatory coarticulation for speech control (Floegel et al., 2023; Kasper et al., 2026). We suggest that anticipatory coarticulatory information in both auditory and somatosensory feedback can also contribute to self-comprehension by constraining speakers' interpretation of their own unfolding speech. Because coarticulatory information about upcoming speech can become available before the relevant speech segment is articulated, sensory information may become informative substantially earlier than assumed by accounts that estimate monitoring latency from post-articulatory feedback alone. We propose that upcoming errors can likewise be reflected, at least partly, in this anticipatory coarticulatory information. Multi-Scale Pattern Completion During Self-Comprehension

Speech comprehension is widely thought to involve anticipatory processing based on recent speech context (e.g., Kuperberg & Jaeger, 2016; Pickering & Gambi, 2018). Such anticipatory processing is often interpreted as evidence that listeners predict upcoming speech. For example, in a classic study, Altmann and Kamide (1999) showed that eye movements toward an image of a cake began prior to the onset of the word cake when participants heard the sentence "the boy will eat the cake," but only after the onset of cake when the verb was instead move, showing that sentence context can lead listeners to anticipate upcoming speech. Evidence interpreted as reflecting prediction or anticipatory processing has been reported at lexical, semantic, syntactic, and phonological levels, as well as in turn-taking during dialogue (see Falandays et al., 2021; Kuperberg & Jaeger, 2016). As we have seen, prediction has also been proposed as a central mechanism for verbal selfmonitoring (e.g., Bradshaw et al., 2026; Hickok, 2012 Hickok, , 2014;; Pickering & Garrod, 2013; Tourville & Guenther, 2011). While anticipatory or prediction-like effects clearly do occur during speech comprehension-strongly suggesting that they may also play a central role during self-comprehension and self-monitoring-there are many different suggestions, and no current consensus, regarding the cognitive mechanisms that produce these effects (see, e.g., Huettig, 2025; Ryskin & Nieuwland, 2023, for discussion). 4 In a recent paper, Falandays et al. (2021) reconceptualize prediction during speech comprehension as multiscale pattern completion (MSPC), which does not rely on explicit representations of future states to be generated and compared with input. In this section, we first examine Falandays et al.'s (2021) proposal and then consider how its core principles may be applied to verbal self-comprehension. Falandays et al. (2021) draw on research in visual perception documenting both perceptual completion, whereby occluded or otherwise incompletely visible stimuli can nevertheless be perceived as complete (e.g., Grossberg, 2003; Mendola, 2003; Weil & Rees, 2011), and forward-looking perceptual effects, such as representational momentum, whereby objects that disappear while moving are judged or remembered as having progressed slightly farther along their trajectory than their actual final position (e.g., Freyd, 1987; Freyd & Finke, 1984; Jordan & Hunsinger, 2008). Falandays et al. (2021) propose that the same general principle of pattern completion can account for analogous phenomena in language comprehension. For example, listeners can identify or generate hypotheses about words after hearing only an initial fragment in gating paradigms (e.g., Cotton & Grosjean, 1984; Grosjean, 1980; Marslen-Wilson, 1987; Tyler & Wessels, 1983), and can perceptually restore speech sounds that are absent from the auditory signal (e.g., Samuel, 1981 Samuel, , 1996;; Warren, 1970). Falandays et al. relate these phenomena to distributed cell assemblies or population-level representations, in which activation of part of a learned representation can spread to other components of that representation (see also Buzsáki, 2010; Huyck & Passmore, 2013; Pulvermüller, 1999 Pulvermüller, , 2002 Pulvermüller, , 2018)). Thus, partial phonological input may activate not only the remaining phonological components of a word but also associated lexical, semantic, and grammatical information. Falandays et al. (2021) further propose that cell assemblies associated with temporally separate but repeatedly co-occurring events can become linked through experience into larger assemblies, which they call hypercodes. Such hypercodes preserve relationships among the component events, including their temporal ordering (see also Almeida-Filho et al., 2014; O'Reilly & Rudy, 2001). Second, Falandays et al. (2021) present a simplified homeostatic reservoir model demonstrating that prediction-like behavior can emerge through unsupervised learning. The model consists of interconnected nodes whose coupling strengths and target activity adapt locally in pursuit of homeostasis. Taken together, Falandays et al.'s (2021) arguments and computational demonstrations show how prediction-like anticipatory processing can emerge through temporally forward-reaching pattern completion. Their proposal has subsequently received increasing attention as a possible mechanism for anticipatory processing that does not require the generation of explicit predictions of future input (e.g., Drouin & Davis, 2024; Huettig, 2025; Mansell et al., 2025; Ryskin & Nieuwland, 2023). We suggest that the same general principle of MSPC can operate during self-comprehension. Crucially, the information driving MSPC during self-comprehension need not originate in a representation of the speaker's intended utterance. As in other-comprehension, preceding linguistic and communicative context can activate learned continuations at multiple levels. During selfcomprehension, the speaker's own previously produced speech forms part of this unfolding context, together with the wider discourse and communicative situation. We therefore propose that, as speakers comprehend their own unfolding speech, MSPC can generate anticipatory activation 5 at phonological, lexical, syntactic, and semantic levels on the basis of the linguistic and communicative context. Such anticipatory activation does not require a separate explicit representation of the predicted future input. Importantly, the resulting anticipation differs from the production-derived predictions proposed in forward-model accounts: rather than stemming from, for example, an efference copy, it is generated from the kinds of contextual regularities that also support anticipation during othercomprehension. MSPC thus provides one source of anticipatory information during selfcomprehension. We next consider a second-anticipatory sensory information arising from coarticulation-and how such information may allow sensory feedback to become informative earlier than is commonly assumed in accounts of speech monitoring.

Anticipatory Coarticulation as a Source of Information During Self-Comprehension

Sensory feedback has long been proposed to play a central role in verbal selfmonitoring (e.g., Levelt, 1989; Levelt et al., 1999; Pickering & Garrod, 2013; but see Nozari et al., 2011; Nozari, 2018). However, sensory feedback has often been considered too slow to account for the most rapid instances of error detection and interruption, motivating the proposal of faster, pre-articulatory monitoring mechanisms. Yet acoustic analyses of anticipatory coarticulation suggest that this assumption warrants reconsideration: acoustic information about upcoming speech can become available well before the onset of the relevant speech segment (e.g., Salverda et al., 2014; Schreiber & McMurray, 2019). Floegel et al. (2023) introduced an informational account in which anticipatory coarticulation may provide speakers with advance information about their own upcoming speech that can be used to control ongoing speech production, an account subsequently developed and empirically investigated by Kasper et al. (2026). Crucially, Kasper et al. (2026) argued that the early availability of such information challenges the common assumption that sensory feedback is necessarily too slow for direct use in speech control. We extend this proposal to self-comprehension and speech-error monitoring, suggesting that speakers may exploit anticipatory sensory information generated through coarticulation to constrain their interpretation of their own unfolding speech. A related possibility was raised much earlier by Postma and Kolk (1992; see their footnote 3), who considered whether speakers might use acoustic coarticulatory cues to anticipate upcoming speech errors, thereby potentially accounting for rapid error interruptions without recourse to internal monitoring. Although they were skeptical that "such subtle acoustic information" (p. 542) could be used during verbal self-monitoring, subsequent research, reviewed below, prompts renewed consideration of this possibility. The present proposal additionally considers anticipatory information available through somatosensory coarticulation. If speakers exploit such auditory and somatosensory anticipatory information during self-comprehension, sensory feedback may become informative about an upcoming speech error substantially earlier than has generally been assumed, changing the temporal constraints traditionally attributed to sensory-feedbackbased speech monitoring. We first examine the origins of these commonly assumed temporal constraints and then review the observed distribution of error-interruption latencies, including the rapid interruptions that have motivated proposals for pre-articulatory monitoring.

Based on speech shadowing and word-monitoring studies (Marslen-Wilson, 1973; Marslen-Wilson & Tyler, 1980), Marslen-Wilson and Tyler (1981) estimated that words in utterance contexts can typically be recognized within approximately 200 ms of word onset. Levelt (1989) subsequently applied this estimate to the external monitoring of self-produced speech, treating approximately 200 ms as the minimum time required to detect an error through the auditory perceptual loop. Hartsuiker and Kolk (2001) further argued that the perceptual latency should be supplemented by the time required to implement an interruption once an error has been detected. We refer to this second component as the error-interruption implementation latency. On the basis of approximately 200-ms stopping latencies in externally cued interruption tasks involving speech (Ladefoged et al., 1973) and finger movements (Logan & Cowan, 1984), and subtracting an estimated 50 ms for perception of the stop signal, Hartsuiker and Kolk (2001) arrived at an interruptionimplementation latency of approximately 150 ms. Combined with the assumed 200-ms auditory processing latency, this yields an estimated lower bound of approximately 350 ms from error onset to interruption for errors detected solely through post-articulatory auditory feedback. A separate influential estimate concerning somatosensory feedback comes from Lackner and Tuller (1979), who assumed that proprioceptive and auditory feedback could not contribute to error detection until approximately 300-400 ms after an error (a figure for which they cite Woodworth & Schlosberg, 1955, but which we were unable to locate in that reference).

Three studies of spontaneous speech errors (Blackmer & Mitton, 1991; Hartsuiker & Kolk, 2001; and Levelt, 1983) provide evidence that error interruptions can occur considerably earlier than predicted by the proposed ~350-ms lower bound, while also revealing a broad distribution from very early to much later interruptions. Examining these studies more closely provides information both about the types of problems speakers interrupt and about the temporal distribution of those interruptions. Levelt (1983) asked Dutch participants to describe visual patterns of colored dots connected by lines. He identified 959 spontaneous repairs and distinguished covert repairs, where the only indications of trouble were editing terms or word repetitions, from several categories of overt repair: appropriateness repairs-where what was said did not match the desired message, was ambiguous, or was contextually inappropriate; different repairs-where the ongoing utterance was interrupted and replaced with a different message; and error repairs (hereafter production-based repairs, following Blackmer & Mitton, 1991)-where a lexical, syntactic, or phonetic error was made. Although Levelt did not report millisecond error-to-cutoff times, the location of interruption provides information about its relative timing. Of the 777 repairs classified as involving immediate or delayed interruption, approximately 22% were interrupted within a word and 78% after it (51% immediately after the trouble word and 26% only after intervening speech had been produced). Crucially, Levelt (1983) found that 3.8% of all repairs (36 of 959) were interrupted very early, on the initial consonant or consonant cluster of the troublesome word (which in Dutch can span from several tens to a few hundred milliseconds; cf. Jongman, 1998). An often-cited example is a very early interruption of vertical in "… is a [v] … a horizontal line" (p. 64). Blackmer and Mitton (1991) reported millisecond error-to-cutoff times for 339 overt repairs identified in calls to a Canadian radio show. Of these interruptions, 5.3% occurred within 100 ms of error onset and 14.5% within 150 ms. Hartsuiker and Kolk (2001) presented the full distribution of error-to-cutoff times for 95 production-based repairs from Oomen and Postma's (2001) dataset, in which participants described the path of a dot through networks of objects and lines at normal and fast speaking rates. As shown in Figure 2, 66% of the errors were interrupted within the proposed ~350-ms lower bound for error-to-cutoff times based on post-articulatory auditory monitoring. 6 Together, these studies indicate that observed interruption times form a broad distribution, ranging from interruptions within the first 100 ms after error onset to interruptions occurring many hundreds of milliseconds, and sometimes likely more than a second, later.

As suggested above, however, sensory information about upcoming speech can become available substantially earlier than has generally been assumed. We therefore propose that such early sensory information can contribute to self-monitoring; if so, rapid error interruptions would no longer, by themselves, require the postulation of a prearticulatory monitoring channel. To address this possibility, we first review research demonstrating that acoustic coarticulatory cues allow listeners to anticipate upcoming speech. We then review evidence that articulatory movements themselves contain extensive anticipatory information about upcoming speech, which is potentially available to the speaker through somatosensory feedback. Crucially, we propose that these auditory and somatosensory cues are used by speakers during self-comprehension to anticipate upcoming properties of their own utterances, thereby sometimes allowing sensory information to contribute to self-comprehension substantially earlier than has generally been assumed. Finally, we review evidence suggesting that the error-interruption implementation latency may be considerably shorter than previously estimated.

Coarticulatory Anticipatory Cues in Auditory Feedback

As reviewed above, the approximately 200-ms estimate derived from spoken-word recognition was subsequently applied to the temporal constraints on auditory self-monitoring.

However, it has long been established that listeners can use acoustic coarticulatory information in preceding speech to anticipate upcoming speech (e.g., Fowler, 1984; Gow, 2001; Martin & Bunnell, 1982; Sereno et al., 1987; Warren & Marslen-Wilson, 1987 , 1988; Whalen, 1984; Yeni-Komshian & Soli, 1981), and more recent studies examining the precise time course with which such information can influence speech comprehension suggest very early effects. Following the same logic outlined above of applying findings from othercomprehension to self-comprehension and self-monitoring, we therefore propose that speakers use coarticulatory information during self-comprehension as well. We focus here on studies that reveal how early such coarticulatory information can influence spokenlanguage comprehension and the temporal advantage it can provide relative to target onset. Gow and McMurray (2007) investigated the between-word effects of coronal place assimilation, in which a word-final coronal consonant takes on place-of-articulation properties of a following word-initial non-coronal consonant, so that, for example, green boat is pronounced as greem boat. Participants heard two versions of utterances: one containing the assimilation and one in which it had been neutralized by splicing in green from an utterance that did not produce assimilation. Gow and McMurray (2007) found an earlier increase in the probability of fixating the target image (e.g., a green boat) following the assimilated compared with the non-assimilated version. This difference became significant 140 ms after the onset of the target word (boat), with a marginally significant effect evident already at 120 ms. Given an approximate 200-ms latency for planning and launching an eye movement (e.g., Allopenna et al., 1998), Gow and McMurray (2007) concluded that coarticulatory information was already influencing processing during the final portion of the assimilated word. The timing of the effect implies that such influence began approximately 60 ms before target-word onset. Importantly, Gow and McMurray (2007) characterized this effect as graded, with continuous acoustic variation in assimilation influencing the dynamics of lexical activation. Salverda et al. (2014) investigated the between-word anticipatory advantage provided by coarticulatory information in the vowel of the definite article, the. Across two experiments, participants heard utterances such as "the ladder is the target." n the first experiment, coarticulatory information was removed by splicing a token of the definite article produced in a neutral context into the sentence initial position and adding a brief pause after it (e.g., "the… ladder is the target"). n the second experiment, the determiner and target word were naturally produced as a single phonological phrase and contained the relevant coarticulatory cues. When anticipatory coarticulation was absent, the earliest languagedirected saccades were initiated within 200-240 ms after target-word onset. When it was available, however, the earliest saccades were launched approximately 70 ms earlier, in the 130-170 ms window, with marginal effects already evident in the 110-150 ms and 120-160 ms windows. Salverda et al. (2014) noted that this temporal advantage corresponded closely to the duration of the preceding determiner vowel (M = 53 ms, range = 30-78 ms).

Furthermore, examination of the formant structure of the determiner vowel showed that potentially informative coarticulatory cues about the upcoming speech sound were present very early, within approximately the first 10-20 ms of the vowel. Together, these findings show that anticipatory coarticulatory information about the upcoming word was available very early in the preceding segment and that listeners could exploit this to gain a substantial temporal advantage in spoken-word processing.

Within-word coarticulation can similarly constrain lexical processing before the relevant segment is heard. Dahan et al. (2001) cross-spliced monosyllabic CVC words such that the initial CV portion of a word (e.g., net) contained coarticulatory information consistent either with that word or with a competitor sharing the same onset (e.g., neck). When the vowel contained coarticulatory information appropriate for the competitor, participants looked more frequently toward the competitor and less frequently toward the target, with fixation patterns diverging around 600 ms after target-word onset. Because the coarticulatory presplice portion ended at approximately 380 ms and eye movements are assumed to reflect speech processing with a delay of approximately 200 ms, Dahan et al. (2001) argued this divergence reflected processing of the coarticulatory information in the vowel, before the subsequently presented final consonant could have produced the observed eye-movement effect. Similar results have been reported for Dutch stimuli (Dahan & Tanenhaus, 2004).

Building on research showing that anticipatory coarticulatory vowel nasalization facilitates perception of the subsequent nasal consonant (e.g., Beddor, 2009; Fowler & Brown, 2000), Beddor et al. (2013) examined fixations to target and competitor images whose names differed in the presence of a post-vocalic nasal consonant (e.g., bend vs bed).

They manipulated the point during the vowel at which anticipatory nasalization became available: in the early-nasalization condition, nasalization extended over approximately 80% of the vowel, whereas in the late-nasalization condition, it extended over approximately 40%.

When nasalization distinguished the target from the competitor, participants looked at the target significantly earlier when nasalization began early rather than late in the vowel, with an advantage of approximately 20-40 ms for early nasalization. Across items, divergence between target and competitor fixations began approximately 240-250 ms after the onset of vowel nasalization, with the difference becoming significant at approximately 260-270 ms.

Assuming an approximately 200-ms eye-movement programming latency, these results indicate that listeners can exploit anticipatory coarticulatory nasalization very rapidly after it becomes available, well before the onset of the nasal consonant, and, crucially, that the timing of perceptual processing can vary with the timing of the coarticulatory information in the speech signal.

Studies have shown that coarticulation in word-initial fricatives can be used to anticipate upcoming vowels (McMurray & Jongman, 2016; Nittrouer & Whalen, 1989; Yeni-Komshian & Soli, 1981). Focusing specifically on the temporal dynamics of such anticipation, Schreiber and McMurray (2019) manipulated acoustic coarticulatory cues associated with anticipatory lip-rounding in word-initial /s/ and /ʃ/ by cross-splicing fricatives across vowel contexts. The fricatives had a mean duration of 264 ms, and acoustic analysis showed that coarticulatory cues to the upcoming vowel were present within the first ~50 ms of the fricative. The coarticulatory manipulation produced a divergence in fixation patterns already around 30 ms into the subsequent vowel, with participants looking more frequently toward the object consistent with the vowel anticipated by the coarticulatory cues. Given an approximately 200-ms latency for planning and launching an eye movement, this indicates that the coarticulatory information had begun influencing processing during the preceding fricative, potentially as early as approximately 170 ms before vowel onset. Remarkably, listeners were able to use the coarticulatory information to anticipate the upcoming vowel before they had identified the fricative carrying this information. These findings demonstrate that listeners can begin processing information about an upcoming speech segment well before that segment is heard, and even before the segment carrying the anticipatory information has itself been identified.

Using a gating paradigm, Lulaci et al. (2024) similarly demonstrated that listeners can exploit coarticulatory information about an upcoming vowel from extremely short fragments of a preceding fricative. For /s/-initial words, the roundedness of the upcoming vowel significantly influenced word-identification accuracy after participants had heard only the first 15 ms of /s/, whereas useful information about vowel height and backness emerged after 75 ms for /f/-initial words. Thus, listeners can exploit anticipatory coarticulatory information about upcoming speech from as little as 15 ms of preceding acoustic input, although the timing with which such information becomes useful varies across speech sounds and articulatory features.

As we saw, Beddor et al. (2013) showed that making coarticulatory information available earlier within a word can lead to earlier effects on comprehension. There are indications that a similar principle may apply to between-word anticipatory coarticulation as well. Altmann (2011), reanalyzing data from Kamide et al. (2003) and Altmann (2004), found that language-mediated saccades were sometimes initiated as early as 80-120 ms post target-word onset, earlier than in both Gow and McMurray (2007) and Salverda et al. (2014).

Assuming an approximately 200-ms saccade-planning latency, this suggests that speech information could have begun guiding saccade planning as early as approximately 120 ms before target-word onset. Although Altmann (2011) did not analyze coarticulatory information in these stimuli, Salverda et al. (2014) noted that the mean durations of the determiners preceding the target words were substantially longer in Kamide et al. (2003; 176 ms) and Altmann (2004; 170 ms) than in their own stimuli (100 ms; see also Mitterer & Reinisch, 2013). One possibility is therefore that the longer determiners allowed relevant coarticulatory information to become available earlier, contributing to the earlier saccades observed in Altmann's (2011) analysis. More generally, prolongation of speech segments carrying anticipatory coarticulatory information-for example, in slow or carefully articulated speech or for pragmatic purposes-could potentially increase the temporal advantage afforded by such information.

The studies recounted above show how several different types of coarticulatory cues in various speech segments allow listeners to anticipate upcoming speech both within and between words, with perceptual effects emerging before the onset of the speech segment to which the cues pertain. Evidence from spontaneous and experimentally elicited speech errors indicates that error-related information penetrates the articulatory implementation of speech and participates in its context-sensitive organization (e.g., Alderete et al., 2021; Goldstein et al., 2007; Slis & Van Lieshout, 2016). These findings provide a basis for proposing that an upcoming error can be reflected, at least partly, in anticipatory coarticulatory information before the conventional locus of the error. Following the logic outlined above of applying findings from other-comprehension to self-comprehension and self-monitoring, we propose that speakers similarly use auditory coarticulatory cues during self-comprehension, allowing them to anticipate aspects of their own upcoming speech before the relevant segment is produced. This proposal converges with Floegel et al.'s (2023) and Kasper et al.'s (2026) proposal that speakers use sensory information arising from anticipatory coarticulation to guide ongoing speech production. Crucially, such anticipatory self-comprehension need not require a separately generated production-derived prediction or privileged monitoring access to pre-articulatory production representations.

Coarticulatory Anticipatory Cues in Somatosensory Feedback

Acoustic coarticulatory cues arise from movements and configurations of the articulatory apparatus. For example, movements of the tongue alter the resonant properties of the vocal tract and hence formant frequencies, whereas lowering of the velum couples the oral and nasal cavities and produces acoustic correlates of nasalization. Crucially, the articulatory requirements of an upcoming speech sound can begin to reshape the speech apparatus during preceding sounds, sometimes several segments before the target. These changes also generate somatosensory information, including tactile, proprioceptive, and pressure-related information from the oral, pharyngeal, laryngeal, and respiratory apparatus (Kent, 2024). There is direct evidence that speech-related somatosensory information can carry phonetic information: speech-like facial skin stretch systematically alters heard phoneme identity (Ito et al., 2009), and speakers can categorize vowels from tongue somatosensation alone, with performance comparable to categorization from whispered speech (Patri et al., 2020). Somatosensory information from the speech apparatus can also be processed rapidly. Unexpected tongue stretch during a speech posture elicits a speechenhanced muscular response at approximately 50 ms, followed by compensatory tongue movement at approximately 130 ms even when auditory feedback is masked (Bourhis et al., 2024; Ito et al., 2024). These findings demonstrate that speech-related changes in articulatory state can be registered rapidly through somatosensory feedback. Anticipatory labial activity associated with an upcoming /u/ has been observed hundreds of milliseconds before the acoustic onset of the vowel (Bell-Berti & Harris, 1979; Perkell & Matthies, 1992), with Bell-Berti and Harris (1979), for example, reporting onsets of approximately 140-290 ms before /u/. In more recent audiovisual measurements, lip aperture regularly began to distinguish rounded from unrounded vowels more than 200 ms before vowel onset; individual speakers' mean divergence points ranged from 1 2 ms to 331 ms in German and from 182 ms to 352 ms in English (Lo et al., 2023). Even earlier onsets are possible: Lubker (1981) reported labial activity associated with a rounded vowel as early as approximately 600 ms before its acoustic onset. Lip protrusion has also been observed to begin during the preceding vowel or at the first consonant of clusters containing four to six consonants, thereby extending across several consonants preceding /u/ (Benguerel & Cowan, 1974; Daniloff & Moll, 1968).

Studies of VCV sequences have shown that tongue configuration and movement during an intervening consonant can depend on the identity of the upcoming vowel (Gay, 1977; Parush et al., 1983), with other studies finding anticipatory effects extending into the preceding vowel. Tongue position during the production of an initial /g/, for example, already reflected the following vowel 100 ms before the consonant's release, even before consonantal closure had been achieved (MacNeilage & DeClerk, 1969). Tongue-palate contact patterns associated with an upcoming vowel have similarly been found to extend through substantial portions of an intervening stop closure and, in some cases, into the preceding vowel (Butcher & Weiher, 1976). Recasens et al. (1997) likewise found V2dependent changes in dorsopalatal contact extending into the preceding vowel, while subsequent EMA measurements yielded a mean anticipatory extent of 147 ms for horizontal tongue-dorsum position (Recasens, 2002). Transconsonantal and still longer-range anticipatory tongue-dorsum effects have also been observed in VCV and VCVCV sequences (Recasens, 1984 (Recasens, , 1989)).

Anticipatory velum lowering toward an upcoming nasal can begin during one or even two preceding vowels, or within a consonant cluster well before the nasal itself (Benguerel et al., 1977a (Benguerel et al., , 1977b;; Kent et al., 1974; Moll & Daniloff, 1971; Ushijima & Hirose, 1974). Direct real-time MRI measurements have likewise demonstrated anticipatory velum lowering during the vowel preceding a nasal consonant, with the gesture in some cases beginning even before the onset of the vowel (Carignan et al., 2021). Using nasal intensity as an indirect index of velum opening, Pouplier et al. (2024) estimated mean anticipatory extents of approximately 190 ms in American English, 130 ms in German, and 90 ms in French; the effect sometimes extended beyond the entire pre-nasal vowel.

Anticipatory changes also occur in the pharyngeal walls. Movement of the superior lateral pharyngeal walls toward the configuration associated with an upcoming nasal began as much as roughly 80 ms before the voice onset of the vowel preceding that nasal-that is, before the preceding vowel's voicing onset (Parush & Ostry, 1986). At the lower lateral pharyngeal walls, pharyngeal position during an intervening consonant depended on the identity of the upcoming vowel, with movement away from the configuration for the initial vowel beginning as much as roughly 200 ms before the final vowel's voice onset (Parush & Ostry, 1993).

Laryngeal preparation can likewise begin well before the supralaryngeal realization of an upcoming consonant. For intervocalic voiceless stops, posterior cricoarytenoid activity associated with the glottal-opening gesture began approximately 70-160 ms before oral closure (Hirose & Gay, 1972). Laryngeal articulation associated with an earlier consonant can also vary as a function of upcoming speech: Löfqvist and Yoshioka (1980), for example, found that the glottal gesture associated with a word-final /t/ differed depending on whether the following word began with a glottal stop or a voiceless aspirated stop.

Jaw trajectories can likewise contain information about an upcoming vowel during the movement toward an intervening consonant. In VCV sequences, the identity and openness of the final vowel affected the amplitude and duration of the initial vowel-to-consonant jawclosing movement (Ostry et al., 1996). Similarly, Sussman et al. (1973) found that, in one speaker, jaw elevation toward an intervening bilabial consonant varied inversely with the openness of the following vowel. In longer sequences, jaw displacement associated with an upcoming /æ/ extended across two preceding consonants and, in several cases, across three (Amerman et al., 1970).

There is also evidence that respiratory adjustments can anticipate relatively global properties of upcoming speech, such as utterance length and linguistic structure (see, e.g., Fuchs et al., 2013; Winkworth et al., 1994; Winkworth et al., 1995), although it remains unclear to what extent such effects can support anticipation of upcoming speech at a more fine-grained level.

In this section, we have seen detailed evidence for highly context-sensitive anticipatory coarticulation across multiple articulatory structures, with target-dependent articulatory differences sometimes emerging hundreds of milliseconds before the relevant speech segment. Consistent with this literature, Kasper et al. (2026) found that articulation during preceding phonemes systematically differed according to the identity of an upcoming phoneme, with effects extending up to approximately 300 ms before its canonical onset in longer vowels and 200 ms in shorter consonants. Together, these findings establish that target-dependent articulatory differences capable of generating potentially informative somatosensory feedback can emerge well before the speech segments to which they pertain. Kasper et al. (2026) propose that speakers use such early sensory information to control ongoing speech, although their study did not directly test its use. We extend this proposal to self-comprehension, hypothesizing that anticipatory somatosensory coarticulatory information constrains speakers' interpretation of their own upcoming speech.

Together with coarticulatory cues in the auditory modality, such information could diverge from MSPC-based anticipation and thereby provide a cue that the unfolding utterance is problematic or constitutes a speech error before the relevant speech segment has been articulated.

Error Interruption Implementation Latency

How quickly can an interruption be implemented once some error signal is processed? As we have seen, the previously proposed ~150 ms interruption implementation latency for speech was derived by Hartsuiker and Kolk (2001) from stop-signal studies by Ladefoged et al. (1973) and Logan and Cowan (1984). There is, however, evidence that events registered by somatosensory and auditory feedback can elicit relevant responses much more rapidly. Mechanical perturbations to speech articulators can elicit task-specific compensatory responses within tens of milliseconds (e.g., Gracco & Abbs, 1985; Kelso et al., 1984; see Grimme et al., 2011; Kent, 2024, for reviews). Cortical responses to stimulation of oral structures can likewise emerge within tens of milliseconds, with reported latencies of approximately 10-55 ms for the tongue and around 50-60 ms for the lips, tongue, and gingiva (Möttönen et al., 2005; Nakahara et al., 2004; Sakamoto et al., 2010).

Similarly, responses to unpredictable real-time auditory-feedback manipulations via headphones have been found to be initiated as quickly as 60 ms for pitch shifts during vowel utterances (Burnett et al., 1998), while compensatory responses to upward and downward F1 shifts during monosyllabic CVC utterances began as early as 87 ms and 55 ms (Tourville et al., 2008). If implementing these subtle and precise modifications in response to altered feedback is at least as time-consuming as interrupting ongoing speech, these findings suggest that interruption implementation latencies could be considerably shorter than 150 ms. 8 Once divergent anticipatory information provides a cue that an error is upcoming, the processing of that information and the initiation of speech interruption could partially overlap in time, just as sensory processing and compensatory responses to perturbations appear to be rapidly coupled (see Altmann, 2011, for a related discussion about how speech comprehension and saccade planning may proceed in parallel rather than sequentially).

(2008). 9 It is admittedly not likely that the ranges of fastest-toslowest responses to somatosensory and auditory perturbations outlined above can be directly applied to the range of speech-interruption implementation latencies. However, these ranges do suggest that, when error interruption is based on sensory feedback, interruption implementation latency may vary from very short to considerably longer. As mentioned above, neither tone-prompted interruptions nor somatosensory or auditory responses to perturbed feedback provide a perfect analogy to interrupting erroneous speech-empirical work is needed. We therefore emphasize that, as will be clear in the following sections, even if the error-interruption implementation latency is in fact approximately 150 ms, as Hartsuiker and Kolk (2001) proposed, speech could still be interrupted, for example, 50 ms after error onset if anticipatory coarticulatory information allowed the error to be detected 100 ms before its onset. We next consider how MSPCbased anticipation and auditory and somatosensory information from the unfolding utterance-including anticipatory information arising from coarticulation-may contribute to the detection and interruption of speech errors.

Detecting and Interrupting Speech Errors

We have seen how anticipations based on MSPC and on coarticulatory cues can activate knowledge at the phonological and lexical levels very rapidly, and we have argued that these anticipations are also integral to verbal self-comprehension and self-monitoring.

Before detailing how error monitoring may operate in the self-comprehension model, we again emphasize that self-comprehension is likely not achieved through a single mechanism or process. Rather, the context will dictate what information-including anticipatory cues-is available to the speaker, and when it is available. For example, Huettig and Mani (2016; see also Pickering & Gambi, 2018) reviewed the available data on prediction during speech comprehension and questioned the view that prediction is an inherent and ubiquitous aspect of human cognition (e.g., Clark, 2013; Friston, 2005) and of language processing. Instead, Huettig and Mani (2016) argued that prediction-which, following Falandays et al. (2021), can on the present account be reconceptualized as MSPC-based anticipation-does not appear to be necessary for language comprehension, with some individuals appearing not to use it under conditions in which others do. Rather, prediction can be used opportunistically when there are strong enough contextual cues, and enough cognitive resources, such as working memory. Huettig and Mani (2016) concluded by stating that "[s]ometimes, processing words when they occur may be more efficient and economical than predicting them" (p. 28). Moreover, the available evidence suggests that anticipatory processing based on MSPC and coarticulation may generally provide weaker or less sustained constraints than post-articulatory speech comprehension (i.e., "processing words when they occur"). Falandays et al. (2021) showed that phonemes anticipated via MSPC receive partial activation in the absence of corresponding bottom-up input. Schreiber and McMurray (2019)found that coarticulatory cues exert an early but transient influence, whereas postarticulatory input has a later but more sustained influence. We suggest that these observations apply to MSPC-and coarticulation-based anticipations and post-articulatory processing during self-comprehension as well: anticipatory processing can be highly informative when it is available, but it is not necessary, and post-articulatory selfcomprehension is likely often more efficient, precise, and certain-but slower. This suggests that, while errors are sometimes detected and interrupted rapidly based on anticipatory cues, many errors will be detected and interrupted later, via postarticulatory self-comprehension. We propose three characteristic routes to error detection: (Blackmer & Mitton, 1991; Hartsuiker & Kolk, 2001; Levelt, 1983) without privileged access to pre-articulatory production representations. Quicker interruptions, we suggest, arise primarily through route (a), while progressively slower interruptions arise primarily through routes (b) and (c) (although, as discussed further below, there is unlikely to be a hard temporal dividing line between interruption latencies of errors detected based on (a), (b), or (c) 10 ). In the following sections, we will spell out routes (a)-(c) in detail, focusing on their temporal implications.

Error Interruption Based on Anticipatory Cues

We suggest that anticipatory somatosensory and auditory coarticulatory cues provide rapidly available information about upcoming speech that can reveal a mismatch between the developing utterance and the anticipation generated by MSPC, potentially allowing rapid error detection. We illustrate this principle with Levelt's (1983) "… is a [v] … a horizontal line" interruption and repair example (p. 64). The original utterance in Dutch was "… zit een [v] … een horizontale lijn." f horizontale was anticipated on the basis of MSPC, but verticale was ultimately spoken, the unfolding speech signal could provide cues to this mismatch before the onset of verticale. Somatosensory cues could include, for example, movement of the lower lip toward the configuration for the labiodental /v/, rather than the lip-rounding associated with the upcoming /ɔ/ of horizontale, together with a more front/central rather than retracted tongue-body configuration. Auditory consequences could include relatively higher F2 values during the preceding unstressed vowel, rather than the lower F2 expected from anticipation of /ɔ/, and comparatively less F3 lowering associated with lip rounding.

Thus, coarticulatory cues could potentially reveal, in real time, that the developing utterance is inconsistent with the speech event anticipated by MSPC. Coarticulatory cues toward verticale need not generate a specific anticipation of verticale (although, given the constrained context in this particular case, such anticipation is possible). Crucially, we therefore propose that the relevant contrast is not between coarticulation suggesting the utterance anticipated with MSPC (here horizontale) and anticipatory coarticulation suggesting the upcoming erroneous utterance (verticale)-rather, the relevant contrast is between horizontale and something sufficiently different from horizontale, signaling that what is anticipated is not what is about to be uttered. 10 See Nooteboom and Quené (2017) for a related discussion of why there need not be a one-to-one mapping between interrupted versus completed speech errors and internal versus external error detection, respectively.

As we have seen, within-word coarticulatory cues can also provide rapidly available anticipatory information about the remainder of the word. We therefore suggest that withinword errors-such as the phonological anticipation errors "John dropped his cuff of coffee" or "alsho share" (Fromkin, 1971)-can be detected due to a contrast between MSPC-based anticipation and such within-word coarticulatory information. The two errors above were not interrupted, but they could nevertheless have been, as illustrated by "delayed audifauditory feedback" (also from Fromkin, 1971). As demonstrated by Lulaci et al. (2024), auditory coarticulatory cues in /s/ and /f/ can, in isolation, provide information about an upcoming vowel as early as 15 ms and 75 ms into the fricative, respectively. Schreiber and McMurray (2019) likewise found that coarticulatory information in /s/ and /ʃ/ influenced anticipation of the upcoming vowel. Their phonetic analysis indicated reliable coarticulatory information within the first 50 ms of both fricatives, while the timing of their eye-movement effects suggests that listeners had used this information by approximately 170 ms before vowel onset. Thus, coarticulation could rapidly signal that an upcoming vowel does not match the vowel anticipated with MSPC (e.g., "sat him an exam" instead of "set him an exam," "fuwt meeving" instead of "feet moving" [Fromkin, 1973, Appendix], or shape instead of sheep). Assuming mean fricative durations of 178 ms and 166 ms for word-initial /s/ and /f/, respectively, when followed by a vowel in spoken English (Jongman et al., 2000), and an interruption implementation latency of 150 ms-i.e., the longest latency considered here- When an error is made on the first word of an utterance, there is no preceding speech from which auditory coarticulatory information about the initial speech sound can arise. However, because articulatory movements can begin well before acoustic onsetsometimes by several hundred milliseconds when the upcoming response has been prepared (Jouen et al., 2021; Kawamoto et al., 2008; Tilsen et al., 2016)-such movements should generate somatosensory information during articulatory preparation that could, in principle, provide advance information about the speech that is about to be produced. If, for example, a café visitor is asked for their order and starts answering canpakes instead of pancakes (Fromkin, 1973), somatosensory information from articulatory preparation could enable very early error detection and interruption.

As we have seen, not all detected errors lead to rapid interruptions. We propose that rapid interruptions depend on specific preconditions: (a) that the relevant MSPC-based anticipation is distinct, clear, and unambiguous enough for divergent auditory and/or somatosensory information to provide a salient cue that the unfolding utterance is problematic; (b) that the utterance anticipated with MSPC and the utterance being produced differ in articulatory properties capable of generating distinct anticipatory somatosensory and/or auditory information; (c) that these differences are sufficiently expressed in the unfolding articulation to produce sensory information that diverges saliently from the MSPCbased anticipation (the extent of this divergence may vary, for example, with the degree to which different articulatory patterns are co-expressed in error segments and with how clearly the speaker enunciates); and (d) that the interruption implementation latency is sufficiently short. An additional, less certain variable is (e) whether the speaker's attention is focused on their own speech. However, the satisfaction of these preconditions does not guarantee that a rapid interruption will occur. Specifically, detection latency need not map directly onto overt interruption latency. Speakers sometimes hold off interrupting an error until a repair is ready (Seyfeddinipur et al., 2008), meaning that two errors-one detected rapidly through anticipatory mechanisms and one detected later through post-articulatory sensory feedback-could in principle have similar overt interruption latencies.

Beyond these preconditions, several linguistic, articulatory, and comprehensionbased factors are likely to contribute to variability in the interruption latency of errors detected through anticipatory processing. First, the point at which useful anticipatory coarticulatory information becomes available differs across speech sounds and articulatory features (Lulaci et al., 2024; Schreiber & McMurray, 2019). Second, speakers show variability in both the magnitude and temporal extent of coarticulation during speech, both between speakers (Lo et al., 2023; Perkell & Matthies, 1992) and within speakers across utterances (Perkell & Matthies, 1992). Third, production-based speech errors vary in the extent to which they exhibit phonetic accommodation to their new context (Pouplier et al., 2014). Finally, listeners display individual variability both in the degree to which they use coarticulatory cues to anticipate speech (Beddor et al., 2013) and in the degree to which they engage in anticipatory processing during speech comprehension more generally (Huettig & Mani, 2016)-the latter reflecting, on our account, variability in MSPC-based anticipation.

Error Interruption Based on MSPC and Post-Articulatory Sensory Feedback

As we saw above, anticipatory error detection may fail because the coarticulatory contrast between the anticipated and produced utterances is insufficiently large, or because the produced coarticulatory cues are insufficiently prominent to signal the divergence. We propose that errors-including all the errors referenced in the previous section-can also be detected based on divergence between lexical or phonological activation generated by MSPC-based anticipation and activation generated through post-articulatory selfcomprehension via auditory and somatosensory feedback. This route to error detection should generally result in slower interruption times because the relevant sensory information becomes available only after articulation. While we propose that the most rapid error interruptions arise from MSPC-based and coarticulatory anticipation, the quicker interruptions resulting from divergence between MSPC-based anticipation and postarticulatory sensory feedback may nonetheless overlap temporally with the slower interruptions based on anticipatory coarticulatory cues.

Direct estimates of the processing times required to identify individual speech sounds in the absence of preceding coarticulatory cues are difficult to obtain. 11 Nonetheless, the studies reviewed above on the temporal advantage provided by coarticulatory cues offer some indirect constraints on lower-bound speech processing latencies. For example, Salverda et al. (2014) found that anticipatory coarticulatory cues advanced the onset of language-mediated eye-movement effects by approximately 70-90 ms and that, in the absence of such cues, target-driven saccades started emerging roughly 200-240 ms after target-word onset. Assuming a saccade-planning latency of approximately 200 ms (Allopenna et al., 1998), this suggests that useful speech information may sometimes begin to be extracted from the acoustic signal within only a few tens of milliseconds after informative acoustic material becomes available (see Altmann, 2011, for discussion).

For example, if an utterance concludes with "a Tanadian from Toronto" and the context has led to MSPC-based anticipation of Canadian (error from Fromkin, 1973), but anticipatory coarticulatory cues preceding /t/ in Tanadian are insufficient to distinguish the upcoming /t/ from /k/, then post-articulatory information may within a few tens of milliseconds after error-segment onset provide a cue that the produced sound is inconsistent with the MSPC-based anticipation. Assuming an interruption implementation latency of approximately 150 ms-the longest of the implementation latencies considered above (e.g., Hartsuiker & Kolk, 2001)-this could in principle yield interruption latencies on the order of 180 ms.

Importantly, such rapid phoneme-level error detection would likely rely on graded probabilistic activation rather than stable categorical phoneme identification, and many errors will likely be detected only at longer latencies.

Articulatory and acoustic studies have shown that production-based speech errors can exhibit gradient, overlapping, or co-produced patterns (e.g., Alderete et al., 2021; Goldrick & Blumstein, 2006; Goldstein et al., 2007; Mowrey & MacKay, 1990; Pouplier, 2007; see also Pouplier & Hardcastle, 2005). Such patterns could provide an additional source of sensory information for rapid error detection, potentially even in the absence of a sufficiently informative MSPC-based anticipation. For example, the /v/ in Levelt's (1983) v-horizontale error could, in principle, exhibit articulatory or acoustic properties associated with both verticale and horizontale, potentially providing rapidly available sensory evidence that the unfolding utterance is problematic. Consistent with the possibility that rapidly interrupted errors can contain unusual sensory information, Blackmer and Mitton (1991) observed irregularities in the pronunciation of rapidly interrupted erroneous segments, including truncation, decreased fundamental frequency, and lower amplitude, although it is also possible that these anomalies resulted from the interruption process itself.

Error Interruption Based on Post-Articulatory Self-Comprehension

Finally, we suggest that speech errors can be detected through post-articulatory selfcomprehension in the absence of sufficiently informative prior anticipation. Post-articulatory speech input will, as we saw above, generally provide stronger activation than anticipatory information based on MSPC (Falandays et al., 2021) or coarticulation (Schreiber & McMurray, 2019). We suggested that post-articulatory auditory feedback is similarly the strongest cue for self-comprehension. While anticipatory self-comprehension based on MSPC and coarticulation can sometimes provide enough information to elicit rapid error detection, it is unlikely to provide speakers with as comprehensive an understanding of the meaning and consequences of their utterances as post-articulatory self-comprehension. We therefore suggest that self-comprehension in the wider sense of understanding and contextualizing one's utterances within a given communicative situation-something that is more time-consuming-is likely to depend particularly strongly on post-articulatory information.

Similarly, to achieve self-comprehension in general, including the detection of errors (especially appropriateness errors), integration-that is, the linking of current linguistic input with prior context and general background knowledge (e.g., Abashidze et al., 2019; Ferreira & Chantavarin, 2018)-is likely to be crucial alongside anticipation.

More broadly, spoken communication is ultimately directed toward the communication and comprehension of meaning rather than merely phonological or lexical processing (see Meekings & Scott, 2025), and we suggest that this is also true for the selfcomprehending speaker. During post-articulatory self-comprehension, the emphasis is likely to be on auditory feedback and the broader contextual information, meaning that this form of self-comprehension is most similar to other-comprehension (but it is not identical, as we will detail in a section below).

Implications for Error Detection and Interruption Latencies

The characteristics of the three routes to self-comprehension and self-monitoring discussed above lead to several predictions concerning error detection patterns and timings.

Post-articulatory self-comprehension should, as a rule, result in longer error-interruption latencies than self-comprehension based on anticipatory information. Speakers can likely detect all error types through post-articulatory self-comprehension, but this mechanism should generally not support the very rapid error detection and interruption enabled by the anticipatory routes. In fact, given the uncertain and incomplete nature of anticipatory speech processing via MSPC and coarticulation, many errors, regardless of error type, should be detected through post-articulatory self-comprehension. At the same time, there is likely to be a difference in interruption latency between appropriateness and production-based errors.

The problems triggering appropriateness repairs should be considerably easier to detect through post-articulatory than anticipatory self-comprehension. Production-based speech errors, by contrast, will often-but, as we have seen, not always-provide a clearer contrast with MSPC-based anticipation at the phonological and lexical levels and should therefore be more likely than appropriateness errors to be detected rapidly through anticipatory selfcomprehension. Additionally, appropriateness errors should show greater variability in interruption latency, both because they will primarily be detected by contextualizing the utterance through post-articulatory self-comprehension and because the locus of the problem will often be less clearly defined than for production-based errors.

These predictions are consistent with the available data on interruption timing. Levelt (1983) reported that a majority of both production-based repairs (308 of 399, 77%) and appropriateness repairs (270 of 290, 93%) were interrupted at some point after the troublesome word. Conversely, those figures also reveal that the proportion of quick interruptions within the troublesome word (as opposed to after it) was greater during production-based repairs (91 of 399, 23%) than during appropriateness repairs (20 of 290, 7%). Appropriateness repairs also tended to be interrupted later relative to the troublesome word: 53% were interrupted immediately after the troublesome word and 40% only after intervening speech had been produced, compared with 48% and 29%, respectively, of production-based repairs. Blackmer and Mitton (1991), who reported millisecond error-tocutoff intervals, likewise found considerably shorter mean intervals for production-based repairs (426 ms, SD = 300 ms) than for their two categories of appropriateness repairs (919 ms, SD = 691 ms, and 601 ms, SD = 367 ms). 12 The larger standard deviations for the appropriateness repairs are also consistent with greater variability in their interruption latencies compared with production-based repairs.

Error Detection Under Noise-Masked Auditory Feedback

Speakers can detect errors even when auditory feedback is masked by white noise (Lackner & Tuller, 1979; Nooteboom & Quené, 2017; Oomen et al., 2001; Postma & Kolk, 1992; Postma & Noordanus, 1996). This finding-together with the arguments that external feedback channels are too slow to account for rapid online error interruption that we have reviewed above-has played a central role in motivating proposals for some form of internal monitoring channel (e.g., Gauvin et al., 2016; Hartsuiker, 2014; Hartsuiker & Kolk, 2001; Hartsuiker et al., 2005; Hickok, 2014; Huettig & Hartsuiker, 2010; Levelt, 1989 Levelt, , 1992;; Oomen & Postma, 2001; Postma, 2000; Roelofs, 2020; Slevc & Ferreira, 2006). In this section, we detail Lackner and Tuller's (1979) data, including Levelt's (1989) influential interpretation of these findings, and show how they can be accounted for within the self-comprehension model without requiring a separate internal, pre-articulatory monitoring channel. Lackner and Tuller (1979) asked participants to repeat syllable sequences such as pi-di-ti-gi, ae-i-o-u, or pi-ae-ti-o, one sequence per second for 30 seconds each in two conditions: with normal auditory feedback and with auditory feedback masked by white noise. Participants were instructed to press a button whenever they noticed that they made a speech error. For consonant-vowel syllables, Lackner and Tuller (1979) found that when auditory feedback was masked, participants were somewhat less accurate in detecting place-of-articulation errors (e.g., saying [ti] instead of [pi]), but substantially worse at detecting voicing errors (e.g., saying [bi] instead of [pi]) than when auditory feedback was available. For vowel syllables, noise masking substantially impaired detection of vowel-tovowel errors, whereas detection of vowel-to-consonant errors was much less affected.

Notably, however, this reduction in vowel-to-vowel error detection was concentrated primarily among errors involving small articulatory differences. Vowel errors involving large articulatory differences were generally detected even under auditory masking. Importantly, Lackner and Tuller (1979) also found that a slightly larger proportion of error-offset-to-button press latencies fell within the 0-50 ms range under auditory masking (2.3%) than with normal auditory feedback (0.4%). 14Lackner and Tuller (1979) proposed that speech errors can be detected by comparing corollary-discharge representations of intended speech with corollary-discharge signals from motor programming, and with auditory and proprioceptive feedback. They argued that voicing errors during CV syllables and vowel errors involving small articulatory differences are detected primarily through auditory feedback, whereas under noise masking, detection of place-of-articulation errors for CV syllables and of vowel errors involving larger articulatory differences can draw more strongly on proprioceptive information. They further proposed that the rapid detections during both normal and noise-masked speech indicate additional monitoring via corollary-discharge information (as we saw above, Lackner & Tuller, 1979, assumed that overt use of somatosensory and auditory feedback requires a latency of 300-400 ms). Levelt (1989) agreed that auditory feedback contributes to the detection of voicing errors and small articulatory vowel errors, but interpreted the rapid detections as evidence for the internal monitoring channel of his perceptual-loop theory. He further argued that, under noise masking, the slower external loop is suspended, leaving detection to the internal loop.

In the self-comprehension model, we suggest that somatosensory feedback-not pre-articulatory monitoring of a phonetic (Levelt, 1989) or phonological (Wheeldon & Levelt, 1995) code-is relied upon during noise masking (for a related proposal concerning somatosensory and proprioceptive feedback, see also Nooteboom & Quené, 2017). As discussed above, anticipatory coarticulation can provide somatosensory information about upcoming speech (Floegel et al., 2023; Kasper et al., 2026). Crucially, rather than proposing that this feedback is compared with a representation of the intended utterance, we suggest that divergence between somatosensory feedback and MSPC-based anticipation can provide a cue that the unfolding utterance is problematic. Errors may also be detected through post-articulatory self-comprehension based on somatosensory feedback in the absence of sufficiently informative prior anticipation. However, contrary to Lackner and Tuller's (1979) proposal, the self-comprehension model suggests that the quicker detections may be based on anticipatory coarticulatory information, with both auditory and somatosensory feedback contributing during normal speech, and somatosensory feedback becoming particularly important under noise masking. Thus, whereas Postma and Kolk (1992) noted that acoustic coarticulatory cues would be strongly, and perhaps completely, diminished by noise masking, we extend the possibility they raised for verbal self-monitoring to somatosensory coarticulatory information, which remains available when auditory feedback is masked. We suggest that the distribution of button-press latencies can be understood in the same way as the broader distribution reviewed above (Blackmer & Mitton, 1991; Hartsuiker & Kolk, 2001; Levelt, 1983): the most rapid detections may rely on anticipatory coarticulatory information, whereas progressively longer latencies may reflect increasing reliance on post-articulatory feedback. Levelt (1989 Levelt ( , 1992) ) interpreted the higher proportion of button presses in the earliest time interval (0-50 ms) during noise-masked speech as evidence that detection relied on the internal channel of the perceptual-loop theory. He argued that masking suspended the external auditory channel, which he assumed to be slower, and thereby led to faster responses (see also Roelofs, 2020). However, two issues complicate this interpretation.

First, the proportion of button presses within 50 ms was very low (0.4% with normal feedback and 2.3% with noise masking). Second, and critically, error-to-button-press latency in Lackner and Tuller's (1979) first experiment was measured from error offset rather than error onset (the error-to-cutoff times reported by Blackmer & Mitton, 1991, and Hartsuiker & Kolk, 2001, were measured from error onset). Consequently, these offset-based latencies provide no direct estimate of how long after error onset the button press occurred and therefore cannot establish that the responses in the 0-50 ms interval reflected exceptionally rapid error detection. Moreover, relating these offset-based latencies to error onset requires knowing how long the erroneous production lasted, and we do not know how much of each error was spoken. Although the instructions were to produce each four-syllable sequence over one second, it is difficult to keep a precise rhythm, meaning that individual syllable durations were unlikely to be fixed at exactly 250 ms. Furthermore, some erroneous syllables may have been interrupted before completion. In a second experiment, described in detail below, Lackner and Tuller (1979) measured error-to-button press latencies from both error onset and offset; for self-produced errors, the mean difference between these measures was 186 ms. Using this as an approximate guide, a button press occurring 0-50 ms after error offset in the first experiment would correspond to roughly 186-236 ms after error onset. Taken together, these considerations mean that the higher proportion of button presses occurring 0-50 ms after error offset under noise masking does not establish exceptionally rapid error detection under masking, nor does it establish that errors were detected more rapidly under masking than with normal auditory feedback. Lackner and Tuller's (1979) second experiment compared button press reaction times for errors in the same types of sequences as in the first experiment between speech perception and speech production with noise masking. Mean button-press latencies were around 150 ms shorter during speech production than speech perception, whether measured from error onset or offset. 15 Lackner and Tuller (1979) suggested, as we have seen, that monitoring of self-produced speech under auditory masking was based on proprioception and corollary discharge. Levelt (1989), on the other hand, took this to mean that, because auditory feedback was masked, self-produced errors were detected more rapidly through the internal loop of the perceptual-loop theory. However, a methodological issue complicates interpretation of the data. To create the speech-perception stimuli, Lackner and Tuller (1979) spliced recordings of syllables into the same sequences as in the production task, with errors created by occasionally splicing in an incorrect syllable (e.g., /ti/ instead of /pi/). This means that the perception stimuli lacked the naturally produced anticipatory information extending from the preceding speech into the erroneous syllable that could have been present in naturalistic continuous speech. In the production task, by contrast, anticipatory coarticulatory information was potentially available through somatosensory feedback. We therefore suggest that the observed reaction-time difference cannot be taken as a straightforward estimate of the difference between error detection during natural speech perception and production. 16 In summary, the self-comprehension model provides an account of error detection under noise-masked auditory feedback that does not require a separate pre-articulatory monitoring channel.

The Special Status of Self-Comprehension Distinguishes It from Other-Comprehension

If self-monitoring does not rely on privileged access to internal representations specifying upcoming speech-and if speakers do not, for example, have access to an internal monitoring channel (e.g., Levelt, 1989), to predictions based on the intention (e.g., Pickering & Garrod, 2013), or to conflict in the production system (Nozari et al., 2011)-does this mean that self-comprehension is essentially identical to other-comprehension? We suggest that this is not so, and that self-comprehension is at least partly distinct from othercomprehension, for at least five reasons. First, during self-comprehension, but not during other-comprehension, the speaker has access to speech-production-related motor information and somatosensory feedback. Thus, before listeners receive the first acoustic information from an utterance, the speaker already differs from the listener both in the production-related motor and somatosensory information available and in the state of the auditory system through which the ensuing speech signal will be processed. We hypothesize that these pre-acoustic production-related states can contribute to the distinctive processing conditions of selfcomprehension.

Fourth, the contextual information constraining comprehension may differ in richness and specificity depending on the speaker. Ryskin et al. (2020) demonstrated that listeners can use contextual knowledge about a specific speaker to constrain anticipation of that speaker's upcoming speech. Within an MSPC account, such talker-specific knowledge could contribute to differences in the precision of anticipations across speakers. In particular, the speaker has an exceptionally rich history of self-specific linguistic, conceptual, and contextual regularities that could constrain comprehension of their own speech. Consistent with the possibility that self-specific knowledge can facilitate anticipation, Hadley et al. (2020) found that listeners predicted turn endings more precisely when the speaker's speech style was more similar to their own. Thus, unlike the speaker-exclusive sensory information described above, talker-specific contextual information need not be unique to selfcomprehension, but it may nevertheless provide richer constraints when comprehending one's own speech.

Finally, the speaker, but not the listener, has experiential access to whatever inner experiences (e.g., Hurlburt & Heavey, 2006)-including inner speech (e.g., Hurlburt et al., 2013; Hurlburt & Heavey, 2018) and inner visual imagery (Heavey & Hurlburt, 2008)-might precede, coincide with, or follow overt speech (Linell, 2009, pp. 122-130; Linell, 2024). Such inner experiences should not, however, be equated with speech intentions or with conceptual representations specifying the content of an utterance. Neither should they be equated with the contents of an internal monitoring channel or with other internal representations of upcoming speech. They may nevertheless be related to overt speech in a variety of ways and could therefore contribute to the contextual state constraining MSPC during self-comprehension. At present, however, very little is known about these relationships. We consider these relationships further in Lind et al. Taken together, these differences suggest that self-comprehension operates on a richer and partly distinct set of sensory, motor, contextual, and experiential information than other-comprehension.

Cortical Attenuation During Speech: MSPC as an Alternative to Explicit Prediction Accounts

Several observations at the cortical level have been interpreted as evidence for predictive processing during verbal self-monitoring. First, neural responses, particularly in auditory cortex, are often reduced during speaking relative to passive playback of matched self-produced speech, a phenomenon commonly termed speaking-induced suppression (SIS; e.g., Chang et al., 2013; Curio et al., 2000; Gonzalez et al., 2024; Greenlee et al., 2011; Heinks-Maldonado et al., 2005 , 2006; Kort et al., 2014; Kurteff et al., 2023; Ozker et al., 2024; Ventura et al., 2009; see Whitford, 2019, for review). Importantly, SIS can be sensitive to the correspondence between produced and heard speech: Heinks-Maldonado et al. (2005) and Heinks-Maldonado et al. (2006), for example, found the strongest suppression for unaltered self-voice, with less suppression when feedback was pitch-shifted or replaced by another speaker's voice. Second, several studies have reported enhanced cortical responses to perturbed auditory feedback during speaking relative to passive playback of the same perturbed feedback, termed speech-perturbation response enhancement (SPRE; Behroozmand et al., 2009; Chang et al., 2013; Greenlee et al., 2013; Kort et al., 2014).

Other studies have reported enhanced neural responses to pitch or formant perturbations or delayed auditory feedback during speech production without a matched playback comparison (e.g., Ozker et al., 2022; Tourville et al., 2008), which are more precisely characterized as perturbation-related auditory-feedback responses rather than SPRE. SIS and perturbation-related enhancement appear to involve partly overlapping cortical populations (Chang et al., 2013; Kort et al., 2014; Ozker et al., 2024).

Within forward-model accounts, SIS is typically interpreted as attenuation associated with the expected sensory consequences of one's own actions, whereas enhanced responses to perturbed feedback are generally interpreted as reflecting processing triggered by discrepancies between expected and actual sensory consequences (e.g., Guenther & Hickok, 2015; Hickok, 2012; Houde & Chang, 2015; Pickering & Garrod, 2013). SIS and perturbation-related enhancement have consequently been taken as evidence for forward modeling and, more generally, predictive processing during speech. Predictive mechanisms have further been proposed to contribute to speakers' ability to source-monitor-that is, to distinguish self-produced from externally produced speech (e.g., Ford & Mathalon, 2005; Ford et al., 2007; Heinks-Maldonado et al., 2005; Heinks-Maldonado et al., 2007; Houde & Nagarajan, 2011; Houde et al., 2002)-and to the sense of agency over their own utterances (e.g., Ohata et al., 2022; Sugimori et al., 2011). Perturbation-related responses have similarly been interpreted as auditory error signals that contribute to corrective control of speech (e.g., Chang et al., 2013; Guenther & Hickok, 2015; Ozker et al., 2022). Consistent with a role for these responses in corrective control, SPRE, but not SIS, was correlated with compensatory responses to pitch-shifted auditory feedback in Chang et al. (2013), although Kort et al. (2014) did not find the same relationship. Thus, cortical attenuation and perturbation-related enhancement have been used to support a range of proposed functions of predictive processing in speech production and self-monitoring.

Importantly, however, action-related sensory attenuation is an effect rather than a mechanism and is therefore not uniquely diagnostic of predictive cancellation. Press et al. (2020) and Press et al. (2023)caution that many paradigms interpreted as evidence for predictive cancellation compare sensory processing during action with passive conditions, although action itself can produce more general sensory attenuation that is not specific to a predicted outcome. Sensory attenuation should therefore not, by itself, be treated as a proxy for prediction. Related concerns about inferring prediction from action-related auditory attenuation have been raised by Horváth (2015), who reviewed evidence that auditory attenuation can occur even in the absence of a contingent action-sound relationship. A related interpretive problem applies to both SIS and SPRE, which are defined by differences in auditory-cortical responses between speaking and passive-playback conditions. Such contrasts cannot by themselves identify which of the multiple differences between sensory processing during speaking and playback gives rise to the observed cortical effects. More specifically, the vocalization-related changes in middle-ear function demonstrated by Terband et al. (2024), including stronger reflex activation during self-vocalization than during listening, provide a concrete peripheral mechanism relevant to SIS. Because the middle-ear muscle reflex reduces low-frequency input to the cochlea, part of the cortical attenuation measured as SIS could arise from differences in auditory input already present at the auditory periphery. More generally, the neuroimaging evidence for cortical auditory-feedback error-monitoring mechanisms is itself inconclusive. In a systematic review and metaanalysis, Meekings and Scott (2021) concluded that the available fMRI and PET evidence was insufficient to establish that the posterior superior gyrus functions as an error monitor during speech production. Taken together, these considerations caution against treating cortical attenuation and perturbation-related responses as uniquely diagnostic of the predictive error-monitoring mechanisms proposed by forward models, while leaving open the possibility that at least some attenuation associated with speaking reflects peripheral and more general action-dependent modulation of sensory processing rather than predictive cancellation.

A similar interpretive issue arises in language comprehension, where reduced neural responses to expected input have often been taken as evidence for prediction. For example, semantically unexpected words, such as dog in I take coffee with cream and dog, elicit an enhanced N400 compared with expected completions such as sugar, during both reading (e.g., Kutas & Hillyard, 1980 , 1984) and listening (Friederici et al., 1993; Hagoort & Brown, 2000; Holcomb & Neville, 1991). Although such N400 differences are compatible with prediction, they are not uniquely diagnostic of it and may, for example, reflect differences in the ease with which encountered words are integrated with their preceding context (Pickering & Gambi, 2018). Luthra et al. (2021) examined the more general inference from reduced activation for expected input to predictive coding using the TRACE model (McClelland & Elman, 1986). As we have seen, TRACE does not explicitly represent predictions, but its interactive activation dynamics can produce anticipatory activation of phonemes that have not yet been encountered. Crucially, Luthra et al. (2021) found lower activity at both lexical and phoneme levels when the input was expected. In TRACE, unexpected input is compatible with more lexical competitors, producing greater aggregate activity and top-down feedback. Thus, TRACE produces reduced activation for expected input without explicitly generating a prediction and computing the discrepancy between that prediction and the incoming signal (see also Falandays et al., 2021; Ryskin & Nieuwland, 2023). Luthra et al.'s (2021) demonstration therefore provides a computational example of a broader point: reduced activation for expected input need not uniquely identify an explicit predictive computation (see also Aitchison & Lengyel, 2017). Together with Falandays et al.'s (2021) demonstration that anticipatory processing can emerge through multi-scale pattern completion without explicit representations of predictions or prediction errors, this raises the possibility that both anticipatory activation and reduced activation for expected input can emerge from the dynamics of an interactive pattern-completion system.

We propose that the same possibility should be considered for cortical attenuation during self-produced speech. When a speaker prepares to produce an utterance-or to continue an ongoing utterance-MSPC may constrain the processing of concurrent and forthcoming sensory information on the basis of the evolving linguistic and contextual state, with the additional sensory, motor, contextual, and other information available during selfcomprehension providing additional constraints. As discussed above, these constraints can include anticipatory auditory and somatosensory information generated by coarticulation, which may become informative about upcoming speech before the relevant segment itself is produced. We propose that, by analogy with the TRACE dynamics demonstrated by Luthra et al. (2021), auditory input that is consistent with an already constrained processing state may require less additional activation than input encountered in a less constrained state.

During speaking, auditory feedback is processed within the evolving, multimodally constrained state characteristic of self-comprehension, including MSPC-based constraints together with the production-related sensory, motor, and contextual information described above. During passive playback, MSPC can likewise constrain processing on the basis of the unfolding linguistic and communicative context, although the particular constraints available to MSPC need not be identical across speaking and playback. More importantly, the same acoustic signal is encountered without the same combination and temporal configuration of sensory, motor, contextual, and other constraints available during ongoing self-comprehension. The same acoustic input could therefore elicit less auditory-cortical activity during speaking than during playback, producing SIS without requiring predictive cancellation. This account is also compatible with the observation that suppression is sensitive to the correspondence between produced and heard speech (e.g., Heinks-Maldonado et al., 2005; Heinks-Maldonado et al., 2006): feedback that is less consistent with the evolving state should recruit more additional processing than feedback that is consistent with it. Crucially, this account does not require that a detailed auditory representation first be generated as an explicit prediction and subsequently compared with the incoming signal.

A similar, although more tentative, argument can be made for perturbation-related enhancement. Luthra et al. (2021) directly demonstrate an alternative explanation for attenuation rather than SPRE, and sensorimotor-gating accounts have likewise primarily targeted sensory attenuation rather than perturbation enhancement. Nevertheless, enhanced responses to unexpected input need not imply an explicitly computed prediction error: in TRACE, for example, unexpected input produces greater activation because it engages a larger set of competing representations, rather than because the system computes a discrepancy between a sensory prediction and its outcome. As proposed above, naturally occurring coarticulatory cues that diverge from MSPC-based anticipation may signal a developing speech error. Experimentally perturbed auditory feedback may similarly provide sensory information that is inconsistent with the evolving MSPC-and sensoryconstrained state. Such perturbations could therefore recruit additional processing, potentially contributing to the heightened cortical responses observed during speaking, without requiring comparison against an explicitly generated auditory prediction. Cortical attenuation and perturbation-related enhancement are therefore compatible with predictive processing but do not, by themselves, establish an explicit predictive mechanism.

General Discussion

The concept of self-comprehension refers to speakers using feedback from their own speech in conjunction with wider contextual information to understand the meaning and significance of their own speech without assuming access to well-defined pre-articulatory speech representations. We have proposed that verbal self-monitoring can be understood as a natural sub-function of self-comprehension, rather than as a dedicated process that evaluates the unfolding utterance against information derived from pre-articulatory representations. In our model, speakers determine what they are saying, and whether it is erroneous or otherwise problematic, through a cue-integrative process in which contextual, linguistic, motor, and sensory information jointly constrain the interpretation of their unfolding speech. Two sources of anticipatory information are particularly important: anticipation based on multi-scale pattern completion (MSPC; Falandays et al., 2021) and anticipation based on auditory and somatosensory information generated through coarticulation. When these sources of information support sufficiently divergent states, that divergence can provide a cue that the unfolding utterance is problematic, without requiring either an intended-utterance representation or a separate comparator mechanism. Depending on which information is available and when it becomes informative, error detection may therefore arise through interactions between MSPC and anticipatory coarticulatory information, between MSPC and post-articulatory sensory feedback, or exclusively through post-articulatory self-comprehension in the wider linguistic and communicative context. These routes should, on average, be associated with progressively longer but substantially overlapping error-interruption latencies, and could in principle accommodate the observed distribution of interruption latencies-including differences in the prevalence and interruption latencies of different error types-without requiring privileged monitoring access to internal production representations specifying upcoming speech. On this account, verbal selfmonitoring does not require a dedicated monitoring system with privileged access to speechproduction representations; instead, monitoring emerges as part of the speaker's ongoing comprehension of their own speech.

While we have presented MSPC and anticipatory coarticulatory information as intimately intertwined, their respective contributions to verbal self-comprehension could also be evaluated separately. MSPC provides a reconceptualization of anticipatory phenomena commonly interpreted as evidence for explicit prediction during speech comprehension (Falandays et al., 2021). The proposal has received increasing theoretical and empirical attention as part of a broader interest in whether anticipatory or prediction-like behavior requires explicitly predictive mechanisms (e.g., Drouin & Davis, 2024; Falandays et al., 2024; Huettig, 2025; Huettig et al., 2022; Mansell et al., 2025; Ryskin & Nieuwland, 2023).

The specific mechanisms proposed by Falandays et al. (2021) may ultimately prove incomplete or require revision, and the viability of MSPC relative to alternative accounts remains an empirical question. More generally, however, MSPC illustrates how anticipatory processing might emerge without explicit representations of future states or computations of prediction error. Importantly, the proposed contribution of anticipatory coarticulatory information to self-comprehension does not depend on MSPC, or indeed on any particular account of how context-based anticipation is implemented. Even if errors are in fact detected against clearly specified pre-articulatory information, speakers could nonetheless exploit coarticulation in unfolding sensory feedback to obtain anticipatory information about what is about to be uttered (Floegel et al., 2023; Kasper et al., 2026). As Kasper et al. (2026) argue in relation to speech control, the early availability of such anticipatory sensory information changes the temporal constraints traditionally attributed to sensory feedback. We extend this The present model concerns how erroneous or otherwise troublesome speech can be detected by the speaker; it does not presently attempt to explain how such speech arises during speech production. More generally, because the model does not posit a fixed prearticulatory standard against which an unfolding utterance is judged, it raises the question of what makes an utterance an 'error' in the first place. On the present account, error status emerges from the integration of sensory information about the unfolding utterance with the anticipatory, linguistic, and communicative constraints available during self-comprehension.

On this view, an error need not be defined solely by deviation from a single internally represented intended utterance. What counts as problematic can depend on multiple constraints, including linguistic well-formedness, the developing interpretation of the utterance, the communicative context, and the responses of an interlocutor. A fuller account of how these constraints determine error status lies beyond the present model, which concerns primarily how cues that the unfolding utterance is problematic can give rise to error detection.

The present account lends itself to several direct empirical tests. We give three examples. First, a sufficiently large corpus of spontaneous speech errors recorded with highfidelity audio could test whether the prominence, onset, and temporal extent of anticipatory acoustic coarticulation predict error interruption latency, particularly in relation to contextual constraint. Second, using RSE (Lind et al., 2014a (Lind et al., , 2014b)), the contribution of within-word anticipatory coarticulatory information in real-time auditory feedback could be manipulated through cross-splicing (Beddor et al., 2013; Dahan et al., 2001). In a task where participants are instructed to interrupt utterances when the feedback relays a different word from the one they are producing, would they interrupt an utterance of, for example, best while hearing bend more rapidly when the feedback contains the natural anticipatory coarticulatory cues of bend than when its initial vowel material is cross-spliced from best? Finally, the contribution of somatosensory feedback-including anticipatory coarticulatory information-could be tested by extending Lackner and Tuller's (1979) auditory-masking paradigm with conditions in which oral somatosensation is disrupted through local anesthesia (see, e.g., De Letter et al., 2020).

In summary, the self-comprehension model shifts the explanatory problem of verbal self-monitoring. Rather than asking how speakers compare what they say with a prearticulatory representation specifying what they intended to say, it asks how speakers determine what they are saying-and whether it is problematic-from the evolving information available during speaking. On this view, monitoring is not a separate inspection of speech production, but an emergent consequence of making sense of one's own unfolding speech.

Second, the experience of one's own voice has a special status for several reasons. Orepic and Pinheiro (2026) argue that the self-voice is exceptionally familiar and selfrelevant, that it serves as a primary means of communication and self-expression, and that it carries information about physical characteristics, identity, and personality (see also Van Lancker Sidtis & Zäske, 2021). Neuroimaging, psychophysiological, and behavioral studies reviewed by Orepic and Pinheiro (2026) further indicate that self-voice is processed differently from other voices (see, e.g., Conde et al., 2015; Graux et al., 2013; Graux et al., 2015; Kaplan et al., 2008; Kirk & Cunningham, 2025; Nakamura et al., 2001). More importantly for self-comprehension, the sensory signal available during speaking differs from that available when listening to another speaker. Whereas listeners receive an interlocutor's voice primarily through air conduction, speakers perceive their own voice-beyond somatosensory feedback-through a combination of air-and bone/soft tissue conduction together with associated vibrotactile information (e.g., Orepic et al., 2023; Orepic & Pinheiro, 2026; Reinfeldt et al., 2010; Reinfeldt et al., 2013; Shuster & Durrant, 2003). The auditory signal available during self-produced speech is further modulated by activation of the middleear muscles during vocalization, which alters transmission to the cochlea and attenuates particularly low-frequency components of self-generated sound (Schneider et al., 2018; Terband et al., 2024; Ugarteburu et al., 2022). Thus, even at relatively peripheral levels of sensory processing, the information available for comprehending one's own ongoing speech differs from that available for comprehending another person's speech.

Third, speech-production-related information becomes available to the speaker before the onset of audible speech. Articulatory, respiratory, and laryngeal activity can begin tens or even hundreds of milliseconds before acoustic onset (e.g., Cavallo & Baken, 1985; Jouen et al., 2021; Kawamoto et al., 2008; McFarland, 2001; McFarland & Smith, 1992; Tilsen et al., 2016), and speech-related readiness-potential and motor-preparation activity likewise precedes speech onset (e.g., Brumberg et al., 2015; Wohlert, 1993). Moreover, production-related changes can occur in the auditory system itself before acoustic onset. Terband et al. (2024) found that the middle-ear muscle reflex was activated in anticipation of temporal argument to verbal self-comprehension and self-monitoring: anticipatory coarticulatory information could allow sensory-feedback-based monitoring to account for interruption latencies that have previously been assumed to require an internal monitoring mechanism.

The present proposal also raises further questions about the temporal dynamics of self-comprehension. Schreiber and McMurray (2019) found evidence that coarticulatory information in a fricative can sometimes influence processing of an upcoming vowel before disambiguating information about the fricative itself has fully unfolded in the acoustic signal.

More broadly, Muegge et al. (2026) reviewed evidence suggesting that the dynamics of speech processing may be more flexibly related to the temporal structure of the acoustic input than traditionally assumed. Applied to self-comprehension, this raises the possibility that MSPC and coarticulatory information are not temporally isolated sources of anticipation.

Rather, sensory information from the unfolding utterance may contribute to the evolving context within which subsequent pattern completion occurs, while MSPC-based anticipation may simultaneously constrain the processing of that sensory information.

Although we have focused on coarticulation, other sources of anticipatory sensory information-particularly in the acoustic modality-could similarly constrain processing of upcoming speech and potentially contribute to the detection of errors and other problems during self-comprehension. For example, various prosodic cues have been demonstrated to support forward anticipation during other-comprehension, including prosodic structure (e.g., Nakamura et al., 2012; Weber et al., 2006b), contrastive pitch accent (e.g., Ito & Speer, 2008; Ito et al., 2012; Weber et al., 2006a), and Swedish word accents (Kwon & Roll, 2024).

Voice-onset time may provide another source of anticipatory information by constraining processing of later-arriving acoustic-phonetic information (McMurray et al., 2008a (McMurray et al., , 2008b)).

Coarticulation should therefore be understood as one particularly well-documented source of anticipatory sensory information rather than as the only possible sensory route through which the unfolding utterance can constrain self-comprehension in advance of later speech events.
