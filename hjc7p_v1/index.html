Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Why do we keep touching ourselves? Here, I propose the hypothesis that recurrent selfgenerated sensations may contribute to maintaining embodied self-experience by providing reliable evidence about the body as a source of action and sensation. Self-touch generates predictable sensory consequences of one's own actions, thereby potentially reinforcing bodily agency, ownership, self-location, and self-other-distinction. Within this account, sensory attenuation follows from successful prediction. Social touch extends this process beyond the individual, as sensory and affective signals are regulated between interacting bodies, highlighting the relational character of self-other-differentiation. The framework predicts that destabilizing bodily self-experience should increase self-evidence-generating behaviors, that reliable selfgenerated feedback should accelerate recovery, and that disrupting action-sensory contingency should abolish this benefit. Autism and schizophrenia illustrate how altered sensory and social conditions may interact with such self-maintenance processes.

Why do we touch ourselves?

During the covid pandemic, we humans became suddenly aware of the frequency at which we touch our own face. In fact, a study found it was around 50 times per hour (Rahman et al., 2020).

But as an energy-preserving species, why do we keep touching ourselves?

For some touches, it is easy to find a reason: we rub a painful spot, scratch an itch, or remove hair from the face (Zhang et al., 2020). However, most of our self-directed touches go unnoticed to our own conscious awareness. Self-directed actions are not confined to grooming or responding to irritation: self-touch is frequent in everyday life, begins early in development (Castiello et al., 2010; DiMercurio et al., 2018), and occurs in both solitary and social situations.

Infants use self-touch in self-soothing (Moszkowski & Stack, 2007), and also increase it in response to positive social stimulation (Filippa et al., 2020). In adults, facial self-touch becomes more frequent during stress, nervousness, and cognitive demand (Grunwald et al., 2014; Mueller et al., 2019; Spille et al., 2021). These observations raise a question that is easily overlooked when self-touch is treated primarily as a sensory consequence to be filtered out: what does touching oneself accomplish for the organism?

Self-touch encompasses behaviors with different, potentially overlapping functions. Rubbing, stroking, squeezing, and scratching should not be assumed to have the same sensory or regulatory effects. Alongside pain modulation and itch relief (Kammers et al., 2010), proposed functions include regulating arousal, directing attention towards the body, and supporting emotional and cognitive regulation (Dreisoerner et al., 2021). During a working-memory task, spontaneous facial self-touch occurred particularly during distraction, suggesting a possible role in refocusing attention (Mueller et al., 2019). Self-touch in social interactions may additionally serve communicative functions (Harrigan, 1985; Streeck, 2020). Its significance therefore depends on the form of contact and the context in which it occurs; neither its frequency nor its association with stress establishes a single underlying mechanism.

In experimental tasks, self-generated tactile stimuli are commonly perceived as less intense or less ticklish than externally generated stimuli (Sarah-Jayne Blakemore et al., 2000; Kilteni et al., 2020).

The classical explanation invokes an efference copy of the motor command, which allows the sensory consequences of an action to be predicted and attenuated (Von Helmholtz, 1867).

Human studies implicate interactions between the cerebellum and somatosensory cortex, with the supplementary motor area proposed as a source of the efferent signal (Kilteni et al., 2020). This attenuation is not fixed: introducing a delay between action and touch can reduce it, whereas repeated exposure can produce adaptation to the delay (Fritz & Zimmermann, 2023; Kilteni & Ehrsson, 2024). Illusory self-touch produces comparable attenuation (Pyasik et al., 2021), as does the self-touch of an embodied prosthesis (Fritsch et al., 2021). These findings indicate that the processing of self-generated touch depends on learned relations between action and sensation.

The efference copy hypothesis implies that only the predicted sensation should be attenuated.

However, in rats, self-grooming was associated with widespread inhibition of somatosensory cortex and reduced responses to simultaneous external touch (Ishiyama et al., 2019); similarly in humans, self-touch leads to widespread cortical deactivations in functional imaging and can also reduce detection of additional tactile stimuli (Boehme et al., 2019). In illusory self-touch, some deviations from a "perfect" prediction seem to be accepted by the nervous system (White et al., 2010) and that illusory alterations of body ownership or perturbation of trajectories do not always affect attenuation (Van Doorn et al., 2014; Van Doorn et al., 2015). At odds with this is recent evidence showing spatially specific attenuation (Knoetsch & Zimmermann, 2021). One possible explanation for these somewhat contradictory findings is that tolerance of sensory discrepancies could depend on the current estimated of certainty of sensory input and of internal model priors (Chancel & Ehrsson, 2023; D'Angelo et al., 2026). In how far this accounts for differences between self-touch paradigms remains to be tested.

In any case, attenuation cannot always mean that self-touch disappears from experience or loses its behavioral relevance (Boehme & Olausson, 2022). Sexual self-touch provides a particularly clear illustration of the distinction between sensory attenuation and functional insignificance: genital self-stimulation evokes identifiable somatosensory responses and can culminate in orgasm involving sensory and reward-related networks (Komisaruk et al., 2011; Wise et al., 2017). This article will focus on non-sexual self-touch behaviors. We recently showed that the positive qualitative evaluations of such self-touches, i.e. their pleasantness, softness, and smoothness, depend on complex interactions between agency and predictability (Enmalm et al., 2026), supporting the idea that self-touch, while attenuated, is still perceived as a meaningful stimulus.

Here, I propose that one function of recurrent self-touch is to help maintain a coherent bodily self-experience. We previously suggested that self-touch may provide evidence for updating internal models of the body, potentially reducing uncertainty despite sensory attenuation (Boehme & Olausson, 2022). Building on this possibility, I hypothesize that predictable, controllable relations between action and sensation can provide ongoing evidence of the body as both a source of action and a locus of sensation. I refer to actions serving this proposed function as self-confirming behaviors. This does not imply that all self-touch serves self-maintenance, or that its effects can be reduced to a single process. It means that self-touch offers a potential window into how bodily self-experience is actively sustained (Figure 1).

What is bodily self-experience?

Contemporary cognitive science increasingly rejects the idea that selfhood arises from isolated brain activity. Instead, recent work shows that self-experience depends on the continuous interplay of perception, emotion, bodily regulation, social interaction, and environmental engagement (Fotopoulou & Tsakiris, 2017; Seth & Tsakiris, 2018). Yet despite this convergence on embodiment and relationality, a central conceptual tension remains: Is the self best understood as a model, a process, a pattern, or an illusion? In this article, I will use the working hypothesis of bodily self-experience as an actively maintained organization continuously (re-)generated through processes that establish, confirm, and stabilize a distinction between self and other across neural, bodily, environmental, and social systems. Importantly, this view implies that the self is relational, i.e. the organization of the self depends on its ongoing differentiation from not-self.

A water whirl provides an intuitive analogy for the process-oriented assumption underlying this hypothesis: the whirlpool as a recognizable pattern can persist even as the water constituting it changes. Similarly, continuity of bodily self-experience may be supported by ongoing activity rather than by an unchanging bodily state. The analogy is intended only to illustrate stability through change; it does not establish a mechanism of self-touch or require a particular position on the ontological status of the self.

The central arising question is how recurrent self-touch may contribute to maintaining self-other distinction (Figure 1). By generating predictable sensory consequences, such actions may provide repeated evidence of the body as a distinct locus of sensation and source of action. This gives the process-oriented perspective a concrete behavioral focus: the contribution of self-touch to the continuity of bodily self-experience.

Self-touch as a candidate mechanism of bodily self-maintenance

Self-touch may help sustain the experience of a coherent bodily self. I use bodily selfmaintenance to describe the processes through which experience of the body as one's own, and of oneself as a source of action, remains coherent over time. From this working perspective, bodily self-experience is not simply established once and then retained unchanged; its continuity may depend in part on recurrent interactions between action, bodily signals, and sensory feedback. During self-touch, we produce the movement and feel the resulting sensation simultaneously, allowing the relationship between action and bodily percept to be examined directly.

Bodily ownership, agency, and self-other distinction refer to different aspects of bodily selfexperience: experiencing the body as one's own, experiencing oneself as initiating an action, and distinguishing self-generated from externally generated events. Self-touch brings these aspects together as we move a part of our own body, produce contact, and feel its consequences on the body. The proposed self-confirming function may involve all three, although a change in one does not necessarily imply a corresponding change in the others. Experimental tests should therefore specify which aspect is being assessed.

My central hypothesis is that self-touch can contribute to this continuity by generating predictable and controllable relations between movement and sensation. The correspondence between an action and its tactile and proprioceptive consequences may provide evidence that the acting and sensing body belong to the same organism. In this view, attenuation follows from the match between predicted and actual sensory consequences: the sensation is attenuated because it confirms the expected outcome of one's own action. This proposed contribution to bodily selfexperience is compatible with other functions of self-touch, including attentional and affective regulation -it does not have to explain every instance of the behavior.

What does self-touch add to movement alone? Movement provides ongoing proprioceptive feedback, while self-touch also couples the act of touching with the sensation of being touched at the corresponding place and time (Schutz-Bosbach et al., 2009). The contact produced by one part of the body is simultaneously received by another, providing a close correspondence between action and tactile feedback from both sides of the contact. This additional correspondence may strengthen confirmation of the body as both acting and sensing. Our findings show that brain activity during self-touch differs from that during touching an object (Boehme et al., 2019) and from touching while being touched (Enmalm et al., 2025), suggesting that its processing depends on more than the presence of movement and tactile input alone.

For self-touch, the relevant candidate mechanism is the recurrent action-feedback cycle: movement generates tactile and proprioceptive consequences, these are related to expectations about the action, and subsequent behavior can adjust the sensory feedback available or the internal predictive model. The empirical question is whether this cycle helps sustain or restore bodily self-experience, beyond any effects on arousal or attention. The hypothesis therefore directs attention to whether the predictability and controllability of self-touch matter for its effects on the bodily sense of self, particularly when bodily self-experience has been perturbed.

Neurocognitive mechanism of bodily self experience

Using neuroimaging paradigms involving self-referential judgments, autobiographical reflection, and self-related stimuli, foundational studies identified cortical midline structures -including medial prefrontal cortex, anterior and posterior cingulate -that are consistently engaged during self-related processing (Northoff et al., 2006; Qin et al., 2020; Shin et al., 2025). From the present perspective, these regions should not be interpreted as the neural location of the self, but as components of a distributed system that differentiates self-and non-self-related information (Açıl et al., 2026; Zapparoli et al., 2026).

Research on embodiment challenges the notion that the experience of a coherent sense of self can be understood solely in terms of brain-based self-referential cognition. Experimental paradigms such as the rubber hand illusion, full-body ownership illusions, and manipulations of agency demonstrated that bodily self-experience can be experimentally altered through manipulating multisensory integration (Blanke et al., 2015; Ehrsson et al., 2005). These findings are mechanistically important because they show: When multisensory coupling is experimentally reorganized, the boundaries of the bodily self shift, indicating that the felt body is continuously maintained through sensorimotor integration and calibration. Similarly, the discovery of a peripersonal space and its malleability show that the direct environment impacts the bodily self and its boundaries (Bogdanova et al., 2021; Grivaz et al., 2017). Furthermore, interoception, the perception of internal bodily states, is increasingly recognized as a core component of selfhood. Interoceptive signals and affective aspects of touch engage the insula and related viscerosensory networks, linking bodily state to ongoing perceptual and regulatory processes (Craig, 2003; Damasio, 2003; McGlone et al., 2014). Allostatic accounts propose that the brain continuously predicts bodily demands and regulates physiological states accordingly (Theriault et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). In this framework, the organism maintains itself by continuously predicting, detecting, and correcting deviations from viable bodily states.

Taken this one step further, Tallon-Baudry suggests that interoceptive signals do not merely inform the organism about bodily states, but provide an ongoing coordination mechanism that temporally binds distributed neural, sensory, and visceral processes into a coherent organismlevel organization (Tallon-Baudry et al., 2026). Interoception may also contribute to determining whether external sensory events are relevant to the self. Recent work shows that cardiac-related neural activity can facilitate the encoding of the self-relevance of external stimuli (Loescher et al., 2025). This suggests that bodily signals do not merely provide background information about internal state, but they can help organize incoming sensory information according to its relation to the organism, thereby helping regulate the experiential boundary between the own body and the external world. At the same time, touch, both from others and from oneself, has been shown repeatedly to impact autonomic processes, e.g. heartrate variability (Püschel et al., 2022; Triscoli et al., 2017), sympathetic-parasympathetic balance (Candia-Rivera, Boehme, et al., 2024; Candia-Rivera, de Vico Fallani, et al., 2024), and stress regulation (Ditzen et al., 2007; Dreisoerner et al., 2021; Eckstein et al., 2020).

Importantly, regulation is not exclusively individual. Increasing evidence suggests that bodily regulation is fundamentally social, that it occurs in the form of co-regulation (Atzil & Barrett, 2017; Bastos et al., 2025; Fotopoulou & Tsakiris, 2017). Human infants rely on caregivers for affective and physiological co-regulation, often mediated through touch (Ciaunica & Crucianelli, 2019). Social interactions continue to influence autonomic regulation throughout adulthood (Gründahl et al., 2023; Riečanský et al., 2020; Sevi et al., 2022), contributing directly to allostasis.

From this perspective, social relationships become part of the regulatory architecture through which a bodily self-experience is maintained.

Accordingly, self-other distinction in a social context emerges from ongoing processes of mutual prediction, coordination, and differentiation. Interindividual interactions continuously calibrate the boundaries through which a self remains distinct while remaining open to exchange. This is supported by neuroscientific studies on interpersonal neural synchrony (Mayo & Shamay-Tsoory, 2024) and mutual neural predictions of interaction partners in humans (Wang et al., 2025) and in animals (Kingsbury et al., 2019). Intersubjectivity is therefore not added onto an already established self but participates in its constitution and maintenance.

Taken together, these findings situate bodily self-experience within ongoing interactions between sensory processing, bodily regulation, and social exchange. Self-touch brings several of these processes together in a single action: we generate a movement, predict its sensory consequences, and feel the resulting contact on our own body. The correspondence between prediction and sensation may repeatedly confirm the body as both the source and the recipient of an actionand thereby also differentiate it from the non-self, specifically from tactile sensation arising from touching the surrounding world and social touch from others. This raises the possibility that everyday self-touch contributes to the continuity of bodily self-experience, linking its familiar regulatory functions to the maintenance of self-other distinction.

Touching the body to experience self-other-differences

As discussed, contemporary accounts of selfhood emphasize the role of interoception and predictive processing. However, comparatively less attention has been paid to the unique role of touch in the constitution and maintenance of the experience of the bodily self. This omission is surprising, as touch occupies a privileged position at the boundary between self and world (Boehme, 2025b). Unlike vision or audition, touch is inherently reciprocal: to touch is always to simultaneously be touched (Boehme, 2025b; Legrand, 2006). As such, tactile experience directly engages the distinction between self and non-self while at the same time revealing their continuous interaction (Boehme, 2025b; Boehme et al., 2019; Boehme & Olausson, 2022).

Therefore, touch is not merely a sensory contribution to the experience of the body. It is one of the key constitutive mechanisms through which a coherent bodily self-experience is maintained.

The importance of touch becomes apparent when considering the development of the bodily self representation (Cascio, 2010). From the earliest stages of life, tactile interactions with caregivers contribute to physiological regulation, affective development, and the establishment of bodily boundaries (Ciaunica & Crucianelli, 2019; Croy et al., 2022). The human experience of their sense of self does not emerge in isolation but develops through repeated cycles of bodily interaction and co-regulation, first during pregnancy only with the body of the mother, later also with other bodies of caregivers (Ciaunica, Constant, et al., 2021; Ciaunica & Crucianelli, 2019).

Already in the womb, the developing human performs self-touch and touches the surroundings -the wall of the womb, the non-self (Castiello et al., 2010). The tactile sense therefore provides one of the earliest mechanisms through which a distinction between self and other can emerge (Castiello et al., 2010; Myowa-Yamakoshi & Takeshita, 2006).

At the neurophysiological level, affective touch pathways, particularly the CT-afferent system (McGlone et al., 2012), have been implicated in bodily awareness (Craig, 2002; Jenkinson et al., 2020), emotional regulation (Della Longa et al., 2025), and social bonding (Huzard et al., 2022; Krahé et al., 2018). Importantly, these effects extend beyond the transmission of sensory information. Social touch modulates autonomic activity (Mazza et al., 2023), reduces stress responses (Ditzen et al., 2007; Dreisoerner et al., 2021), and promotes physiological and neural synchrony between individuals (Nguyen et al., 2021; Reddan et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2024).

Through tactile co-regulation, bodily states become partially coordinated across individuals (Chatel-Goldman et al., 2014; Debrot et al., 2013). This way, the social other becomes part of the maintenance of the organism's internal experience and homeostasis (Fotopoulou et al., 2022; Püschel et al., 2022).

A very clear example of affective touch contributing to bodily self-experience is the study of stroke patients with a disturbed sense of limb ownership who experienced increased embodiment after affective touch to this body part (Jenkinson et al., 2020). Interestingly in this case, only affective other-touch showed this effect. However, it has to be considered that the patients suffered from sensory alterations in the affected limb, which might evoke prediction errors in the close action-prediction-loop of self-touch -therefore this finding can be interpreted as support for the here suggested mechanism, i.e. in this clinical case, the self-touch did not evoke the predicted sensations and therefore did not strengthen ownership.

From the perspective developed here, touch is significant not only because it provides passive or circumstantial information about self-other-differentiation (Boehme et al., 2019), but especially because it allows the individual to actively constitute, probe, and confirm the self (Boehme, 2025b). Humans continuously engage in self-touch throughout the lifespan (Spille et al., 2021).

Perhaps the general tendency to seek predictable or confirmatory states (Kaanders et al., 2022) can be understood as one way organisms stabilize existing organization -and, at the bodily level, this includes the organization underlying self-other-distinction. Self-touch behaviors increase during states of uncertainty, stress, or cognitive demand (Grunwald et al., 2014; Mueller et al., 2019). Within the present hypothesis, these situations may increase the value of reliable bodily feedback, motivating the proposal that self-touch helps maintain bodily self-experience when self-other boundaries become less certain (Boehme, 2025a (Boehme, , 2025b)). Whether this contribution extends beyond sensory and affective regulation remains unknown.

In line with this, experimental evidence shows that self-touch restores proprioceptive spatial awareness (Cataldo et al., 2024) and enhances body ownership (Hara et al., 2015; Jenkinson et al., 2020; Lesur et al., 2021; Van Stralen et al., 2011). Recent work demonstrated that even unusual forms of experimental self-touch, such as voluntary eye movements generating tactile sensations, can generate body-ownership effects, suggesting that the critical factor may be the binding of voluntary action to its sensory consequences rather than just movement, proprioception, or somatosensation per se (Cataldo et al., 2023). Studies of illusory self-touch also reveal constraints on how tactile correspondence contributes to bodily self-experience: The illusion of touching one's left elbow arose faster and was rated more strongly when touch was administered with the anatomically plausible right index finger rather than the implausible left index finger (White & Davies, 2011). Anatomical plausibility is not, however, the only relevant constraint. Crossing the hands strengthened subjective illusory self-touch during synchronous stimulation without increasing proprioceptive drift (Pozeg et al., 2014), while large angular misalignment could diminish the illusion even when the prosthetic hand remained anatomically plausible (White et al., 2015). Together, these findings suggest that the experience of self-touch depends on the integration of tactile and proprioceptive information within spatial and anatomical constraints, rather than on sensory synchrony alone.

Autism and psychosis: altered conditions of self-maintenance

Autistic people show substantial heterogeneity in sensory processing, interoception, and social experience (Bolis et al., 2018; Cascio, 2010; Garfinkel et al., 2016; Kimber et al., 2024), indicating potential differences in how autistic individuals maintain their sense of self (Russo et al., 2025).

Behaviors such as stimming, self-touch, and repetitive sensory engagement can, in this view, be reconceived -not as epiphenomenal symptoms, but as active self-confirmation strategies that stabilize the sense of self. Importantly, this does not imply a deficient sense of self. On the contrary, the self-experience may become more salient precisely because its boundaries require more active maintenance and negotiation (Ciaunica, Roepstorff, et al., 2021). The body may appear less transparent and more continuously present within experience. This perspective offers a novel interpretation of stimming behaviors: Repetitive sensory behaviors are typically understood in terms of arousal regulation, sensory modulation, or predictive stabilization (Baranek et al., 1997; Russo et al., 2025). While these functions are important, stimming may also serve a more fundamental role: that of active self-maintenance.

Repetitive tactile, proprioceptive, vestibular, or auditory stimulation generates highly predictable, reliable, and controllable sensory feedback that provides evidence for the bodily self-model and supports self-other distinction, particularly under conditions of uncertainty, overstimulation, or interpersonal overwhelm.

Importantly, stimming is often described not only as distress-reducing but as pleasurable, grounding, joyful, or organizing (Kapp et al., 2019; Morris et al., 2025; Pfuhl & Ekblad, 2025). In self-touch, the correspondence between self-generated movement and familiar bodily sensations may contribute to this grounding experience. This suggests that repetitive sensory engagement may be intrinsically reinforcing because of its key function of predictable and controllable bodily feedback -also in neurotypical individuals. Within the present hypothesis, this correspondence provides repeated confirmation of the body as a source of action and sensation, helping sustain a coherent bodily self-experience. Exploring when autistic individuals seek self-touch, and which aspects of the resulting feedback they find grounding or pleasurable, could help clarify how this proposed function relates to sensory and emotional regulation.

At the same time, it is important not to treat autistic experiences as homogeneous (Cheong et al., 2025). Sensory processing, interoceptive awareness, emotional regulation, and social experience vary substantially across individuals. Moreover, increased attention to bodily sensations does not necessarily imply greater objective interoceptive accuracy (Proff et al., 2022). Some work suggest that autistic individuals may show altered interoceptive processing [ (Craik et al., 2026; Garfinkel et al., 2016), but see (Klein et al., 2025)]. The present proposal should therefore be understood not as a generalization but as a theoretical framework for exploring how different forms of sensory and bodily percepts may emerge through different modes of self-world regulation.

More broadly, neurodivergent lived experience may offer a particularly valuable perspective on the nature of bodily self-experience. Because many neurotypical individuals experience the body as relatively transparent during everyday life, the active processes through which it is maintained often remain implicit (Ciaunica, Roepstorff, et al., 2021). Neurodivergent experiences may render these processes more visible by revealing what happens when self-other boundaries become unusually salient, effortful, permeable, or in need of active sensory stabilization (Alekseeva & Ciaunica, 2025). In this sense, neurodivergent forms of embodiment may provide not merely clinical deviations from a norm, but important phenomenological insights into the biological and relational foundations of subjectivity.

Schizophrenia provides a different case in which self-maintenance has become unstable.

Schizophrenia-spectrum conditions involve disturbances of agency, self-reference, and self-other differentiation (Gallese & Ferri, 2014; Huang et al., 2026; Raballo et al., 2025). Classic work has shown altered self-touch perception in schizophrenia patients, who perceived self-touch as more ticklish than controls (S-J Blakemore et al., 2000). Recent neuroimaging evidence shows that distributed neural signatures can distinguish self-from other-related mentalizing in healthy adults, but this separation is reduced in schizophrenia (Açıl et al., 2026), suggesting altered neural processing of self-related cognition (Piani et al., 2025) and altered neural differentiation of selfand other-related processing. This is in line with the findings that schizophrenia patients show reduced attenuation of self-produced sensations (Shergill et al., 2014) and a smaller distinction of self-and non-self-generated tactile stimuli both at the cortical but even already at the spinal cord level (Salamone et al., 2025). Reduced self-other-distinction could express itself through typical psychotic symptoms like experiences of diminished agency (Synofzik et al., 2010), alien bodily sensations, thoughts experienced as externally imposed, or feelings of presence (Blanke et al., 2014).

When predictions about the consequences of one's own actions or bodily states are not reliably integrated with incoming signals, normally implicit distinctions between self-generated and externally generated events may become unstable. It remains unknown whether this is primarily driven by altered incoming sensory signals, altered predictions, or an altered integration across systems, levels, or time (Djimbouon et al., 2025; Dzafic et al., 2021; Schmack et al., 2015).

Current theories suggest that the underweighting of low-level sensory priors triggers aberrant prediction errors that are subsequently compensated for by the formation of hyper-rigid, overweighted conceptual beliefs (Goodwin et al., 2026). Within the present framework, such beliefs can be understood as attempts to restore coherence when lower-level processes supporting self-other distinction become unreliable. Thus, when the basic bodily distinction becomes unstable (e.g., "this is my hand touching me"), higher-order inference may provide an alternative explanation (e.g., "a demon is touching me and controlling my actions" -paraphrased citation from a patient commenting on the relevance of our research). This raises the possibility that restoring reliable self-generated sensory feedback could help re-stabilize the underlying selfmaintaining organization, although whether behaviors such as self-touch can do so remains an empirical question.

Falsifiable predictions and experimental tests

The framework yields three predictions.

1. Destabilization should increase self-evidence-generating behavior.

Experimentally destabilizing bodily ownership, agency, or self-location should increase behaviors that generate reliable self-produced sensory evidence, relative to a matched control condition. Opportunities for movement and touch should be equivalent, and arousal and cognitive demand should be measured or controlled. No selective increase despite verified destabilization would challenge this behavioral prediction.

2. Reliable self-generated evidence should accelerate recovery.

Following destabilization, access to contingent self-generated tactile feedback should accelerate recovery of the prespecified bodily-self measure compared with matched movement without touch, passive or externally generated feedback, and a no-feedback condition. Recovery should be assessed repeatedly rather than inferred from a single post-intervention rating. Equivalent recovery across active and matched passive conditions would weaken the claim that active generation has a distinctive restorative contribution.

3. Disrupting action-sensory contingency should abolish the restorative benefit.

Introducing unpredictable delays or spatial mismatches into the consequences of self-touch should eliminate the additional recovery benefit of reliable contingent feedback, while preserving the amount of stimulation and motor activity. If restoration remains unchanged despite a verified disruption of perceived predictability or contingency, the proposed mechanism would be challenged.

Convergent support would require changes in bodily-self outcomes that cannot be explained solely by reduced distress, increased attention, or generic sensory stimulation. The initial experimental scope is self-touch, with extension to other behaviors requiring separate evidence.

Concluding remarks

Why do we touch ourselves? Alongside its roles in sensory, emotional, and cognitive regulation, self-touch may help sustain a coherent bodily self-experience. I propose that the predictable relation between movement and its tactile and proprioceptive consequences provides repeated confirmation of the body as both a source of action and a locus of sensation. Within this account, sensory attenuation follows from successful prediction: the sensory consequences confirm the expected outcome of one's own action. Recurrent self-touch may thus contribute to the ongoing maintenance of bodily agency, ownership, and self-other distinction.
