Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

We try to exclude figure and table notes like “* p < .05”, but may not succeed at excluding all false positives.

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

Youth must navigate both the opportunities and risks associated with digital media. This scientometric review examined 120 empirical studies on digital media interventions for youth and used the 4C framework (content, contact, conduct, and contract) to map their thematic focus. Scientometric mapping techniques were applied to explore keyword co-occurrence patterns and collaboration networks. Most interventions addressed either risks or opportunities separately (58.3%), whereas 39.2% combined risks and opportunities within the same 4C dimension. Only 2.5% integrated multiple 4C dimensions. Interventions predominantly focused on content and conduct, with limited attention to contact and contract, including topics like social interactions, privacy, digital rights, and commercialization. Collaboration patterns were fragmented, with researchers working within thematic clusters rather than across them, despite drawing on a shared conceptual foundation. These findings suggest that current interventions cover only part of the 4C framework. Future research should encourage more integrated, cross-dimensional interventions and stronger collaboration among researchers.

1 Introduction

In an era where digital media is increasingly integral to everyday life, young people are at the forefront of both the promises and the perils of rapidly evolving technology (Livingstone, 2016). The online environment offers opportunities, such as socializing, learning, entertainment, and identity development (Granic et al., 2020; Soh et al., 2024; Stoilova et al., 2021), but also risks, like cyberbullying, harmful content, algorithmic bias, unwanted contact or screen addiction (Livingstone & Smith, 2014; Montag et al., 2024; Sotnikova et al., 2021b; Timmis et al., 2016). In response to perceived risks and opportunities, researchers and practitioners have developed digital media interventions. In this study, digital media interventions are defined as planned, structured programs designed to equip young people with the knowledge, skills, and habits they need to navigate digital life responsibly (Anonymous, 2026). Interventions might occur with or without caregiver and teacher support, sharing a common goal: to foster sustained and responsible digital engagement. A scoping review of existing media interventions has argued that the current field conceptualizes young people's online experiences primarily as binary terms, categorizing interventions as either risk-reducing or opportunity-enhancing (Anonymous, 2026). This binary framing, however, says little about the more specific dimensions of digital experience that interventions target, leaving open which aspects of young people's digital lives are well covered and which are neglected.

A likely source of this binary framing is the way youth are conceptually positioned in digital media discourse, reflecting broader tendencies to frame children as either vulnerable or empowered (Bitto-Urbanova, 2023; Stavropoulos et al., 2021; Carcelen-Garcia et al., 2023; European Institute for Gender Equality, 2019). The vulnerability framing has dominated the field: research on children's digital media has historically centered risks and media effects, often at the expense of children's rights, participation, and opportunities (Livingstone, 2016; Niestadt, 2025). This historical emphasis persists in current interventions, which continue to veer towards risk reduction rather than realizing opportunities (Anonymous, 2026). Risk-only interventions can entrench overprotective narratives of digital childhood, while opportunity-only ones may obscure structural and interpersonal threats. Given this tension, scholars argue that media literacy should integrate both aims -protecting young people while also enabling them to engage with media critically, creatively, and actively (Hobbs & Cappello, 2011; Hobbs, 2017). Protection and empowerment, in this view, are not opposing but complementary goals (OECD, 2025).

To provide greater specificity, research commonly draws on the 4Cs classification of online risks (Livingstone & Stoilova, 2021) (referred to in this review as the 4C framework), which organizes children's online experiences into four dimensions: content (exposure to online information and media), contact (interactions with peers and adults online), conduct (children's own online behaviors and participation), and contract (engagement with data practices, commercial activities, and digital agreements) (Livingstone & Stoilova, 2021; Mascheroni, 2018). Within each of these dimensions, young people may encounter both risks and opportunities. For example, content may expose youth to harmful or misleading information while also supporting learning; contact may involve grooming but also foster social connection; conduct may encompass harmful online behavior as well as creativity and civic participation; and contract may involve exploitative data practices alongside opportunities to develop privacy awareness and digital agency. Although the 4C framework is widely used to classify digital risks, its application to opportunities remains underdeveloped in the intervention literature. We therefore extend the framework to examine how interventions address both risks and opportunities across the content, contact, conduct, and contract dimensions. This enables a more nuanced understanding of intervention design than a simple binary distinction between risk reduction and opportunity enhancement. Based on this approach, interventions can be differentiated according to whether they address risks, opportunities, or a combination of both. The operationalization of these categories is detailed in the Methods section.

While the 4C framework helps identify what digital media interventions target, it does not reveal how research on these intervention themes is organized or how the field has developed over time. To address these questions, a scientometric perspective is needed. Intervention priorities do not exist in a static environment. New technologies and platforms constantly reshape youth's online experiences, generating emerging risks (e.g., commercialization of youth data; Rozendaal & Buijzen, 2022b and opportunities (e.g., AI-enabled personalized learning; Pataranutaporn et al., 2021), and intervention development may struggle to keep pace with these shifts, leaving gaps in both research coverage and practical implementation. Indeed, mapping the thematic content of interventions at a single point in time offers only a snapshot of a field that is itself a moving target; a fuller understanding of how these themes are organized, interconnected, and have evolved over time is needed. Scientometric approaches are particularly well suited to this task because they reveal the intellectual structure of a research field, identify dominant and emerging topics, and trace changes in research emphasis over time (Chen & Song, 2019; Donthu et al., 2021). To trace how thematic emphasis has shifted, intervention type and 4C risk/opportunity categories are displayed separately, showing which intervention types and 4C categories became more prominent over time.

Lastly, understanding which topics are gaining or losing prominence can be complemented by examining who is producing this knowledge, and how well-connected that knowledge base is. Digital media interventions address complex, multidimensional problems that span education, youth care, and public health (Anonymous, 2026). Fields of this kind benefit from cross-disciplinary and cross-institutional collaboration, because integrating diverse expertise supports more comprehensive intervention design (Leydesdorff & Rafols, 2011b. Conversely, fragmented collaboration networks, in which researchers cluster within narrow sub-specialties, can lead to siloed knowledge production and duplicated efforts, slowing the field's collective progress (Newman, 2001; Uddin et al., 2013). The extent to which researchers collaborate across these boundaries may shape how effectively the field addresses the full scope of young people's digital experiences identified above. To tackle this, we examine co-authorship patterns to assess how knowledge production in this field is organized, and whether collaboration reflects the same fragmentation observed in the field's thematic focus. Citation patterns and bibliographic coupling further reveal how sub-communities within the field relate to one another intellectually, beyond who co-authors with whom.

2 Present Study

The three elements mentioned above (i.e. the thematic focus of digital media interventions, the evolution of intervention priorities over time, and the structure of research collaboration and knowledge production) can inform decision-making among researchers, policymakers, educators, and funding agencies, supporting continued development of digital media interventions and better outcomes for youth. Specifically, we address the following questions:

RQ1: How are digital media interventions distributed across the 4C risk and opportunity dimensions (or categories), and to what extent do they address these in interconnected or combined ways?

RQ2: How have the risks and opportunities targeted by digital media interventions changed over time? RQ3: What collaboration networks, citation patterns, and bibliographic coupling clusters characterize the field, and what do these reveal about its structural and disciplinary evolution?

Together, these methods constitute a hybrid review that combines scoping elements with the main scientometric approach to examine the content, evolution, and structure of research on digital media interventions.

3 Methods

This scientometric review builds on a dataset of empirical studies on digital media interventions among youth that was originally compiled for a previous scoping review (Anonymous, 2026). That review followed the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology and was reported according to the PRISMA-ScR guidelines. While the scoping review mapped the content of digital media interventions, the present study uses the same dataset to examine publication patterns, research networks, and thematic clusters through scientometric analyses using VOSviewer (Van Eck & Waltman, 2010). The search strategy targeted three core concepts: youth, digital media interventions, and effectiveness evaluation. This search was executed across four databases (Scopus, Web of Science Core Collection, ERIC, and the Psychology & Behavioral Sciences Collection) without restrictions on publication date or full-text language. Studies were included if they: (1) involved young people aged less than or equal to 23 years, or their caregivers/educators, which was chosen to reflect the educational and legal provisions that extend into young adulthood; (2) examined planned digital media interventions aimed at developing digital skills, literacy, safe/healthy online behaviors, or related competencies; (3) used experimental or quasi-experimental group designs; and (4) were published as peer-reviewed articles with at least an English-language abstract. Studies focusing solely on nondigital media or belonging to the grey literature were excluded. In addition to database searches, citation tracking and targeted searches identified additional studies. After deduplication, screening, and full-text assessment, the final dataset comprised 120 studies. The 120 empirical studies described 131 distinct evaluations of digital media interventions. Complete search strings, screening procedures, and the PRISMA flow diagram are reported in Anonymous (2026).

3.1 Coding the 4C Framework

To supplement the variables collected in the original scoping review, we identified the specific risks and opportunities addressed and categorized them under the 4C framework (i.e. content, contact, conduct, and contract) (see Table 1). Although the 4C framework can be combined with additional dimensions, such as risk typologies (e.g., aggressive, sexual, or values-related risks), preliminary coding revealed limited variation across these dimensions. Therefore, we focused exclusively on the 4C typology. Based on this coding, interventions were classified into four overarching categories: (1) risk-only, addressing only risks; (2) opportunity-only, addressing only opportunities; (3) dual-linked, addressing both risks and opportunities within the same 4C dimension; and (4) dualmixed, addressing risks and opportunities across different 4C dimensions. For example, an intervention that teaches adolescents to critically evaluate online advertising (contract risk) while also building digital literacy skills (content opportunity) would be coded as dual-mixed. A program addressing social media interactions as a harmful conduct risk and as a conduct opportunity to build creative participation would be coded as dual-linked. Two researchers independently coded the first 50 studies, showing substantial agreement (PABAK = 0.68-0.88; Byrt et al., 1993). The remaining 70 studies were coded by one researcher.

For RQ1, we quantified how often each 4C dimension, and each intervention type, appeared across the included studies, allowing us to map which risks and opportunities are most frequently targeted and how they co-occur within interventions. For RQ2, we tracked how these same intervention types and 4C categories shifted in frequency over the study period, visualizing publication trends to identify which types and categories became more or less prominent over time, as well as the emergence of new thematic emphases. For RQ3, we used VOSviewer (Van Eck & Waltman, 2010) to examine co-authorship networks, citation relations, and bibliographic coupling, identifying influential publications, and collaborative communities. RQ3 analyses revealed how scholarly impact was distributed, which researchers or institutions functioned as central nodes in the field, and how different streams of intervention research were interconnected. Of the 120 included studies, 27 were retained in the citation network analysis, whereas 93 studies had no direct citation links with other publications within the dataset and were therefore excluded from the visualized citation network. We used association strength normalization for all network visualizations. An additional keyword co-occurrence analysis was conducted with VOSviewer on author keywords with a minimum of 2 occurrences per keyword. Clustering used a resolution of 1.00. 5 Discussion

This scientometric review with scoping elements examined how digital media interventions for youth are distributed across risk-and opportunity-focused approaches, how these emphases have evolved over time, and how the research field is structurally organized in terms of collaboration and knowledge production. The key scoping finding is that, although most interventions still treat risks and opportunities separately, integration within the same 4C dimension has become more common: dual-linked interventions form the largest single category and are now the most frequent type. Integration across different 4C dimensions, in contrast, remains rare. Regarding intervention objectives, most interventions focus on individual behavior and content-related outcomes (i.e., conduct and content), while social interaction and data privacy concerns (i.e., contact and contract) receive comparatively limited attention. Bibliographic coupling showed that, despite the presence of distinct thematic clusters, some studies shared a common body of foundational literature. Studies on digital literacy, media literacy, cyberbullying, online information evaluation, and digital citizenship shared several core references and formed areas of intellectual overlap. Taken together, the scoping and scientometric findings suggest that the field has developed considerable expertise within specific intervention dimensions, but that integration across dimensions remains limited. While such specialization may be valuable for generating depth and advancing knowledge for particular topics, it may also mean that insights from areas such as digital literacy, cyberbullying, digital citizenship, and online information evaluation are not always combined within the same intervention approach. This interpretation is consistent with our finding that interventions rarely integrate risks and opportunities across different 4C dimensions.

5.1 From Protection to Integration

The distribution of intervention types suggests that the field is moving from a protection-focused perspective toward a more integrated understanding of youth's digital media use. However, this integration remains bounded: it operates within a single dimension rather than across the full complexity of youth's digital lives. Dual-mixed designs, which combine risk mitigation and opportunity enhancement across different 4C dimensions, remain marginal. This gap is conceptually significant. Since youth's digital experiences do not naturally partition into risks or opportunities, nor unfold within a single dimension at a time, a single interaction or platform can simultaneously afford learning, social connection, exposure to harmful content, and data exploitation. This suggests that intervention design should be informed by an awareness of these interdependencies, so that even narrowly scoped interventions account for how the dimension they target relates to others, rather than treating risks and opportunities as if they operate in isolation. Additionally, the temporal data offer some grounds for cautious optimism. Risk-only approaches were most common in early intervention research, consistent with a protective paradigm in which children were primarily positioned as vulnerable subjects requiring safeguarding. From the mid-2010s onward, opportunity-oriented and dual-linked designs became increasingly prevalent, signaling a gradual reconceptualization of young people as active digital participants. This trajectory, from protection toward empowerment and, tentatively, toward integration, mirrors broader shifts in the theoretical framing of childhood and digital media (Livingstone et al., 2017; Stavropoulos et al., 2021). Yet this transition remains incomplete and uneven. The field has begun to move beyond purely protective models but has not yet arrived at holistic frameworks of digital wellbeing that reflect the interconnected nature of youth's online experiences.

5.2 Thematic Narrowness in Intervention Coverage

A more detailed finding concerns the imbalance between the 4C dimensions, with relatively more attention being paid to content and conduct risks and opportunities, and less attention to contact and contract. The dominance of conduct-focused interventions, particularly anti-cyberbullying programs, and content-focused approaches centered on media and digital literacy reflect historical patterns, yet their prominence may partly be an artifact of legacy paradigms rather than a deliberate response to current digital realities. Less attention has been paid to contact-based risks and opportunities, encompassing peer dynamics, social networking, and interactions with unknown adults. Those experiences remain underrepresented despite their centrality in young people's everyday digital lives (Livingstone & Smith, 2014; Cernikova et al., 2016). The contract dimension being least represented is perhaps the most striking finding in this regard. As youth's digital environments are increasingly shaped by data collection practices, algorithmic personalization, and platform-driven commercialization, the absence of interventions addressing these structural forces suggests that the field has yet to fully grapple with the datafication of childhood (Rozendaal & Buijzen, 2022b. Emerging risks such as microtargeting, dark-pattern design, and the commercial exploitation of youth data fall almost entirely outside the current intervention landscape. Taken together, these patterns suggest that the field is well-resourced in dimensions where risks are visible and pedagogically tractable but poorly equipped for the platform-governance and data-rights challenges that define contemporary digital childhood.

5.3 A Fragmented Research Field

Across all three bibliometric analyses, a consistent picture emerges: collaboration, citation, and shared intellectual foundations each map onto the same thematic boundaries. Researchers tended to publish, collaborate, and cite work within established domains such as cyberbullying, digital literacy, news literacy, online information evaluation, and digital citizenship. At the same time, the bibliographic coupling analysis indicated that these domains were not entirely disconnected. Publications occupying relatively central positions in the network, including work by McGrew, Kleemans, and Valcke, drew on core literature related to digital literacy, media literacy, online information evaluation, cyberbullying, and digital citizenship, creating areas of overlap across otherwise distinct research strands. These findings suggest that the field combines specialization with a limited set of shared intellectual foundations. Such specialization is not necessarily problematic and may support the development of theoretical depth and domain-specific expertise. However, when considered alongside the scoping findings, it appears that insights from these different domains are only occasionally translated into intervention approaches that integrate multiple risks and opportunities. For example, research on cyberbullying, digital literacy, online information evaluation, and privacy-related issues develops within separate intervention traditions, despite drawing on some of the same foundational ideas. The relatively small number of dual-mixed interventions identified in this review may therefore reflect the continued organization of the field around specific thematic domains, even where common conceptual foundations exist.

5.4 Practical Implications

The findings carry distinct implications for three groups of stakeholders: intervention designers and practitioners, funders and policymakers, and researchers.

For researchers, on collaboration. First, our network analysis shows that intervention researchers mostly stay within their own topic area. They collaborate with, cite, and build on the work of others in the same niche (e.g., cyberbullying or digital literacy), but rarely cross into other author clusters. Professional organizations could actively create opportunities for different teams to work together, for example by funding joint projects that pair a cyberbullying researcher with a privacy researcher. Second, our bibliographic coupling results show that some studies from different clusters already draw on shared foundational sources, even without directly citing or collaborating with each other. These existing points of overlap could be used as natural starting points for collaborative reviews, workshops, or shared research frameworks.

For intervention designers and practitioners. The 4C imbalance reveals that contact and contract dimensions present knowledge gaps for intervention. Concretely, this means developing curricula that address peer-network navigation and interactions with unknown adults (contact) with the same rigor currently applied to anti-cyberbullying programming and developing data-literacy and algorithmic-awareness modules (contract) that go beyond generic "privacy settings" tutorials to address microtargeting, dark-pattern recognition, and commercial data exploitation specifically. Because dual-mixed designs remain marginal, practitioners should consider utilizing crossdimensional pairing in intervention design. For example, a module combining contract-based data literacy content with identity-dimension social-connection content could test whether teaching youth to recognize how platforms monetize their self-expression changes how they engage in identity related risk-taking. This requires moving intervention development towards including content that specifies how a single online interaction can simultaneously produce risk exposure and developmental opportunity.

For funders and policymakers. Funders should prioritize the allocation of resources towards the development of contract dimension interventions (data rights, algorithmic transparency, commercial exploitation) and dual-mixed designs that cut across different topic dimensions. Policymakers aiming to improve youth's online safety should recognize that the current evidence base is heavily concentrated on cyberbullying and digital literacy, while datafication-related issues receive considerably less attention. As a result, current intervention evidence may provide only a partial picture of the challenges youth face online, and policy decisions should not be guided solely by the areas that have received the most research attention.

Taken together, closing the 4C gap requires funders, journals, and professional networks to actively engineer collaboration and gather of resources, which the scientometric analysis shows is not occurring on its own.

5.5 Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, only the first 50 studies were independently coded by two researchers (PABAK = 0.68-0.88; Byrt et al., 1993). The remaining 70 were coded by a single researcher using the resulting codebook. Second, the dualmixed category contains only three interventions, so findings for this category are descriptive and should not be generalized. Finally, the corpus is limited to experimental and quasi-experimental evaluations. Our conclusions therefore describe this particular segment rather than the full field of digital media intervention research.

6 Conclusion

This study set out to understand not only what research on digital media interventions for youth addresses, but how the research field studying these interventions is organized. The value of the hybrid approach lies precisely in showing that these two questions are connected. A field that develops in disconnected thematic clusters is, perhaps unsurprisingly, one that produces disconnected interventions; the content gaps identified in the scoping review and the structural fragmentation identified in the scientometric analysis are best understood as two expressions of the same underlying dynamic rather than separate problems. Addressing this will require more than isolated calls for "more integrated interventions", as it points to a need for the field's incentive structures, funding bodies, and publication venues to actively reward cross-cluster collaboration and cross-dimensional design, rather than leaving integration to individual researchers working within already-existing niches. As digital platforms continue to reshape the risks and opportunities young people encounter, closing this gap between fragmented knowledge production and the integrated nature of youth's digital lives will be essential for intervention research to remain relevant to the environments it seeks to address.
