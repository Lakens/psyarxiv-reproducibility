Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p-values > .001 Overall, most demographic variables measured at baseline were not significantly (all p-values > .001) predictive of attrition status.

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p-values > .001 Overall, most demographic variables measured at baseline were not significantly (all p-values > .001) predictive of attrition status.
p = .694 The EXT PGS was negatively associated with the RI from the youth-report of positive parenting (β = -.06) but not parent report (β = -.01, p = .694).

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

Background: Parenting practices are a potential mechanism through which adolescent delinquent behavior can be modified. However, it remains unclear whether associations between parenting and delinquency reflect stable differences between families or dynamic processes unfolding within families over time. The current study disentangled the between-and within-person associations between delinquency and parenting across adolescence and whether genetic propensity toward externalizing, indexed by a polygenic score (EXT PGS), was associated with stable between-person differences in delinquency and parenting Methods: Participants were 2,184 adolescents assessed annually from ages 12 to 17 as part of the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development-Social Development sub-study. Parents and youth reported on delinquency and parenting dimensions (corporal punishment, inconsistent discipline, poor monitoring, positive parenting). Random-intercept cross-lagged panel models separated between-person and within-person associations. We evaluated the association between the EXT PGS and the random intercepts.

Results: Between-persons, adolescents with higher average delinquency experienced higher average levels of corporal punishment, inconsistent discipline, and poor monitoring and lower positive parenting. EXT PGS was associated with higher levels of delinquency and with higher corporal punishment, inconsistent discipline, and poor monitoring, as well as lower youthreported positive parenting and accounted for approximately 4%-8% of variance shared between delinquency and parenting. Within-person, higher delinquency was associated with subsequent increases in youth-reported corporal punishment, but not parent-reported corporal punishment. Reciprocal associations emerged between delinquency and poor monitoring in both parent-and youth-report models and between delinquency and youth-reported inconsistent discipline. No longitudinal associations emerged between positive parenting and delinquency.

Conclusions: Associations between parenting and delinquency reflected both stable individual differences and dynamic developmental processes. The EXT PGS contributed to stable differences in delinquency and parenting, while longitudinal associations varied across parenting dimensions. These findings suggest that a focus on decreasing inconsistent discipline and increasing parental monitoring may be particularly relevant for mitigating delinquency.

Introduction

Delinquent behaviors (e.g., rule breaking, aggression, and other antisocial behaviors), fall within the broader domain of externalizing behavior 1 and represent a costly public health problem, with links to substance use disorders, legal system involvement, and broader psychosocial dysfunction. 2, 3 Mean-levels of delinquency follow an age-related pattern 3, 4 , with behaviors increasing during adolescence, as youth have increased independence and autonomy over their activities, behaviors, and peer groups. The parent-child relationship has been identified as a particularly important factor that can modify risk for engaging in delinquent behaviors. Harsh and inconsistent disciplinary practices, poor parental monitoring, and low levels of positive involvement are all associated with increased risk for delinquent behavior among adolescents. 5, 6 However, we must understand how parenting practices and delinquent behavior co-develop, parsing between-and within-person effects, to inform etiology as well as identify targets for early intervention.

Theories regarding the development of delinquent behavior posit that negative child-parent transactions may increase the risk of increased delinquent behavior over the course of development. 7, 8 Parenting practices, such as discipline, monitoring, and involvement, as well as adolescent delinquency may directly influence each other through within-person, reciprocal processes. For example, a child engaging in increased delinquent behavior may lead to a change in their parents' behaviors. If their parents' reaction is to increase supervision and monitoring, this may lead to a subsequent decrease in delinquent behavior. However, if increased delinquent behavior leads to parents' implementing increasingly harsh punishments, these actions may serve to exacerbate delinquent behavior. Therefore, if we can understand how these processes develop over time, intervention can be applied to disrupt these reciprocal relationships and deflect trajectories of adolescent delinquent behavior.

Despite the importance of understanding how this developmental process unfolds, research to formally tease apart these developmental trajectories has been hampered by small sample sizes and methodological approaches that conflate between-and within-person processes, which has produced overall inconsistent results. For example, some studies have found that positive parenting in childhood is protective against risk taking during adolescence 9 , while others have found that youth behavior impacts parental monitoring, but not the reciprocal effect 10 , while other studies have found evidence for a bidirectional longitudinal association between delinquency and parenting practices. 11 For these studies, sample sizes were relatively small (N's <500) and many used cross-lagged panel models (CLPM) which cannot parse the between-and within-person associations between variables needed to isolate short-term, dynamic effects. 5, 6 One method for addressing this is the random intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM), which extends the traditional CLPM by accounting for the between-person developmental trends in variables of interest (e.g., between-person trajectory of delinquency over time) along with the fluctuating within-person auto-regressive (e.g., within-person effect of delinquency over time) and cross-lagged (e.g., the within-person association of delinquency at time one on parental involvement at time two). One recent study employing RI-CLPMs to disentangle the development of externalizing behaviors and parenting has found evidence for reciprocal associations between parental monitoring and aggression 12 ; however, another study examining the association between parental knowledge and externalizing behavior found no evidence for within-person cross-lagged associations from ages 8-15. 13 Therefore, additional research is needed to more fully characterize the association between delinquency and parenting, including behaviors beyond just parental knowledge and monitoring.

Both delinquency and parenting behaviors are individually heritable, and the association between parenting and adolescent externalizing behaviors is itself partly genetically influenced. 14, 15 Genetically informed designs suggest that these influences can operate through multiple pathways. They can work passively, when parents transmit both genes and rearing environment, or evocatively, when youths' heritable characteristics elicit parenting responses.

Evidence has generally favored evocative processes. [16] [17] [18] [19] [20] [21] Regardless of pathway, these findings suggest that part of the association between parenting and delinquency may reflect shared genetic propensity rather than purely environmental transmission. Such effects would be expected to show up largely as stable differences between families, the component that RI-CLPMs separate from within-person dynamics.

Measured genetic data, such as polygenic scores (PGS), offer a complementary approach.

One study found that youths' genetic propensity toward externalizing behavior was associated with both higher youth externalizing behavior and lower parental monitoring and knowledge. 22 Because youth PGS capture variants inherited from parents, associations between youth PGS and parenting may reflect passive or evocative processes, which cannot be distinguished without parental genotypes. However, youth PGS can index the extent to which stable betweenperson differences in delinquency and parenting, and the overlap between them, are associated with genetic propensity toward externalizing. Integrating a PGS into the RI-CLPM framework therefore makes it possible to quantify how much of the stable association between delinquency and parenting is linked to genetic propensity.

The current study disentangled the between and within-person associations between delinquency and parenting practices during adolescence using RI-CLPMs using data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development-Social Development (ABCD-SD) Study. This sub study of the ABCD study focuses on measuring self-and caregiver-report data on delinquency and victimization as well as including detailed assessment of parenting practices. We also examined the role of genetic propensity towards externalizing, measured via a polygenic score (EXT PGS), on the development of both delinquency and parenting behaviors, to quantify the role of genetics on this process. We hypothesized that over time, levels of delinquency would increase, consistent with results from other epidemiologic samples examining delinquency during this developmental period. We also anticipated that facets of parenting including supervision and monitoring, would decrease over time, consistent with previous studies showing that parental oversight declines as youth gain autonomy. 23 We further predicted that the EXT PGS would be associated with the between-person, mean levels of delinquency and parenting practices over time. Regarding within-person effects, we hypothesized that there would be significant cross-lagged associations between delinquency scores and parenting behavior scores. However, given that previous studies have been limited in power and found mixed results, we did not advance specific hypotheses regarding the direction of effects.

Methods

Sample

Data were drawn from waves 1-6 of the ABCD-SD study (Release 7.0). The ABCD-SD study involves coordinated data collection among five existing ABCD sites (University of Pittsburgh, University of Michigan, University of Maryland, Yale University, University of Florida).

Sample details are included in Table 1. Overall, the ABCD-SD study consists of 2,424 youth (2,146 families) with data available at 6 time points reported by youth and their caregiver or parent (referred to as parent in text). Due to attrition, the analytic sample for these analyses ranged from N= 2,184 (age 12) to N = 1,514 (age 17). See Supplemental Table 1 for descriptive statistics for the study measures at each wave.

Measures

Delinquency

Delinquency was measured with the Self-Reported Delinquency Scale (SRD) 24 . In this 48-item measure, informants reported on a variety of delinquent behaviors including vandalism, theft, violence, and harassment. At the baseline ABCD-SD visit participants were asked about these behaviors occurring during their lifetime as well as in the past 12 months. At subsequent study visits participants report on the event happening "since the previous SD study visit". The current analyses only used the past 12 months measure from baseline to ensure measurement was consistent across time. Nine items were excluded due to being outcomes of delinquent behavior (e.g., arrest, suspension) or not an indicator of delinquency (e.g., fighting with a sibling; items listed in Supplemental Methods). Informants (parent and child) also indicated if the behavior has been done "never" (coded as 0), "once or twice" (coded as 1) or "more often" (coded as 2). As parents and youth often have access to different information about delinquent behavior, integrating reports across informants allows behaviors reported by either source to contribute to the overall score. Consistent with this multi-informant approach and because our analyses focused on overall levels of delinquency rather than informant-specific perspectives, we chose to summed responses across reporters. This resulted in a Delinquency score with a possible range from 0-156, reflecting delinquent behaviors a youth has engaged in during the past 12 months at each wave of data collection.

Parenting behaviors

Parenting behaviors were measured by the Alabama Parenting Questionnaire (APQ). 25 APQ assesses five constructs: Use of corporal punishment, Inconsistent discipline, Poor supervision and monitoring, Positive involvement with children, and Use of positive parenting techniques. For the purposes of these analyses, and consistent with previous studies of the factor structure of the APQ, 26 we combined the Positive involvement and Use of positive parenting techniques scales into one scale by summing across all items (Positive parenting). These subscales are highly correlated and often load onto a single factor in adolescent samples. Both child and parent completed the APQ and we anticipated that reporters would differ on their assessment of parenting behaviors. We therefore examined four APQ subscales separately for each reporter (eight reporter-specific measures in total). See Supplemental Methods for additional details.

Externalizing Polygenic Score (EXT PGS)

The EXT PGS was derived using a multivariate GWAS of ADHD, alcohol problems, cannabis use, age of first sex, number of sexual partners, risk taking, and smoking to identify shared genetic propensity associated with externalizing problems. 27 To create the EXT PGS, we used a unified analytic pipeline to construct the PGS in individuals most genetically similar to Europeans (EUR), Africans (AFR), and an admixed American (AMR) reference population. The pipeline relied on two software packages: PRS-CS 28 for adjusting original GWAS beta weights for linkage disequilibrium (LD) and Plink2 29 for constructing the PGS from LD-adjusted beta weights. Prior to PGS construction, we performed LD-adjustment of the original GWAS beta weights with PRS-CS 30 . We used the 1000 Genomes European reference files distributed with the software to estimate LD. We generated PGS using only HapMap 3 SNPs (~1.3 million) 31 as restricted by the PRS-CS method (all other parameters = set to the PRS-CS default). Plink2 was used to separately compute PGS among EUR, AFR, and AMR individuals using the LDadjusted beta weights.

PGS were pooled across EUR, AFR, and AMR individuals 32, 33 . First, we created scaled, residual PGSs among EUR, AFR, and AMR individuals separately, controlling for the top 10 within-group genetic similarity PCs. Then, these residualized PGS were combined for all genetic analyses. Interactions between the PGS and similarity group were run as a sensitivity test to determine if overall effects are being driven by one group (see Supplemental Results).

Demographic covariates

Caregiver report of annual household income was coded: under $50,000, $50,000 to $100,000, and above $100,000. Highest level of parental education was coded: less than high school, high school, some college, bachelor's degree, graduate degree. Sex assigned at birth was coded: male, female. Caregiver-reported race of the child was condensed into the following options: White, Black, more than one race. Caregiver-reported ethnicity was coded: Hispanic, non-Hispanic. Baseline site ID (5 sites) was included in all models. Family ID was included as random effects in all models. Sample descriptives are reported in Table 1.

Genetic covariates

Genetic similarity group (EUR, AFR, AMR) was included in all models involving PGS; race and ethnicity were not included.

Analyses

All data cleaning and manipulation were performed using R 34 . Attrition analyses used the lme4 R package, 35 which accounts for familial nesting. All developmental models used Mplus (version 8 36 ) and MplusAutomation. 37

Attrition Analyses

We examined if baseline (wave 1) characteristics differed significantly from nonresponders at each wave of data collection. Participants at each follow up wave (waves 2-6) were coded as 0 (did not participate) or 1 (participated). We evaluated the effect of the following variables on attrition: sex, household income, parental education, race, ethnicity, and delinquency score. Overall, most demographic variables measured at baseline were not significantly (all p-values > .001) predictive of attrition status. We did find evidence that baseline household income was significantly associated with missing parent data at wave 6, such that families in the high-income range, compared to the lowest income range, were significantly more likely to provide data at wave 6 (OR: 1.77, 95% CI: 1.30, 2.41). We also found that baseline delinquency scores were significantly associated with missing parent data at wave 2, such that higher scores at baseline were less likely to provide data at wave 2 (OR: 0.922, 95% CI: 0.920, 0.924).

Multiple Imputation

With the goal of retaining all participants and reducing bias that could be introduced by excluding participants with missing data as highlighted in the attrition analyses, we imputed missing data using multiple imputations by chained equations and the mice package. 38, 39 For imputation of binary variables, we used logistic regression method (logreg), for categorical variables used multinomial logit method (polyreg), and for continuous variables we used predictive mean matching method (pmm). Twenty datasets were imputed and used for the univariable growth models and random intercept cross-lagged panel models. The average or range of fit indices are reported as appropriate. Estimates and standard errors were pooled using Rubin's rules. 40 For each parameter, the pooled estimate was calculated as the mean of the estimates across 20 imputations. Within-imputation variance was computed as the mean of the squared standard errors from each model, and between-imputation variance was calculated as the variance of the parameter estimates across imputations. This resulted in a total variance which was derived by combining within-and between-imputation variance. The pooled standard error was obtained as the square root of the total variance. Two-tailed p-values and 95% confidence intervals were computed based on the pooled estimates and standard errors. As a sensitivity check, the models were also run with all available raw data (details and results in Supplemental Materials).

Random Intercept-Cross-Lagged Panel Models

We modeled the co-development of delinquency and parenting behaviors in 8 separate RI-CLPMs, modeling delinquency with each parenting subscale for each reporter (delinquency with 4 subscales, per reporter). Family ID was included as random effects in all models. These models fit two random intercepts to account for stable between-person differences in delinquency and parenting across time. Within-person fluctuations were modeled using autoregressive paths (AR) within each construct and cross-lagged (CL) paths between delinquency and parenting across time, along with within-wave covariances between the withinperson components. AR paths capture the persistence of within-person deviations within each construct across time, and the CL paths test whether a deviation from a child's expected level of delinquency is associated with a subsequent deviation from their expected level of parenting behavior at the next wave, controlling for the prior within-person level of parenting behavior, and vice versa. To identify the most parsimonious model, we compared parameters between a fully constrained (all CL paths of the same direction and AR paths for each variable constrained to the same value over time) and fully unconstrained (all CL and AR paths allowed to vary over time) models. Changes in model fit were evaluated using chi-square difference test.

EXT PGS Model

As outlined by 41 , we included the EXT PGS as a time-invariant predictor on each best fitting RI-CLPM. We evaluated the effect of including the EXT PGS as a predictor of the random intercepts.

Results

Descriptive Statistics

Descriptives for study variables by wave are reported in Supplemental Table 1.

Delinquency was modestly correlated with the APQ scales across waves (average r's ranged from = -.13 [for parent report of positive parenting] to .22 [for youth report of poor monitoring]; see Supplemental Table 2 for all bivariate correlations).

Random Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Models

All RI-CLPMs were fit with the CL and AR paths fully free and fully constrained (described above). For all models, the best fit had the same-direction CL paths and withinvariable AR paths constrained to the same values across assessments. Fit indices from all models are reported in Supplemental Table 3. As a sensitivity check, the models were also run with all available raw data (results reported in Supplemental Results). Overall, the results from the models based on raw data were parallel to those based on imputed data.

Between-Person Effects

For each best-fitting RI-CLPM there were significant associations between the random intercepts. Random intercept (RI) factors for delinquency were positively associated with the RI factors for corporal punishment (parent-report r = .32; youth-report r =.28), inconsistent discipline (parent-report r =.35; youth-report r = .34), and poor monitoring (parent-report r =.33; youth-report r = .35), and negatively associated with the RI for positive parenting (parent-report r = -.21; youth-report r = -.25). These results indicated that youth who exhibited higher levels of delinquency on average across ages 12-17 also experienced higher levels of corporal punishment, inconsistent discipline, and poor monitoring, and lower levels of positive parenting.

EXT PGS Effects.

The EXT PGS was significantly associated with delinquency RIs across all models (βs ~ .17) as well as significantly associated with RIs for corporal punishment (parent-report β = .11; youth-report β =.13), inconsistent discipline (parent-report β = .10; youthreport β =.12), and poor monitoring (parent-report β = .07; youth-report β =.11). The EXT PGS was negatively associated with the RI from the youth-report of positive parenting (β = -.06) but not parent report (β = -.01, p = .694). Based on these results, the EXT PGS accounted for about 6-8% of the between-person association between delinquency and corporal punishment, 5-6% of the between-person association between delinquency and inconsistent discipline, 4-5% of the between-person association between delinquency and poor monitoring, and 4% of the betweenperson association between delinquency and youth-report of positive parenting. See Table 2 for all estimates.

Within-Person Effects

All model estimates are included in Supplemental Tables 4-11. Summary of withinperson results reported in Table 3.

Delinquency with Corporal Punishment.

Significant positive within-person AR effects were observed for delinquency and corporal punishment across all adjacent assessments, indicating that within-person deviations in both constructs tended to persist over time. There were also significant positive, concurrent within-person associations between delinquency and corporal punishment at ages 13-15 in both reporter models and at ages 16-17 in the youthreport model. Cross-lagged (CL) analyses revealed that higher than expected delinquency at one assessment was associated with youth report of higher-than-expected levels of corporal punishment at the subsequent ages (βs ranging from .03 to .07; Figure 1), but this pattern of associations was not observed for the parent-report model.

Delinquency with Inconsistent Discipline.

Significant positive within-person AR effects were observed for delinquency and inconsistent discipline across all adjacent assessments, indicating that within-person deviations in both constructs tended to persist over time. There were also significant positive, concurrent within-person associations between delinquency and inconsistent discipline across both reporter models at age 13-16 and age 17 in the youth-report model. In both models, CL paths revealed that higher than expected delinquency at one assessment was associated with report of higher-than-expected levels of inconsistent discipline at subsequent ages (parent βs ranging from .04 to .08; youth βs ranging from .05 to .11). In the youth-report model (Figure 2) higher than expected levels of inconsistent discipline at one assessment were also associated with higher-than-expected levels of delinquency at subsequent ages (βs ranging from .06 to .07). Results from the youth-report model suggest a reciprocal transactional process in which inconsistent discipline and delinquency mutually reinforce one another over time (Figure 2).

Delinquency with Poor Monitoring.

Significant positive AR effects were observed for both delinquency and parent-reported poor monitoring across all adjacent assessments. There were also significant positive, concurrent within-person associations between delinquency and poor monitoring across both reporter models at age 13-17. In both models, CL paths revealed higher than expected delinquency scores at one assessment was associated with higher-thanexpected levels of poor monitoring at subsequent ages (parent βs ranging from .05 to .10; youth βs ranging from .07 to .15). In both models the CL paths from poor monitoring to delinquency were also significant (parent βs ranging from .06 to .07; youth βs ranging from .09 to .10), such that higher-than-expected levels of poor monitoring scores were associated with higher-thanexpected delinquency scores at the next wave of data collection. Together, these findings suggest a reciprocal transactional process in which poor monitoring and delinquency mutually reinforce one another over time (Figure 3).

Delinquency with Positive Parenting.

Significant positive AR effects were observed for delinquency and positive parenting across all adjacent assessments. There was limited evidence for concurrent within-person associations in both the parent-report model (significant negative associations at age 13 and 17 and in the youth-report model (significant negative associations at ages 14-17). No significant CL associations emerged in either direction, for either model, indicating an absence of reciprocal within-person effects between positive parenting and delinquency over time (Figure 4).

Discussion

The present study examined the longitudinal interplay between delinquency and multiple dimensions of parenting during adolescence, while explicitly distinguishing stable betweenperson differences from within-person developmental processes. Consistent with prior metaanalytic evidence, we found robust between-person associations between delinquency and multiple dimensions of parenting. 5, 6 However, the within-person findings revealed a more nuanced picture: adolescent delinquency consistently predicted subsequent increases in corporal punishment, inconsistent discipline, and poor monitoring, while poor monitoring and youth-report of inconsistent discipline were predictive of subsequent delinquency as well. In contrast, positive parenting, which is comprised of items related to positive involvement and use of positive parenting techniques, was associated with lower delinquency at the between-person level but showed no prospective within-person associations with delinquency. Finally, the EXT PGS was associated with stable levels of both delinquency and several parenting dimensions.

Together, these findings provide evidence for a transactional interplay between certain parenting behaviors and adolescent delinquency in which stable individual and family characteristics coexist with dynamic processes through which adolescents and parents shape one another over time.

At the between-person level, youth who exhibited higher average levels of delinquency across ages 12-17 also experienced higher average levels of corporal punishment, inconsistent discipline, and poor monitoring and lower average levels of positive parenting, with associations similar across parent-and youth-report models. This pattern is consistent with a large literature demonstrating that parenting behaviors, particularly parental monitoring and harsh and neglectful parenting, are associated with adolescent delinquency. 5, 6 The EXT PGS was associated with the random intercepts for both delinquency and parenting, indicating that genetic propensity towards externalizing is linked to these stable between-person differences.

These results align with genetically informed studies showing that associations between adolescent externalizing behavior and negative parenting partly reflect genetic influences [16] [17] [18] 42 The present findings extend this literature by demonstrating that the EXT PGS accounted for approximately 4-8% of the covariance between the random intercepts of delinquency and parenting behaviors, suggesting that adolescents' measured polygenic liability statistically accounts for a modest but meaningful proportion of the stable covariation between these constructs. Because youth PGS reflect variants inherited from parents, these associations may arise through passive processes (parents transmitting both genetic risk and a rearing environment), evocative processes (youths' genetically influenced characteristics eliciting particular parenting responses), or both. Distinguishing these pathways will require designs that include parental genotypes, such as trio or within-family PGS approaches. Because the RI-CLPM partitions stable between-person variance from within-person change, the within-person associations discussed below are independent of these stable, partly genetically influenced differences.

The within-person cross-lagged results provide evidence of prospective associations between adolescent delinquency and subsequent parenting behaviors, particularly poor monitoring and youth-reported inconsistent discipline, where we found evidence for reciprocal within-person associations. Across both parent-and youth-report models, higher-than-expected levels of poor monitoring was associated with subsequent increases in delinquency, while higher-than-expected delinquency also was associated with subsequent increases in poor monitoring. Thus, poor monitoring and delinquency appear to be embedded in a reciprocal feedback process, whereby changes in one construct are followed by changes in the other. Several mechanisms may account for this cycle. On one side, parents facing escalating delinquent behavior may become worn down or discouraged, and may withdraw from monitoring efforts they perceive as ineffective or not worth the conflict they provoke. 43, 44 Youth engaging in delinquency may also become more secretive and disclose less about their activities, further limiting parents' knowledge of their whereabouts and companions. 45 On the other side, reduced supervision gives youth more unstructured, unsupervised time and greater opportunity to affiliate with deviant peers, a robust proximal predictor of delinquent behavior. 46 Over time, these processes may amplify one another, with each increase in delinquency further eroding monitoring and each lapse in monitoring creating new opportunities for delinquent behavior.

We found similar evidence for youth-reported inconsistent discipline, suggesting that adolescents' behavior may be associated with subsequent changes in how parents monitor and discipline them. Although parental monitoring has traditionally been conceptualized primarily as a parental influence on adolescent behavior, developmental research increasingly demonstrates that adolescents' behavior can also shape parents' monitoring practices. 47 Our findings also align with prior evidence identifying parental monitoring as one of the parenting dimensions most strongly associated with adolescent delinquency. 5, 6 Moreover, prior longitudinal research has documented reciprocal associations between parental behavioral control, which included monitoring and discipline, and delinquency, 48, 49 with some evidence suggesting that these associations may be stronger during adolescence than childhood. 6 Together, these findings highlight the importance of conceptualizing parenting and adolescent delinquency as dynamically interrelated processes rather than as unidirectional influences.

There was also a unidirectional within-person association from delinquency to youthreport of corporal punishment, such that higher-than-expected levels of delinquency predicted higher-than-expected levels of corporal punishment at subsequent assessments. These associations remained after accounting for stable between-person differences and prior withinperson deviations in both constructs, suggesting that increases in adolescent delinquency may prompt increases in punitive parental responses. Much of the parenting and delinquency literature has focused on identifying parenting behaviors that predict subsequent delinquency; however, these results highlight that adolescent behavior itself may be an important contributor to changes in parenting behaviors during early and middle adolescence. Given the known negative outcomes associated with use of corporal punishment, 50, 51 these results suggest the potential value of providing resources and support to parents whose children are exhibiting problem behaviors to promote more effective, non-punitive parenting behaviors.

The findings for positive parenting provide an important counterpoint to the results from corporal punishment, inconsistent discipline and poor monitoring. At the between-person level, youth who experienced consistently higher levels of positive parenting across adolescence also exhibited lower levels of delinquency, consistent with the broader literature linking supportive and responsive parenting with more adaptive adolescent outcomes. 5, 6 Yet, at the within-person level, higher-than-usual positive parenting was not associated with subsequent deviations in delinquency, nor was higher-than-usual delinquency associated with subsequent changes in positive parenting. This divergence between between-person and within-person effects suggests that the protective association of positive parenting may reflect stable characteristics of youth and families rather than short-term fluctuations in positive parenting. Families characterized by consistently high levels of positive parenting may also differ in other stable characteristics (e.g., family functioning, socioeconomic resources, broader environmental supports) that contribute to lower levels of delinquency. Therefore, while positive parenting is still important to the development of delinquency, these results suggest that the association between positive parenting and delinquency may operate differently from the dynamic processes observed for adverse parenting.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations should be considered. First, although the RI-CLPM provides an improvement over traditional cross-lagged models, the cross-lagged effects should not be interpreted as causal effects. The models cannot rule out unmeasured time-varying factors that influence both parenting and delinquency, and temporal precedence alone is insufficient to establish causality. 41, 52 Second, the EXT PGS was based on EUR-only GWAS summary statistics. EUR-only GWAS have decreased power and accuracy in non-EUR samples and has the potential to bias estimates from the pooled PGS analyses reported here. Therefore, these results will need to be replicated as multiancestry GWAS summary statistics become available.

In addition, ABCD sample is now in mid-adolescence (Mage = 16.58, SD = 0.94 at the most recent assessment), and data from the later waves of data collection remain incomplete. Thus, these analyses will need to be extended as additional waves become available, and the sample progresses through adolescence.

Taken together, these findings support a developmental model in which adolescent delinquency emerges from the intersection of stable individual differences, genetic propensity, and dynamic family processes. The results are broadly consistent with transactional models of development and demonstrate that reciprocity is not uniform across parenting behaviors.

Rather, the direction and strength of prospective associations depend on the specific parenting behavior being considered. The reciprocal association between poor monitoring and delinquency suggests that interventions may benefit from simultaneously addressing adolescent behavior and parental monitoring rather than treating either as an isolated target, consistent with intervention models that conceptualize adolescent externalizing problems within the broader family system. Moreover, because delinquency was associated with subsequent increases in corporal punishment, inconsistent discipline, and poor monitoring, interventions to help parents disrupt this feedback loop (e.g., the Family Check Up 53 ) may be particularly valuable for supporting effective parental responses to escalating adolescent behavior.

Key Points and Relevance

What's known?

• Parenting practices and adolescent delinquency are consistently associated, but these links may reflect both stable individual differences and dynamic developmental processes that previous studies have been unable to disentangle.

What's new?

• This study disentangled stable between-person associations from longitudinal withinperson processes across adolescence.

• Genetic propensity for externalizing was associated with stable differences in both delinquency and parenting, accounting for approximately 4%-8% of shared variance.

• Within-person associations varied by parenting dimension: delinquency was associated with increases in youth-reported corporal punishment, while delinquency and poor monitoring showed reciprocal associations.

What's relevant?

• Clinical interventions targeting adolescent delinquency may benefit from simultaneously addressing parenting behaviors and providing supports and resources specifically for parents to strengthen parental monitoring and promote consistent, non-punitive responses to adolescent behavior.
