Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a leading condition examined using network analysis.

However, meta-analytic evidence is limited for Major depressive disorder (MDD) symptom network studies. Following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses reporting guidelines, we searched Embase, MEDLINE, PsycINFO, CINAHL and bibliographies of previous literature for articles that have produced MDD symptom networks.

After screening, 153 articles (N = 1,580,674) were included in this review, consisting of 130 cross-sectional and 23 temporal network analysis studies. A random-effects Meta-analytic Gaussian Network Aggregation (MAGNA) of MDD symptom networks was estimated based on available data. Despite a pronounced variation in MDD measures and sample demographics, findings consistently showed that depressed mood emerged as the most central symptom. Collectively, this systematic review and meta-analysis underscores network analysis as a technique for understanding MDD symptom dynamics and one that has the potential to improve psychological interventions. Ongoing efforts toward intensive longitudinal datasets and improved reporting practices represent two key avenues for MDD network analysis research.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is comprised of multiple symptoms that can be expressed differentially (Athira et al., 2020; Jermy et al., 2021). According to the fifth edition of the

Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders Text Revised (DSM-5-TR; American

Psychiatric Association [APA], 2022), to reach the diagnostic threshold for MDD an individual must report experiencing at least five of nine symptoms, including either depressed mood or anhedonia over the same two-week period. These nine symptoms include: 1) depressed mood; 2) anhedonia; 3) loss or increase in appetite; 4) insomnia or hypersomnia; 5) psychomotor agitation; 6) fatigue; 7) feelings of worthlessness; 8) impaired concentration; and 9) suicidal ideation. A body of research has demonstrated that this categorisation of MDD has produced a high degree of variation in presentation (Buch & Conor, 2021; Goldberg 2011; Lynall & McIntosh, 2023). Consistent with the DSM-5 guidelines (APA, 2013), more than one thousand unique MDD phenotypes have been identified with individuals commonly endorsing distinct symptom profiles despite sharing the same MDD diagnosis (Fried & Nesse, 2015). The heterogeneity of MDD is further highlighted by the considerable difference in item content across depression instruments, with 52 distinct symptoms identified from seven widely used depression rating scales (Fried, 2017).

Consequently, ongoing research attempting to uncover the aetiology and prevent the associated outcomes of MDD (e.g., increased risk of chronic illness; Cui et al., 2024) may largely be aiming at a poorly defined target.

Network analysis has marked a paradigm shift to mental disorder diagnostic systems (Borsboom, 2008; Cramer et al., 2010). Under the network analysis paradigm, MDD is theorised to unfold out of complex interactions among clusters of symptoms (Marman & Rhemtulla, 2022). These networks are visualised structures, represented by nodes (i.e., symptoms) and edges (i.e., relationships between symptoms) which conjoin these nodes (Epskamp et al., 2017). Edges may be weighted to indicate the strength of association and directed to reflect predictive or causal relationships between nodes (Hevey, 2018). While MDD has traditionally been modelled as a latent construct where associations among symptoms occur due to a common cause, according to network analysis, associations instead occur due to direct causal relationships among symptoms (Zachar & Kendler, 2017). For example, depressed mood may cause insomnia which then causes impaired concentration.

Once these kinds of relationships become self-sustaining, a stable network structure is "activated" (Cramer et al., 2010a; McNally, 2016). While the network approach is not primarily concerned with reclassifying mental disorders, it offers both a theoretical and statistical framework for exploring symptom relationships and how they transition (Eaton et al., 2023).

Advantages of the Network Analysis Approach

Numerous properties of network analysis have been argued to provide advantage over latent modelling techniques such as structural equation modelling (SEM; Stein et al., 2017), especially for a MDD research context (Kim & Mackinnon, 2024; Park et al., 2021). Most prominent of these features includes node centrality, which indicates the relative influence of a symptom within a network structure (Epskamp & Fried, 2018). Standard centrality measures include: (i) strength, which measures how connected a given symptom is to other symptoms within the same network; (ii) betweenness, which identifies how frequently a symptom appears on the shortest path between other symptoms; (iii) closeness, which is a measure of the shortest path lengths from that symptom to other symptoms; and (iv) expected influence (EI), which was introduced specifically for psychopathological networks by accounting for both positive and negative edge weights (see Robinaugh et al., 2016).

Symptoms with high strength and EI centrality have been theorised to activate network structures (Boschloo et al., 2015) and cause the recurrence of mental disorders (Robinaugh et al., 2016). As such, symptoms are more strongly connected to other symptoms, allowing them to propagate more efficiently. It has been speculated that symptoms with high strength and EI centrality should therefore be considered candidate targets that may prevent the further development of mental disorders (Rodebaugh et al., 2018) including MDD when treated (Castro et al., 2014).

A second advantage of the network analysis approach is the detection of community structures (Briganti et al., 2024). Community structures are clusters of symptoms that are closely interconnected within a network structure, providing further information about the MDD phenotype under investigation. It is well-established that MDD can vary in presentation, with the DSM-5-TR introducing 'subtypes' and 'specifiers' to account for the inherent complexity in patient experiences of MDD (APA, 2022; Fiorillo et al., 2024), such as the melancholic specifier (e.g., profound anhedonia, early morning awakening, psychomotor changes). Though rarely applied within MDD network analysis research (Malgaroli et al., 2021), community detection may provide further information regarding the substructure of MDD.

Finally, network structures can be directly compared (Van Borkulo et al., 2023).

Network structures vary in connectivity (also referred to as global strength or density), structural configuration, and patterns of centrality (Epskamp et al., 2018). The direct comparison of network structures affords insight when exploring differences between groups (e.g., sex differences in depression) and within groups (e.g., changes to a depression network structure following an intervention). It has been suggested, for example, that depressive symptoms become more variable as network connectivity increases (Kelley et al., 2023).

Capturing such differences between network structures is an opportunity for further inferences for how a person or group experiences changes in depressive symptomatology.

Temporal MDD Network Analysis

MDD symptom networks are overwhelmingly modelled on cross-sectional data, classically estimated using gaussian graphical models (GGMs) or Ising models (see Hevey (2018) for an overview). While cross-sectional network analyses are often a fundamental starting point, MDD is a dynamic condition unlikely to be comprehensively examined crosssectionally (Fried, 2026). Cross-sectional network analyses are notably limited in (i) the extent to which predictive or causal relationships can be identified between symptoms and (ii) the capacity to detect within-person effects. Both limitations are especially problematic within psychopathological research for two reasons. First, the temporal sequencing of symptoms (i.e., the order in which symptoms present) is often not theoretically clear. For example, does anhedonia precede poor sleep quality, vice-versa, or is this relationship potentially bidirectional? Second, in certain instances, within-person and between-person effects may show opposite directions (i.e., Simpson's paradox; Kievit et al., 2013). A classic example of this can be seen within medical research: on the group level, exercise is associated with improved cardiovascular health. Conversely, on the individual level, a heartattack is most likely to occur during periods of exercise (Ebrahimi et al., 2021; Johansson et el., 2022).

The limitations of cross-sectional MDD network analysis generated a drive towards temporal network models based on longitudinal datasets, typically modelled using multilevel vector autoregressive (VAR) analysis or cross-lagged panel networks (CLPN; Wysocki et al., 2025). Within temporal network models, within-person and between-person symptom interactions are mapped across time. This allows for the inclusion of directed edges (where centrality is typically measured using in-strength and out-strength; Bringmann et al., 2019) and predictive relationships to be established between symptoms (Bringmann et al., 2015). A critical advantage permitted from this approach includes the uncovering of autoregressive effects (i.e., does a symptom at timepoint one predict itself at timepoint two) and crosslagged effects for each changepoint (i.e., does a symptom at timepoint one predict another symptom at timepoint two). For instance, fatigue in the morning may predict subsequent fatigue later in the day while depressed mood may precede later feelings of worthlessness.

Aims of the Current Review Malgaroli et al. (2021) published a systematic review consisting of 23 cross-sectional network analysis studies of MDD and found that depressed/low mood had a consistently high strength centrality. Malgaroli et al. (2021) also noted a high degree of variability in the network estimation technique used to model MDD symptoms and populations explored.

There are now several opportunities to expand upon this review. First, in the past five years there has been a notable increase in the number MDD network analysis studies (Aitken et al., 2025; Alcalde et al., 2024; Jang et al., 2024). This suggests a need for an updated review of the MDD network analysis literature. Second, Malgaroli et al. (2021) did not include temporal MDD network analyses in their review. Third, the recent emergence of quality appraisal tools for network analysis (Oostendorp et al., 2025) now enables assessment of the methodological rigour of the existing evidence base, which was not previously feasible.

Fourth, Malgaroli et al. (2021) could not conduct a meta-analysis given a lack of metaanalytic framework for network analysis. With the advent of Meta-analytic Gaussian Network Aggregation (MAGNA; Epskamp et al., 2022), a meta-analysis of MDD network analysis studies is now made possible.

The current systematic review and meta-analysis addresses these opportunities through the following aims: 1) summarise the characteristics of MDD symptom networks, specifying estimation technique, population of interest, and key reported outcomes; 2) undertake a quality appraisal for the studies included in this review, highlighting any inconsistencies in reporting; and 3) estimate a MAGNA of MDD symptom networks based on available cross-sectional data. Given that network analysis models are largely exploratory in nature, no a priori hypotheses were made for the current review.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was preregistered on PROSPERO (ID: X). Studies were systematically searched, screened, and selected for inclusion using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Page, 2021).

Search Strategy

Initial studies were identified through bibliographies of previous systematic reviews and the following databases on November 17, 2025: MEDLINE, PsycINFO, EMBASE, and CINAHL. The following Boolean search terms were used: (network structure OR network model OR network approach OR network analysis OR network analyses) AND (depression OR depressive disorder OR major depressive disorder OR depressive symptom) AND (crosssectional OR longitudinal). Full searches are provided in the Supplementary Materials Table S1. Searchers were updated on October 1, 2026.

To be eligible for inclusion for the systematic review, studies had to meet the following criteria: 1) be a published and original empirical study; 2) include a depression diagnosis or measurement of depressive symptoms; 3) conduct a symptom network analysis of symptoms consistent with the DSM-5 guidelines of MDD; 4) be a peer-reviewed study; 5) be written in English. Studies were excluded if they: 1) were a review article; 2) did not include at least one network analysis consistent with MDD symptoms; 3) included variables in the network other than symptoms consistent with MDD (e.g., comorbid network models).

Studies were first exported to Covidence and then screened based on study title and abstracts by three reviewers (CS, PLD, EL) independently. All studies that included a network analysis of depressive symptoms were included at this stage. There was no restriction based on date of publication. There was moderate interrater reliability for title and abstract screening (Cohen's Kappa 55 -65%). Two reviewers (CS, PLD) independently reviewed studies included in full text review (Cohen's Kappa 38%). Disagreements during both the screening and full-text review stages were resolved through consultation with third reviewer (CM).

Data Extraction and Analysis

The following information was extracted and cross-checked by two reviewers (CS, PLD) independently: 1) authors, year of publication, country, data source and recruitment setting; 2) demographic information, including population type, sample size, mean age, gender split; 3) methodological choices including measure of depressive symptoms, diagnostic criteria, analysis technique, number of models, number of nodes included in each network, and intervention for depression where applicable; 4) reported outcomes including most central symptoms, network stability, network characteristics (e.g., global strength), community structures, node predictability; and 5) open science practices, including whether the article was pre-registered, open access, and if data and code were publicly available.

Quality Assessment

For studies that included a cross-sectional network analysis, we adapted from the quality assessment criteria developed by Oostendorp et al. (2025). Within this quality assessment criteria, studies which include a cross-sectional network analysis are rated with respect to 1) sample and reporting quality; 2) quality of analysis; and 3) open science practices. Scores range from 0 to 18, where greater scores are indicative of higher quality network analysis practices. Two reviewers (CS, PLD) independently appraised the quality of the cross-sectional network studies included in the current review. Disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer (CM). As temporal network models use time-series data, they have different assumptions (e.g., stationarity) and allow for model fit checking. While there are currently no established quality assessment tools for temporal network analysis studies, we adapted from Oostendorp et al.'s (2025) guidelines in conjunction with a temporal network tutorial paper by Haslbeck et al. (2026) to form a basis for our quality assessment for temporal network studies included in this review. Consistent with Oostendorp et al.'s (2021), studies are rated with respect to 1) sample and reporting quality; 2) quality of analysis; and 3) open science practices. Scores within this quality appraisal range from 0 to 19, where greater scores are indicative of higher quality network analysis practices. Two reviewers (CS, ML) independently appraised the quality of the temporal network studies included in the current review. Disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer (CM). Further information about the quality assessment measures and procedures is provided in the Supplementary Materials (Table S4 and Table S5).

Node Strength Centrality Ranking

For the centrality rankings of MDD symptoms, we focused on strength centrality for cross-sectional networks and in-and out-strength for the temporal networks. Across the studies included in this review, we rank ordered the MDD symptoms in terms of relative centrality to other symptoms within the same network structure. Further information of this procedure is provided in the Supplementary Materials Files 1 and 2.

Meta-Analysis

To be eligible for inclusion in the meta-analysis, studies had to have either made their dataset openly available for use, provide raw datasets, or summary statistics (i.e., Pearson listwise correlation matrices) upon request. As the MAGNA framework (Epskamp et al., 2022) can only be estimated on cross-sectional data, only studies that estimated a crosssectional network were considered for analysis. Following the initial search, corresponding authors were contacted on 26 January 2026. Follow up emails were sent on 18 February 2026 to authors who did not respond to the initial request. For the updated search, corresponding authors were contacted on 6 October 2026.

All statistical analyses were conducted in R software (version 4.5.2: R Core Team, 2024). The meta-analysis was performed via the psychonetrics package in R software (Epskamp, 2020; Epskamp et al., 2022) and followed the MAGNA framework procedures as described in Epskamp et al. (2022) and Isvoranu et al. (2021). Under MAGNA, a pooled network analysis is estimated on raw correlation matrices drawn from individual network studies, weighted by the sample size for each dataset. Provided the large number of datasets, each varying with respect to sample size and participant characteristics, we estimated a random-effects MAGNA model which accounts for cross-study heterogeneity (pruning set to 0.5 to control for false discovery rate). Within this MAGNA, we plotted nine nodes to represent each MDD symptom described by the DSM-5-TR (APA, 2022). We note that while not all studies included in this analysis contained all nine MDD symptoms, full information likelihood estimation in MAGNA can handle missing data. Conversely, some depression measures used across studies have many items (e.g., the CESD-20; Radloff et al., 1977) to capture a larger spectrum of depressive symptomology. In such instances, we collapsed these items onto their corresponding core depressive symptom before generating a correlation matrix from which the MAGNA was estimated. A further breakdown of this procedure is provided in the Supplementary Materials File 3.

We visualised the random-effects MAGNA model of MDD using the R software package qgraph (Epskamp et al., 2012). Here, standard deviations between study correlation matrices were estimated as random-effects via cross-heterogeneity tests whereby greater scores indicate greater differences between correlation matrices. Consistent with standard network practices (Burger et al., 2023), we estimated strength, EI, betweenness, and closeness centralities for this MAGNA model (Epskamp et al., 2012). To identify community clusters nested within the MAGNA, a clique percolation method (CPM) was applied using the CliquePercolation package in R software (Lange, 2021). Under this method, the parameter k identifies the number of nodes that can form a community, and I (intensity) establishes the minimum edge-weight required for a node to be included within this community. Permutation tests were run to identify the optimal k and I values for the MAGNA. The CPM technique is argued to be preferable to other community detection analyses (i.e., walktrap algorithm; Pons & Latapy, 2005) as nodes can belong to multiple communities simultaneously. Further information regarding our MAGNA analyses including the parametric bootstrapping procedure is available on Open Science Framework (OSF) and in the Supplementary Materials File 3 (ID: X).

Results

A total of 5517 records were initially identified. After removing duplicates, 2166 titles and abstracts were screened for eligibility, and 173 records went on to full-text review. Of these, 153 studies were included in the final review, representing a total of 501 MDD networks (see Supplementary Materials 4 for a reference list of included studies). Figure 1 presents the PRISMA flow diagram of this selection process. Our search also indicated a proliferation in published cross-sectional and temporal MDD network analyses over the past decade, as displayed in Figure 2.

Study Characteristics and Design

Study characteristics and findings are reported in Tables S2 and S3, respectively. A total of 118 studies included cross-sectional MDD networks. Of these studies, 29 estimated cross-sectional networks at two separate time points in an intervention design (k = 14) and a longitudinal study design (k = 16). Most cross-sectional networks were estimated using GGM (k = 73) and based model selection on the Extended Bayesian Information Criteria (EBIC) (k = 80).

Twenty-three studies estimated temporal MDD networks (k = 23). The most common temporal network models were cross-lagged panel networks (CLPN) (k = 12), followed by panel and multilevel graphical vector autoregressive (GVAR) models (k = 7) (Aitken et al., 2025; Bringmann et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2026; Mathai et al., 2024; Quinn et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026a; Zhang et al., 2026a), dynamic time warp analysis (DTW) (k = 2) (Hebbrecht et al., 2020; van Zelst et al., 2022), dynamic exploratory graph analysis (k = 1) (Pereira et al., 2026), and time-lagged logistic regression model (k = 1) (Savelieva et al., 2021).

An additional 12 studies (k = 12) reported both temporal and cross-sectional network models (Chen et al., 2026; Dai et al., 2026; Feng et al., 2025; Jia et al., 2026; Peng et al., 2026; Quinn et al., 2026; Xie et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026a; Zhang et al., 2026b; Zhao et al., 2023). Among these studies, GGM was the most common method of estimating cross-sectional networks (k = 7), while CLPN was the most common type of temporal network (k = 9).

Participant Characteristics

The total number of participants included in the current review was 1,580,674 (M %female = 61.22%). Sample size ranged from 96 (Janssen et al., 2024) to 201,952 (Alcalde et al., 2024). The weighted mean age across studies was 35.27 years (SD = 24.84), with ages ranging from 10 to 90 years old. Most studies recruited from community samples (k = 113), followed by clinical samples (k = 34) and combined clinical and community samples (k = 6).

Participants reported receiving treatment for depression in 25 studies, most commonly medication (k = 14) and psychotherapy (k = 9).

Most studies were conducted in adult populations (k = 101). Some studies focused on adults with a diagnosis of MDD or endorsed moderate to severe depressive symptoms (k = 35), middle-aged and older adults (aged 45 and older) (k = 8), older adults only (aged 60 and older) (k = 16), adults with chronic disease or physical health conditions (k = 15), women in the perinatal (k = 12) and menopausal (k = 1) stage, university students (k = 3), and adult samples recruited during the COVID-19 pandemic (k = 7). Few of these studies included generic adult samples without further inclusion criteria (k = 9). One study recruited a mixed sample of high school students, university students, and older adults aged 60 and above during COVID-19 (Tao et al., 2023). The remaining 27 studies were conducted in adolescents or children, of which one study examined adolescents diagnosed with MDD or comorbid MDD and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) (Strouphauer et al., 2024), and two studies investigated depressive symptom networks in children or adolescents and their parents (Moradi et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2026a). Many studies were conducted in China (k = 46), followed by the US (k = 22), the Netherlands (k = 7), and the UK (k = 6).

Interventional Designs

Of the 14 studies that examined cross-sectional networks in an intervention design, nine studies evaluated the efficacy of biomedical therapies, including antidepressant medication (k = 5) (Berlim et al., 2021; Bos et al., 2018; Byrne et al., 2025; Kautzky et al., 2025; Madhoo & Levine, 2016), ketamine treatment (k = 3) (Curtiss et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026; Mathai et al., 2024), and electroconvulsive therapy (k = 1) (Lussignoli et al., 2025).

The remaining five studies evaluated the efficacy of psychotherapies, including cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT), a brief psychological treatment, and short-term psychoanalytical psychotherapy (k = 1) (Aitken et al., 2025), behavioural activation (k = 1) (Janssen et al., 2024), depression-specific and non-specific psychotherapy (k = 1) (Schumacher et al., 2023), an early psychological intervention for adolescents (k = 1) (Zhang et al., 2025b), and an internet-based emotion regulation intervention for adults (k = 1) (Xu et al., 2026a).

Depression Measures and Diagnostic Criteria

The majority of studies used self-report measures of depressive symptoms (k = 125), followed by clinician-rated instruments (k = 19). Five studies administered more than one measure of depressive symptoms (Feiten et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2024; Wrobel et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2021), of which three studies used both self-report and clinicianrated instruments (Feiten et al., 2021; Wrobel et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2021). A total of 18 self-report measures were used across studies, with the most frequently used being variants of the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ; k = 45) (Kroenke et al., 2001) and the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D; k = 41) (Radloff et al., 1977). Three versions of the PHQ-9 were used, including the PHQ-9 (k = 42) (Kroenke et al., 2001), the PHQ-8 excluding the last item assessing suicidal or self-injurious thoughts (k = 1) (Kroenke et al., 2009), and the PHQ-9 for adolescents (PHQ-9-A) (k = 2) (Johnson et al., 2002). Four versions of the CES-D were used, including the CES-D-8 (k = 10) (Turvey et al., 1999), CES-D-9 (k = 1), CES-D-10 (k = 20) (Andresen, 1994), and CES-D-20 (k = 11) (Radloff et al., 1977). Clinician-rated measures most commonly included the Composite International Diagnostic Interview (CIDI; k = 6) (Wittchen, 1994), the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D/HDRS; k = 5) (Hamilton, 1960), and the Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS; k = 5) (Montgomery & Åsberg, 1979).

Most studies did not require participants to have a diagnosis of MDD or endorse a minimum level of depressive symptoms at recruitment (k = 92). Among studies that used diagnostic interviews to select participants with MDD (k = 39), most were based on the DSM-IV (APA, 1994) or the DSM-IV-Text Revised (DSM-IV-TR; k = 27) (APA, 2000), while others relied on the DSM-III-TR (k = 1) (APA, 1987), DSM-5 (k = 3) (APA, 2022), and the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 (k = 7) (WHO, 2004). Among studies that recruited participants using a minimum threshold score on a depressive measure (k = 21), most employed the PHQ-9 (k = 9), followed by the CES-D (k = 4).

Stability

Most studies tested edge-weight accuracy using bootstrapped confidence intervals.

Likewise, with respect to node centrality stability, most studies (k = 110) used case dropping bootstraps and reported correlation-stability coefficients (CS-C). Of the 75 studies that reported CS-C values for strength centrality, the median CS-C value was 0.62 (IQR = 0.44-0.75), indicating adequate stability. Only six studies reported CS-C values below the recommended threshold of 0.25 (Bos et al., 2018; Gomez-Cumplido et al., 2024; Izquierdo et al., 2023; Kautzky et al., 2025; Liu & Le, 2025; Shukla et al., 2026).

Network Comparison, Community Detection Analysis and Node Predictability

Of the studies that conducted a network comparison test (k = 80), most did not report any significant structural differences in global strength between groups or changes in global strength across timepoints. A common comparison in network structures was made between males and females (k = 15) (Alcalde et al., 2024; Belvederi Murri et al., 2018; Cheung et al., 2021; Fong et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2023; Izquierdo et al., 2023; Lee & Hu, 2022; Manfro et al., 2021; van Zelst et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2021). Community detection analyses were rarely conducted (k = 15) (Belvederri Murri et al., 2021; Bos et al., 2018; Bringmann et al., 2015; Byrne et al., 2025; Feiten et al., 2021; Hakulinen et al., 2020; Hebbrecht et al., 2020; Kendler et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2026; Park & Kim, 2020; van Zelst et al., 2022; Whiston et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025; Yoon et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025), and fewer studies computed the predictability of nodes included within a network (k = 11) (Baez et al., 2021; Bos et al., 2018; Ebina et al., 2025; Feiten et al., 2021; Hakulinen et al., 2020; Hartung et al., 2019; Lin et al., 2026; Sharpley et al., 2022; Tao et al., 2023; Valencia et al., 2026; Vetter et al., 2021). Mean node predictability ranged from 21% (Bos et al., 2018; Vetter et al., 2021) to 81.0% (Ebina et al., 2025) across studies. Further details of these outcomes are reported in Table S3.

Discussion

Extending Malgaroli et al.'s (2021) review on MDD networks and other MAGNAs of anxiety and PTSD symptom networks (Belli et al., 2026; Isvoranu et al., 2021), this review is the first to apply the MAGNA technique to MDD and the first to examine temporal MDD symptom networks. A total of 501 networks across 153 studies were included in the current review.

Three primary findings are worth noting. First, central symptoms of MDD differed between cross-sectional and temporal networks with depressed mood and fatigue had the highest strength centralities across the 130 cross-sectional MDD network studies. In contrast, depressed mood and psychomotor agitation had the highest in-strength and out-strength centralities among the 23 studies that examined temporal MDD networks. Second, MAGNA of cross-sectional studies also highlighted depressed mood as the most central symptoms and indicated that cross-study edge heterogeneity was present. Third, there was notable variability in methodological designs, including MDD measures, network analysis estimation techniques, and standards of reporting.

Central Symptoms of MDD Differed Between Cross-sectional and Temporal Networks

A key finding of this review was the difference in symptom centrality between crosssectional and temporal MDD networks. In cross-sectional networks and consistent with Malgaroli et al. (2021), depressed mood and fatigue emerged with the highest strength centralities in cross-sectional networks. In contrast, depressed mood exhibited the highest instrength and out-strength centrality in temporal networks, closely followed by psychomotor agitation for in-strength and insomnia or hypersomnia for out-strength. Previous studies have identified similar discrepancies between cross-sectional and temporal symptom networks of MDD (Bos et al., 2017) and alcohol use disorder (Conlin et al., 2022). These findings suggest that cross-sectional and temporal network techniques capture different aspects of psychological disorder presentations and, as such, may serve different purposes. For instance, cross-sectional networks may be better suited to examine patterns of symptom co-occurrence across individuals, while temporal networks reflect how symptoms within a psychological disorder trigger each other over time (Bos et al., 2017). Echoing past research, the present findings caution the over-interpretation of cross-sectional MDD networks as representative of temporal changes or causal influences between symptoms and highlight the need for more temporal network models of MDD, particularly in identifying targets for intervention and delineating causal symptom dynamics that maintain MDD (Huang et al., 2023).

The prominence of depressed mood and fatigue in MDD symptom networks is not surprising. Depressed mood has been regarded as a hallmark feature of MDD. Under both the DSM-5-TR (APA, 2022) and the ICD-11 (WHO, 2022), either depressed mood or anhedonia is required in order to diagnose a depressive episode. Similarly, fatigue occurs in more than 90% of patients with MDD and can be a prodrome, a baseline symptom that presents within a major depressive episode, or a residual symptom following successful treatment of depression (Ghanean et al., 2018).

It should be noted, however, that fatigue in the context of MDD is heterogeneous, complex, and may overlap with the construct of psychomotor agitation depending on the instrument of use (Demyttenaere et al., 2005). For instance, in common self-report measures such as the CES-D (Radloff et al., 1977) and the PHQ-9 (Kroenke et al., 2001), items describing fatigue tend to converge on a perceived loss of energy or tiredness, which is congruent with the definition of fatigue in the DSM-5-TR (APA, 2022). In contrast, observerrating scales such as the HRDS (Hamilton, 1960) and the MADRS (Montgomery & Åsberg, 1979) assess fatigue as difficulty getting started or slowness in initiating and performing daily activities, which covers both fatigue and psychomotor retardation. A factor analysis of depressive symptoms as measured by the Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology (both clinician-rated and self-report), HDRS, and the BDI revealed that self-rated items measuring fatigue loaded on a latent "lack of energy" factor, while observer-rated items loaded on a separate "hedonic capacity" factor (Gullion & Rush, 1998). Similar issues have been identified with defining and measuring psychomotor dysfunctions in MDD (Douka et al., 2025). Consistent with Malgaroli et al. (2021), in this review, one approach taken to establish central nodes was through rank ordering the MDD symptoms in terms of relative centrality to other symptoms within the same network structure. Given the wide array of depression rating scales and differences in symptom coverage between instruments, the distinction in symptom centrality between cross-sectional and temporal MDD networks may not be as pronounced as the data suggests.

Symptoms with high strength centrality are argued to be candidate targets that psychological interventions should be structured around (Xu et al., 2026). Building on Malgaroli et al.'s (2021) review of cross-sectional networks, temporal network findings from the present review provide further support for the contribution of depressed mood to the onset, maintenance, and treatment of MDD. Evidence also highlights the potential importance of psychomotor agitation, aligning with accruing literature showing that psychomotor agitation can reliably predict treatment response to selective serotonin uptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and electroconvulsive therapy in MDD and bipolar disorder (Di Luch et al., 2026).

However, these interpretations are tentative, given the small number of studies that investigated temporal MDD networks (k = 23). Moreover, the clinical utility of a symptom's centrality is still not widely accepted within the literature, owing to a lack of consensus on how to interpret symptom centrality and tailor psychological interventions accordingly (Bringmann et al., 2019; Deblander & Hinne, 2019; Hallquist et al., 2019). For example, some research suggests that strength centrality is associated with disorder remission, while others proposed that strength centrality more closely reflects disorder onset (Boschloo et al., 2016).

MAGNA Highlights Depressed Mood as a Central Node and Indicates Cross-study Edge Heterogeneity

Partly congruent with the aggregated strength rankings reported above, the current MAGNA indicated that depressed mood had the highest strength centrality. Depressed mood also shared strong edges with fatigue and suicidal ideation, which aligns with considerable literature showing that depressed mood is a key predictive factor for suicidal ideation (Riera-Serra et al., 2024). Complementing the prior and only review of MDD networks by Malgaroli et al. (2021), the application of the MAGNA technique to MDD networks in this review enabled the quantification of edge heterogeneity between studies. Based on the randomeffects standard deviations in the current MAGNA, variability in edge strength was apparent among MDD network studies. Of note, the edge that conjoined anhedonia and feelings of worthlessness had the highest standard deviation, indicating variation in how this edge presents between individual MDD network structures. Although there has yet to be a threshold for determining low, moderate, and high level of edge heterogeneity at the time of conducting this review, the MAGNA results indicate that the generalisability of individual MDD network analyses should be interpreted with caution.

Stable community structures were not detected in the current MAGNA, contrasting robust community structures that were identified in individual MDD networks (Bos et al., 2018; Hakulinen et al., 2020; Hebbrecht et al., 2020; Kendler et al., 2018; Belvederi Murri et al., 2021). One reason for this discrepancy is that the current MAGNA only included nine MDD symptoms to reflect the symptoms described by the DSM-5-TR guidelines (APA, 2022). Many prior studies that identified communities used measures with higher item counts. For example, Bos et al. (2018) and Hebbrecht et al. (2020) used the BDI-II, which consists of 21-items. As MDD instruments capture a larger spectrum of depressive symptomology, modelling MDD networks based on higher item counts may increase the likelihood that non-trivial communities are formed.

Methodological Differences between Studies

Similar to Malgaroli et al. (2021), there was considerable inconsistency in measurement scales used to derive MDD networks. In particular, cross-sectional and temporal networks in the current review were constructed from a total of 23 depression rating scales, including self-report and clinician-rated measures. The heterogeneity in measures of MDD symptoms poses several concerns regarding the validity of our findings. First, research suggests that self-report ratings of depressive symptomatology tend to be more severe and identify lower efficacy of depression treatments than observer reports (Levis et al., 2020; Miguel et al., 2025; von Glischinski et al., 2021). Second, depression measurement scales often cover different content and have been shown to have weak correlations with one another (Fried et al., 2016; Furukawa et al., 2019; Sayer et al., 1993). This partly reflects the atheoretical nature of depression scale development and the different purposes for formulating different scales (Fried et al., 2022). The HDRS, for instance, was developed as a benchmark for antidepressant trials in patients already diagnosed with depression and heavily emphasises observations of somatic and behavioural features by the clinician (Hamilton, 1960). In contrast, the BDI primarily captures cognitive and affective symptoms (Beck, 1961). Compared to both the HDRS and the BDI, the CES-D focuses on self-report problems that are more common in non-clinical settings, such as feeling lonely or bothered, due to its intended purpose of being a depression screening tool in the general population (Radloff et al., 1977).

A review of the seven most commonly used depression rating scales, including the CES-D, HDRS, and BDI, found that the scales encompassed 52 distinct MDD symptoms, 40% of which is covered in only one scale (Fried, 2017). The potential for self-report ratings to yield different results from observer reports and low symptom overlap among depression scales may have confounded our results on symptom centrality. Whilst we have attempted to unify measures by mapping their respective items onto the nine DSM-5-TR criteria for MDD (APA, 2022), not all measures neatly corresponded to these symptom domains. This aligns with the previous finding that content overlap between the most common depression scales and core MDD symptoms defined by the DSM-5 is only moderate (Fried, 2017). Moreover, the DSM-5 has been developed independently of theories and primarily relies on expert and clinical consensus (Kendler & Solomon, 2016). Taken together, these evidence highlights an urgent need for explicit theories of depression to unify the way we diagnose, monitor, and study depression (Fried, 2017). Until such objective is achieved, the results of this review should be interpreted with caution.

While the overall standards of reporting were moderate across the included studies, the quality appraisal tools highlighted inconsistency surrounding the handling of missing data and risk of Berkson's Bias for studies investigating clinical samples, potentially biasing the final samples (Ron et al., 2019). These findings are consistent with Belli et al.'s (2026) review on symptoms of generalised anxiety disorder. Accordingly, future network analysis research should make an effort to recruit more representative samples and handle missing data through the appropriate means (e.g., multiple imputation). For temporal network analysis studies specifically, very few accounted for uncertainty measures or systematic model checking. This aligns with previous findings by Haslbeck et al. (2026) which found that very few studies using VAR performed model checking. These issues may reflect the fact that many network analysis studies were published prior to Burger et al.'s (2023) reporting standards for cross-sectional network analysis, and there are currently no widely accepted quality appraisal tools for temporal network analysis studies.

Limitations of the Current Review

There are several limitations of the current review. First, although our review included 117 cross-sectional studies, only 14 studies were included in our MAGNA. This relatively small number of available datasets prevented MAGNA sub-analyses that could have been clinically relevant. For example, both Isvoranu et al. (2021) and Belli et al. (2026) compared MAGNAs between clinical and non-clinical samples. While the MAGNAs of these subgroups were similar, it is unclear if these trends would replicate in an MDD MAGNA as presented in the current review.

Second, our review did not include grey literature or studies not written in English, and many the datasets included in the current MAGNA were recruited from the same cohort (e.g., the CHARLS; Zhao et al., 2014). Because these samples were drawn from the same cohort, we cannot rule out the potential of a clustering effect (Jak et al., 2013) that may have inflated certain characteristics of the MAGNA (e.g., edge strength). Noting this, we were careful to not violate the assumption of independence by only including each dataset once in our MAGNA.

Third, MAGNA is currently limited in its ability to produce heterogeneity analyses typical of standard meta-analytic approaches (Epskamp et al., 2022; Martinez et al., 2022).

Within MAGNA, because cross-study heterogeneity is indexed based on pooled correlation matrices as opposed to raw data, conventional moderation analysis using mean age, proportion of females and other clinical features seen in other meta-analyses could not be conducted. While most studies recruited from community samples, many studies in the current review included participants with formal MDD diagnoses (Bos et al., 2018; Bringmann et al., 2015; Hao et al., 2025) or other medical conditions (Aitken et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). A useful avenue for future research would be to include individual participant data (IPD) and the examination of clinical moderators into MAGNA, when such an analysis is feasible.

Future Directions

A relatively large share of the studies included in this review performed some form of NCT (k = 79), with gender differences in network structure and global strength being a typical comparison. However, evidence on the relationship between the severity of a disorder and the global strength of a network has been mixed. Some research demonstrates that network global strength directly corresponds to symptom intensity (Robinaugh et al., 2020; van Borkulo et al., 2015), while other studies demonstrate this relationship is not as linear as presumed (Bos et al., 2018; Deflorin et al., 2025; McElory et al., 2019). The data in the current study suggest that the global strength of an MDD network is not necessarily tied to more severe MDD symptomatology, as indicated by a general lack of significant change in global strength post-intervention. Instead, the global strength of an MDD network may better represent symptom variability (Kelley et al., 2023) or network temperature (Grimes et al., 2025). Here, a further integration of psychological interventions and network analysis is a necessary step for refining our understanding of MDD networks and how this relates MDD symptom severity.

While the current review underscores the ascendancy of MDD network analysis research, the past decade has also seen a major increase in comorbid network analyses (Beard et al., 2016; Kaiser et al., 2021; Strathdee et al., 2026). One key advantage of comorbid networks is the potential to uncover 'bridge' symptoms, which are theorised to conjoin symptom clusters from distinct conditions (Jones et al., 2021). In this regard, bridge symptoms are said to help explain comorbid symptom profiles and are considered transdiagnostic (O'Driscoll et al., 2021). This influx of comorbid symptom network analyses may eventuate in comorbid MAGNAs, with the potential to converge on bridge symptoms. Systematic reviews for comorbid depression and anxiety symptom networks have already been established (Cai et al., 2024). Meta-analyses of comorbid networks using the MAGNA technique may be a natural progression.

Another area that network analysis research can improve on is open research practices

and open science practices. Echoing the issues raised by Malgaroli et al. (2021), only a handful of studies included in the current review made their correlation matrices available in the supplementary materials, constraining the statistical power of the current study's MAGNA. Improvements in MAGNAs will likely require collective efforts to report matrices where feasible. While quality appraisal tools are a recent innovation with this field (Burger et al., 2023), they may prove fundamental to orienting researchers to this task of improving research transparency.

Clinical Implications

Notwithstanding the methodological caveats of the current meta-analytic review, the current findings provide some useful insights for monitoring and treating depressive symptoms. Network analysis was partly developed as a tool to improve interventions, such that central symptoms can be regarded as key treatment targets (Borsboom & Cramer, 2013; Briganti et al., 2024; Bringmann et al., 2022). Based on the reviewed literature, interventions focusing on depressed mood, fatigue, and psychomotor agitation should be highly effective for treating MDD. To date, CBT remains the most extensively researched nonpharmacological treatment for depression and one of the most efficacious interventions for depressive symptoms (Cuijpers et al., 2023). Evidence from network intervention analyses suggests that CBT can have a symptom-specific effect (Scott et al., 2022; Lancee et al., 2022).

However, as only 14 studies included in the current review were interventional in design, it is still unclear whether interventions could target single symptoms and which interventions demonstrate superior efficacy for treating MDD (Bringmann & Eronen, 2018; Cuijpers et al., 2023). Interventions that have demonstrated efficacy in reducing MDD include mindfulness-based approaches, relaxation exercises, behavioural activation, or a combination of these strategies and cognitive restructuring in a CBT protocol. To better evaluate the clinical utility of network analysis, MDD network analyses would optimally be estimated, and psychological interventions are subsequently tailored to the information conveyed from the MDD network's structure. For instance, researchers might first identify the most central symptoms in an MDD network, and then directly target those symptoms through a symptom-focused cognitive behavioural therapy (Kaiser et al., 2022; Lancee et al., 2022; Scott et al., 2022). This will likely provide clarity on the behaviour of an MDD network structures over the course of an intervention.

Conclusion

The current review adds to the field of network analysis through providing the first MAGNA of MDD, highlighting the central role of depressed mood and adding to the growing MAGNA research (Belli et al., 2026; Isvoranu et al., 2021). With the development of MDD symptom network analysis research, both cross-sectional and longitudinal in design, this review highlights areas where current literature converges and diverges. With many areas across MDD network analysis research that remain for improvement, further efforts toward temporal network analysis and adherence to standard network analysis reporting practices are two fruitful directions. Together, developments within these places may allow for a more unified and clinically actionable research agenda for MDD.
