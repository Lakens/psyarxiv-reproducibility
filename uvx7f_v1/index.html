Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Background: Cannabis use and binge eating often co-occur; however, the order and timing of their onset are not well described in population-based samples, limiting our understanding of potential risk pathways between the two behaviors.

Method: We examined the order and timing of onset of these behaviors among 12,314 adults from the Collaborative Psychiatric Epidemiology Surveys. Cannabis initiation and recurrent binge eating (i.e., binge eating at least twice weekly for 3 months) were dated using ages of onset reported at a single interview. Survey-weighted survival and discretetime models estimated cumulative probabilities and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) for onset of each behavior after the other.

Results: Among 251 adults reporting both behaviors, cannabis initiation came first for 62.3% (95% CI [52.9, 71.7]) and binge eating came first for 33.2% (95% CI [23.8, 42.5]). The median interval between behaviors was 4 to 5 years. Within 10 years of cannabis initiation, 2.6% (95% CI [1.7, 3.6]) had reported binge eating onset; in contrast, within 10 years of binge eating onset, 35.6% (95% CI [24.7, 45.0]) had reported cannabis initiation. Prior cannabis initiation was associated with higher annual odds of binge eating onset (OR = 2.23, 95% CI [1.46, 3.39], p < .001), and prior binge eating was associated with higher annual odds of cannabis initiation (OR = 1.61, 95% CI [1.07, 2.42], p = .024). In secondary analyses, the association of prior cannabis initiation with binge eating onset was significant among women (OR = 2.63, p < .001) but not men (OR = 1.81, p = .113). The sex interaction was not significant after correction (q = .063).

Conclusions: Each behavior was associated with later onset of the other, suggesting a twoway association rather than a single direction of influence. Thus, clinicians treating either behavior should consider asking about the other. However, because onset ages were recalled, prospective research is critical to test causal direction.

Introduction

Binge eating is defined as eating an unusually large amount of food in a discrete period while experiencing a subjective sense of loss of control over eating (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). When binge eating recurs at least once weekly for 3 months, it is the defining feature of binge eating disorder and a core feature of bulimia nervosa (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Binge eating disorder is common in the United States; in the National Comorbidity Survey Replication, up to 2.8% of adults met lifetime criteria for the diagnosis (Hudson et al., 2007). In contrast, in the larger and more recent third National Epidemiologic Survey on Alcohol and Related Conditions, lifetime prevalence was 0.85% for binge eating disorder and 0.28% for bulimia nervosa (Udo & Grilo, 2018).

Importantly, many adults also report regular binge eating below these diagnostic thresholds (Keski-Rahkonen, 2021), indicating that binge eating spans a spectrum of severity. Binge eating disorder and other specified feeding or eating disorders (OSFED) together account for most eating disorder cases and disability worldwide (Santomauro et al., 2021). OSFED includes sub-threshold (e.g., lower frequency) presentations of binge eating disorder and bulimia nervosa; as such, the burden of binge eating extends well beyond the full diagnoses.

Binge eating rarely occurs in isolation. A meta-analysis of prevalence studies documented that substance use disorders commonly co-occur with eating disorders (Bahji et al., 2019). The order in which these problems emerge also matters for care. In a Danish register cohort of 20,759 patients with eating disorders and 83,038 matched controls, anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, and unspecified eating disorders were each followed by elevated risk of illicit substance use disorders over the next 10 years (Skøt et al., 2022). This finding indicates that an eating disorder can precede substance use problems and that elevated risk for illicit substance use disorders may then persist for a decade. Shared biology may also contribute to this clinical overlap. For example, a recent genome-wide study reported a positive genetic correlation between cannabis initiation and anorexia nervosa with binge eating (Munn-Chernoff et al., 2021). Such a correlation suggests that some of the same inherited influences may raise risk for both behaviors.

Cannabis is a particularly plausible contributor to binge eating given its direct effects on appetite. In a randomized, placebo-controlled trial of 82 adults, vaporized cannabis increased food intake irrespective of dose or gender (Hume et al., 2025). Parallel rodent experiments traced this effect to greater reward value of food and greater motivation to obtain it (Hume et al., 2025). These results identify a potential biological route by which cannabis could amplify the drive for food. While greater appetite does not by itself amount to binge eating, an increase in food reward offers a credible path from cannabis use to binge eating given that food reward appears to be heightened among individuals who binge eat.

For example, a systematic review of neuroimaging studies found that individuals who binge eat show structural and functional changes in the brain reward system that are linked to greater sensitivity to food (Leenaerts et al., 2022). Patients with binge eating disorder also showed greater pleasantness and desire to eat in response to food pictures than controls when negative emotions were induced (Arend et al., 2022). Together, these studies suggest that individuals prone to binge eating already respond strongly to food reward; as such, a cannabis-related rise in that reward could matter more for them than for others.

Cross-sectional studies are consistent with this idea but cannot confirm it. Among 1,568 young adults, 24% of women who used cannabis reported binge eating compared with 13% of women who did not. No such association appeared among men (Elran-Barak et al., 2025). These results indicate that the two behaviors are linked among young women, and as such sex is an important factor to examine here. A second study points to a possible pathway. Among 805 college students who used cannabis, cannabis-related problems were linked with eating pathology through loss-of-control eating (Maynard et al., 2023). In other words, loss of control over eating may be the step that connects problematic cannabis use to other disordered eating symptoms. Cannabis use is also common among individuals already seeking treatment for binge eating. Among 165 treatment-seeking adults, more than 23% reported cannabis use in the previous 3 months; however, cannabis use was not associated with eating disorder symptoms in that sample (Wilkinson et al., 2024). This finding suggests that cannabis use is common among individuals who binge eat but may not mark more severe symptoms. Thus, its role may be better examined through timing than through severity.

All of the above studies measured both behaviors at a single time point and, as such, cannot establish which behavior came first. Existing prospective evidence partially addresses this limitation but points in the opposite direction (i.e., from binge eating to later cannabis use). In a cohort of 16,882 adolescents and young adults, binge eating predicted later initiation of cannabis use (OR = 1.85, 95% CI [1.27, 2.67]; Sonneville et al., 2013). This result suggests that binge eating may itself raise the likelihood of later trying cannabis. No studies to date have examined the reverse longitudinal pathway, wherein cannabis use may raise risk for later binge eating.

Taken together, these findings leave the direction of influence unsettled. Appetite research suggests that cannabis could lead to binge eating, whereas prospective data suggest the reverse. Further, several underlying mechanisms that may drive cannabis use and binge eating also lead to ambiguous directionality. For example, emotional distress consistently precedes binge eating (Borm et al., 2025), and depression is associated with past-month cannabis use among US adults (Gorfinkel et al., 2020). Thus, distress alone could generate both behaviors in either order, and shared genetic risk as described above could do the same (Munn-Chernoff et al., 2021). Given that these accounts predict different orders and timing of onset, describing the actual sequence is a critical first step. This task has become increasingly urgent as the popularity and legality of cannabis use have grown.

In 2022, daily or near-daily cannabis users outnumbered daily or near-daily drinkers for the first time (Caulkins, 2024). As such, more individuals are exposed to any effect of cannabis on eating than in earlier decades. Notably, binge eating has also become more common. In six surveys of Australian adults conducted between 1998 and 2015, the prevalence of binge eating increased over time (Mitchison et al., 2017). Thus, the overlap between the two behaviors may matter for a growing number of individuals.

To address this gap, the current study used the Collaborative Psychiatric Epidemiology Surveys to answer three questions. First, what was the usual order and interval between cannabis initiation and recurrent binge eating onset among adults who reported both? Second, how likely was the second behavior to begin within 1, 5, and 10 years after the first? Third, after adjustment for age, sex, birth cohort, race and ethnicity, and source survey, was the onset of one behavior associated with later onset of the other? Secondary analyses also examined the role of sex and age at cannabis initiation. Given that onset dates for both binge eating and cannabis use were retrospectively self-reported at a single interview, this study should be considered descriptive and hypothesis-generating.

Methods

Participants

Data came from the public-use Collaborative Psychiatric Epidemiology Surveys (CPES), which combine the National Comorbidity Survey Replication, the National Latino and Asian American Study, and the National Survey of American Life (NSAL) (Alegria et al., 2024; Heeringa et al., 2004). The combined data set is labeled 2001 to 2003. These population surveys used probability sampling and a shared interview framework to assess adults in the United States. We examined the reported order of cannabis initiation and recurrent binge-eating onset, the interval between them, and their associations with subsequent annual onset.

The analysis file contained 20,013 records. Of these, 12,591 had responses to both lifetime screening questions, an observed interview age, and a positive CPES long-form sampling weight. Eligibility reflected questionnaire administration as well as response availability. The NSAL White sample was not administered the substance-use or eatingdisorder sections and was therefore not represented in this analysis (Alegria et al., 2024).

Refused, unknown, and other negative response codes were treated as missing. Ages of onset below 5 years or above age at interview were considered unusable. We excluded 277 participants who reported either behavior without a usable onset age, leaving 12,314 adults. Onset ages were not imputed. All model covariates were observed in this final sample.

Onset histories were collected at a single adult interview. We reconstructed the time between behaviors from reported ages, rather than following participants prospectively. Eligibility for each elapsed-time analysis depended on which behavior had begun first. The age-based models retained the full eligible sample and treated prior onset as a predictor that changed over time.

This study was a secondary analysis of de-identified, publicly available data from the CPES and thus does not constitute human subjects research. Informed consent for the original data collection was obtained by the study investigators, and the original protocols were approved by the relevant ethics boards.

Measures

Cannabis initiation. The substance-use section of the World Health Organization Composite International Diagnostic Interview (CIDI) assessed lifetime marijuana or hashish use and age at first use (Kessler & Üstün, 2004; National Comorbidity Survey). We used these two items to identify whether cannabis had ever been used and the reported age of initiation. Yes and no responses defined lifetime use. The age item supplied the onset year for respondents reporting use. Cannabis initiation did not require regular use or a diagnosis of cannabis use disorder.

Recurrent binge eating. The CIDI was developed around DSM-IV criteria, but the recurrent-binge measure used here does not establish a DSM-IV or DSM-5 diagnosis of binge-eating disorder (Hudson et al., 2007; Kessler & Üstün, 2004; National Comorbidity Survey; Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2016). The lifetime screening item asked about binge eating at least twice per week for several months. The onset question asked when this pattern first lasted for 3 months or longer at any time in the participant's life (Hudson et al., 2007; National Comorbidity Survey). This was not restricted to the 3 months before the interview. DSM-IV research criteria required binge eating on at least 2 days per week for 6 months, whereas DSM-5 requires at least one episode per week for 3 months (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2016). We used the recurrent-binge screening item and the numeric onset-age field available in the extract.

Alternative numeric onset reports were not combined. We did not apply the additional diagnostic criteria concerning loss of control, distress, or compensatory behaviors, and no summed questionnaire score was calculated. The available extract did not contain the information needed to reclassify participants under DSM-5 criteria, and reclassification using the original respondent-level data was not performed.

Available onset ages were analyzed as whole years. Same-age reports were retained as a separate sequence because within-year ordering could not be established.

Subsequent onset meant that the second behavior began at a later reported age than the first. For example, cannabis initiation at age 16 followed by recurrent binge eating at age 20 gave an interval of 4 years. The CIDI includes exact and approximate onset-age questions (National Comorbidity Survey). The CPES codebook lists separate fields for approximate onset ages, but these fields were not included in the supplied extract (Alegria et al., 2024).

The reported results use the exact-age field only. Some participants excluded because this field was missing may have provided an approximate age elsewhere in the original data. The original respondent-level file was unavailable for verification, so the approximate-age responses could not be checked. Adjustment factors. Models included sex, source survey, approximate birth cohort, race and ethnicity, and a flexible function of age. Birth year was approximated as 2003 minus interview age and grouped as 1944 or earlier, 1945 to 1959, 1960 to 1974, or 1975 onward. Ancestry was grouped as non-Latino White, Black, Latino, and Asian or other. Black included African American and Afro-Caribbean respondents, and Latino included Cuban, Puerto Rican, Mexican, and other Hispanic respondents. Education, cigarette smoking, alcohol use, psychiatric symptoms, and childhood adversity were not included.

Statistical Analysis

Survey design. Analyses followed the CPES guidance for design-based analysis (Heeringa & Berglund, 2007). We accounted for the complex survey design (Lumley, 2010) and used R version 4.5.1 (R Core Team, 2025), survey version 4.5 (Lumley), and survival version 3.8-3 (Therneau, 2024). We retained the CPES long-form sampling weight, strata, and primary sampling units, with clusters nested within strata. The CPES user guide identifies the long-form weight as the weight for pooled analyses involving Part II variables (Heeringa & Berglund, 2007). The available documentation did not establish whether this weight also accounted for the additional selection into the eating-disorder module within Part II. No separate module-selection adjustment was applied, and whether any further adjustment is required remains unconfirmed. The original weights were used without an additional adjustment for onset-age availability. Design-based variance estimation used Taylor linearization and the survey package adjustment for strata with a single primary sampling unit. Counts are unweighted. Percentages, interval quantiles, survival estimates, and regression coefficients used sampling weights.

Reported sequence and intervals. Among participants reporting both behaviors, we classified onset as cannabis first, binge eating first, or the same reported age. We calculated weighted proportions with 95% confidence intervals. Within each strictly ordered sequence, the positive difference between onset ages supplied the interval summarized by a weighted median and interquartile range. These estimates are conditional on reporting both onsets by interview. We also described onset ages and interval ranges within each sequence.

Elapsed time to the second onset. We formed two risk sets. The cannabis-to-bingeeating analysis included participants who had initiated cannabis and either had no recurrent binge eating or reported binge-eating onset at a later age. The reverse analysis included participants with recurrent binge eating who either had never used cannabis or initiated it at a later age. Each analysis excluded participants whose second behavior occurred earlier or at the same reported age, and those with no positive observed elapsed time. Time began at the first behavior. An event occurred at the second onset, and participants without that onset were censored at age at interview. These analyses used all available elapsed time without the age-60 cap used in the age-based models below.

We estimated survey-weighted survival functions using the hazard-based Aalen estimator implemented by svykm with standard errors (Lumley). Cumulative onset probability was one minus survival. Confidence limits were calculated on the log-survival scale and transformed to probabilities. We report estimates at 1, 5, and 10 years, with unweighted numbers at risk. Curves were not covariate-adjusted. Censoring at interview assumes that, within the represented population, remaining observation time is not related to an unobserved second onset in a way that distorts the estimates. The two curves describe different eligible populations and were not compared as a test of causal direction.

Numbers at risk included participants whose event or censoring time was at least the stated year. We also reported unweighted event counts and the observed duration from first onset to the second onset or interview. This duration includes censored observations. It differs from the interval among participants reporting both behaviors.

Age-based bidirectional models. We fitted two models using a discrete-time survival framework (Singer & Willett, 1993). Participants contributed one record for each year of age from 5 through the earliest of outcome onset, age at interview, or age 60. An event indicator identified the outcome-onset year. The other behavior was a time-varying predictor that became positive only in the year after its reported onset. Thus, same-age onsets did not contribute as exposed outcome events. The primary models estimated a common prioronset odds ratio across age. Separate survey-weighted logistic models used a quasibinomial distribution with a logit link and the adjustment factors described above. The age spline had boundary knots at 5 and 60, with internal knots at 14, 23, and 35 for the binge-eating model and 12, 21, and 34 for the cannabis model. Reference categories were male sex, birth cohort 1944 or earlier, Asian or other ancestry, and the National Comorbidity Survey Replication. The survey design accounted for repeated person-year records within the original sampling clusters. Exponentiated coefficients are adjusted odds ratios for onset within a year, not Cox hazard ratios. Regression confidence intervals used the coefficient plus or minus 1.96 standard errors. Two-sided p values used the survey model degrees of freedom.

Secondary analyses. The cannabis-to-binge-eating model was repeated separately for women and men, omitting sex from the adjustment set. A further model replaced prior cannabis initiation with three time-varying categories. Person-years before cannabis initiation or among never users were the reference, compared with years after initiation at age 15 or older and years after initiation before age 15. All other covariates and the age spline were unchanged. In post hoc analyses, we tested a prior-initiation-by-sex interaction in a pooled model with the same covariates and directly compared the two initiation-age coefficients in the categorical model.

Among participants aged at least 30 at interview, we estimated the weighted proportion reporting binge-eating onset by age 30, separately for cannabis initiation before age 18 and no initiation before age 18. The latter included later initiators and never users.

These unadjusted proportions used logit-transformed confidence intervals. Because this comparison did not require cannabis initiation to precede binge eating, it was treated as descriptive.

All regression tests were two-sided, with p below .05 considered nominally significant. Confidence intervals were 95%. Primary and original secondary p values were not corrected for multiple testing. As an additional check, we applied the Benjamini-Hochberg correction to the two primary directional tests. The two new post hoc subgroup comparisons were corrected as a separate family of two, with q below .05 indicating significance after correction (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). These additional analyses were not prespecified.

Model sensitivity checks. We repeated both directional models without the age-60 cap, after excluding all participants who reported both onsets at the same age, and using 2001 instead of 2003 to approximate birth year. We also used age splines with 3 or 5 degrees of freedom instead of 4 and fitted models with a complementary log-log link. Splines were recalculated in each model. Other covariates and the one-year exposure lag were unchanged. Logit models are reported as odds ratios; exponentiated complementary log-log coefficients describe associations on a proportional-hazards scale. We checked convergence and model-matrix rank. To assess the effect of unavailable onset ages on reported ordering, we calculated conservative bounds that allowed any sequence for excluded participants reporting both behaviors. These bounds did not use partial onset information and are not confidence intervals.

In further post hoc checks, we added interactions between prior onset and the four age-spline terms to each directional model. Joint survey-adjusted Wald F tests assessed age variation. Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied to these two tests as a separate exploratory family. These checks did not replace the primary models or change the sampling weights.

Results

Sample and Reported Onset Sequence

The final sample included 12,314 adults, comprising 2,898 from the National Comorbidity Survey Replication, 4,556 from the National Latino and Asian American Study, and 4,860 from the National Survey of American Life. The weighted mean age was 44.0 years, and 52.7% were women. Participants ranged from 18 to 97 years. The weighted racial and ethnic distribution was 56.9% non-Latino White, 17.6% Latino, 16.7% Black, and 8.9% Asian or other.

Exclusions for unusable cannabis use and/or binge eating onset ages represented 2.2% of the initially eligible sample. Exclusion was more common among participants reporting recurrent binge eating. Of 731 initially eligible participants endorsing this behavior, 236 (32.3%) were excluded. The extracted binge-eating onset field was missing for 230 and outside the analysis range for 4. Two additional participants had a usable binge-eating onset but an unusable cannabis onset. Among the 367 initially eligible participants reporting both behaviors, 116 (31.6%) were excluded. The onset-sequence and interval estimates below describe the remaining 251 participants. The available data is not able to establish how many excluded participants supplied an approximate onset age in a separate field.

In unweighted counts, 4,418 participants reported lifetime cannabis use and 495 reported recurrent binge eating. In unweighted counts, 7,652 reported neither behavior, 4,167 reported cannabis use only, 244 reported recurrent binge eating only, and 251 reported both. Cannabis began first in 152, binge eating first in 82, and both began at the same reported age in 17. After weighting, these groups represented 62.3% (95% CI [52.9%, 71.7%]), 33.2% (95% CI [23.8%, 42.5%]), and 4.6% (95% CI [1.2%, 7.9%]) of participants with both behaviors and usable onset ages, respectively. Among cannabis-first cases, the weighted median interval to recurrent binge eating was 5 years, with an interquartile range of 3 to 10 years. Among binge-eating-first cases, the median interval to cannabis initiation was 4 years, with an interquartile range of 3 to 9 years (Table 1).

Among cannabis-first cases, the unweighted median age at cannabis initiation was 16 years and the median age at recurrent binge-eating onset was 22 years. Among bingeeating-first cases, the corresponding medians were 14.5 years for binge eating and 18 years for cannabis initiation. Participants reporting both onsets at the same age had a median onset age of 16 years.

Positive intervals ranged from 1 to 33 years when cannabis began first and from 1 to 20 years when binge eating began first. The unweighted median intervals were 6 and 4 years, respectively. Across all participants reporting both behaviors, the weighted median onset ages were 17 years for cannabis and 19 years for recurrent binge eating. These pooled ages include both sequences and same-age reports.

The 62.3% cannabis-first estimate was restricted to participants with usable onset ages. Allowing any ordering for the 116 excluded participants who reported both behaviors gave conservative bounds of 40.2% to 75.7% for the weighted cannabis-first proportion among all 367. These bounds do not establish that cannabis began first in a majority of the broader group.

Elapsed Time From Cannabis Initiation to Binge Eating

The cannabis-first risk set included 4,303 participants, of whom 152 subsequently reported recurrent binge eating. The remaining participants were censored at interview. The estimated cumulative probability of binge-eating onset was 0.5% within 1 year (95% CI [0.2%, 0.7%]), 1.8% within 5 years (95% CI [1.2%, 2.5%]), and 2.6% within 10 years (95% CI [1.7%, 3.6%]). At 10 years, 3,223 participants remained at risk immediately before that time (Table 2 and Figure 1).

Of the 4,418 participants who had initiated cannabis, 82 reported earlier binge eating and 17 reported both onsets at the same age. An additional 16 had no positive interval between cannabis initiation and interview and were not included in the elapsed-time analysis. Among the 4,303 eligible participants, 4,151 had no reported second onset and were censored at interview. The unweighted median observed duration from cannabis initiation to binge-eating onset or interview was 20 years, with an interquartile range of 9 to 27 years and a range of 1 to 76 years.

The unweighted numbers reporting binge-eating onset within 1, 5, and 10 years were 18, 75, and 101, respectively. Numbers at risk decreased from 4,303 at year 1 to 3,918 at year 5 and 3,223 at year 10. The weighted probabilities account for sampling weights and censoring rather than dividing these event counts by a single denominator.

Elapsed Time From Binge Eating to Cannabis Initiation

The reverse risk set included 312 participants, of whom 82 subsequently reported cannabis initiation.

Adjusted Associations With Annual Onset

The binge-eating model included 443,334 person-years and 494 onsets through age 60. Prior cannabis initiation was associated with higher annual odds of recurrent bingeeating onset (adjusted OR = 2.23, 95% CI [1.46, 3.39], p < .001). The cannabis-initiation model included 365,919 person-years and 4,416 onsets through age 60. Prior recurrent binge eating was associated with higher annual odds of cannabis initiation (adjusted OR = 1.61, 95% CI [1.07, 2.42], p = .024; Table 3). One reported binge-eating onset and two cannabis onsets occurred after age 60 and were outside these models.

The two models used different person-year totals because records stopped when the outcome in that model occurred. Each estimate compared annual onset odds after the other behavior had begun with odds in person-years when it had not yet begun or was never reported. No formal comparison between the two directional odds ratios was performed.

Secondary Analyses

In sex-specific models, prior cannabis initiation was associated with annual bingeeating onset among women (adjusted OR = 2.63, 95% CI [1.76, 3.94], p < .001). The estimate among men was 1.81 (95% CI [0.87, 3.76], p = .113; Table 4). In the post hoc pooled model, the sex interaction had p = .032 and q = .063 after correction for the two new subgroup comparisons. It did not meet the corrected significance threshold.

Compared with person-years before cannabis initiation or among never users, annual odds of binge-eating onset were higher after initiation before age 15 (adjusted OR = 2.07, 95% CI [1.30, 3.30], p = .003) and after initiation at age 15 or older (adjusted OR = 2.30, 95% CI [1.43, 3.70], p < .001). The direct comparison of initiation before age 15 with initiation at age 15 or older gave an odds ratio of 0.90 (95% CI [0.57, 1.42], p = .657; q = .657). These estimates did not establish a difference between initiation-age groups. The descriptive analysis by age 30 included 9,229 participants who were at least 30 at interview. Weighted prevalence of recurrent binge-eating onset by age 30 was 4.4% (95% CI [3.1%, 6.1%]) among participants who initiated cannabis before age 18 and 1.6% (95% CI [1.3%, 2.0%]) among those who did not. This comparison combined participants with different onset sequences and was not adjusted for covariates.

Model Sensitivity Checks

The two directional associations remained positive across the age-cap, same-ageonset, approximate-cohort, and age-spline checks. Adjusted odds ratios ranged from 2.12 to 2.36 for subsequent recurrent binge eating and from 1.58 to 1.61 for subsequent cannabis initiation; all corresponding 95% confidence intervals excluded 1. Complementary log-log models gave exponentiated coefficients of 2.23 (95% CI [1.46, 3.39]) and 1.56 (95% CI [1.06, 2.30]), respectively. All models converged and had full-rank model matrices. Both primary directional tests also remained significant when corrected as a family of two (q < .001 and q = .024). These checks do not resolve selection arising from unavailable onset ages or unmeasured confounding.

The age-interaction test did not show clear variation in the association between prior cannabis initiation and binge-eating onset (F with 4 and 147 degrees of freedom, 0.87; p = .482; q = .482). The reverse association varied with age in this diagnostic (F with 4 and 147 degrees of freedom, 4.74; p = .0013; q = .0025). The overall reverse-direction odds ratio of 1.61 should therefore not be assumed to apply equally at every age. Older-age estimates were unstable. Only five cannabis onsets followed prior binge eating at ages 35 to 44, and none occurred at ages 45 to 60. We did not interpret the extreme estimates in the older-age tail as evidence of a protective association.

Discussion

In this study, we examined the order and timing of cannabis initiation and recurrent binge eating onset in a pooled sample of US adults from three national surveys. Overall, findings indicate that each behavior was associated with later onset of the other. After adjustment for age, sex, birth cohort, race and ethnicity, and source survey, prior cannabis initiation was associated with 2.23 times the annual odds of binge eating onset, while prior binge eating was associated with 1.61 times the annual odds of cannabis initiation. In practical terms, individuals who have begun either behavior have higher odds of beginning the other in a given year than same-aged individuals who have not. Both associations remained significant after correction for multiple testing and were stable across sensitivity checks. The descriptive results are consistent with this interpretation. Specifically, among adults who reported both behaviors with usable onset ages, cannabis came first for 62.3% and binge eating came first for 33.2%, with the second behavior beginning a median of 4 to 5 years after the first.

Taken together, these results describe a two-way association rather than a single fixed sequence.

Importantly, the majority of cannabis-first reports should not be taken as evidence that cannabis drives binge eating. Cannabis initiation typically occurred in the teen years in these data. Among adults who reported both behaviors, the weighted median onset age was 17 years for cannabis and 19 years for recurrent binge eating. Binge eating also typically begins from the late teens to the early twenties (Kessler et al., 2013). As such, the two behaviors share a developmental window. Given that their onsets cluster within a few years of each other, a small difference in recalled age can decide which behavior appears to come first. Thus, cannabis would come first in many cases even if the two behaviors were unrelated. The adjusted models account for this age pattern; as such, the associations they show are not simply a product of cannabis starting earlier in life. However, the remaining associations are observational and should be interpreted as links rather than causal effects.

The 62.3% cannabis-first estimate also applies only to participants with usable onset ages.

Allowing any ordering for the 116 excluded participants who reported both behaviors gave bounds of 40.2% to 75.7% for the cannabis-first proportion among all 367. A cannabis-first majority is therefore not established for the broader group. The onset ages within each sequence offer additional nuance. When binge eating came first, it began early (unweighted median age 14.5 years) and cannabis initiation followed at a median age of 18 years. When cannabis came first, cannabis began at a median age of 16 years and binge eating followed at a median age of 22 years. These medians may indicate two patterns: 1) early-onset binge eating that precedes cannabis initiation and 2) binge eating that emerges in the early twenties after cannabis use has begun. These patterns may reflect different pathways that future work can separate.

The cumulative probabilities should also be interpreted with care. Among adults who had begun using cannabis and had not yet reported binge eating, only 2.6% reported recurrent binge eating within 10 years. In contrast, among adults with recurrent binge eating who had not yet used cannabis, 35.6% reported cannabis initiation within 10 years. This large gap likely reflects differences in how common each behavior is. Cannabis initiation was reported by 4,418 participants while recurrent binge eating was reported by only 495, and the two risk sets also differed greatly in size and composition. As such, the gap says little about which direction of influence is stronger. The adjusted odds ratios offer a fairer comparison, as they contrast person-years with and without the earlier behavior at the same age. Those estimates were 2.23 and 1.61, respectively, and their confidence intervals overlapped, which may indicate comparable strengths of association (although we did not formally test this difference). Thus, the current data do not show that either direction is stronger than the other. The reverse-direction estimate also should not be assumed to apply equally at every age. A diagnostic age-interaction test indicated variation across ages for cannabis initiation after binge eating but not for binge eating after cannabis initiation, and older-age estimates were unstable.

Turning first to binge eating as an antecedent, our estimate is similar in size to a prospective estimate from adolescents and young adults (OR = 1.85; Sonneville et al., 2013). Notably, agreement across different samples and designs increases confidence that this direction is not specific to a single sample. This finding is also consistent with evidence of elevated risk for illicit substance use disorders following an eating disorder diagnosis for up to 10 years (Skøt et al., 2022). Taken together, these studies suggest that a history of binge eating may be a marker of later cannabis exposure that clinicians can anticipate.

The opposite direction (i.e., cannabis initiation preceding binge eating onset) represents the novel contribution of the current study to the literature. Earlier evidence for this direction was purely cross-sectional (Elran-Barak et al., 2025). Our findings add to that evidence by demonstrating that prior cannabis initiation was associated with later binge eating onset after adjustment for age. This association was stable across sensitivity analyses. Several mechanisms could account for this association. Laboratory work shows that cannabis increases food intake and the reward value of food (Hume et al., 2025). Loss of control over eating may connect cannabis-related problems with eating pathology (Maynard et al., 2023). Distress may feed both pathways given that it precedes binge eating (Borm et al., 2025). Shared genetic risk could also contribute (Munn-Chernoff et al., 2021).

These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive, and our models did not include mood or other psychiatric symptoms. As such, our findings cannot determine which mechanism, if any, is operating on these relationships.

Although the current data cannot identify the operative mechanism, the evidence reviewed above can be integrated into a single reciprocal account that is consistent with our results. Specifically, shared vulnerability in adolescence (e.g., emotional distress and inherited risk) may raise the likelihood of both behaviors and thus produce both orders of onset. Distress also heightens the response to food among patients with binge eating disorder (Arend et al., 2022). Once cannabis is initiated, its effect on food reward (Hume et al., 2025) could add to this baseline sensitivity and make loss of control over eating more likely (Maynard et al., 2023). Once binge eating is established, distress and loss of control could in turn raise the appeal of cannabis given that depression is associated with cannabis use (Gorfinkel et al., 2020). Under this account, each behavior raises the odds of the other, which would explain three features of our results: 1) associations in both directions, 2) odds ratios of similar size (2.23 and 1.61), and 3) a gap of several years rather than a fixed sequence. However, this account remains a hypothesis given that our data contain no measures of the proposed mediators, and future research should empirically evaluate these pathways.

Interestingly, the strength of the cannabis-to-binge-eating association appeared to vary by sex, such that it was larger among women (OR = 2.63) than among men (OR = 1.81). A post hoc test of the sex interaction was nominally significant but did not meet the corrected significance threshold. Thus, this contrast should be considered suggestive rather than established. However, this pattern is consistent with a prior report of an association among young women but not young men (Elran-Barak et al., 2025). Biological differences alone seem an unlikely explanation given that controlled studies of vaporized cannabis found no systematic sex differences in acute effects (Arkell et al., 2022). Other explanations (e.g., differences in why or how women and men use cannabis) should be tested in future research. Age at initiation also did not meaningfully change this pattern; initiation before age 15 and at age 15 or older were both significantly associated with later binge eating onset, and earlier initiation did not carry a clearly larger association. A descriptive comparison gave a complementary view. Among adults aged 30 or older, 4.4% of those who initiated cannabis before age 18 had reported binge eating onset by age 30 compared with 1.6% of those who did not initiate before age 18 (i.e., later initiators and never users). This comparison was unadjusted and combined participants with different onset sequences. It therefore cannot show that early initiation caused later binge eating.

These findings have important clinical implications. Cannabis use is already common among adults seeking treatment for binge eating. In one clinical sample, more than 23% reported recent use (Wilkinson et al., 2024). Our estimate that 35.6% of adults with recurrent binge eating who had not yet used cannabis initiated within 10 years indicates that cannabis initiation after binge eating onset is common. Thus, clinicians who treat binge eating should consider asking about cannabis use at intake and again over time. The reverse is also true.

Given that cannabis came first for most adults with usable onset ages who reported both behaviors (although this majority is not established for the broader group), clinicians who treat cannabis use should consider asking about binge eating. Loss of control over eating may be a useful focus for those questions (Maynard et al., 2023). This need is likely growing given that frequent cannabis use is now more common than when these surveys were conducted (Caulkins, 2024).

Several directions for future research follow from these findings. First, prospective cohorts that repeatedly assess cannabis use and binge eating are critical for establishing order without relying on recall, which is the main limitation of the sequence estimates reported here. Second, future research should measure the frequency, dose, and potency of cannabis use to determine whether heavier or more potent use carries greater risk. Third, repeated sampling of mood, cannabis use, and eating in daily life (e.g., via ecological momentary assessment) could test whether cannabis use changes the likelihood of loss-ofcontrol eating on the same day. This approach has already been used to study distress and binge eating (Borm et al., 2025). Fourth, models that add education, childhood adversity, other substance use, and psychiatric history with their ages of onset are needed to test whether the associations persist after adjustment. Fifth, replication of the sex difference in larger cohorts that use the once-weekly DSM-5 threshold is needed to determine whether the pattern among women is robust and generalizable. Sixth, cohorts with complete onsetage data are needed to address the selection that arises when onset ages are missing.

Strengths and Limitations

The current study has several notable strengths. First, the data come from probability samples that include several racial and ethnic groups. Second, survey weights and design variables allow the estimates to approximate the population rather than only the sample.

However, it remains unconfirmed whether the long-form weight fully accounts for selection into the eating-disorder module. Third, censoring methods retained adults who had not yet reported the second behavior, which avoided discarding the many adults who had not yet developed it. Fourth, each direction was analyzed separately so that neither depended on the other. Finally, the primary associations remained significant after correction for multiple testing and were stable across sensitivity checks.

Findings from the current study should be interpreted in light of several limitations.

First, onset ages were retrospectively recalled by adults at a single interview. Recall of past psychiatric history is imperfect and may differ by age and by how severe or recurrent the problem was (Simon & VonKorff, 1995). As such, misdated onsets could reverse some sequences. Ages were reported in whole years and 17 participants reported the same age for both behaviors, so those cases could not be ordered. Second, few participants reported both behaviors (n = 251). Sequence and interval estimates are imprecise, particularly for the binge-eating-first group (n = 82). Third, binge eating was defined by two interview items without a requirement for loss of control or distress. Further, the twice-weekly threshold is stricter than the once-weekly DSM-5 threshold (American Psychiatric Association, 2013).

Accordingly, adults whose binge eating was less frequent were classified as not having recurrent binge eating, and the measure does not identify DSM-5 consistent binge eating disorder. Likewise, cannabis initiation does not indicate regular use or DSM-5 consistent cannabis use disorder. The findings apply to the onset of these behaviors rather than to clinical diagnoses. Fourth, the models did not adjust for education, tobacco use, alcohol use, psychiatric symptoms, or childhood adversity. Residual confounding is likely, and thus the associations should not be interpreted as causal. Education and childhood adversity are plausible confounders that should be included in future models. Tobacco use, alcohol use, and psychiatric symptoms are also plausible confounders; however, they may begin after cannabis use or binge eating or lie on the pathway between them. Adjusting for these variables would thus require their ages of onset and a time-varying design. Fifth, survival curves were not adjusted for covariates and the yearly odds ratios are not hazard ratios, both of which limit direct comparison with studies that report hazard ratios. Sixth, primary and original secondary tests were not corrected for multiple testing. The two primary directional tests remained significant after Benjamini-Hochberg correction. However, the post hoc analyses were not prespecified. Isolated p-values near .05 should therefore be interpreted with caution. Seventh, onset ages were unusable for 116 of the 367 participants who reported both behaviors (31.6%), and exclusion was more common among participants reporting recurrent binge eating (32.3%). The sequence estimates therefore describe only participants with usable onset ages, and conservative bounds for the full group (40.2% to 75.7% cannabis first) were wide. Approximate onset-age fields were unavailable and could not reduce this loss. Eighth, the NSAL White sample was not administered the substanceuse or eating-disorder sections. The pooled sample therefore does not represent all US adults. It also remains unconfirmed whether the long-form weight accounts for selection into the eating-disorder module. Ninth, the association between prior binge eating and cannabis initiation varied with age (p = .0013), and older-age estimates were unstable. The overall odds ratio of 1.61 should therefore not be assumed to apply at every age. Finally, the surveys were conducted in 2001 to 2003. Cannabis use has become more frequent since then (Caulkins, 2024). Thus, the timing and strength of the associations may differ in current cohorts.

Conclusions

In US adults, cannabis initiation and recurrent binge eating were each associated with later onset of the other. Cannabis most often came first among adults who reported both behaviors with usable onset ages, while binge eating came first for about one third. Taken together, these findings are more consistent with a two-way association than a single direction of influence. Thus, asking about each behavior in the care of the other is a reasonable response, and prospective research is needed to clarify causal direction.
