Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The language used to discuss autism both reflects and shapes perceptions of Autistic people, yet research on terminology preferences has largely been confined to English. This study examines autism-related terminology in Greek. A sample of 1,006 Greek-speaking adults (124 Autistic adults, 434 parents/caregivers, and 448 professionals) completed an online survey, selecting, from a list of 61 terms, those they use to refer to autism, Autistic people, autism-related difficulties, and non-autistic people, and explained their choices. Data were analysed using demographics-adjusted group comparisons, principal component analysis, and thematic analysis. The groups shared a core vocabulary ("autism", "difficulties", "is on the autism spectrum") but differed in their use of 39 terms, with small effects, and the main contrasts persisted after demographic adjustment. Autistic adults preferred identity-first and neurodiversity terminology, professionals favoured clinical terminology, and parents/caregivers resembled professionals on identity terms and Autistic adults on clinical terms.

Identity and clinical vocabularies were independent dimensions on which every group was represented. Open-ended responses tied these choices to identity among Autistic adults, others' reactions among parents/caregivers, and audience and setting among professionals. We discuss these findings in the context of autism research and their implications for practice and public discourse.

P r e p r i n t Introduction

The language used to describe autism influences how Autistic people are perceived, accepted, and view themselves. Autistic self-advocates and researchers have consistently linked language to experiences of recognition, stigma, and exclusion (Sinclair, 1999; Brown, 2011; Kapp et al., 2013; Botha et al., 2023; Botha & Cage, 2022). The words chosen in clinical, educational, research, and media contexts shape public attitudes and affect Autistic people's sense of identity and self-worth (Gernsbacher, 2017; Bottema-Beutel et al., 2021; Karaminis et al., 2023; Dickinson & Karaminis, 2026).

A central debate has been between person-first language (person with autism), promoted by disability-rights advocates and professional guidelines to emphasise the person (APA, 2010; Dunn & Andrews, 2015), and identity-first language (autistic person), which treats autism as an intrinsic and valued aspect of identity and aligns with the neurodiversity movement (Kapp et al., 2013; Robertson, 2010). Current APA guidance recognises both forms and emphasises individual preferences (APA, 2020). While the contrast between the two is grammatical, it reflects a deeper disagreement about whether autism is a condition someone has or a way of being. This divide is also evident in broader vocabulary, with terms like disorder, impairment, and deficit contrasted with difference, neurotype, and neurodivergence (Bottema-Beutel et al., 2021; Botha et al., 2021; but see Vivanti, 2020; Singer et al., 2023).

Evidence from English-speaking contexts A growing body of survey research has documented the preferences for autism-related terminology.

The first large study, in the United Kingdom (Kenny et al., 2016; N = 3,470), showed that autistic was endorsed by a majority of Autistic adults and about half of parents, but by far fewer professionals. In contrast, person with autism was endorsed by almost half of professionals but by only a minority of Autistic adults and parents. Autism and on the autism spectrum were endorsed across groups. The authors concluded that no way of describing autism is universally accepted.

Subsequent studies in the United States, and multilingual Singapore replicated this stakeholder contrast (Taboas et al., 2023; Suhumaran et al., 2026). A survey of 654 English-speaking Autistic adults across 30 countries found strong support for identity-first language, with little national variation (Keating et al., 2023; see also Lei et al., 2021; Robertson et al., 2026). A systematic review of 19 studies (N = 6,350) similarly found that Autistic adults generally favoured identity-first language, although preferences varied and people with intellectual disabilities were under-represented (Schuck et al., P r e p r i n t 2025). Thus, despite consistent group-level patterns, individual terminology preferences remain important.

These preferences exist within a changing linguistic landscape. While research writing still tends to favour person-first language, journals, books, and news media are starting to shift towards identity-first forms (Zajic & Gudknecht, 2024; Kang et al., 2026). There is also increasing attention to the broader vocabulary of neurodiversity and neurodivergence (Bury et al., 2026; Grant et al., 2025; Pearson et al., 2026).

The role of measurement and analytical methodologies Research on preferences for autism-related terminology has employed different measurement and analytical approaches, and these can influence the patterns observed. For example, studies asking participants which terms they use or find acceptable often find endorsement of both person-first and identity-first language (Kenny et al., 2016; Keating et al., 2023). Other studies rate liking and offensiveness separately (Bury et al., 2023a; Lao et al., 2026; Pearson et al., 2026), and the top term can depend on how responses are summarised (Bury et al., 2023b). Question format is also important, as forced-choice questions may yield clearer majorities than multiple-selection formats (Taboas et al., 2023), , although results vary across samples (Flowers et al., 2023), while a 'no preference' option attracts 4-37% of participants (De Laet et al., 2025; Schuck et al., 2025). Furthermore, reported usage may reflect context rather than personal preference, particularly among professionals, whose terminology may vary across settings (Kenny et al., 2016).

With regard to sample characteristics, community-recruited Autistic samples may show greater engagement with advocacy than registry samples (Buijsman et al., 2023). Factors such as age, diagnostic status, and identification with the Autistic community are associated with language preferences (Buijsman et al., 2023; Bury et al., 2023a; Bosman & Thijs, 2024; Bureau et al., 2025), implying that comparisons between stakeholder groups with different demographic profiles may mix up stakeholder position with demographic factors. Including parents or professionals alongside Autistic adults can reveal group differences (Kenny et al., 2016; Taboas et al., 2023; Suhumaran et al., 2026), and open-ended responses can provide additional insight.

Finally, group averages can hide individual differences. Analytic approaches using clustering may reveal the presence or absence of strong preferences or consensus (Dwyer et al., 2026) or endorsement patterns that cut across stakeholder groups (Lao et al., 2026).

Anglocentric bias

Crucially, the language-preferences literature remains Anglocentric, reflecting a broader lack of cross-linguistic and cross-cultural work in autism research (de Leeuw et al., 2020), and the few studies in other languages suggest that linguistic and cultural context matters. French-speaking Autistic adults endorse identity-first language (Geelhand et al., 2023; Bureau et al., 2025) and, in their open comments, a social model of disability (Geelhand et al., 2023), whereas non-autistic adults in France and professionals in French-speaking Canada lean towards person-first and medical-model terms (Bureau et al., 2025; Fecteau et al., 2024). In Spanish, where even persona autista ('autistic person') places the noun first, person-first terms and diagnostic labels predominate in a sample of mostly professionals and students (García-Molina, 2019).

Dutch is the exception, as Autistic adults prefer person-first language (Buijsman et al., 2023; Bosman & Thijs, 2023; De Laet et al., 2025), as do parents (Buijsman et al., 2023). This reversal has been attributed to cultural traditions that value conformity (Buijsman et al., 2023) or, more tentatively, to stigmatising connotations of the Dutch identity-first term (De Laet et al., 2025). Mandarin is a different case, as word order does not carry the person-first/identity-first contrast and, because each character carries meaning, both everyday terms for autism visibly end in 症, meaning 'disease' or 'symptom' (Lao et al., 2026). This raises the possibility that what matters for the preferences of different groups is whether a term's characters imply pathology rather than phrase position. Finally, on social media, identity-first forms outnumbered person-first forms in all five languages examined, French and Spanish included (López-Resa et al., 2026), echoing Autistic adults rather than professionals.

Overall, cross-cultural studies indicate that stakeholder differences are generally consistent across contexts, but their size, and occasionally their direction, vary depending on language, instrument, and sample. Each new language provides an opportunity to test these patterns further.

P r e p r i n t

The current study This study extends the autism language-preferences literature to Greek, a language spoken by about 13.5 million people in Greece, Cyprus, and diaspora communities worldwide (Eberhard et al., 2021), not yet examined in this context. Four features make the Greek case particularly informative.

First, the word autism comes from Greek, combining εαυτός ('self') and the suffix -ισμός (Kassas et al., 2021). This etymology is clear to Greek speakers, as is the origin of much medical terminology (Wulff, 2004), including terms from the autism lexicon, such as empathy, echolalia, alexithymia, and monotropism.

Second, Greek is a distinct branch of the Indo-European family, with rich inflectional morphology marking gender, number, and case, and flexible word order (Holton et al., 2012). As a result, the identity-first and person-first distinction may be less clear in Greek than in English (see Geelhand et al., 2023, for a similar point on French), and gendered forms raise additional questions about inclusive language.

Third, official disability discourse in Greek uses person-first language (άτομα με αναπηρία, 'persons with disability'; Law 4074/2012). Clinical and administrative contexts still use ICD-10 terms like Asperger's syndrome and pervasive developmental disorder (Papanikolaou & Pehlivanidis, 2025), and the vocabulary of neurodiversity is only recently emerging.

Fourth, research and resources for autism in Greek-speaking contexts are limited. There are few validated diagnostic and assessment tools (though see Karaminis & Stavrakaki, 2022; Papanikolaou et al., 2009; Pipelias et al., 2026; Zarokanellou et al., 2024a), and health and research systems were strained by the financial crisis in Greece (Kentikelenis et al., 2014) and Cyprus (Petrou, 2019) and more recently by the pandemic (Agorastos et al., 2025). Public awareness and acceptance of autism are still developing (Papoudi & Antonopoulou, 2025; Zarokanellou et al., 2024b Zarokanellou et al., , 2025)), as is Autistic advocacy. Autistic people and their families continue to report uneven support and ongoing stigma.

This study offers the first systematic evidence on the language used by Greek-speaking Autistic adults, parents and caregivers, and professionals. We examine not only which terms each group uses, but also how the groups differ when demographic factors are considered, how individuals combine different terms, and the reasons behind their choices. We designed a brief online survey, adapted from earlier English-and French-language studies, and distributed it through community networks and P r e p r i n t social media. Our approach combines group comparisons that account for demographic differences, person-level analysis of term combinations, and thematic analysis of participants' explanations. This design aims to capture both the diversity of language use and the underlying reasons for these choices.

Methods

Participants

Participants were 1,006 Greek-speaking adults recruited through autism advocacy networks, professional associations, universities, community organisations, and social media in Greece, Cyprus, and Greek-speaking diaspora communities. Inclusion criteria were being an adult, speaking Greek fluently, and belonging to one of three stakeholder groups: Autistic adults (n = 124; 78 with a formal diagnosis and 46 self-identified), parents/caregivers of Autistic people (n = 434; 394 parents or guardians of a child with a formal diagnosis, 26 of a child without one, and 14 other relatives), and professionals (n = 448; 427 practitioners, such as psychologists and special education teachers, and 21 researchers). Participants with more than one of these identities (e.g., an Autistic parent) were allocated to the group closer to Autistic identity, and 15 respondents who belonged to none of the groups were excluded.

Most participants lived in Greece (93.5%) or Cyprus (4.0%). The groups differed markedly in demographic profile (Supplementary Table S1): half of Autistic adults were aged 18-29, whereas four in five parents/caregivers were 40 or over; women made up 69% of Autistic adults and over 90% of the other two groups; and 51% of Autistic adults, 68% of parents/caregivers, and 94% of professionals held a university degree or higher.

Materials

The online questionnaire comprised 13 items adapted from Keating et al. (2023) and informed by the French adaptation of Geelhand et al. (2023). It was translated and adapted by the research team (all native Greek speakers) and piloted with an Autistic adult, a parent of an Autistic child, and a special educational needs educator (Supplementary Section A.2). Six items recorded age, gender, stakeholder group, highest level of education, country of residence, and whether Greek was the participant's first language. Six terminology items asked which of the listed terms participants use for autism itself, for an Autistic person, for a person's diagnostic characterisation (in separate sub-questions for P r e p r i n t formulations with has and with is), for autism more broadly (as a condition, disability, disorder, and so on), for autism-related difficulties, and for a person without a diagnosis, 61 terms in all (Supplementary Table S3). Following Keating et al. (2023), participants could select as many terms as they wished; to keep the survey brief, no favourite term, ratings, or rankings were requested. Each terminology item also had an 'other' textbox field, and a final item invited participants to explain the reasons behind their choices in a text box with no length limit.

Three features of the Greek context were reflected in the questionnaire (Supplementary Section A.2).

First, adjectives were given in their masculine, feminine, and neuter forms (αυτιστικός/ή/ό, autistic).

Second, two Greek words for neurodiversity were offered. In νευροδιαφορετικότητα, the second constituent (διαφορετικότητα) suggests both diversity and difference, hence the translation neurodiversity/neurodivergence. By contrast, νευροποικιλότητα (translated here as neurodiversity) is a more direct analogue of biodiversity, as it builds on its Greek equivalent, βιοποικιλότητα. Finally, Asperger's syndrome and pervasive developmental disorder were retained because they remain in Greek diagnostic, administrative, and everyday use (Law 3699/2008; Papanikolaou & Pehlivanidis, 2025). The full questionnaire is provided in Supplementary Section A.1.

Procedure and ethics

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Aristotle University of Thessaloniki (ref: 192691/2023). The survey was administered online (LimeSurvey) between July 2024 and March 2025. Participation was voluntary and unpaid. Participants read an information page and gave electronic informed consent before proceeding; no identifying information was requested, and participants were assured that their responses were anonymous and confidential. The survey took a median of 6 minutes to complete.

Participatory methods[not in word count]

This study was designed and led by academic researchers and was not co-produced. Community input entered at three points. First, in adapting the questionnaire to Greek, a pilot version was reviewed by an Autistic adult, a parent of an Autistic child, and a special educational needs educator, all native The authors are Greek-speaking researchers in psychology, special education, and linguistics based in the United Kingdom and Greece. None of us identifies as Autistic. We approached terminology use as an empirical question without treating any form as inherently correct. In reporting the findings, we generally use identity-first language in recognition of its endorsement by many Autistic adults, while preserving participants' own terminology when describing their responses.

Data analysis

The study followed a convergent mixed-methods design and is reported according to the GRAMMS guidelines (O'Cathain et al., 2008). The analysis asked three questions in turn: which terms each stakeholder group selects, and whether the differences between groups survive adjustment for their different demographic profiles; how the endorsements of the 61 terms combine within individuals; and, from the open-ended responses, how participants account for their choices. Quantitative analyses were conducted in Python 3.13 (details in Supplementary Methods, Section A.3; data and code in the Data Availability Statement).

Term-by-term comparisons. Each of the 61 terms was coded as selected or not selected by each participant ('other' entries were summarised separately). For each term, the three groups were compared with a 3 × 2 chi-square test of independence, with p-values adjusted across the 61 tests by the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995; adjusted values denoted p BH , with p BH < 0.05 treated as significant). Because a sample of 1,006 can make small differences significant, Cramér's V is reported alongside (0.10 small, 0.30 medium; Cohen, 1988). Significant omnibus tests were followed by pairwise 2 × 2 comparisons, adjusted in the same way. To compare the identity-first, person-first, spectrum, and neurodiversity framings directly, participants were also classified by the terms they selected for an Autistic person (Supplementary Table S2) and compared with chi-square tests.

Adjusting for demographic differences. Because the three groups differed markedly in age, gender, and education (Supplementary Table S1), the group contrasts were re-estimated with binary logistic P r e p r i n t regressions for twelve focal terms representing the framings at the centre of the terminology debate (listed in Figure 3). Each model predicted selection of the term from stakeholder group (reference: Autistic adults), age band, gender, and education (specification and diagnostics in Supplementary Section A.3). Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Within the Autistic group, formally diagnosed and self-identified adults were compared with chi-square tests and with logistic regressions adjusting for age band and gender.

Dimensions of use. To examine how the 61 endorsements combine within individuals, we conducted a principal component analysis (PCA) of the participant × term matrix. Whereas the term-by-term comparisons ask which group selects a term more often, PCA asks which terms tend to be selected together, summarising co-selection in a small number of dimensions on which each term receives a loading and each participant a score. The PCA was run on the centred binary data using the covariance matrix, so that a term's influence is proportional to how often it was selected. The number of dimensions was decided by parallel analysis, the drop in variance explained, and interpretability. An unrotated solution is reported, oriented so that higher scores denote greater use of the vocabulary naming each dimension, and it was stable under bootstrap resampling.

We preferred the PCA-based dimensional description to an exploratory cluster analysis because, as our data included binary endorsements, the cluster solutions proved unstable (Supplementary Methods, Section A.3). Group differences in dimension scores were tested with one-way ANOVA (η²) and Cohen's d, and re-estimated with linear regressions adjusting for age band, gender, and education.

Thematic analysis.

Finally, the open-ended responses were analysed separately for each stakeholder group with inductive thematic analysis, following the six phases of Braun and Clarke (2006). Two of the authors (DP and SS) independently coded the responses and developed candidate themes, which were compared, reviewed, and refined against the data in online meetings until agreement was reached (Supplementary Section A.4). Quotations, translated from Greek by the authors, are labelled by stakeholder group (A, P, Pr) and survey response number.

Identity-first language prevails only among Autistic adults Classifying participants by the framings they selected for referring to an Autistic person (Figure 2; Supplementary Table S2) showed that identity-first language outweighed person-first language only among Autistic adults (55.6% vs 35.5% selecting any term of each kind; 40.3% vs 20.2% selecting one kind only). Among professionals, the balance was reversed (36.2% vs 60.5%), whereas parents/caregivers leaned neither way (35.9% vs 34.8%) and were the group most likely to select neither framing (38.9%) and to rely on spectrum phrasing alone (33.4%). For parents/caregivers, as for professionals, the single most selected term for an Autistic person was person on the autism spectrum (50.5% and 55.6%, vs 41.1% of Autistic adults), whereas for Autistic adults it was autistic (52.4%). The two framings were not mutually exclusive, as 15.3% of Autistic adults and 19.4% of professionals selected both, and neurodiversity terms were selected by half of Autistic adults (49.2%) but by only a quarter of parents/caregivers (28.6%) and professionals (25.9%).

Themes in the open-ended responses

Of the 915 open-ended responses, 788 were substantive (103 Autistic adults, 335 parents/caregivers, 350 professionals). Table 1 lists the themes identified in each group (theme definitions and further quotations in Supplementary Section C). Three concerns ran through all three groups, autism as difference, the stigma attached to autistic (αυτιστικός), and the sources of one's vocabulary, each approached from the group's own position.

[Insert Table 1 about here] Autistic adults wrote primarily from an identity perspective. Autism was something a person is rather than has: autistic described an integral part of the self, whereas person-first language could sound medicalised ('I feel it is something I am, an inseparable part of my biology and my temperament' [A-103]). In response to the use of autistic as an insult, some avoided the word in favour of neurodivergent (νευροδιαφορετικός) or on the spectrum [A-1498], while others deliberately used it to challenge its derogatory connotations [A-1481]. A few also objected that softer terms such as P r e p r i n t neurodiversity downplayed disability [A-1549]. Views on Asperger (Άσπεργκερ) were similarly divided: many rejected it as outdated or offensive [A-1232, A-667], while some retained it as part of their identity since childhood [A-470].

Parents/caregivers drew on their experiences of raising a child. They too described autism as a difference rather than an illness [P-651] and called for greater public awareness [P-226]. However, some associated autistic and disorder (διαταραχή) with the negative meanings others attached to them, including insult, low intelligence, or insanity [P-376]. They therefore preferred person on the (autism) spectrum or neurodivergent as less stigmatising alternatives, even when aware of Autistic adults' preferences ('I know "autistic" is the preferred term, but when I used it I noticed that people take it to mean something very ugly' [P-1568]). Others adopted autistic out of respect for those preferences [P-1542]. By contrast, some families facing lifelong support needs considered disability (αναπηρία) or disorder more accurate and objected to the 'beautification' (ωραιοποίηση) of autism [P-808, P-668]. Some also retained Asperger as a more specific and less stigmatising term [P-1531].

Their terminology choices reflected competing influences, including professionals [P-483] and Autistic adults [P-650].

Professionals approached terminology through the demands of different settings. They described autism as a difference [Pr-10], emphasised respecting individual preferences ('it is not for us, as neurotypical people, to decide the "correct" terminology' [Pr-1426]), and sought to avoid stigmatising language. Some did so by choosing words carefully when speaking with children [Pr-1428], while others used autistic openly to challenge its negative connotations . Many adapted their terminology to their audience, using on the autism spectrum with parents who might not be ready to hear autistic [Pr-1529], and clinical terms such as disorder with colleagues . Others drew on diagnostic manuals and clinical training [Pr-541] or followed the terminology preferred by the Autistic community.

Taken together, these accounts suggest that terminology choices reflect considerations of identity, anticipated stigma, disability, and communicative context, rather than fixed preferences associated with stakeholder group.

P r e p r i n t Discussion

This study provides, to our knowledge, the first systematic evidence on autism-related terminology use in Greek, based on responses from 1,006 Autistic adults, parents/caregivers, and professionals. Our analysis produced four main findings. First, all three groups converged on a core set of terms (autism, difficulties, is on the autism spectrum) and rarely selected strongly negative terms, but differed in their use of 39 of the 61 terms examined. These differences were generally small, and the main contrasts persisted after adjustment for age, gender, and education.

Second, the pattern of differences broadly resembled that reported in English-language research. Autistic adults selected identity-first and neurodiversity-related terms more often, whereas professionals selected clinical and diagnostic terminology more often. Parents/caregivers did not consistently occupy an intermediate position, instead aligning with professionals on identity-related terminology and with Autistic adults on clinical terms, with person on the autism spectrum as their most frequently selected expression.

Third, identity-related and clinical vocabularies emerged as independent dimensions rather than opposite ends of a single continuum. All three groups were represented across combinations of these dimensions, and stakeholder group membership accounted for less than a tenth of the variance in either score.

Fourth, open-ended responses revealed concerns shared across groups, including the understanding of autism as a difference, the stigma associated with autistic, and the influences shaping terminology choices. These concerns were articulated from different perspectives, reflecting identity, caregiving experience, and professional expectations.

Greek follows the English and French pattern, not the Dutch The ordering of groups in their selection of autistic (52%, 32%, and 30% of Autistic adults, parents/caregivers, and professionals, respectively) resembles that reported in the United Kingdom (Kenny et al., 2016), the United States (Taboas et al., 2023), and Singapore (Suhumaran et al., 2026).

French-language studies show a similar stakeholder contrast, with Autistic adults favouring identity-first terms (Bureau et al., 2025; Fecteau et al., 2024; Geelhand et al., 2023), and non-autistic adults and professionals leaning towards the diagnostic label person with ASD (Bureau et al., 2025; Fecteau et al., 2024). Greek Autistic adults resembled English-speaking Autistic adults (Keating et al., P r e p r i n t 2023) in favouring is autistic and in their use of has autism (37% vs 39%), but selected the noun phrase autistic person far less often (20% vs 80%) and Asperger's syndrome somewhat more often (39%).

Person-first terms were selected by 36% of Autistic adults, within the 5-53% range of acceptance reported in the systematic review by Schuck et al. (2025), although the studies used different measures. Moreover, 15% selected both identity-first and person-first forms. Identity-first language was therefore more frequently endorsed, but without a clear consensus.

The Greek findings also bear on the Dutch exception, where most Autistic adults (Buijsman et al., 2023; De Laet et al., 2025) and parents (Buijsman et al., 2023) favour person-first language. Two explanations have been proposed. One concerns cultural traditions that downplay difference (Buijsman et al., 2023), while the other, offered more tentatively, points to the stigmatising connotations of the Dutch identity-first term (De Laet et al., 2025). Evidence from Dutch-speaking Belgians, who showed a similar person-first tendency, weighs against a narrowly Netherlands-specific cultural explanation (De Laet et al., 2025). The Greek findings complicate a purely stigma-based explanation. In Greek, too, the identity-first term (the bare adjective αυτιστικός) was reported by participants to be used as an insult, yet Greek Autistic adults more frequently selected identity-first forms. Qualitative accounts revealed contrasting responses to this stigma, as some participants avoided the word, whereas others deliberately used it to challenge its derogatory connotations. The French evidence points the same way, as autiste is also reported to be used as an insult, yet French-speaking Autistic adults favour identity-first forms (Fecteau et al., 2024; Geelhand et al., 2023). Thus, the presence of stigma alone does not appear to determine whether identity-first or person-first language predominates, although the intensity and social meanings of stigma cannot be directly compared across the two languages.

The most frequently selected identity-first constructions were the bare adjective and the copular form rather than the noun phrase. Among Autistic adults, autistic (52%) and is autistic (61%) were selected considerably more often than autistic person (20%), and the adjective also outranked the noun phrase in the other two groups. This ordering is not universal. French-speaking Autistic adults endorsed the noun phrase personne autiste (72%) about as often as autiste (67%) and est autiste (81%; Geelhand et al., 2023), and English-speaking Autistic adults endorsed autistic person at 80% (Keating et al., 2023).

These findings suggest that restricting identity-first language to noun phrases may underestimate its use, particularly in languages where adjectival constructions are common.

P r e p r i n t

That said, although the Greek case was motivated partly on linguistic grounds, including transparent etymology, a morphology that allows the adjective to stand alone, and a predominantly person-first official vocabulary, participants often explained their choices in sociocultural terms. Concerns about the term for autistic (αυτιστικός/ή/ό) centred on its derogatory use rather than its grammatical form, while terminology choices were influenced by professionals, diagnostic manuals, and the Autistic community, often encountered online. Linguistic structures may therefore shape the available forms, while awareness, stigma, community identification, and trusted sources influence which are adopted.

Beyond identity-first and person-first

The most pronounced differences between Autistic adults and the other groups lay not in the identity-first versus person-first distinction but in neurodiversity-related vocabulary. Neurodiversity (νευροποικιλότητα, 32% vs 7-12%), neurodivergent person (44% vs 18-19%), and the contrastive term non-autistic (27% vs 7-8%) were selected considerably more often by Autistic adults. Self-identified Autistic adults selected νευροποικιλότητα at twice the rate of those with a formal diagnosis, a difference that persisted after adjustment for age band and gender. Of the two Greek renderings of neurodiversity, νευροδιαφορετικότητα was used more widely across stakeholder groups, with approximately half of parents/caregivers and professionals selecting it, whereas νευροποικιλότητα remained largely concentrated among Autistic adults. This pattern is consistent with the broader uptake of neurodiversity language among neurodivergent adults (Pearson et al., 2026).

The third principal component distinguished direct autism terminology from alternative labels, including neurodiversity-related terms and Asperger's syndrome. Qualitative accounts of avoiding autistic offer one possible interpretation of some of these endorsement patterns, although the component may also reflect other distinctions, including identity, diagnostic history, and perceived accuracy.

The quantitative patterns were also consistent with themes in the qualitative responses. Autistic adults scored higher on the identity and neurodiversity dimension, whereas professionals scored higher on the clinical-diagnostic dimension. Nevertheless, substantial variation remained within each group, consistent with findings from other contexts (Dwyer et al., 2026; Lao et al., 2026). Professionals' qualitative responses suggested that clinical terminology often reflected diagnostic conventions and professional contexts rather than necessarily indicating opposition to identity-first language. Many P r e p r i n t emphasised that the appropriate term is the one preferred by the Autistic person, and several noted that it is not for neurotypical professionals to determine the correct terminology.

Alternating alignment of parents/caregivers with the other groups

The alternating alignment of parents/caregivers with the other two stakeholder groups is one of the clearer findings in our study. Mixed alignment among parents is not specific to Greek. In French-speaking Canada, parents rated autistic, autistic person, and person with ASD about equally, whereas Autistic adults favoured the first two and professionals the third (Fecteau et al., 2024). In the United Kingdom, parents rarely endorsed person with autism deviating frm profressionals, yet over half described their child with has autism, similarly to professionals (Kenny et al., 2016).

The open-ended responses help explain this pattern. Some parents/caregivers, like some Autistic adults, resisted disorder and deficit terminology because they regarded it as pathologising a child they understood as different rather than ill. At the same time, some avoided autistic because of anticipated negative reactions, favouring person on the autism spectrum as a less stigmatising alternative. Others, particularly those describing lifelong support needs, considered disability and disorder more accurate.

Consistent with this variation, 42% of parents/caregivers selected disorder, compared with 32% of Autistic adults and 62% of professionals. Their terminology choices also reflected potentially competing influences, including professionals involved in supporting their children and the Autistic community.

For some parents/caregivers, anticipated stigma thus had immediate implications for how they described their children. Their accounts highlight the potential importance of public education in challenging the derogatory associations of αυτιστικός, while also showing why terminology choices cannot be reduced to a single parental perspective.

Implications

For practice in Greek-speaking settings, the findings support the principle emphasised in English-language research: to ask about and respect the individual's terminology preferences (Keating et al., 2023). This principle was also endorsed by professionals in our study. Where individual preferences cannot be established, we take the view, consistent with Dwyer (2022) and Schuck et al. (2025), that the preferences of Autistic adults should carry greater weight than those of parents/caregivers or professionals, because the terminology concerns Autistic people themselves.

P r e p r i n t

On this basis, the Greek findings support three more specific recommendations. First, where individual preferences are unknown, Greek-language guidance should give particular consideration to is autistic (είναι αυτιστικός/ή/ό, 61%) and the bare adjective autistic (52%), the identity-first forms most frequently selected by Autistic adults in this study. At the same time, is on the autism spectrum, selected by a majority in every group (53%, 64%, and 75%), offers widely used terminology for communication across stakeholder groups.

Second, the contrast between has and is, raised unprompted by participants in all three groups (Supplementary Table S4), suggests that Greek-language guidance should address predicate constructions as well as noun phrases when discussing identity-first and person-first language. In particular, is autistic (είναι αυτιστικός/ή/ό) deserves consideration alongside autistic person (αυτιστικό άτομο), rather than treating the noun phrase as the sole identity-first form.

Third, the stigma attached to autistic (αυτιστικός) in everyday Greek (see also Papoudi & Antonopoulou, 2025; Zarokanellou et al., 2024b Zarokanellou et al., , 2025)), which contributed to avoidance among some parents/caregivers and professionals, is a matter of public discourse rather than terminology alone.

Media language both reflects and may influence attitudes towards autism (Karaminis et al., 2023; Dickinson & Karaminis, 2026). The media, as well as educational institutions, therefore have a potential role in challenging the derogatory associations of this word, as some Autistic adults in our study already sought to do through its deliberate use.

Limitations and future directions Our sample, recruited through advocacy and professional networks, was predominantly female and highly educated, with Autistic adults who were mostly young and, in approximately one-third of cases, self-identified. Recruitment through these networks, together with voluntary participation, may have attracted respondents with a particular interest in autism-related terminology. The endorsement percentages should therefore not be treated as population estimates. Autistic people with intellectual disabilities or those who do not communicate in writing were also likely to be under-represented, consistent with broader limitations in the literature (Schuck et al., 2025). Consequently, the terminology patterns observed among participating Autistic adults should not be assumed to represent all Autistic people. Future research will require alternative survey formats and appropriate involvement of supporters to include a wider range of communication and support needs.

P r e p r i n t

The study measured which terms participants reported using, rather than their attitudes towards those terms. Infrequently selected terms may therefore be tolerated rather than actively rejected, and the survey did not include a 'no preference' option. Future studies should combine terminology selection with ratings of liking and perceived offensiveness (Bury et al., 2023a; Pearson et al., 2026).

They should also consider terms suggested in the 'other' fields, including typically developing (τυπικής ανάπτυξης), support needs, allistic (αλλιστικός), autistic neurotype, and social-model expressions such as barriers and lack of accommodations.

The study was designed and led by academic researchers rather than co-produced with the Autistic community. Although community members contributed through piloting, recruitment, and discussions of preliminary findings, a stronger participatory approach involving Greek-speaking Autistic people in questionnaire development, analysis, and interpretation would help address this limitation (Karaminis et al., 2025).

Finally, stakeholder-group differences were generally small, and the three components interpreted here accounted for approximately one-quarter of the variance in term endorsements. Four additional components exceeded chance levels but were not examined in detail, leaving other dimensions of terminology use to be explored. The findings also represent a snapshot from 2024-2025 of a vocabulary that continues to evolve, highlighting the value of repeated surveys.

Conclusion

Greek-speaking Autistic adults, parents/caregivers, and professionals shared a core autism-related vocabulary but differed in their use of identity-first, neurodiversity-related, and clinical terminology, broadly reflecting patterns reported in English and French. These group differences coexisted with substantial variation within groups, and identity-related and clinical vocabularies emerged as independent dimensions. Qualitative accounts showed how terminology choices reflected identity, anticipated stigma, caregiving experiences, and professional context. The findings support giving greater weight to Autistic adults' terminology preferences in Greek-language guidance while respecting individual choices. They also highlight the importance of challenging the stigma associated with αυτιστικός, rather than allowing its derogatory use to determine how Autistic people are described.
