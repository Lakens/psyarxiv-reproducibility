To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Conceptual abstraction and gender are two equally central aspects of the human mind, yet they have traditionally been studied in isolation from one another, as though they were fundamentally separate. Here, we find gender and abstraction are systematically linked throughout the space of concepts spanning single (N = 40,000) and multi-word expressions (N = 67,000): across billions of words and millions of images on the internet -and across numerous measurement techniques, including human and AI classifications -more abstract concepts are more associated with men, a pattern we show replicates in languages around the world. This male abstraction bias not only holds across semantic domains, but it also maps onto grammatical structure: verbs and adverbs are more abstract and more male-coded, revealing a system-wide trace of stereotypes accentuating agentic qualities in men. Furthermore, we show this bias readily emerges in the free associations humans and AIs produce in controlled online tasks, indicating its psychological internalization and its algorithmic reproduction. These findings identify a latent structure in conceptual space that unifies a range of stereotypes: for example, framings of women as more embodied, emotional, and warm, versus men as more cognitive, rational, and competent; as well as erroneous views of men as better at mathematics, science, and engineering. More broadly, by analyzing an unprecedently large, multimodal sample of online data, we provide culture-wide evidence of gender as a core organizing principle shaping conceptual space far beyond the characterization of people and social roles.

Significance

Gender stereotypes are primarily studied as biased beliefs about people and social roles: who is warm, who is competent, who belongs in science. Here, we show that gender runs far deeper, organizing the structure of concepts themselves. Across billions of words and millions of images online, spanning 100,000 common words and phrases, we find that more abstract (concrete) concepts are systematically more associated with men (women). This male abstraction bias replicates across semantic domains and languages, maps onto fundamental grammar (verbs and adverbs are more abstract and male-coded), and surfaces in the free associations of humans and large language models. These findings reveal a hidden structure in conceptual space that unifies many stereotypes, a structure AI is learning and reproducing.

Main

Does social identity shape the foundational structure of concepts? Traditionally, cognitive science has examined the structure of human concepts by identifying computational principles and constraints operating in the individual's mind [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] , treating social factors as inputs that shape concept learninge.g., by influencing exposure to and adoption of concepts -rather than as constituents of conceptual structure itself. This is especially the case when considering the most foundational dimensions of concepts like abstractness, taxonomic hierarchy, and statistical inference. For example, the abstractness of concepts is often defined by how easy it is to perceive and physically interact with the referents for a given category (e.g., "chairs" are more concrete and perceivable, whereas "beliefs" and "nations" are more abstract), such that decades of work have argued that various individual-level factors relating to cognitive efficiency skew concepts toward an optimal middle-range ("basic level") of abstraction 1, 2, [6] [7] [8] . While recent accounts explore the social grounding of abstract concepts, they focus on social interaction as the route by which abstract concepts are acquired and stabilized 9 , rather than on whether social information (e.g., relating to identity) shapes conceptual abstraction itself. By contrast, aspects of human cognition relating to personal identity, such as gender and race, are often viewed as socially constructed and historically contingent, and as thus belonging to emergent communication and cultural dynamics, rather than to the foundations of conceptual structure 10, 11 . However, recent work has discovered numerous ways in which gender influences various cognitive processes, from logical reasoning and emotion to the perception of everyday objects [12] [13] [14] , suggesting that gender may play a more foundational role in conceptual structure than has been previously recognized 15 . Here, we investigate whether abstraction and gender are systematically and structurally linked throughout the space of human concepts.

A key challenge in the study of concept abstractness is the disconnect between formal definitions and human judgements. Initially, cognitive science defined abstractness via hierarchical generality 16, 17 ; a superset like "animal" is more abstract than its subset "dog", which is more abstract than its subset "border collies", with each step down the tree restricting the range of referents. However, a large body of work has since found that human judgments depart from this hierarchical definition of concept generality [18] [19] [20] . In a now classic study, Brysbaert et al. 21 recruited many human participants to evaluate the 40,000 most common concepts in the English language in terms of how concrete or abstract they are. Not infrequently, general concepts like "animal" were rated as concrete, whereas specific concepts like "string theory" (a narrow subset of all scientific theories) were evaluated as abstract. This led cognitive scientists to distinguish hierarchical generality ('concept specificity') from the concreteness-abstractness continuum 18, 19 . In the current view, concrete concepts are those whose referents are easily perceivable and imageable (e.g., a "rock" or a "house"), while abstract concepts lack clear and perceivable sensory structure (e.g., "justice" and "knowledge") [21] [22] [23] [24] . The limitations of relying on human subjective judgments notwithstanding 25 , research spanning over a decade has found that judgments of concept concreteness-abstractness are not only reliable across raters and replicable across languages 26, 27 , and closely approximated by large language models 28 , but are also predictive of several conceptual features and processes, including language acquisition 29 , sentence processing 30 , emotion 31 , metaphor 32, 33 , and persuasion 34, 35.

Despite the minimal cross-pollination between the study of conceptual abstraction and gender, recurring themes across widespread stereotypes suggest that concept concreteness and abstractness may be deeply tied to gender associations. A number of popular gender stereotypes skew toward associating women with more concrete, embodied qualities, such as emotions and interpersonal warmth 13, [36] [37] [38] [39] , while associating men with more abstract qualities like reasoning, problem solving, and competence 12, 37, 38, 40 . This generalizes to stereotypes related to occupational sorting in which men are (falsely) assumed to be more competent in math and STEM disciplines than women [41] [42] [43] , skewing people toward assuming men are more qualified for highly cognitive, knowledge work. Importantly, prior work has focused on a definition of stereotypes as directly connected to social roles, with a focus on domain-specific biases and patterns of discrimination (e.g., hiring biases in STEM fields). However, this leaves open the question of whether stereotypes are primarily byproducts of role dynamics in these social contexts (e.g., normative biases in the kinds of work women and men are expected and hired to do), or whether these domain-specific role judgments are extensions of deeper, more general gender biases in the structure of concepts themselves, and specifically in systematic differences between concrete and abstract concepts. This question is important because while stereotyped role dynamics may be possible to shift in particular contexts (e.g., by increasing women's leadership in organizations 44 ), if gender is structurally entangled with abstraction as a fundamental structure throughout human concepts, including our most basic and common notions, this suggests the challenge of achieving enduring changes in gender inequality may require far more fundamental shifts in human thought and culture.

In this direction, a developing stream of research in social psychology has begun to identify ways in which abstractness may be related to gender. However, much of this literature is based on construal level theory (CLT) 45, 46 which defines abstractness as psychological distance -e.g., via the level of spatial, temporal, or social distance between an observer and what is being described -and thus is not directly related to or engaged with questions about concept abstractness and conceptual structure specifically, since it relates more to the stance of the observer rather than to the concepts themselves. Nevertheless, using CLT, Joshi et al. 47 show-across a meta-analysis of experiments, a corpus of blog posts, and the floor speech of members of the US Congress-that men tend to describe their experiences and work in more abstract language than women. Dodson et al. 48 similarly draw from CLT across six preregistered studies to show that people implicitly associate women's names with low-construal terms and men's names with high-construal terms, and that they describe women as more concrete than abstract and men as more abstract than concrete, both in general and across 48 occupations-although in one study women were also described as more abstract than men. Otherwise, in a recent opinion piece, Bailey and Leshin 49 argue that, absent an explicit gender frame, people represent women specifically as women but represent men as generic people, such that men come to be associated with a wider variety of referents and attributes 50 ; although the authors do not frame this asymmetry in terms of abstractness, it maps onto concept specificity-the breadth of referents a category subsumes-which is related to, but empirically separable from, rated concreteness 18, 19 . Notably, Bailey and Leshin 49 evaluate the construal-level stereotypes 48 from prior work as an instance of 'balanced' gender representation-women as one thing, men as its complement-that is distinct from the categorical asymmetry they propose, with women represented as one thing and men as many.

Overall, despite these suggestive developments, the link between gender and abstraction at the level of conceptual structure remains unclear. In addition to the fact that CLT focuses on observers and not concepts, the methodological variation and modest scale of prior studies limit their ability to examine whether gender is systematically entangled with conceptual structure. Joshi et al. 47 focus on the supply side-the abstraction of the language that men and women produce-rather than on the gendered associations that concepts themselves carry, and their finding that the gender gap closes when audience construal distance is made salient leaves open whether abstraction is male-coded in the semantics of concepts or only for the people using these concepts to particular audiences. Bailey and Leshin's study 49 is an opinion piece without new empirical analyses or data; and Dodson et al.'s studies 48 rely on survey samples of a few hundred participants who describe or rate individual men and women, with a primary focus on descriptions of men and women in different occupations. Altogether, it remains open whether gendered abstraction merely reflects (i) supply-side differences in how women and men speak, (ii) differences in psychological distance unrelated to concepts themselves, (iii) role dynamics shaping the representation of men and women in limited, specific contexts (e.g., the representation of occupations and biases relating to labor market sorting), (iv) a difference in the breadth and specificity of the categories 'men' and 'women', unrelated to concreteness-abstractness specifically, or (v) a far more general and fundamental gendering of how concrete and abstract concepts are represented across domains.

Another feature constraining prior work is its focus on textual data as the modality through which gender associations and concept abstractness are measured. Yet, examining the concrete vs. abstract dimension of concepts benefits from examining more immediately embodied communication modalities, such as images, in which concepts are frequently represented through concrete, visual anchors. Indeed, concreteness is often defined with respect to the imageability of concepts. Decades of work in cognitive science have not only shown that human conceptual knowledge is richly multimodal, involving both symbolic and image-based representations 22, 51, 52 , but also more recent work demonstrates that images are an especially psychologically potent modality that amplifies gender stereotypes [53] [54] [55] . A full treatment of how gender is linked to the concreteness-abstractness dimension of concepts should therefore incorporate analyses of more directly embodied and concrete representations of concepts like images.

In this study, we address this gap by conducting a large-scale examination of the connection between gender and conceptual abstractness across the entire space of human concepts using billions of words and millions of images from the internet, as well as using behavioral response data of humans and large language models in controlled online tasks [56] [57] [58] [59] [60] [61] . The scale of our approachspanning conceptual domains and communication modalities -allows us to examine not only whether gender and concept abstractness are associated across semantic domains, but also to examine which aspects of concepts, e.g., their grammatical form, account for underlying patterns organizing these associations. By examining the gender associations of the same concepts across both text and image, we are able to further test whether the male abstraction bias manifests both in how concepts are described, and also in how they are captured by the concrete, visual representation of concepts, which includes the use of concrete referents in images to metaphorically depict more abstract and difficult to perceive concepts 32, 33, 62.

We begin by comparing crowdsourced concreteness judgments of the 103,428 most common concepts in English (comprising single 21 and multi-word expressions 63 ) with their corresponding gender associations (female or male), which we identified using the 'geometry of culture' method 53, 64 applied to the co-occurrence patterns among billions of words on the internet according to 12 distinct language models, spanning static embedding models and frontier generative models, both closed and open source (Supplementary Table 1; see 'Language Models' and 'Measuring Gender Associations' in Materials and Methods). We find that increasingly abstract concepts carry increasingly male gender associations across all models, and that this pattern is stable across semantic domains according to WordNet's model of English concepts ('Identifying Semantic Domains' in Materials and Methods). Given our ability to decompose the male abstraction bias across a large corpus of word forms, we further identify a clear underlying pattern across conceptual domains, namely that the male abstraction bias is tightly mapped onto grammatical word form: regardless of topic domain, verbs and adverbs are more abstract and more male-coded compared to nouns, suggesting that established stereotypes ascribing more agentic, action-oriented qualities to men [65] [66] [67] [68] [69] [70] [71] may go beyond role dynamics in specific contexts and may reach into the grammatical foundations of language. Moreover, these findings are not unique to English. Supplementary analyses show that the male abstraction bias replicates across distinct language families that vary in whether they explicitly grammaticalize gender. We replicate our findings by analyzing extensive concreteness norms provided by native speakers of Mandarin Chinese, Persian, Dutch, and Estonian, which we map to gender associations identified using embedding models trained specifically on each language (Supplementary Sections 2 to 5, Supplementary Figs. 1 to 4 and Supplementary Tables 2 to 9). Smaller sets of concreteness norms for five further languages point in the same direction (Supplementary Section 6, Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 10).

Next, we test whether this clear correlation between concept abstractness and masculine association holds beyond the textual modality by examining its presence in a large sample of online images collected for this study. This multimodal approach is a valuable test of our theory given widespread concerns that textual representations are a limited measurement of both cultural associations and human thought 53, 72 . This is, based on the present review of the literature, the first large-scale examination of the relationship between concreteness judgments and image-based associations. For this analysis, we collected roughly 300 Google images for each of the 40,000 concepts associated with crowdsourced human concreteness judgments in Brysbaert et al.'s study 21 , yielding 11,346,925 images. We then applied a recent 11 billion parameter, open-source large language model (Llama-3.2-11B-Vision-Instruct) to classify all of the objects in each of the images, including the number of human faces in each image, as well as their associated gender (male or female) 73, 74 ('Image Data' and 'Image Classification' in Materials and Methods).

Using this approach, we test whether people are more likely to appear as concrete anchors in online images for increasingly abstract concepts, and moreover, whether people are more likely to be men when functioning as concrete anchors. The evidence shows clearly that the probability of observing people in online images increases for more abstract concepts, and -following from the established over-representation of men online 53 -men are increasingly more likely to appear in association with abstract concepts. This sheds mechanistic light on a statistical driver behind the male abstraction bias: as visual representations of people skew male -and as visual representations of abstract concepts skew toward displaying people -by statistical sampling alone, abstract concepts become more associated with men. We go further yet to reveal the ability for image analyses to provide a novel empirical test of an outstanding hypothesis in current computational work on gender stereotypes; building on Bailey and Leshin's opinion piece 49 , we present an empirical test of the hypothesis that women are represented in a more unitary manner, while men are represented with greater breadth and diversity of association; specifically, by examining whether the images associated with more male-coded concepts (according to image and textual measures of gender) are associated with a wider variety of objects (referents) compared to female-coded concepts. Indeed, across all techniques for measuring the gender association of a concept, more abstract concepts are not only more male, but are also associated with more diverse sets of visual referents.

Given that the analyses presented above rely on large-scale observational data, an open question is whether this bias is instantiated and measurable in the response patterns of individual humans and AI agents. We conclude by testing whether the male abstraction bias is detectable in existing experimental data collected by separate teams to examine the free associations that humans and large language models provided when given a seed concept and requested to state, with no constraint, three concepts that come to mind 60, 61 . Across both datasets, we find that humans and all the large language models involved are significantly more likely to generate male-coded (female-coded) responses when asked to free associate for more abstract (concrete) seed words, despite no explicit prompting or priming relating either to gender or to abstractness. This suggests that the male abstraction bias is not only a statistical pattern observed in large social datasets, but is also implicitly encoded in the spreading activation of free association patterns in human and AI psychology. The implications for the algorithmic amplification of the male abstraction bias online -in light of the increasingly male-coded discourse surrounding the framing and theorizing of AI itself -are discussed.

The Male Abstraction Bias in Online Text

We began by examining the relationship between the gender association of a concept and its concreteness in text. Concreteness was measured using human judgements, on a scale from 1 (abstract) to 5 (concrete), for the 39,953 most common words in English 21 and for a recent extension of these norms to 62,889 multiword phrases 63 ('Concreteness Norms' in Materials and Methods). The gender association of each term was measured separately in each of 12 language models that vary in their architecture, training data and year of release (see 'Language Models' and 'Measuring Gender Associations' in Materials and Methods). Averaged across models, terms in the most feminine decile had a mean concreteness of 3.42, compared with 2.83 for terms in the most masculine decile. Cross-domain Stability and Grammatical Grounding

The above findings provide evidence of a stable male abstraction bias across the most common words and phrases in the English language. However, these analyses aggregate over a wide range of concepts, topics and word forms, which raises the question of whether this pattern holds across conceptual domains or is instead driven by a subset of them. For this reason, we decomposed our results by semantic domain. We matched each word and phrase in the concreteness norms to WordNet 82 , a canonical lexical database of English, and assigned it the semantic domain that WordNet records for its primary sense (for example, noun.food or verb.communication); words were matched on their dominant part of speech, and phrases through their own WordNet entry or, failing that, through their head noun (see 'Identifying Semantic Domains' in Materials and Methods). To ensure that these analyses capture the gendering of concepts rather than of people, we excluded function words, lexically gendered terms (such as 'waitress') and nouns that denote persons, leaving 31,591 words in 35 domains and 56,879 phrases in 33 domains. Within each domain, we compared the gender association of the most abstract third of terms with that of the most concrete third, averaging with equal weight across language models.

Figure 2a,b reveals a stable pattern. For words, the most abstract third of a domain was more male-associated than its most concrete third in 33 of 35 domains (P = 3.7 × 10 -8 ; two-tailed binomial sign test; median difference across domains = 0.10 s.d. ; P < 0.05 in 15 domains; two-tailed paired t-tests across 12 models). For phrases, this held in 32 of 33 domains (P = 7.9 × 10 -9 ; median difference = 0.40 s.d. ; P < 0.05 in 29 domains; 12 models). In no domain, for words or phrases, was the most concrete third significantly more male-associated. The pattern spans domains describing physical entities (for example, noun.substance: 0.07 s.d. for the abstract third versus -0.12 s.d. for the concrete third), events and states (noun.communication: 0.07 versus -0.24) and actions (verb.creation: 0.18 versus -0.21). This suggests that our findings are not driven by particular domains, but instead reflect a system-wide bias towards associating more abstract concepts with masculine connotations, regardless of topic. Supplementary Section 10 illustrates this pattern at the level of individual words: for three example domains (acts of communication, general adjectives and artifact nouns) and three embedding models, it plots the continuous within-domain association and highlights the individual words that anchor its two poles-for example, in the domain of acts of communication, we find crying, singing, and whisper at the female-concrete pole against justify, condone and vindicate (all explicitly related to epistemic work) at the male-abstract pole (Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table 17). Supplementary Section 11 and Supplementary Table 18 show that these within-domain results are not sensitive to how words are assigned to WordNet senses and domains.

Our analyses do, however, reveal systematic variation by word form. Figure 2c,d locates each grammatical word form, across all domains, in terms of its mean concreteness and its mean gender association. For words, verbs (n = 6,198) and adverbs (n = 2,622) are not only more abstract than nouns that denote entities (n = 6,524; mean concreteness of 2.91 and 2.04 versus 4.34), but also more male-associated: the mean gender association was 0.18 s.d. for verbs (95% CI across models = 0.09 to 0.26; positive in 12 of 12 models) and 0.22 s.d. for adverbs (95% CI = 0.11 to 0.32; 12 of 12 models), compared with -0.12 s.d. for entity nouns (95% CI = -0.20 to -0.03). Relative to entity nouns, verbs were 0.29 s.d. more male-associated (95% CI = 0.16 to 0.42; P = 0.002) and adverbs 0.33 s.d. (95% CI = 0.19 to 0.47; P = 0.001; two-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank tests across models). The same pattern was roughly twice as strong for phrases: verb phrases (n = 12,601; 0.39 s.d. ; 95% CI = 0.30 to 0.47) and adverb phrases (n = 2,520; 0.31 s.d. ; 95% CI = 0.21 to 0.40) were more male-associated than entity noun phrases (n = 21,234; -0.21 s.d. ; 95% CI = -0.25 to -0.17), a difference of 0.60 s.d. between verb phrases and entity noun phrases (95% CI = 0.48 to 0.71; positive in 12 of 12 models; P < 0.001). Adjectives and nouns for events and states were also abstract, yet they were not male-associated (Fig. 2c,d), which indicates that word form shapes gender associations beyond abstractness alone. That the most male-coded word forms are those that describe actions, and the manner in which actions are carried out, resonates with established stereotypes that ascribe agentic and more action-oriented qualities to men more than to women [36] [37] [38] 71 . These findings suggest that gender stereotypes relating to agency may go much deeper than role dynamics (e.g., perceptions of leadership in organizations), and may connect to fundamental grammatical structure differentiating dynamic (verbs and adverbs) and more static (noun-based) concepts. 19 and 20 report these associations for the search terms whose images contained at least one face, with statistical controls.

The Male Abstraction Bias in Online Images

Next, we examined whether the male abstraction bias generalizes beyond text to the visual modality, in which we examine the role of people as concrete anchors in the representation of both concrete and abstract concepts. For each of the 39,956 terms in the single-word concreteness norms, we collected the top images returned by Google Image search (approximately 300 images per term and 12 million images in total; see 'Image Data' in Materials and Methods), which allowed us to map the features of images onto the concreteness of the concepts they depict. We used a recent multimodal language model (Llama-3.2-11B-Vision-Instruct 73 ) to automatically detect the faces in each image and to classify their gender. From these classifications we derived two measures for each term: the probability that one of its images depicts a person, that is, the fraction of its images in which a face was detected, and its male association, that is, the fraction of male faces among all faces detected in its images ('Image Classification' and 'Image Analyses' in Materials and Methods).

We first examined whether abstract and concrete concepts differ in their rate of depicting people in general. Figure 3a shows that more abstract search terms are more likely to return images of people than more concrete terms. Among the most abstract quartile of terms, 12.8% of a term's images contained a human face on average (95% CI = 12.6% to 13.0%; n = 9,722 terms), compared with 10.1% among the most concrete quartile (95% CI = 9.8% to 10.4%; n = 9,721 terms; difference = 2.7 percentage points; 95% CI = 2.4 to 3.1; Welch's t = 14.65; d.f. = 17,513; P = 2.4 × 10 -48 ). Across all terms, the probability that an image depicts a person declined with concreteness (r = -0.09; 95% CI = -0.10 to -0.08; P < 2.2 × 10 -16 ; n = 38,885 search terms; two-tailed Pearson's correlation). This pattern is consistent with the long-standing argument that abstract concepts are understood through reference to concrete anchors from embodied experience 9, 24, 32, 52 ; we add to this the use of large-scale visual data to examine the role of people as concrete, embodied anchors leveraged in the representation of abstract concepts. If human bodies are the embodied anchors of abstract concepts, this raises the question of who is more likely to appear when people are used to represent them.

Consistent with prior work revealing the over-representation of men in online images 53, 54 , our analyses identify a clear statistical bias toward displaying men when images of people appear in general. Across all images from all concepts, 67.26% are male, indicating a strong and significant bias toward male representation (815,096 male versus 396,719 female; Wilson 95% CI = 67.2% to 67.4%; one-sample z = 405.0; P < 10 -300 against parity; two-tailed), and the male dominance remains far above parity when search terms rather than faces are treated as the unit of sampling (cluster-robust 95% CI = 67.1% to 67.5%; z = 156.0; P < 10 -300 ; n = 35,913 search terms with at least one face). This points toward a simple and consequential statistical mechanism leading toward the over-representation of men as concrete anchors in visual depictions of abstract concepts. As visual representations of people skew male -and as visual representations of abstract concepts skew toward displaying people -it follows that by statistical bias alone, it is expected that abstract concepts will be more associated with men.

Indeed, Fig. 3b,c shows that, when people are depicted, they are disproportionately men, and increasingly so for more abstract concepts. More abstract concepts are systematically more likely to be depicted with male faces, both when abstraction is measured using human judgements of concreteness (Fig. 3b; r = -0.22; 95% CI = -0.23 to -0.22; P < 2.2 × 10 -16 ; n = 39,956 search terms) and when it is measured using perceptual strength (Fig. 3c; r = -0.08; 95% CI = -0.09 to -0.07; P < 2.2 × 10 -16 ; n = 39,707 search terms; two-tailed Pearson's correlations). This association remains when examining only the 35,913 terms whose images contained at least one face (r = -0.07; 95% CI = -0.08 to -0.06; P < 2.2 × 10 -16 ); it is also robust to (i) controlling for the number of faces associated with each term (that is, the number of its images in which a face was detected), word frequency, the confidence of the gender classifier and part of speech (β = -0.010; s.e. = 0.001; t = -8.99; P < 2.2 × 10 -16 ; ordinary least squares regression with robust standard errors), (ii) examining only terms associated with at least ten faces, and (iii) modeling the counts of male and female faces directly; all of these results replicate using perceptual strength as an alternative measure of concept abstractness (Supplementary Section 12 and Supplementary Tables 19 and 20).

The direction of the association is likewise robust to how terms without faces are treated-whether they are retained with an indicator for returning any face, excluded, or scored as neutral rather than as 0% male-and to requiring at least 5, 10 or 20 faces per term, although its magnitude is an upper bound: among terms whose images contain faces, a one-standard-deviation increase in concreteness or in perceptual strength is associated with a decrease of approximately 0.05 standard deviations in the fraction of faces that are male (Supplementary Table 21). We next combined our text and image data to provide an empirical test of a recent hypothesis by Bailey and Leshin 49 , namely that people think of men as many things and of women as one: men are conceptualized in varied and wide-ranging ways, whereas women are conceptualized in a more unitary way. If the male abstraction bias is connected to this asymmetry, then concepts that are more abstract, and concepts that are more male-typed, should be depicted through a wider range of visual content. To test this, we used Llama-3.2-11B-Vision-Instruct to classify the full set of objects that appear in each image, and we counted the number of unique objects detected across the images associated with each term. Because the number of unique objects grows with the number of images examined, and the number of images retrieved varied across terms (median = 265; mean = 256; s.d. = 39), all estimates in Fig. 4 control for the number of images in each term's image set: we regressed the number of unique objects on each predictor together with the log-transformed number of images, and we report the partial correlation and slope for the predictor, with each term's count adjusted to the mean number of images in the figure (Supplementary Section 13).

Figure 4a shows that more abstract concepts are depicted through a substantially wider range of objects (partial r = -0.44; 95% CI = -0.45 to -0.43; P < 2.2 × 10 -16 ; partial Spearman's ρ = -0.41; n = 36,124 search terms), such that each one-point decrease in concreteness corresponds to 85.8 additional unique objects at a given number of images. More male-typed concepts, which are more abstract, are likewise depicted through a wider range of objects. This holds when the male association of a term is measured using its images (Fig. 4b; partial r = 0.10; 95% CI = 0.09 to 0.11; P < 2.2 × 10 -16 ; ρ = 0.07; n = 35,603 search terms with at least one face) and when it is measured using its gender association in text (Fig. 4c; partial r = 0.09; 95% CI = 0.08 to 0.10; P < 2.2 × 10 -16 ; ρ = 0.10; n = 36,124 search terms; two-tailed partial Pearson's correlations controlling for the log-transformed number of images). All three associations replicate when the number of images is not controlled for: r = -0.38, 0.11 and 0.07 for Fig. 4a-c, respectively, each with P < 2.2 × 10 -16 ; they also replicate under alternative specifications of the control, when additionally controlling for the total number of objects detected, and when examining only terms with at least 200 images; Supplementary Section 13 and Supplementary Table 22. Importantly, recent work shows that the representation of people in online images has measurable effects on strengthening gender biases in people's representation of concepts 53, 55 . We thus proceed to test whether the male abstraction bias is indeed measurable in people's implicit associations with a wide variety of concepts. So far, all of our findings rely on aggregate patterns in large observational datasets. This raises the question of whether the male abstraction bias is merely an emergent statistical regularity of cultural data, or whether it is also an implicit bias in the minds of individuals. Asking this question amounts to an out-of-sample test of whether patterns discovered in large-scale cultural data yield insights into individual cognition. This applies as well to AI agents, which are trained on this large-scale cultural data, suggesting the ability for biases therein to propagate through the AI and its responses. To this end, we analyzed existing data on the free associations that both humans and AI agents generate in response to a wide range of cue words: the Small World of Words dataset (SWOW) 60 , in which human participants listed the first three words that came to mind for each of more than 12,000 cues, and the LLM World of Words dataset (LWOW) 61 , which administered the same task to three large language models (Llama 3, Claude 3.5 Haiku and Mistral) alongside the human responses. These datasets were collected by different teams for different purposes, with neither concreteness nor gender in mind, and thus provide a conservative test. We matched each cue word to its human-rated concreteness, and we measured the gender association of every freely associated word using each of our 12 language models, which yielded the mean gender association of the responses to each cue ('Free Association Data' in Materials and Methods).

Evidence from the Implicit Free Associations of Humans and AI

Figure 5 shows that the more concrete (abstract) a cue word is, the more feminine (masculine) the associations that humans and AI agents generate. For example, when the gender association of responses is measured using OpenAI's embedding model, the correlation between cue concreteness and response gender was r = -0.21 for humans in SWOW (95% CI = -0.22 to -0.19; n = 11,179 cues) and r = -0.21 for humans in LWOW (95% CI = -0.22 to -0.19; n = 10,495), and r = -0.35 for Llama 3 (95% CI = -0.36 to -0.33; n = 10,496), r = -0.28 for Claude 3.5 Haiku (95% CI = -0.30 to -0.27; n = 10,527) and r = -0.22 for Mistral (95% CI = -0.24 to -0.20; n = 10,527; all P < 2.2 × 10 -16 ; two-tailed Pearson's correlations). These results are robust to the method used to measure the gender association of responses. Averaging across all 12 scoring models, the mean correlation was -0.18 for humans in SWOW (95% CI across models = -0.25 to -0.11; t(11) = -5.73; P = 1.3 × 10 -4 ), -0.18 for humans in LWOW (-0.25 to -0.11; t(11) = -5.68; P = 1.4 × 10 -4 ), -0.22 for Llama 3 (-0.30 to -0.14; t(11) = -6.34; P = 5.5 × 10 -5 ), -0.18 for Claude 3.5 Haiku (-0.25 to -0.12; t(11) = -6.17; P = 7.0 × 10 -5 ) and -0.14 for Mistral (-0.21 to -0.08; t(11) = -4.97; P = 4.2 × 10 -4 ; two-tailed one-sample t-tests across models). Of the 60 correlations examined, 51 were negative and significant after Bonferroni correction, six were not significant, and three were significantly positive; the latter all involved a single scoring model (ConceptNet) applied to the responses of AI agents (r ≤ 0.10). One possible reason that ConceptNet deviates from the other models is that it is distinctly designed to factor in expert-coded taxonomic knowledge about the relationships among concepts that are not learned via population-level word association patterns. Overall, 9 of the 12 scoring models yielded a significant negative correlation for every source of responses (Fig. 5). These findings provide evidence that both humans and AI agents have internalized the male abstraction bias at an implicit level, as indicated by their patterns of free association.

Discussion

Across the space of everyday concepts, more abstract terms and phrases are more strongly associated with men and more concrete analogs with women. This pattern appears whether abstractness is judged by people or by AI, whether it is measured as rated concreteness or as sensorimotor strength, and whether gender is measured in the distributional structure of billions of words, in the faces that populate millions of online images, or in the free associations that humans and large language models produce in controlled online tasks. Prior work has located gender differences in abstraction in how women and men communicate and construe events 47, 48 , or in how perceivers categorize them in different social roles 49, 50 , based consistently in modest-scale and context-specific observations (e.g., occupations in the workplace). Our results shift the level of analysis to a much broader scope: the male abstraction bias pervades conceptual structure not just as a bias for differentially describing men and women, but as a bias that warps the meanings of most conceptual domains including everyday objects and events.

Moreover, we show that the male abstraction bias maps tightly onto grammatical structure: within and across a wide variety of domains, action-oriented terms-verbs and adverbs-are both more abstract and more male-coded, whereas object-oriented nouns are more concrete and more female-coded, suggesting as a broader pattern that men are afforded dynamic representations as verbs whereas women are represented more statically and passively as nouns. As a theoretical advance, this suggests that gender stereotypes relating to agency are not simply a macro bias that emerges in descriptions of men and women across different social roles and activities (e.g., relating to norms of leadership in the workplace); instead, they appear to be woven into the function of some of the most basic and central concepts organizing grammar. This suggests that interventions relating to changing role dynamics among women and men (e.g., by promoting women to leadership positions), while undoubtedly important and impactful 44 , need to be complemented with broader cultural shifts relating not just to the conceptualization of people, but also to basic, systemic patterns in the representation of concepts across topic domains.

Generally, by showing how the male abstraction bias pervades the cultural record in both online textual and image data, this raises concern for how this bias is poised to be inherited and reproduced by any AI system trained on that record. The free-association results indicate that this male abstraction bias is not merely an aggregate regularity of corpora but is verifiably internalized in implicit concept activation patterns that individuals reproduce without any explicit gender prime, a pattern that AI models reproduce as strongly as people do. This reveals how the male abstraction bias is vulnerable to algorithmic amplification via a cultural evolutionary process: through AI systems, stereotypes increasingly shape decisions and judgments about what is real, and thereby become real in their consequences 55, 83 . Indeed, the male abstraction bias is already visible in consequential settings. In more than half a million LinkedIn recommendations spanning industries and occupations, men are more likely to be recommended in abstract terms and women in concrete ones 48 , and exposure to gender-biased online representations measurably shifts people's beliefs and hiring preferences, which search and generative algorithms then learn and reproduce [53] [54] [55].

These findings also provide mechanistic insights that establish a link between literatures in cognitive science and sociology that are rarely brought together. A key premise of embodied cognition is that humans leverage concrete referents in bodily, perceptual experience to help ground and metaphorically represent less perceivable, abstract concepts. Our findings indicate that people play an outsized role as concrete referents for representing abstract concepts, raising the question of which kinds of people are central as concrete anchors throughout conceptual systems 32, 52, 62, [84] [85] [86] . Our evidence indicates a bias toward male representation when people are used as concrete anchors in abstraction; our image data shows not only that abstract concepts are more likely to be depicted by people, but also in particular by men. This association is shaped by systemic inequalities in human cultural evolution. Historically, men have been privileged in their ability to cultivate abstract reasoning and to specialize in the high-status knowledge work of science, art, technology and politics, and the association of reason with masculinity is a long-standing cultural construction [87] [88] [89] [90] . This has given men unequal opportunity to gain recognition as symbols and role models in domains of abstract reasoning and prestigious knowledge work, on which many foundational cultural narratives are constructed. If men are sociologically favored-through stereotypes, norms, and privileged access to cultivation and resources-to accumulate experience and symbolic recognition in abstract reasoning, then that experience may reinforce the associative bias that licenses it via a self-fulfilling prophecy 91 . As this bias becomes widespread and amplified via AI, it may further entrench the conceptual strategies (e.g., the use of embodied concrete anchors for representing abstraction) that warp the foundations of cognition along gender lines.

As a theoretical advance, our findings help identify an explanatory framework in which many distinct gender stereotypes are manifestations and context-specific expressions of a simpler, more systematic associative bias running through the entire conceptual system. Many stereotypes cast women as communal and warm-concrete, embodied qualities-and men as competent, rational, task-oriented and "colder" [36] [37] [38] 92, 93 ; note, as well, that emotions themselves are often framed as "hot" cognition, in contrast to, in Lakoff's words, "more than cool reason" 94 . In general, men are assumed to be better at mathematics and STEM despite no empirical basis for the belief 12, [40] [41] [42] , with consequences for self-sorting into degree programs and occupations 95 . Each of these stereotype patterns may, in fact, be a subset or extension of a deeper, more systematic semantic association between men and abstraction that pervades human thinking. Such an association may have played a key role in the development of belief systems early in human cultural evolution, given the nearuniform association between men and the leading philosophers, scientists, political leaders and religious thinkers who set the abstract paradigms that have shaped human thought for millennia and continue to shape it today 87, 88, 96.

For broader context on the implications of our findings, it is useful to consider how AI itself is gendered. AI understood as a highly abstract and rationally engineered model of intelligence appears to be male-coded. News imagery about AI is dominated by male faces 97 , people attribute a male gender to chatbots and to ChatGPT when no gender cue is given 98, 99 , and the thinking machine and its makers have long been culturally coded as masculine [100] [101] [102] , with a parallel argument made for its coding as white 103 . However, commercial voice assistants such as Siri, Alexa and Cortana were launched with female voices and names by default in most markets, and most text-based chatbots are designed with female names and avatars 104, 105 , a pattern feminist scholars have read as the automation of feminized service and domestic labor 106, 107 . Experimental work also finds that bots labeled female are judged more human than those labeled male 108 , and that people exploit female-labeled AI partners more than female-labeled human ones 109 . Wong and Kim 99 find that the default perception of ChatGPT as male reverses when its capacity to provide emotional support is made salient, and Han et al. 98 find that the male default weakens to roughly neutral when a chatbot is placed in a nursing role, which suggests AI is read as male when framed as competence and reasoning, and as female when framed as emotional care and service. This division sits closely alongside the gender-abstraction association we document. As AI becomes symbolic of what people believe "superior" intelligence looks like, it may reinforce the perception that intelligence is intrinsically a form of abstract and rationalized reasoning, and that by being so it is also implicitly male, while the assistive and relational work done by the same systems continues to be coded as female. Both tendencies arguably reinforce systemic gender inequalities.

We conclude by highlighting important limitations of our study and thus valuable directions for future research. Our evidence is correlational and cannot by itself establish the direction of causation between gendered experience and conceptual structure. Our aim in this study is to provide a comprehensive identification of the male abstraction bias spanning common concepts and communication modalities (online text and images), and to demonstrate the robustness of this pattern to a myriad of measurement techniques and controls. To ensure our findings are of psychological relevance at the individual level, and are not merely a statistical pattern that emerges in large-scale social data, we successfully conducted out-of-sample tests using existing experimental data to show that the male abstraction bias is measurable in the free associations generated by humans and AI. This provides a foundation for future research aimed at providing controlled experimental evidence of the male abstraction bias and its consequences for various psychological processes. Moreover, while we conducted a number of robustness tests demonstrating the replicability of the male abstraction bias across nine languages from different linguistic families (which vary in whether gender is morphologically grammaticalized), nevertheless, our largest and most comprehensive analyses are from English. Because English is the lingua franca of the internet, findings from English are the most likely to capture patterns of content association and consumption that characterize internet activity around the globe, but there remains substantial cultural variation in gender norms and concept representation that cannot be captured from these analyses. Our cross-linguistic analyses are sufficient for demonstrating that the male abstraction bias generalizes beyond English, but identifying the extent of this generalization is a compelling direction for future studies 58, 110, 111 . Given that men have occupied positions of privilege in the pursuit and dissemination of high-status, abstract knowledge work across cultures and throughout recorded history 10, 87, 89 , we expect the male abstraction bias to hold across cultures; discovering deviations from this pattern and linking such deviations to variation in conceptual, linguistic, and social structure, marks rich new terrain for computational studies of gender stereotypes and cultural evolution more broadly.

Materials and Methods

Textual Analysis (Embedding Methods)

Concreteness Norms. Human judgements of concreteness were taken from the norms of Brysbaert et al. 21 , in which crowdsourced raters evaluated 39,953 generally known English words on a scale from 1 (abstract) to 5 (concrete), and from their extension by Muraki et al. 63 to 62,889 multiword expressions, collected with the same instructions and scale. GPT-generated concreteness ratings for the multiword expressions (n = 62,890; Fig. 1b) were taken from Martinez et al. 28 . Perceptual strength was taken from the Lancaster Sensorimotor Norms 75 , which rate how strongly each word's referent is experienced through each of six perceptual modalities (0-5); we averaged the six ratings for the 39,706 words that also appear in the concreteness norms and extended the measure to phrases by averaging over each phrase's constituent words (Supplementary Section 8).

Language Models. Gender associations were measured in 12 language models that differ in architecture, training data and year of release (Supplementary Table 1; Supplementary Section 1.3): four fixed-vocabulary static word-embedding models (word2vec, FastText, Wikipedia GloVe and ConceptNet Numberbatch); four transformer language models used without a sentence-embedding head (BERT, distilBERT, RoBERTa and GPT2); three open-weight sentence-embedding models (MPNet, Qwen3 Embedding and Nomic Embed v2); and one closed, provider-managed embedding model (OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large, labelled GPT4). Each term is embedded as a complete string; the static models are queried for the phrase itself and otherwise fall back to the mean of the available token vectors. Eight of the models cover the whole vocabulary (or all but ten terms) and the four static models cover between 87,678 and 97,051 terms. The same 12 models are used wherever a text-based gender association appears in the paper (Figs. 1, 2, 4c and 5).

Measuring Gender Associations.

We used the geometry-of-culture method 64 , as applied to gender in our prior work 53, 55 , which represents gender association as a position along a dimension identified via contrasting poles in embedding space. In each model, a gender axis was built from five seed pairs (man/woman, him/her, he/she, male/female and boy/girl): each seed term was embedded and normalised to unit length, the male-minus-female difference was averaged across the five pairs, and the mean difference was normalised (Supplementary Section 1.2, Eq. S1). The gender association of a term is the cosine similarity between its unit-normalised embedding and this axis, with positive values indicating masculine associations; the zero point is fixed by the seed terms rather than by the mean of the vocabulary. Scores were computed for 103,428 terms (39,953 words and 63,475 multiword expressions) in each model. Because the models differ in scale, gender associations are compared within models: deciles (Fig. 1) were computed separately within each model, and where gender associations are combined across models (Figs. 2 and 4c) they were first z-scored within each model and then averaged with equal weight, with confidence intervals computed across models. Our prior work validated this measure against human judgements of the gender associations of the same terms and showed that it is robust to the choice of seed pairs 53, 55 ; seed-pair sensitivity analyses for the cross-language replications are reported in Supplementary Tables 3, 5, 7 and 9.

Identifying Semantic Domains.

To decompose the results by semantic domain (Fig. 2), each word and phrase was matched to WordNet 82 and assigned the domain that WordNet records for its primary sense (for example noun.food or verb.communication). Words were matched within their dominant part of speech as recorded in the concreteness norms, which prevents nouns from entering verb domains through rare verb senses; phrases were matched through their own WordNet entry or, failing that, through their head noun. Function words, lexically gendered terms (for example 'waitress') and nouns that denote persons were excluded, so that the analysis captures the gendering of concepts rather than of people, leaving 31,591 words in 35 domains and 56,879 phrases in 33 domains. Word forms (Fig. 2c,d) follow the same mapping, with noun domains divided into those denoting events and states and those denoting entities. Supplementary Section 11 and Supplementary Table 18 show that the within-domain results do not depend on the method of domain assignment.

Statistical Analysis of Textual Data and Robustness Tests.

For each model we computed two-tailed Pearson's correlations between gender association and concreteness, with Fishertransformed 95% confidence intervals and P values Bonferroni-corrected for the number of models. To summarize across models we treated models as the units of analysis: we report the unweighted mean of the per-model estimates, a 95% confidence interval from the t distribution across models and a two-tailed one-sample t-test against zero, so that no model is weighted more heavily than another. Within-domain contrasts (Fig. 2a,b) use two-tailed binomial sign tests across domains and paired t-tests across models; word-form contrasts (Fig. 2c,d) use two-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank tests across models. Supplementary analyses show that the text-based results replicate for words and phrases analysed separately (Supplementary Section 7); hold for perceptual strength extended to phrases (Supplementary Section 8); survive controls for word frequency, length, polysemy, age of acquisition, valence, arousal, dominance and part of speech, as well as the exclusion of person nouns, lexically gendered terms, function words and the most strongly gendered terms (Supplementary Section 9); are visible at the level of individual words within domains (Supplementary Section 10); and replicate in Dutch, Estonian, Mandarin Chinese and Persian, using native-speaker concreteness norms 26, [76] [77] [78] [79] , language-specific word vectors 80, 81 and gender axes built from kinship and role seed pairs (Supplementary Sections 2 to 5), as well as in smaller norms for five further languages (Supplementary Section 6).

Image Analysis

Image Data. For each of the 39,956 terms in the single-word concreteness norms 21 , we searched Google Images and retained the top results, up to 300 images per term (median 265), yielding 11,346,925 images. Images were downloaded into one directory per search term and named by the MD5 hash of their contents, so that byte-identical duplicates within a term collapse to a single file (Supplementary Section 1.1).

Image Classification.

Every image was classified by Llama-3.2-11B-Vision-Instruct 73, 74 , an openweight vision-language model, run on Stanford's high-performance computing infrastructure behind an OpenAI-compatible inference endpoint. The model was prompted with a single fixed instruction, identical for every image and containing no reference to the search term, so that classification is conditioned on the image alone. From its responses we obtained, for each image, a free-text list of the objects depicted, the number of human faces, and the gender (male or female) of each face together with the model's confidence in that classification. Object labels are open-vocabulary rather than drawn from a fixed taxonomy; they were lowercased, stripped of formatting, digits and punctuation, and matched to the lexicon of the concreteness norms, and labels identical to the search term were discarded. This yields 9,977,592 images with at least one lexicon-matched object across 39,918 search terms, and 1,211,815 detected faces (67.3% classified as male) across the 35,913 terms whose images contained at least one face.

Free Association Tests

Free Association Data. We analysed two existing free-association datasets collected by other teams for purposes unrelated to gender or concreteness. The Small World of Words English norms (SWOW) 60 were collected by continued free association: each participant was shown a cue word and typed the first three words that came to mind, in order, with roughly 100 participants per cue and more than 12,000 cues; we used the released strength file, which aggregates the three response slots into cue-response counts. The LLM World of Words (LWOW) 61 administered the same task to three large language models-Mistral (mistral-7b), Llama 3 (llama3.1-8b) and Claude 3.5 Haiku (claude-3-5-haiku-latest)-alongside human responses to the same cues: each model was given a cue and instructed to write the first three words it associates with it, separated by commas, and the prompt was repeated 100 times per cue, yielding three response slots per repetition, which we aggregated into cue-response counts. In both datasets, cues and responses were lowercased and trimmed, non-responses and responses identical to the cue were removed, and cues were retained where a human concreteness rating 21 was available, leaving 11,179 SWOW cues and 10,495 to 10,527 LWOW cues per source of responses (Supplementary Section 1.4). For each cue and each of the 12 language models, the gender association of the responses is the mean gender association of the responded words, weighted by how often each response was produced; it was correlated with the cue's concreteness using two-tailed Pearson's correlations, giving 60 correlations (five sources of responses × 12 scoring models) that were Bonferroni-corrected and summarized across scoring models as above.

The Male Abstraction Bias in Internet Culture and Large Language Models 1 Data and methods

1.1 Image dataset construction

Our lexicon is the concreteness norms of Brysbaert et al. 21 , which contain 39,954 entries covering generally known English words together with a set of two-word expressions, each rated on a five-point scale from abstract to concrete. We searched Google Images for every term and retained up to 300 images per term. Images were downloaded in advance into one directory per search term, each file named by the MD5 hash of its contents, so that byte-identical duplicates within a term collapse to a single file.

Each image was then labelled by Llama-3.2-11B-Vision-Instruct 73, 74 , an open-weight visionlanguage model built on the LLaMA family, run on university high-performance computing in collaboration with the DARC team. The model was served behind an OpenAI-compatible inference endpoint and prompted with a single fixed instruction, identical for every image and containing no reference to the search term, so that labelling is conditioned on the image alone. The prompt was 41 tokens on every call. The model returns a short comma-separated list of the objects it identifies, in free text, so labelling is generative and open-vocabulary rather than detection against a fixed taxonomy. This produced 11,346,925 labelled images across 39,929 search terms.

Labels were parsed from the model's response and normalised: lowercased, stripped of bullet formatting, digits and punctuation, and collapsed to single spacing. Multiword expressions appearing in the concreteness norms were joined into single labels, labels identical to the search query were removed, and the remainder were retained where they matched the lexicon. After parsing and lexical filtering the analysis set retains 9,977,592 images across 39,918 search terms, with a median of 265 images per term and a mean of 250.

Labels were aggregated across all images for a search term and saved to disk in two forms: a dictionary mapping each label to its occurrence count, and the list of distinct labels. Repeated labels within an image are counted rather than collapsed, so an object token is a label occurrence. No frequency threshold was applied at aggregation. The dataset contains 77,170,514 object tokens across 63,540 distinct object categories, with a mean of 7.73 objects per image and a median of 5. Every one of the 39,918 search terms has at least one recorded object. Per term, the median is 1,952 object tokens and 800 distinct categories.

1.2 Gender scores

We follow the geometric approach of Kozlowski et al. 64 , applied to images and text in Guilbeault et al. 53 , which represents a cultural dimension as a direction in embedding space. The gender axis is built from five seed pairs: man/woman, him/her, he/she, male/female, and boy/girl. Each seed term is embedded and its vector normalised to unit length, the male-minus-female difference is averaged across the five pairs, and that mean difference is itself normalised to give the axis: (S1) A term's gender score is the cosine similarity between its unit-normalised embedding and this axis, g(t) = unit(⃗ v t ) ⊤ ⃗ a, so a positive score falls on the male side.

The zero point is fixed by the anchors and is independent of the mean of any corpus. Scores were generated for 103,428 terms: 39,953 from the concreteness norms above and a further 63,475 multiword expressions from Muraki et al. 63 , deduplicated case-insensitively with the first source taking precedence.

Gender at the object level, for a given search term, is the token-weighted mean of the gender scores of its labels, where c o is the occurrence count of label o and S is the set of labels with a finite score under the model in question.

Labels without a score are excluded from both numerator and denominator. The measure is weighted by occurrence, not computed over distinct labels.

The text-based gender association of a search term in Fig. 4c is the equal-weight mean of its gender scores across the 12 language models of Supplementary Table 1, each first z-scored within the 39,953-word vocabulary; terms scored by fewer than eight models would be excluded, but every term with a concreteness rating is scored by at least eight.

Because the number of unique objects detected for a term rises with the number of images in its image set, which varied across terms (median 265; mean 256; s.d. 39; range 3-300 among the terms analysed), every estimate in Fig. 4 is from a regression of the number of unique objects on the predictor and the log-transformed number of images, and the figure displays each term's count adjusted to the mean number of images. The unadjusted estimates and alternative specifications of the control are reported in Supplementary Section 13.

1.3 Embedding models

Gender scores were computed under 12 language models, which fall into four groups according to how a term is turned into a vector. The same 12 models are used wherever a text-based gender association appears in the paper: Figs. 1 and 2, the text-based gender association of search terms in Fig. 4c, the scoring of free-association responses in Fig. 5, and the text-based supplementary analyses.

Four fixed-vocabulary word-embedding models-word2vec-google-news-300, fasttextwiki-news-subwords-300, 300-dimensional GloVe vectors trained on Wikipedia and Gigaword ('Wikipedia GloVe') and ConceptNet Numberbatch-are queried for the exact phrase first, then for lowercase and underscore variants, and otherwise fall back to the mean of the available token vectors. OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large ('GPT4') is called through the API and returns a 3,072-dimensional embedding of the complete string. Three sentence-transformer models use their own native pooling: all-mpnet-base-v2 ('MPNet'), Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B ('Qwen3') and nomic-embed-text-v2-moe ('Nomic v2 MoE'); Nomic is given the prefix search_document: on both anchors and vocabulary items, as its model card requires. Four transformer language models are used without a sentence-embedding head: gpt2 as a static embedding table, in which a term is tokenised and its vector is the mean of the input embeddings of its subword tokens, with special and padding tokens excluded; and bert-base-uncased, distilbert-base-uncased and roberta-base.

Eight of the 12 models cover the full vocabulary of 103,429 distinct terms (the three BERTfamily models all terms, and text-embedding-3-large, MPNet, Qwen3, Nomic and GPT2 all but ten); the fixed-vocabulary models cover fewer, the shortfall arising from terms absent from their vocabularies (Supplementary Table 1). Scoring ran at a batch size of 128, or 512 for the OpenAI batch jobs, with automatic device selection and no cap on vocabulary size.

1.4 Free association datasets

We draw on two free association datasets, one generated by language models and one collected from human participants. The LLM World of Words 61 provides free association norms produced by three generators: mistral-7b, llama3.1-8b and claude-3-5-haiku-latest. Each generator is given a cue with the instruction to write the first three words it associates with that cue, separated by commas, and the prompt is repeated one hundred times per cue, yielding three response slots on each repetition. We load the processed release, melt the three response columns into a single response column, normalise case and whitespace, drop blank and missing responses, and sum occurrences for every cue-response pair. Our association file retains 10,527 cues for each generator, of which 10,495 to 10,527 (depending on the generator) have a cue concreteness rating and enter the analysis.

The Small World of Words English release 60 provides the human comparison. It was collected by continued free association: a participant is shown a cue and gives three responses in order, with roughly one hundred participants per cue. We use the strength release, strength.SWOW-EN.R123.20180827.csv, which aggregates the three response slots (R123) into cue-response counts: 1,389,711 cue-response pairs across 12,216 cues. Cues and responses are lower-cased and trimmed; non-response strings (no more responses, unknown word, nan, none, na and the empty string) and responses identical to the cue are excluded. Cues are retained where a cue concreteness rating is available in the concreteness norms, which leaves 11,179 cues for every scoring model; the number of responses scored varies by model with its vocabulary coverage (Supplementary Table 1).

In both datasets the outcome measure is the mean gender score of a cue's responses, weighted by how often each response was produced, with the weights normalised to sum to one.

2 Replication of the text-based result in Dutch

To test whether the association between gender and concreteness in Fig. 1a is specific to English, we repeated the single-word analysis in Dutch, the only language other than English for which crowdsourced concreteness ratings exist at a comparable scale. In a different paper, Brysbaert et al. 26 provide ratings of concreteness (1, abstract; 5, concrete) and of age of word acquisition for 30,000 Dutch lemmas, collected with the same instructions and rating scale as the English norms; we used the 25,881 distinct words with a concreteness rating in the distributed file (mean 2.93; s.d. 0.99). Dutch is also informative because its nouns carry no masculine or feminine grammatical gender (the two Dutch genders are common and neuter), so a replication cannot be attributed to grammatical gender marking.

Gender associations were measured in the Dutch-trained static word vectors distributed with spaCy's nl_core_news_lg model (version 3.8.0; 500,000 vocabulary entries, 300 dimensions) 80 , a language-specific model rather than one of the English or multilingual models of Supplementary Table 1. The gender axis was constructed exactly as in Eq. S1, from five seed pairs chosen before any result was examined: man/vrouw, jongen/meisje (boy/girl), mannelijk/vrouwelijk (male/female), vader/moeder (father/mother) and broer/zus (brother/sister). The two pronoun pairs of the English axis were not transferred, because their Dutch counterparts are lexically ambiguous: zij is both she and they, and haar is both her and hair. They were retained as a separate specification so that the consequence of this ambiguity could be reported (Supplementary Table 3, family C). An extended axis added five further kinship and role pairs (zoon/dochter, oom/tante, koning/koningin, heer/dame, opa/oma), and a third variant took the first principal component of the ten unit-normalised pair differences 119 , sign-aligned with their mean. Each rated word was scored as the cosine between its unit-normalised vector and the axis, positive values indicating masculine associations; words absent from the vector table (8.2%) were not scored, leaving 23,758 words. As a check on the axis that does not involve concreteness, we verified that the primary axis orders the male above the female member in all 27 gendered word pairs that were not used as seeds (for example koning/koningin, acteur/actrice, oom/tante, meneer/mevrouw).

The statistics mirror Supplementary Sections 7 and 9: two-tailed Pearson's correlations with Fisher-transformed 95% confidence intervals, Spearman's rank correlations, mean concreteness within deciles of the gender score, and ordinary least squares regressions of the z-scored gender score on z-scored concreteness with heteroskedasticity-robust (HC1) standard errors, whose coefficient is a standardized β. Covariates were word frequency (the Zipf-scaled frequency of the wordfreq library 120 , which combines subtitle, Wikipedia and web corpora; 23,407 words have an entry), length in characters, and age of acquisition from the same norms. We also report the association within each quintile of word frequency, after excluding words that contain a gendered morpheme or a kinship or role stem (for example -man, vrouw-, -ster, vader, koning) together with the seed words themselves, and after excluding the 5% or 10% of words with the most extreme gender scores. Finally, we recomputed the correlation for each axis obtained by dropping one seed pair from the primary set, for each of the ten kinship and role pairs on its own, and for the pronoun pairs. The association is robust to the lexical properties examined for English in Supplementary Section 9 (Supplementary Table 2). Controlling for word frequency left the estimate unchanged (β = -0.269), controlling for length reduced it slightly (β = -0.235), and controlling for frequency, length and age of acquisition together gave β = -0.245 (-0.258 to -0.231; n = 23,398). The correlation was negative in every quintile of word frequency (r = -0.233 to -0.304), and it was unchanged or slightly stronger after excluding words containing a gendered morpheme or kinship stem together with the seed words (r = -0.268; n = 23,062) and after excluding the 5% or 10% of words with the most extreme gender scores (r = -0.281 and -0.284), so it is not carried by a small set of explicitly gendered words. The same holds for the extended axis in every specification (β or r between -0.221 and -0.335).

The Dutch result is, however, more sensitive to the choice of seed pairs than the English results, and Supplementary Table 3 reports this sensitivity in full, together with the five most feminine and five most masculine words under each axis. Every axis built from several unambiguous kinship or role pairs is negative: the primary set (-0.268), the extended set (-0.292), each of the five axes obtained by dropping one pair from the primary set (-0.148 to -0.339), and the first principal component of the ten pair differences (-0.215), which captures 43% of their variance and on which all ten pairs load positively (0.38 to 0.84). The feminine extreme of these axes is populated by words for girls, brides and feminine appearance (bruidsmeisje, tienermeisje, brunette, lingerie) and the masculine extreme by words for leaders and military or feudal roles (aanvoerder, voorman, bevelhebber, veldheer, leenheer), as expected of a gender axis. Single pairs are noisier: six of the ten kinship and role pairs taken alone are negative (from heer/dame, -0.375, to opa/oma, -0.136), whereas oom/tante and mannelijk/vrouwelijk are close to zero and zoon/dochter and man/vrouw are positive (0.122 and 0.239). The man/vrouw axis illustrates why single pairs are unreliable in a static vector table: its masculine extreme consists of colloquial terms for a foolish or idle man (prutser, malloot, mafkees, hooligan), reflecting the use of man as an informal term of address, and its feminine extreme of abstract nouns for sexuality and motherhood (moederschap, seksualiteit, zwangerschap), so that it conflates gender with register and with the concreteness of the seed words themselves. Averaging across several pairs removes this pair-specific variance, which is the rationale for the multi-pair construction of Eq. S1.

The pronoun pairs of the English axis cannot be used in Dutch, and including them reverses the sign (pronoun set, r = 0.217; hij/zij alone, 0.365; hem/haar alone, 0.210). These axes are visibly not gender axes: the "feminine" extreme of the hij/zij axis is wij, we, hun, zij and gezamenlijk (we, they, their, jointly), because zij also means they, and that of the hem/haar axis is van, hun, alsook and rondom (of, their, as well as, around), because haar also means hair and is a possessive. Seed pairs for other languages should therefore be checked for this kind of ambiguity before an axis is built, and the extremes of the resulting axis inspected, as done here.

In sum, with seed pairs that refer unambiguously to women and men, the association between masculine connotation and abstractness replicates in Dutch with a magnitude at the upper end of the English estimates, in a language whose nouns carry no grammatical gender. Extending the test to languages with grammatical gender, and to languages further from English, requires concreteness norms of comparable scale, which currently exist at the scale of ten thousand words or more only for Estonian and Mandarin 76, 121 . The Estonian, Chinese and Persian tests are reported in Supplementary Sections 3 to 5.

The adjective pair mehelik/naiselik shows a further, fastText-specific weakness: because fastText represents words through character n-grams, its masculine extreme includes unrelated words that merely share the suffix string (ahelik, võimeline, liikmeline), which is why that pair contributes little to the axis (loading 0.29) and why its omission leaves the primary estimate unchanged (-0.124). Averaging over several unambiguous pairs removes this pair-specific variance, as it did in Dutch.

In sum, the male abstraction bias replicates in a genderless Finno-Ugric language, in a second embedding family, with a magnitude between the English and Dutch estimates. Across the two replications, the same methodological regularities appear: axes averaged over several kinship and role pairs agree in sign and are validated by held-out gendered pairs, whereas the bare man/woman pair on its own is unreliable in both languages; and the words at the masculine extreme are predominantly nouns for men in roles, whose concreteness as person nouns attenuates rather than produces the association.

8 Sensorimotor results extended to phrases

The Lancaster Sensorimotor Norms 75 provide human ratings, for approximately 40,000 single words, of how strongly the referent of each word is experienced through each of six perceptual modalities (auditory, gustatory, haptic, interoceptive, olfactory and visual), each on a scale from 0 (not experienced at all) to 5 (experienced greatly). We measured the perceptual strength of a word as its mean rating across the six modalities. Fig. 1c of the main text reports the relationship between gender association and perceptual strength for the 39,706 words in the concreteness norms that were rated in the Lancaster norms (Supplementary Table 12, left). Here we ask whether the same relationship holds for multiword phrases, which the Lancaster norms do not cover. We extended the norms to phrases by assigning each phrase the mean perceptual strength of its constituent words that appear in the norms. Of the 62,889 phrases in the concreteness norms, 62,321 contained at least one such word; on average 91% of a phrase's words were covered, and 80% of phrases were covered completely. This extension rests on two simplifying assumptions: that the perceptual strength of a phrase is compositional, which does not hold for idiomatic phrases, and that function words, which are low in perceptual strength, contribute equally to the average. Averaging also compresses the range of perceptual strength among phrases relative to words. The extension should therefore be read as a conservative approximation.

We repeated the analysis described in Supplementary Section 7, replacing concreteness with perceptual strength, for (i) the words alone, as in Fig. 1c, (ii) the phrases alone and (iii) words and phrases pooled. Supplementary Table 12 shows that the relationship replicates in the extended data. For the phrases alone, the mean correlation across models between masculine association and perceptual strength was -0.119 (95% CI = -0.149 to -0.089; t(11) = -8.80; P = 2.6 × 10 -6 ), and the correlation was negative and significant after Bonferroni correction in every model ( 12 of 12). Phrases in the most feminine decile had a mean perceptual strength of 1.47, compared with 1.33 for phrases in the most masculine decile. For words and phrases pooled, the mean correlation was -0.073 (95% CI = -0.101 to -0.044; t(11) = -5.63; P = 1.5 × 10 -4 ; negative in 12 of 12 models and significantly so in 11, the exception being BERT, r = -0.002, P = 0.47). For comparison, the corresponding mean correlation for the words alone (Fig. 1c) was -0.067 (95% CI = -0.095 to -0.039; negative in 12 of 12 models and significantly so in 11; the exception was GPT2, r = -0.005, P = 0.31). The association between masculine connotations and weaker perceptual grounding is therefore not limited to the single words for which the norms were collected; if anything it is stronger among phrases, as was the case for concreteness (Supplementary Section 7).

9 Robustness of the text-based results to lexical covariates and to the composition of the vocabulary

Concreteness is correlated with several other lexical properties that embedding-based gender associations could, in principle, be tracking. Among the single words in the concreteness norms 21 , more concrete words are more frequent (r = 0.17 with log-transformed SUBTLEX-US frequency), shorter (r = -0.29 with length in characters and -0.39 with the number of syllables), acquired earlier (r = -0.38 with age of acquisition 117 ), more positive (r = 0.10 with valence 118 ) and less arousing (r = -0.17). The vocabulary is the 39,953 entries of the Brysbaert et al. 21 concreteness norms scored by our embedding models (Supplementary Section 1.2). We selected three WordNet 82 domains as illustrative examples spanning the major word forms analysed in Fig. 2: acts of communication (verb.communication; for example, whisper, condone), general adjectives (adj.all; WordNet's participial adjective file adj.ppl is folded in), and artifact nouns (noun.artifact; for example, blouse, doll), a domain whose membership nonetheless spans the full concreteness scale because WordNet assigns some abstract-rated words to it through non-physical senses (for example, mainstay, objective). Because the purpose of this analysis is illustrative rather than the aggregate estimation of Fig. 2, domain membership is defined more inclusively than in the main text: rather than assigning each word only the domain of its primary WordNet sense, a word is included in a domain if any of its WordNet senses falls in that domain, subject to a part-of-speech gate that requires the word's dominant part of speech in the Brysbaert norms (Dom_Pos) to match the part of speech of the domain (verbs for verb.*, nouns for noun.*, and so on). The gate is what makes the sense-based mapping safe: without it, common nouns leak into verb domains through rare verb senses (WordNet lists butterfly as a communication verb, "to talk amorously", and skirt as "to evade an issue"), even though the gender score of the word form is dominated by its nominal use. The small set of two-word entries in the norms carries no dominant part of speech, and such entries are admitted only to noun.artifact, and only when WordNet lists them exclusively as nouns (for example, wedding dress). This yields 1,393 words for verb.communication, 5,024 for adj.all and 4,634 for noun.artifact. Unlike Fig. 2, the vocabulary here is not filtered for lexically gendered terms: the aim is to show which words carry the extremes of the association, and the fact that explicitly gendered adjectives (female, macho, manly) surface at those extremes is itself informative; Fig. 2 and Supplementary Section 9 establish that the aggregate bias survives their exclusion.

Models and statistics. The three models shown are text-embedding-3-large (GPT4), Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B and nomic-embed-text-v2-moe, spanning a closed provider-managed model and two open-weight sentence transformers (Supplementary Table 1); gender scores are computed as in Supplementary Section 1.2. For each model and domain we computed the two-tailed Pearson's correlation between gender association and concreteness across all words in the domain, with 95% confidence intervals from the Fisher transformation. P values were corrected for multiple comparisons by the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure across the full family of 48 model-domain cells: the 12 language models of Figs. 1 and 2 (Supplementary Table 1) crossed with the three domains above plus adv.all, the fourth domain family examined in this analysis.

In all nine panels of Supplementary Fig. 6 the association is negative: more concrete words within a domain carry more feminine associations, and more abstract words more masculine ones, with correlations ranging from r = -0.06 (text-embedding-3-large, artifact nouns) to r = -0.22 (Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B, adjectives), all P FDR < 0.002 (Supplementary Table 17). The same holds in the fourth, unshown domain, adv.all (n = 1,654), for Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B (r = -0.10; P FDR = 8.2 × 10 -5 ) and nomic-embed-text-v2-moe (r = -0.14; P FDR = 1.9 × 10 -8 ), though not significantly for text-embedding-3-large (r = -0.03; P FDR = 0.32). Across the full 48-cell family, the correlation was negative in 39 cells and significantly so after correction in 33; the nine positive estimates were all small (r ≤ 0.03), none was significant after correction, and they were confined to the three BERT-family models (bert-base-uncased, distilbert-base-uncased and roberta-base), gpt2 and ConceptNet Numberbatch.

The highlighted words make the two poles of the association concrete. Within each panel, every word was scored for how strongly it exemplifies the bias, as the product -z(gender)×z(concreteness) of its standardized coordinates, and the three highest-scoring words in each of the two bias-consistent quadrants are labelled: abstract-and-male in blue, concrete-and-female in red. At the female-concrete pole the domains fill with garments and domestic artifacts (wedding dress, blouse, lingerie, doll, tiara), gendered descriptors of appearance (blonde, pregnant) and expressive, embodied acts of communication (crying, singing, whisper); at the male-abstract pole sit evaluative and normative terms (heinous, unforgivable, blasphemous), verbs of judgement and authority (justify, condone, vindicate, excommunicate) and abstract-rated nouns (juggernaut, mainstay, motive). 11 Sensitivity of the within-domain results to the WordNet domain assignment

The within-domain analyses of Fig. 2 and of Supplementary Section 10 both depend on a mapping from words to WordNet domains, and WordNet leaves that mapping underdetermined for polysemous words: the main text assigns each word the domain of its primary (first-listed) sense, whereas the preceding section admits a word to the domain of any of its senses, in both cases within the Results. Supplementary Table 18 summarizes the outcome under every scheme. Under the maintext rule, the most abstract third was more male-associated than the most concrete third in 32 of 37 domains (two-tailed binomial P = 7.4 × 10 -6 ; median gap across domains = 0.10 s.d.; significantly positive in 17 domains and significantly negative in none), closely matching Fig. 2 (33 of 35 domains; median gap = 0.10 s.d.) despite the more lightly filtered vocabulary. The result was essentially unchanged under the all-senses rule (35 of 40 domains; P = 1.4 × 10 -6 ; median gap = 0.12 s.d.), and held among the domain-unambiguous words alone (19 of 22 domains; P = 8.6 × 10 -4 ; median gap = 0.12 s.d.), showing that the pattern is carried by words whose domain membership involves no analytic choice at all. Across the 200 random-sense replicates, the share of domains with a positive gap ranged from 83% to 97% (median 92%), the sign test rejected the null in every replicate (largest P = 7.0 × 10 -5 ), and the median gap ranged from 0.10 to 0.15 s.d. No domain showed a significant reversal under any of the three deterministic schemes; across the random replicates, 195 of 200 contained no nominally significant reversal and none contained more than one. Finally, we verified that the word-level example panels of Supplementary Fig. 6 are not a product of their inclusive mapping. Recomputing the nine panel correlations with membership restricted to each word's primary sense (on the common single-word vocabulary; the panel sample sizes in Supplementary Table 17 additionally include two-word artifact entries), every correlation remained negative, ranging from r = -0.05 (text-embedding-3-large, artifact nouns; n = 2,266) to r = -0.24 (Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B, acts of communication; n = 630), all two-tailed P ≤ 0.014. If anything, the primary-sense correlations were stronger-most notably for acts of communication (r = -0.13, -0.09 and -0.18 under the all-senses rule versus -0.24, -0.13 and -0.23 under the primary-sense rule for Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B, text-embedding-3-large and nomic-embedtext-v2-moe, respectively)-indicating that the inclusive mapping used for illustration attenuates rather than inflates the within-domain associations.

12.1 Robustness to the exclusion of terms without faces and to statistical controls

In Fig. 3 of the main text, the male association of a search term is the fraction of male faces among all faces detected in its Google Images, and all 39,956 search terms are shown. Of these, 4,043 terms (10.1%) returned no images containing a detectable face, and these terms take a value of zero. Terms male. (E) We modelled the probability that a term returns no face. In all least-squares models the outcome and all continuous predictors were z-scored within the estimation sample, so coefficients are standardized (β); standard errors are heteroskedasticity-robust (HC1); all tests are two-sided. Supplementary Table 21 reports all estimates. The association was negative in every specification, for both measures, and every 95% confidence interval excluded the null value (zero for β and ρ, one for odds ratios). Its magnitude, however, depended substantially on the treatment of terms without faces.

For concreteness, the main-text association was β = -0.225 (95% CI -0.235 to -0.214). A linear control for the number of faces changed this little (β = -0.204), because a linear term does not absorb the discontinuity at zero faces; controlling for log(1 + faces) reduced it to β = -0.074, an indicator for any face to β = -0.047, and the indicator together with log(1 + faces) and word frequency to β = -0.031 (-0.039 to -0.024). Restricting the sample to terms with at least one face gave β = -0.070 (-0.082 to -0.059), and β = -0.048 (-0.059 to -0.037) with controls for log(1 + faces) and word frequency; estimates remained between -0.041 and -0.056 when requiring at least 5, 10 or 20 faces. Roughly two-thirds of the main-text association with concreteness is thus attributable to terms that return no faces, and the remainder to a lower male fraction among the faces that are shown: among terms with at least one face, the mean male fraction declined from 0.670 in the most abstract bin to 0.624 in the most concrete bin.

For perceptual strength, the main-text association was β = -0.084 (-0.094 to -0.074), and β = -0.049 (-0.060 to -0.038) among terms with at least one face (β = -0.048 with controls). In contrast to concreteness, the association strengthened as the minimum number of faces increased, to β = -0.062, -0.077 and -0.097 (-0.111 to -0.082) for at least 5, 10 and 20 faces.

The estimators that weight terms by their number of faces yielded the smallest estimates for concreteness: β = -0.015 (-0.028 to -0.002; P = 0.020) in the face-weighted regression, and an odds ratio of 0.987 per standard deviation (0.980 to 0.994; P = 1.8 × 10 -4 ) in the face-level quasi-binomial model, indicating that the association with concreteness is weaker among terms that return many faces. The corresponding estimates for perceptual strength were β = -0.065 (-0.077 to -0.053) and an odds ratio of 0.946 (0.940 to 0.953). Rank correlations among terms with faces were ρ = -0.047 for concreteness and ρ = -0.048 for perceptual strength. Treating terms without faces as neutral rather than as 0% male gave β = -0.123 (-0.133 to -0.113) for concreteness and β = -0.062 (-0.072 to -0.052) for perceptual strength.

Taken together, the direction of the association shown in the main text is robust to the number of detected faces, but its magnitude is an upper bound: conditional on a term returning faces, a one-standard-deviation increase in either concreteness or perceptual strength is associated with a decrease of approximately 0.05 standard deviations in the fraction of faces that are male.

13 Robustness of the unique-object results to the number of images per term

In Fig. 4 of the main text, the visual diversity of a search term is the number of unique objects detected across its Google Images. Up to 300 images were retrieved per term, but the number available varied: among the 36,124 terms analysed, the median was 265 images (mean = 256; s.d. = 39; range = 3 to 300), and 1,583 terms (4.4%) had fewer than 200. Because each additional image can only add to the set of distinct objects, the number of unique objects rises with the number of images (r = 0.47 with the number of images and 0.46 with its logarithm), so an association between a predictor and visual diversity could in principle arise if the predictor were correlated with the number of images retrieved. In practice the number of images was only weakly related to the three predictors (r = 0.05 with concreteness, 0.05 with the fraction of male faces and -0.02 with the text-based gender association), but the terms with the most images (280 or more; 14% of terms) were disproportionately concrete (mean concreteness = 3.59, compared with 2.86 for the remaining terms) and yet had fewer unique objects (755 compared with 783), so the bivariate association in Fig. 4a slightly understates the association at a fixed number of images. For this reason, Fig. 4 of the main text controls for the number of images, and this section reports how each of its three panels depends on that choice. For each panel, we estimated an ordinary least squares regression of the number of unique objects on the predictor together with a control for the number of images; the main text and Fig. 4 use the log-transformed number of images (specification (2) of Supplementary Table 22). The partial correlation reported is the Pearson correlation between the residuals of the outcome and of the predictor after each has been regressed on the control, with a 95% confidence interval from the Fisher z transformation; the partial Spearman's ρ is the rank correlation of the same residuals; and the slope is the regression coefficient for the predictor, in objects per unit of the predictor, with its conventional standard error and t statistic. In Fig. 4, each term's number of unique objects is displayed adjusted to the mean number of images, that is, its observed count minus the product of the control's coefficient and the term's deviation from the mean log number of images, and the fitted line is the regression's prediction at the mean number of images. Specification (1) is the bivariate association without the control. Specifications (3)-(5) vary the functional form of the control: the untransformed number of images, a quadratic in the number of images and fixed effects for deciles of the number of images, which make no assumption about functional form. Specification (6) adds the log-transformed total number of object tokens detected for the term; this is a stricter control, because the number of objects detected per image is itself part of what the diversity measure captures. Specifications ( 7) and ( 8) restrict the analysis to the 34,541 terms with at least 200 images (34,149 for panel (b)), without and with the log control.

Supplementary Table 22 shows that all three associations are robust. The association between concreteness and visual diversity strengthened under every control, from r = -0.38 without the control to between -0.39 (decile fixed effects) and -0.48 (log images and object tokens), with the slope changing from 83.5 to between 55.2 and 90.0 additional unique objects per point of concreteness. The association with the fraction of male faces was essentially unchanged by the number of images (r = 0.11 without the control; 0.10 under specifications (2)-( 5)) and remained positive and highly significant under the stricter object-token control (r = 0.06). The association with the text-based gender association rose slightly under the image controls (r = 0.07 without the control; 0.09 under specifications (2)-(4) and 0.07 with decile fixed effects) and was larger still under the object-token control (r = 0.15). Restricting the analysis to terms with at least 200 images gave the same picture. Across all 24 estimates, every association retained its sign, and every P value was below 2.2 × 10 -16.

5 Replication of the text-based result in Persian

Persian provides a fourth test, in a language of the Indo-Iranian branch of Indo-European written in a right-to-left script. Like Estonian and Chinese, Persian is genderless: nouns, adjectives and articles carry no grammatical gender, and the third-person pronoun u covers both he and she.

The concreteness norms are those of the Extrapolated Persian Lexical Affect Norms (E-PLAN) of Nemati et al. 79 , which differ from the other norms used in this paper in how they were collected: rather than rating each word on a scale, 1,071 Persian speakers judged small sets of words, choosing the most and the least concrete word in each set (best-worst scaling), and each word's score is derived from how often it was chosen as most versus least concrete and expressed on a scale from 0 (abstract) to 1 (concrete). The human-rated set covers 2,924 words (mean 0.51; s.d. 0.13), and the authors validate it against earlier Persian rating-scale norms. They also provide extrapolated scores for 85,716 words, obtained by fitting generalized additive models on principal components of Persian skip-gram embeddings to the human judgements (R² = 0.68 for concreteness); because these scores are themselves derived from word embeddings, we use them only as a secondary check and report the human-rated words as the result. Gender associations were measured in fastText's Persian word vectors 81 (Common Crawl and Wikipedia; 2 million words, 300 dimensions); every one of the 2,924 human-rated words has a vector. The seed pairs are the Persian counterparts of the primary and extended sets of the other languages, all kinship and role nouns and one adjective pair, since Persian has no gendered pronouns: mard/zan (man/woman), pesar/dokhtar (boy/girl), mardāne/zanāne (masculine/feminine), pedar/mādar (father/mother) and barādar/khāhar (brother/sister) for the primary axis, with amu/amme (uncle/aunt), shāh/malake (king/queen), āqā/khānom (Mr/Ms), pedarbozorg/mādarbozorg (grandfather/grandmother) and dāmād/arus (groom/bride) added for the extended axis. Both axes order the male above the female member in 10 of 11 held-out gendered pairs (for example mardān/zanān 'men/women', pirmard/pirzan 'old man/old woman', shāhzāde/shāhdokht 'prince/princess'). The procedure otherwise follows the Dutch replication: word frequency is the rank of the word in the fastText vocabulary (the norms carry no frequency counts or rater standard deviations), length is the number of characters, and words containing a gender-marking stem (mard, zan, pesar, dokhtar, pedar, mādar and the like) are the lexically gendered terms for the exclusion check.

The words at the two extremes again show the bias in a direct form, with a different emphasis from Chinese. The feminine extreme of the primary axis consists of words for women (zan 'woman', bānu 'lady', khānom 'Ms', malake 'queen') and, prominently, of words for the female body, sexuality and maternity (pestān 'breast', seksi 'sexy', lokht 'naked', hāmele 'pregnant', zanāne 'feminine'); the masculine extreme consists of kinship terms (barādar, pedar, pesar) followed by titles of military, religious and social rank and by male given names (sardār 'commander', khān, jenāb 'his excellency', mollā, hāji, seyyed, āqā 'sir', Mohammad, Ali). This is the Persian counterpart of the asymmetries seen in Estonian (men as role nouns) and Chinese (men as the vocabulary of reasoning and command, women as women), and it speaks directly to the objectification argument of the Discussion: in each language the feminine pole is anchored in concrete, embodied referents and the masculine pole in roles, rank and abstraction.

In sum, the male abstraction bias replicates in Persian, a fourth language from a third family and a third script, with human concreteness judgements collected by a different method (best-worst scaling) and with the most stable seed-pair behaviour of the four languages. With Dutch, Estonian and Chinese, the text-based result now holds in four languages, three of them without grammatical gender, and in every case the sign is the same whenever the gender axis is built from several unambiguous kinship and role pairs. 7 Text-based results for words and phrases analysed separately

The main text reports the relationship between the gender association of a term and its human-rated concreteness while pooling single words 21 and multiword phrases 63 (Fig. 1a). The two sets of norms were collected with the same instructions and rating scale, but they differ in ways that could matter for our analyses: phrases are predominantly nominal and they are less polysemous than single words. Here we confirm that the male abstraction bias does not depend on pooling the two.

For each language model, we computed the two-tailed Pearson's correlation between the gender association of a term (positive values indicate masculine associations) and its concreteness, separately for (i) words and phrases pooled, (ii) words only and (iii) phrases only, with 95% confidence intervals obtained using the Fisher transformation. Within each of these analyses, P values were Bonferronicorrected for the number of language models examined. To summarize across models, we treated language models as the units of analysis: we report the unweighted mean of the per-model correlations, with a 95% confidence interval from the t distribution across models, and a two-tailed one-sample t-test of the per-model correlations against zero. No model is weighted more heavily than any other.

Supplementary Table 11 shows that the results replicate in each dataset. For words, the mean correlation across models was -0.085 (95% CI = -0.131 to -0.039; t(11) = -4.10; P = 0.002), and the correlation was negative in 11 of 12 models (significantly so after Bonferroni correction in 11). Terms in the most feminine decile had a mean concreteness of 3.31, compared with 3.02 for terms in the most masculine decile. For phrases, the mean correlation was -0.252 (95% CI = -0.295 to -0.210; t(11) = -13.10; P = 4.7 × 10 -8 ), and the correlation was negative in 12 of 12 models (significantly so in 12); mean concreteness was 3.55 in the most feminine decile and 2.74 in the most masculine decile. The one exception among single words was ConceptNet, for which the correlation was small and positive (r = 0.04; 95% CI = 0.03 to 0.05; n = 38,341 words). The bias is thus present in both datasets but is markedly stronger for phrases than for single words. Supplementary Section 9 shows that roughly half of this difference is accounted for by the frequency, length and part of speech of the phrases, and that the association in both datasets is robust to these and other lexical covariates.

Here we show that the central within-domain result-that the most abstract third of a domain is more male-associated than its most concrete third-does not depend on this choice, or more generally on how individual words are assigned to domains.

Design.

All comparisons hold the vocabulary and the gender scores fixed and vary only domain membership, so that any difference between schemes isolates the contribution of the assignment rule. The vocabulary comprises the 25,597 single-word entries of the concreteness norms 21 whose dominant part of speech is a noun, verb, adjective or adverb and that have at least one WordNet 82 sense in that part of speech (two-word entries carry no dominant part of speech and are excluded; WordNet's participial adjective file adj.ppl is folded into adj.all). Mirroring the main text's exclusion of person-denoting nouns, the noun.person domain is excluded; the main text's remaining lexical filters (function words and lexically gendered terms) are not reapplied here, because the manipulation targets the mapping alone and the robustness of the aggregate association to those filters is established in Supplementary Section 9. Within each of the 12 language models of Fig. 2, gender associations are z-scored over this fixed vocabulary. We then recompute the Fig. 2 quantity under each assignment scheme: within every domain containing at least 100 assigned words, words are split into terciles by their concreteness rating; the domain's gap is the mean gender association of the most abstract third minus that of the most concrete third, averaged with equal weight across the 12 models; each domain's gap is tested with a two-tailed one-sample t-test across models (df = 11), and the share of domains with a positive gap is tested with a two-tailed binomial sign test. Four assignment schemes are compared. (i) Primary sense, the main-text rule: each word receives the single domain of its first-listed WordNet sense within its dominant part of speech. (ii) All senses, the rule of Supplementary Section 10: a word joins every domain in which it has a sense within its dominant part of speech, so a polysemous word can contribute to several domains. (iii) Domain-unambiguous words only: the analysis is restricted to the 16,553 words whose senses all fall in a single domain, for which assignment is beyond dispute and the primary/all-sense distinction vanishes. (iv) Random sense: each word receives the domain of one uniformly sampled sense within its dominant part of speech, and the entire analysis is repeated over 200 independent replicates, characterizing the full distribution of results attainable by perturbing the assignment of every polysemous word at once. without faces are not distributed evenly: more concrete terms, which more often depict objects than people, were less likely to return any faces (r = -0.25 between concreteness and whether a term returned at least one face; 95% CI = -0.25 to -0.24; P < 2.2 × 10 -16 ; n = 39,956; two-tailed Pearson's correlation).
