Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Cannabis use is common and increases appetite, which may lead to large eating episodes that overlap with binge eating pathology. However, whether large eating episodes are related to health outcomes among individuals who use cannabis is not known. To address this gap, we analyzed seven cycles of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES; 2005 to 2018) including 18,709 adults aged 20 to 59 years (2,578 current, 7,394 past, and 8,737 never cannabis users). A large eating episode was defined as an eating occasion of at least 1,500 kcal on either of two 24-hour dietary recalls. Among current cannabis users, survey-weighted logistic regression with Benjamini-Hochberg correction estimated adjusted associations between large eating episodes and 29 health outcomes.

The weighted prevalence of a large eating episode was 30.3% among current cannabis users, 22.2% among past users, and 19.3% among never users. Among current users, a large eating episode was associated with higher odds of fair or poor self-rated health (adjusted odds ratio [OR] = 1.75, 95% CI [1.27, 2.41], p < .001, q = .022). No other association remained significant after correction, although nominal associations were observed for household food insecurity, obesity, abdominal obesity, and elevated glycated hemoglobin. Notably, adults with both current cannabis use and a large eating episode had higher odds of fair or poor health than each of the other three groups (ORs = 1.79 to 1.85, all p < .001). In exploratory analyses, the hazard of death was higher for adults with both exposures after adjustment for age and sex (hazard ratio [HR] = 2.08, 95% CI [1.31, 3.31]), though it did not remain significantly higher after full adjustment (HR = 1.40, 95% CI [0.88, 2.21]). Findings suggest that asking about eating patterns may help clinicians identify individuals who use cannabis and may be experiencing health-related distress. Longitudinal studies measuring loss of control over eating, cannabis frequency, and product type are critical for determining whether eating patterns affect health.

Introduction

Cannabis use has risen sharply in the United States. Between 2008 and 2022, the per capita rate of past-year use increased by 120% (Caulkins, 2024), and in 2022, daily or near-daily cannabis users outnumbered daily or near-daily drinkers for the first time (Caulkins, 2024). Importantly, cannabis is also known to stimulate appetite. In a controlled study of 82 adults, vaporized cannabis increased the amount of food eaten in the first 30 minutes of access across fat, carbohydrate, and protein (Hume et al., 2026). The same study showed in rats that this effect depended on cannabinoid receptors in the brain and was linked to a higher reward value of food (Hume et al., 2026). Further, another human laboratory study found that cannabis blunted the rise in insulin after a meal and lowered levels of glucagon-like peptide-1 (Farokhnia et al., 2020), which may upregulate appetite.

However, population studies show a more complicated picture. Consistent with the above, in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from 2005 to 2016, adults who currently used cannabis had lower diet quality than past or never users (Gelfand & Tangney, 2021). Specifically, the Healthy Eating Index was 51.8 in never users compared with 56.2 in past users and 57.7 in current users. Unexpectedly, however, body weight did not follow the pattern that appetite effects would predict. In a 30-year cohort, daily cannabis users had a lower mean body mass index than adults without current use (26.6 compared with 27.7 kg/m², p < .001), and cumulative use and stopping use were not associated with body mass index; the authors attributed the current-use difference to residual confounding (Jakob et al., 2024). Similarly, a meta-analysis of randomized trials found a small reduction in body weight with cannabis products (Reis et al., 2024), though the mean difference was only -1.87 kg (95% CI [-3.71, -0.03]), equating to roughly 4 pounds.

Another meta-analysis of observational studies found lower odds of type 2 diabetes among individuals exposed to cannabis (Mousavi et al., 2023), where the odds ratio was 0.48 (95% CI [0.39, 0.59]). In summarizing this literature, a recent review concluded that most studies find no link between cannabis use and weight gain (Goodpaster, 2025). As such, appetite stimulation by cannabis does not appear to translate into higher average weight or poorer metabolic health. However, how specific eating patterns relate to health among individuals who use cannabis has received relatively little study. This gap is important because average intake can obscure meaningful differences in how individuals eat. One such difference is the presence of binge eating episodes, which involve eating a large amount of food accompanied by a sense of loss of control over one's eating. Binge eating is the defining feature of binge eating disorder and is also a diagnostic criterion of bulimia nervosa, which additionally requires recurrent compensatory behaviors (e.g., purging). In a national interview study of US adults, the lifetime prevalences of binge eating disorder and bulimia nervosa were 2.8% and 1.0%, respectively (Hudson et al., 2007). Both disorders frequently co-occur with other psychiatric disorders (Hudson et al., 2007). Binge eating is also linked to physical health: in a national sample of 36,309 US adults, it was associated with chronic physical conditions as well as psychiatric disorders (Udo & Grilo, 2019). Additionally, reviews describe binge eating disorder as common among adults with type 2 diabetes and suggest that it may worsen glycemic control (Abbott et al., 2018; Harris et al., 2021).

Cannabis use and binge eating are linked. Among 165 adults seeking treatment for binge eating, more than 23% had used cannabis in the past three months (Wilkinson et al., 2024). Those who used cannabis drank more alcohol but did not have more severe eating disorder or depressive symptoms (Wilkinson et al., 2024). In a cohort of 1,568 young adults, past-year cannabis use was associated with binge eating among women but not among men after adjustment for depressive symptoms and other factors (Elran-Barak et al., 2025).

Further, a recent review concluded that cannabis use alone is not consistently related to binge eating, whereas cannabis use disorder is associated with loss of control over eating (Goodpaster, 2025). Substance use disorders are also common among individuals with eating disorders (Bahji et al., 2019; Qeadan et al., 2023), and individuals who use cannabis may hold specific expectancies about its effects on eating disorder symptoms (Scharmer et al., 2020). Among 137 cannabis users with eating disorder symptoms, participants expected cannabis to reduce restrictive eating, compensatory behaviors, and shape and weight concerns but to increase binge eating (Scharmer et al., 2020). These behavioral links have a plausible biological basis, wherein cannabis acts on cannabinoid type 1 (CB1) receptors that are widely expressed in brain regions involved in reward and motivation. Activation of these receptors raises the reward value of food and the motivation to eat (Bourdy & Befort, 2023).

The appetite effects seen in laboratory studies depend on CB1 receptors in the brain (Hume et al., 2026). Thus, individuals who are prone to losing control over eating may be more likely to overeat when cannabis heightens food reward.

Most research on cannabis and eating has focused on binge eating, which requires a subjective sense of loss of control. However, large national health surveys such as NHANES do not assess loss of control; they do record what individuals eat, allowing for identification of objectively large eating episodes from dietary recalls. Every binge eating episode contains a large amount of food by definition (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Thus, a large eating episode is a necessary component of binge eating that can be measured objectively.

Studying large eating episodes has three advantages. First, it allows investigators to examine adults in the general population instead of only individuals who seek treatment for loss-of-control eating. Second, it avoids reliance on an individual's own judgment about whether an episode was out of control. Third, large eating episodes without loss of control may themselves carry risk. For example, among adolescents and young adults, overeating without loss of control predicted the later onset of cannabis and other drug use (Sonneville et al., 2013). However, loss of control, more than episode size, is considered the core feature of binge eating (Goldschmidt & Accurso, 2016; Sonneville et al., 2013), and a large eating episode without assessment of loss of control should be considered a broader and less specific marker than binge eating. As such, associations observed for large eating episodes would reflect the combined contribution of binge eating and overeating without loss of control, and should not be attributed to diagnostic binge eating alone.

Large eating episodes may relate to health through several pathways. The first pathway runs through appetite and diet; specifically, cannabis use is linked to greater food reward and motivation to eat (Bourdy & Befort, 2023; Hume et al., 2026), which may then drive larger eating episodes. Given that individuals who use cannabis report lower diet quality (Gelfand & Tangney, 2021), these larger eating episodes may then translate into poorer cardiometabolic health (Harris et al., 2021; Udo & Grilo, 2019).

The second pathway runs through loss of control and psychological distress.

Cannabis use has been linked to weaker inhibitory control during intoxication (Morie & Potenza, 2021), which may then lead to loss of control over eating and elevated caloric intake. Binge eating, in turn, predicts elevated psychological distress and eating disorder psychopathology (Goldschmidt & Accurso, 2016; Hudson et al., 2007), which may drive poorer overall health outcomes.

A third pathway runs through food insecurity. Cannabis use has been linked to elevated incidence of food insecurity; for example, among young adults, past-month cannabis use predicted 62% higher odds of reporting food insecurity at the next survey wave (Chakraborty et al., 2025). Importantly, the reverse direction is also plausible, as food insecurity was associated with cannabis use and cannabis-related spending in the same study (Chakraborty et al., 2025). Further, food insecurity has been linked to large eating episodes through an externally enforced "binge-restrict" cycle (Abene et al., 2023; Hazzard et al., 2020). Finally, elevated food insecurity is a well-known correlate of poorer physical and mental health, including elevated risk for chronic disease, depression, and anxiety (Arenas et al., 2026).

Co-occurring use of other substances may also explain these relationships. For example, individuals with binge eating who also used cannabis drank more alcohol (Wilkinson et al., 2024). Additionally, in another recent multi-state survey, nearly half of adults who smoked cigarettes also used cannabis (Xue et al., 2026). Overeating with or without loss of control predicts the later onset of cannabis and other drug use (Sonneville et al., 2013), and substance use itself is highly linked to poorer health and higher mortality. For example, current cigarette smoking is associated with more than twice the all-cause mortality rate of never smoking (Thomson et al., 2022). Another explanation could produce the same associations without any of these pathways: shared social and economic disadvantage could raise the likelihood of both exposures and of poorer health.

Given that these pathways span physical, psychological, and social domains, outcomes that integrate several domains are especially informative. Self-rated health is one such outcome, as it is a single question that reflects overall physical and mental well-being and predicts mortality in most community studies of older adults (Dramé et al., 2023). As such, self-rated health may register the combined effect of these pathways before disease can be detected by single clinical tests.

Two features suggest that the combination of cannabis use and large eating episodes may be worse than either exposure alone. First, the pathways described above converge, wherein cannabis may increase the size and frequency of large eating episodes by raising food reward (Hume et al., 2026) and weakening inhibitory control (Morie & Potenza, 2021). Accordingly, adults who have both exposures may have episodes that are larger, more frequent, and more distressing than the episodes of adults who do not use cannabis. Second, adults who have both exposures carry the added risks of each, including lower diet quality (Gelfand & Tangney, 2021), co-occurring alcohol and cigarette use (Wilkinson et al., 2024; Xue et al., 2026), and food insecurity (Abene et al., 2023; Chakraborty et al., 2025). Thus, the two exposures may act jointly to compound risk.

To our knowledge, no study to date has examined whether large eating episodes are related to health outcomes among adults who use cannabis. Thus, the current study used nationally representative data from NHANES to address four aims. First, we estimated how common large eating episodes were by cannabis-use status. Second, we tested whether the presence of large eating episodes was associated with any of 29 health outcomes among current cannabis users. Third, we examined whether poorer health was concentrated among adults who had both current cannabis use and at least one large eating episode. Fourth, in an exploratory analysis, we examined relationships between cannabis use, large eating episodes, and mortality. We hypothesized that large eating episodes would be associated with poorer health among current cannabis users and that poorer health would be most evident among adults who had both exposures.

Method

Measures

Dietary intake and large eating episodes. NHANES collected two 24-hour dietary recalls with the USDA Automated Multiple-Pass Method, which has been evaluated against measured energy expenditure (Moshfegh et al., 2008). For each reported food record, NHANES reported a clock time and a calorie value from the USDA food-composition database (NCHS, n.d.-s, n.d.-t). We combined the foods reported at the same clock time and summed their calories. An occasion containing at least 1,500 kcal on either day was classified as a large eating episode. This procedure was based on a prior clinical study, which used this threshold to reduce misclassification between subjective and objective binge episodes (Keel et al., 2007). In NHANES, however, the measure identifies a large occasion rather than binge eating because loss of control was not assessed. We recorded whether a large eating episode occurred on each recall day and counted the number of days with one, giving a value of zero, one or two. We also repeated the analysis at 1,000-kcal and 2,000kcal thresholds.

Cannabis use. The NHANES Drug Use questionnaire assessed lifetime use, past 30day use and time since last use (NCHS, n.d.-v). Never users reported no lifetime use.

Current users reported lifetime use and use on 1 to 30 of the previous 30 days. Past users reported lifetime use without a valid report of current use, and last use more than 30 days, at least five weeks, at least two months, or at least one year earlier, depending on the response unit. Past use was not assigned when the past 30-day response was coded as refused or unknown. Responses that did not meet these classification rules, including conflicting lifetime and recent-use reports, were treated as missing. This classification measures cannabis-use recency rather than cannabis use disorder.

Statistical Analysis

Survey design and primary analysis. The analyses used R 4.5.1 (R Core Team, 2025) with the survey 4.5 (Lumley, 2024) and survival 3.8-3 (Therneau, 2024) packages.

Models accounted for the NHANES strata, primary sampling units, and sampling weights.

Two-day dietary weights were divided by the number of cycles contributing to each outcome, or by seven when all cycles contributed. Analyses of fasting glucose, insulin resistance, and triglycerides used the corresponding fasting subsample weight divided by seven instead (Chen et al., 2020; NCHS, n.d.-aj). We used Taylor linearization to calculate uncertainty while accounting for the survey design, with an adjustment when a survey stratum contained only one sampled area.

Some measurements were only collected for certain participants or survey years. For example, some laboratory outcomes were available only for specific ages or NHANES cycles. Therefore, each analysis included only participants with complete data for the relevant outcome and adjustment variables and excluded participants with missing data (NCHS, n.d.-u).

We estimated weighted prevalence and 95% confidence intervals for large eating episodes by cannabis-use recency. Among current cannabis users, we fitted 29 separate survey-weighted logistic regression models, one for each health indicator. Each model examined whether having a large eating episode was associated with the health outcome.

Models used a quasibinomial distribution and logit link. Each model adjusted for age, age squared, sex, race and ethnicity, education, income, survey cycle, cigarette smoking and alcohol frequency. Model results were reported as adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. All tests were two-sided, with p < .05 indicating significance before correction for multiple comparisons. Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied across the 29 primary tests, with q < .05 indicating significance after correction (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995).

Models were flagged as sparse when fewer than five participants with or without the outcome were available per fitted nonintercept coefficient.

Secondary and sensitivity analyses. We repeated the primary logistic regression models using the number of recall days with a large eating episode, first as a count from zero to two and then as separate categories. To examine whether the associations differed between never, past and current cannabis users, interaction models included large eating episode status, cannabis-use recency and their interaction term. A separate analysis classified participants into four groups based on current cannabis use and large eating episode status. These groups comprised neither current cannabis use nor a large eating episode, a large eating episode only, current cannabis use only, and both current cannabis use and a large eating episode. We compared participants in the group with both with each of the other three groups. The sensitivity analyses used calorie cutoffs of 1,000 and 2,000 kcal, assessed episodes using only the first recall, required episodes on both recall days, added average daily energy intake, or removed cigarette smoking and alcohol from the models. All other adjustment variables remained unchanged. We corrected for multiple testing separately within each set of related analyses across the 29 health outcomes.

Analyses with two comparisons per outcome included 58 tests, while those with three comparisons included 87 tests. The overall tests of whether associations differed across cannabis-use groups were corrected across 29 outcomes. The four-group analysis examined whether fair or poor health was concentrated among participants who had both current cannabis use and a large eating episode. The comparison groups comprised participants with neither current cannabis use nor a large eating episode, current cannabis use alone, or a large eating episode alone.

Participants with both current cannabis use and a large eating episode had higher adjusted odds of fair or poor health than participants with neither current cannabis use nor a large eating episode (OR = 1.85, 95% CI [1.39, 2.44]), current cannabis use alone (OR = 1.79, 95% CI [1.30, 2.45]), and a large eating episode alone (OR = 1.81, 95% CI [1. 32, 2.48]). Mortality

The mortality sample included 17,201 participants, of whom 522 died during follow- up (3.0%).

After adjustment for age, age squared and sex, participants with both current cannabis use and a large eating episode had 2.08 times the hazard of death compared with participants with neither current cannabis use nor a large eating episode (HR = 2.08, 95% CI [1.31, 3.31]). The estimate decreased after full adjustment and was no longer statistically significant (HR = 1.40, 95% CI [0.88, 2.21]). In the fully adjusted model, the mortality hazard for participants with both current cannabis use and a large eating episode did not clearly differ from current cannabis users without a large eating episode (HR = 1.36, 95% CI [0.74, 2.50]). It was higher than the estimate for participants who were not current cannabis users but reported a large eating episode (HR = 1.96, 95% CI [1.08, 3.55]). This exploratory contrast was not corrected for multiple testing. Fully adjusted analyses restricted to current users were not reported because there were too few deaths for the number of fitted coefficients. Restricting follow-up to the period beyond month 24 among participants still observed at that point produced no clear difference between participants with both current cannabis use and a large eating episode and those with neither (HR = 1.21, 95% CI [0.64, 2.27]). Removing cigarette smoking from the model changed the estimate, indicating sensitivity to the adjustment set.

Discussion

In this nationally representative sample of US adults, large eating episodes were more common among current cannabis users than among past or never users. Among current users, reporting a large eating episode was associated with poorer self-rated health, which was the only one of 29 associations that remained significant after correction for multiple testing. Further, poorer health was most evident among adults who had both current cannabis use and a large eating episode. The excess risk of death was not significant after full adjustment. Key findings are discussed below.

Prevalence of Large Eating Episodes

Large eating episodes became steadily more common from never users to past users to current users. This gradient is consistent with laboratory evidence that cannabis increases both food intake and food reward (Farokhnia et al., 2020; Hume et al., 2026), and it also fits with clinical and survey evidence that links cannabis use to binge eating (Elran-Barak et al., 2025; Goodpaster, 2025; Wilkinson et al., 2024). Past cannabis users fell between current and never users, a pattern that is consistent with an association tied to recent use. Importantly, these results indicate that the link between cannabis use and large eating episodes extends beyond treatment-seeking and young adult samples to the general population of working-age adults. As such, individuals who use cannabis and eat in this way will often be seen in primary care, nutrition, and substance use settings rather than in eating disorder clinics, and clinicians in those settings are well placed to ask about eating patterns in plain behavioral terms (e.g., whether a person ever eats an unusually large amount at one sitting).

Large Eating Episodes and Self-Rated Health

Fair or poor self-rated health showed the clearest association with co-occurring cannabis use and large eating episodes. Specifically, its prevalence among current cannabis users was 23.4% with a large eating episode and 17.8% without one. Self-rated health summarizes physical, mental, and functional aspects of health; thus, poorer self-rated health may reflect more general health risks associated with co-occurring cannabis use and large eating episodes that single physical measures do not capture. This association was not explained by the amount of energy intake and in fact became stronger after adjustment for average daily energy intake, suggesting that the pattern of eating carries information beyond total intake. Lower diet quality among current users (Gelfand & Tangney, 2021) adds to this picture. For clinicians, this means that dietary counseling for adults who use cannabis should address how much an individual eats at one sitting and not only total calories or food choices, and a brief question about unusually large eating occasions could be added to routine intake and substance use screening. Ecological momentary assessment (EMA) can record cannabis use, hunger, mood, and loss of control as each eating occasion occurs, and future studies should use EMA alongside dietary recalls to determine whether episodes follow cannabis use and whether they involve loss of control.

The four-group analysis showed that adults with both current cannabis use and a large eating episode had higher odds of fair or poor health than adults with neither exposure, adults with cannabis use alone, and adults with a large eating episode alone, whereas the other three groups had similar odds of fair or poor health. Thus, only the combination of the two exposures stood apart. This means that neither cannabis use nor a large eating episode alone identified poorer health in this sample. The combination may mark a subgroup that is more vulnerable to the pathways described above (i.e., appetite and diet, loss of control and psychological distress, and food insecurity), and may also mark episodes that are driven by the appetite-inducing effects of cannabis. While the formal test of whether the association differed across never, past, and current users did not remain significant after correction, the overall pattern is best interpreted as a description of who reports poorer health. These findings do not show that cannabis use changes the effect of a large eating episode on health; rather, they identify a subset of patients who may warrant closer attention and targeted intervention. Adults who report both cannabis use and large eating episodes could be prioritized for screening for distress, food insecurity, and disordered eating, and future trials could test whether brief interventions targeting cannabis use and eating behaviors improve health (e.g., helping adults plan regular meals and food access around cannabis use).

Notably, these associations depended on the calorie threshold chosen and were not significant at 1,000 or 2,000 kcal cut-offs. A threshold of 1,000 kcal includes many ordinary large meals and likely mixes typical eating with unusual eating, thereby diluting any association. Conversely, a threshold of 2,000 kcal includes very few eating occasions, meaning that this analysis had little power to detect an association at that level. The 1,500 kcal threshold falls between these and was drawn from a clinical study (Keel et al., 2007). It appears to separate unusual eating occasions from ordinary ones while retaining enough power for detecting meaningful patterns.

Additionally, one large episode day was associated with fair or poor health, while two large episode days were not. This pattern of findings should not be interpreted as evidence that recurrent episodes are harmless; rather, it suggests that a single large eating occasion is already informative. For clinicians and patients, this means that an eating pattern does not need to be frequent or severe enough to meet criteria for an eating disorder to be associated with poorer health. For researchers and surveillance systems, it means that adding a calorie threshold or a question about unusually large eating occasions to health surveys would allow the pattern to be monitored.

Other Health Outcomes and Possible Pathways

Associations with the other health outcomes were smaller. Household food insecurity, obesity, abdominal obesity, and elevated glycated hemoglobin were each more common among cannabis users with a large eating episode before correction for multiple testing, and all four were in the direction of poorer health. However, none remained significant after correction, and these findings should thus be treated as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. These findings are consistent with reports that binge eating is related to chronic physical conditions and to impaired glycemic control (Harris et al., 2021; Udo & Grilo, 2019). The food insecurity finding is also consistent with reports that food insecurity is linked to both binge eating and cannabis use (Abene et al., 2023; Chakraborty et al., 2025; Hazzard et al., 2020). Interestingly, depressive symptoms were not clearly higher among adults with a large eating episode, which argues against a purely affective link between cooccurring cannabis use and binge eating and poor health outcomes, although it does not exclude other forms of distress that were not measured (e.g., shame and anxiety).

Most laboratory markers showed no clear association with a large eating episode, including fasting glucose, insulin resistance, triglycerides, HDL cholesterol, measured blood pressure, and C-reactive protein. Earlier work found lower body mass index, less diabetes, and lower insulin resistance among cannabis users than among non-users (Jakob et al., 2024; Mousavi et al., 2023; Reis et al., 2024). Those studies compared users with nonusers, whereas we compared users with and without a large eating episode. Large eating episodes among cannabis users were not accompanied by clear laboratory evidence of cardiometabolic disease; rather, the difference was seen in how adults rated their own health. Two interpretations fit this pattern. First, self-rated health may register early or functional effects before laboratory values change (e.g., fatigue or discomfort after large meals). Second, the laboratory subsamples were smaller and had less power than the full sample. Thus, clinicians should not rely on laboratory results alone to judge whether an eating pattern is affecting a patient, and should ask patients how they feel and how they rate their own health.

Mortality

In exploratory mortality analyses, adults with both exposures had a twofold higher hazard of death than adults with neither after adjustment for age and sex. However, this estimate was no longer significant after adjustment for social factors, cigarette smoking, alcohol, body mass index, energy intake, and chronic disease. Conversely, the estimate was significant when only cigarette smoking was removed from the fully adjusted model.

Cigarette smoking is common among cannabis users (Xue et al., 2026) and strongly affects mortality (Thomson et al., 2022). Accordingly, much of the mortality risk appears to reflect smoking and other shared risks. Additionally, a meta-analysis found that larger cohort studies gave smaller mortality estimates for cannabis use (Alimoradi et al., 2025). In contrast, excess mortality in cannabis use disorder was seen in adults who needed hospital care (Myran et al., 2025). NHANES current users were not selected for hospital care and probably have less severe use; thus, a smaller excess risk would be expected in this sample. The mortality findings are best interpreted as inconclusive and not as evidence that the combination is safe. Given that the sample was young, deaths were few, and follow-up was short, the analysis had limited power to detect even a moderate risk.

Clinical and Public Health Implications and Future Directions

These findings have important clinical implications. Daily cannabis use is now common (Caulkins, 2024), and a growing share of adults is thus exposed to the appetite effects of cannabis (Hume et al., 2026). Clinicians who treat binge eating are already advised to screen for cannabis and alcohol use (Wilkinson et al., 2024). Our results suggest that the reverse is also useful: providers in primary care, nutrition, and substance use settings can ask adults who use cannabis whether they ever eat unusually large amounts at one sitting and whether they worry about having enough food. A positive answer could prompt a brief assessment of loss of control over eating, mood, and diet, as well as a referral to eating disorder or food assistance services. Additionally, patients can be encouraged to plan regular meals and keep nutritious foods available when they use cannabis, given that the appetite effects of cannabis are predictable (Hume et al., 2026).

For health systems, these findings support adding brief questions about eating patterns and food insecurity to cannabis screening and treatment programs, as well as to tobacco cessation programs, given that cannabis co-use is common in those programs (Xue et al., 2026). The reverse also applies, such that eating disorder and nutrition services can add routine questions about cannabis use. Health systems can also build referral routes that connect cannabis, nutrition, food assistance, and mental health services, given that the pathways identified here cross all four.

For policy makers, the findings point to three actions. First, public health messaging and information provided at the point of sale can describe the appetitive effects of cannabis and suggest planning for them. Second, programs that improve food access can be extended to individuals who use cannabis, given that food insecurity is linked to both cannabis use and large eating episodes. Third, national surveys can add dietary recalls and loss-of-control questions so that these patterns can be monitored as the cannabis market continues to change (Caulkins, 2024), and the role of binge eating psychopathology specifically can be clarified.

Future research should directly test the proposed pathways between co-occurring cannabis use, binge eating, and poor health. Specifically, longitudinal studies that measure loss of control, cannabis frequency, potency, and product type, as well as food insecurity and distress, at several time points can test 1) whether cannabis use precedes large eating episodes with or without loss of control and 2) whether each pathway explains the association with self-rated health, while EMA can be used to capture the timing of cannabis use relative to episodes. Subsequently, trials should test whether interventions that reduce large eating episodes, reduce loss of control over eating, or improve food access improve self-rated health among adults who use cannabis.

Strengths and Limitations

The current study has several notable strengths. First, it used a large nationally representative sample that spans 14 years. Second, it examined a wide range of laboratory and questionnaire outcomes. Third, we applied survey weights and a correction for multiple testing (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995) and included several sensitivity analyses.

Findings from the current study should be interpreted in light of several limitations.

First, the health outcomes were measured at the same time as large eating episodes, and as such the direction of association cannot be determined. Second, dietary intake came from two 24-hour recalls. Although the NHANES method was designed to reduce bias in reported energy intake (Moshfegh et al., 2008), self-reported energy intake is nevertheless underreported more often at higher body mass index (Ravelli & Schoeller, 2020), and underreporting of large episodes would weaken the associations we observed. Two days also cannot capture usual behavior over longer timespans. Third, NHANES does not measure the subjective sense of loss of control that is a diagnostic requirement of binge eating. As such, many large eating occasions will not reflect binge eating pathology, and we cannot assume that our results generalize to binge-spectrum eating disorders. Fourth, cannabis use was classified by recency, and we had no information on frequency, dose, product type, route of use, or cannabis use disorder, all of which influence the effect of cannabis on appetite (Goodpaster, 2025). Fifth, several estimates rested on small sample sizes, ranging from 268 to 2,414 across outcomes. Two estimates (i.e., those for reported osteoporosis and glycated hemoglobin of at least 6.5%) were flagged as sparse because they had fewer than five outcome events or nonevents per fitted coefficient; these estimates are imprecise and should be interpreted with extreme caution. Sixth, unmeasured factors (e.g., physical activity or use of other drugs) may have contributed to the associations and could represent unmeasured confounders. Seventh, correction for multiple testing across 29 outcomes controls the false discovery rate, which lowers the chance of false positive findings but raises the chance of missing true modest associations. Accordingly, the four outcomes that were significant only before correction may reflect real associations or chance and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Notably, the absence of significance for other outcomes should not be read as evidence of no association. Finally, the participants were aged 20 to 59 years and data collection ended in 2018, and thus the results may not apply to adolescents or older adults. Daily cannabis use has increased since 2018 (Caulkins, 2024), and the range of cannabis products and their legal status have also changed. If these changes strengthen the appetite effects of cannabis, the associations in current users could now be stronger than those we observed. Thus, our estimates are best viewed as a baseline for the period before 2018 that can be updated as newer NHANES cycles become available.

Conclusion

In summary, large eating episodes were more common among current cannabis users and were associated with poorer self-rated health in this group. Household food insecurity, obesity, abdominal obesity, and elevated glycated hemoglobin showed associations in the same direction that did not remain significant after correction for multiple testing. Taken together, these findings indicate that specific eating patterns can identify a subgroup of adults who use cannabis and report poorer health. Longitudinal studies that measure loss of control, cannabis frequency, and product type are critical next steps.
