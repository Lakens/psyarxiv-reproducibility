Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Meta-reasoning, the ability to monitor progress and regulate effort during reasoning, comprises monitoring and control. Monitoring, including confidence, is assumed to guide control processes. Yet, whether monitoring and control compete with reasoning for shared cognitive resources is unclear. To address this, we examined the role of working memory in reasoning and meta-reasoning, drawing on Fuzzy Trace Theory and the meta-reasoning framework. Participants (N = 998) solved syllogisms with and without validity-believability conflict in a two-response paradigm under six constraint conditions. Accuracy declined only when load and deadline were combined. Confidence was lower on conflict than on nonconflict problems and reliably predicted answer changes and rethinking time across all conditions. Monitoring resolution, however, was low throughout. These results suggest cognitive constraints impair reasoning generally but largely spare conflict-detection and control sensitivity. Reasoners' limited ability to discriminate correct from incorrect responses appears to reflect a general monitoring limitations rather than constrained resources.

Examining the Role of Working Memory in Reasoning and Meta-reasoning: A Fuzzytrace Approach

A central question in contemporary theories of reasoning concerns the mechanisms that determine when additional or alternative analysis of a problem is needed. Within the metacognition literature, these functions are typically divided into monitoring processes, which assess the current state of cognition, and control processes, which regulate subsequent cognitive activity (Ackerman & Thompson, 2017). Under the assumption that monitoring guides control causally (Metcalfe & Finn, 2008), inaccurate monitoring can lead to suboptimal control decisions. For example, confidence, one of the most widely studied monitoring judgments, signals whether current understanding is sufficient (Prowse Turner & Thompson, 2009). High confidence generally indicates that further analysis is unnecessary, whereas low confidence signals that additional thought or corrective action may be warranted. Importantly, confidence is not always closely aligned with objective accuracy.

Rather than directly reflecting correctness, confidence judgments often rely on heuristic cues, such as the ease or fluency with which an answer comes to mind (Prowse Turner & Thompson, 2009; Thompson et al., 2011). When these cues are well calibrated, monitoring judgments accurately discriminate between correct and incorrect responses, resulting in good monitoring resolution (Koriat, 2008b) and facilitating the effective allocation of cognitive effort. When resolution is poor, however, control behaviours may be misdirected, such that individuals may prematurely terminate analysis of an incorrect response because they are overly confident or continue expending effort on a response that is already correct because they lack confidence in it. The extent to which such monitoring judgments influence subsequent cognitive behaviour is referred to as control sensitivity (Koriat & Goldsmith, 1996).

Working memory (WM) has long been implicated in reasoning performance, with WM resources thought to support the storage, active maintenance, and manipulation of problem information required to reach a solution (Baddeley, 1992; Kyllonen & Christal, 1990). Yet whether monitoring processes themselves draw on the same limited cognitive resources or can be carried out at negligible cost is an open question. The present study investigates the role of WM in both reasoning performance and three key aspects of metareasoning: cue utilization, monitoring resolution, and control sensitivity. We approach this question from an integrated theoretical framework that draws on fuzzy-trace theory (FTT; Reyna et al., 2017), dual-process theory (DPT; De Neys, 2023; Evans & Stanovich, 2013), and models of meta-reasoning (Ackerman & Thompson, 2017). By varying the intensity of different WM loads, we aim to clarify how cognitive resources shape both reasoning outcomes as well as the metacognitive regulation of reasoning.

Dual Process Theories of Reasoning

Traditional approaches to reasoning have commonly evaluated reasoning quality against normative standards drawn from formal logic and mathematical principles (Elqayam & Evans, 2011; Evans, 2002). However, influential work on heuristics and biases has shown that people readily violate elementary logical or probabilistic rules when a task cues an intuitive response (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). Default-interventionist (DI) dual-process theories were proposed to explain such biases, including belief bias, the tendency to judge arguments by the believability of their conclusions rather than their logical validity (Evans et al., 1983). These theories propose that, because people tend to minimize demanding computation, slow, analytic processing of logico-mathematical rules is often bypassed when intuitive processing has already cued a response (Evans & Stanovich, 2013). Accordingly, intuitive or System 1 processes are fast, automatic, and require minimal cognitive effort, whereas analytic or System 2 processes are more time-and effort-demanding and therefore more reliant on WM. On this view, errors often arise when the reasoner defaults to the intuitive response and either fails to detect the need for correction or lacks the WM capacity to carry out the analytic processing once the need is detected. This correspondence is not absolute, however: initial intuitive responses can themselves be normatively correct (Bago & De Neys, 2017), and engaging analytic processing does not guarantee a correct outcome (Evans & Stanovich, 2013; Newman et al., 2017). Consistent with the DI view, studies have shown that constraining resources by requiring people to respond quickly or to perform a concurrent WM task increases reasoning biases (De Neys, 2006; Evans & Curtis-Holmes, 2005). Although researchers have looked at the dispositions that make one more likely to reflect on their intuitive answer and recruit further analytic processing (i.e. Need for Cognition, Cacioppo & Petty, 1982; Actively Open-Minded Thinking; (Stanovich & Toplak, 2023), DI does not specify in detail what determines whether and when a reasoner switches from one mode of processing to another, reflects on and revises an initial answer, or terminates thinking.

Fuzzy-Trace Theory

Fuzzy-trace theory (FTT) offers a parallel-processing account of reasoning that differs from DI theories in both its architecture and its view of gist-based intuition, over analytic, rule-based processing as the basis of advanced reasoning (Reyna et al., 2017). According to FTT, when people encode an experience, they extract two kinds of memory representations in parallel: verbatim traces, which preserve the precise, surface-level details, and gist traces, which capture the bottom-line, essential meaning of information. Because these representations are encoded and stored independently, reasoning often does not depend on memory for verbatim details. In this respect, FTT departs sharply from DI theories in which intuition is the main source of error. Indeed, even when reasoners have access to verbatim representations and can perform the relevant computations, they prefer to reason with the simplest gist that allows them to complete the task, a tendency known as the fuzzy-processing preference, which increases with development and expertise.

FTT identifies several types of interference that lead to reasoning errors, including, but not limited to, representational, retrieval, and processing interference (Brainerd & Reyna, 1990 , 2002; Reyna & Brainerd, 1995; Reyna & Mills, 2007). Representational interference arises from competition between alternative representations of a problem. Consider Piaget's famous class-inclusion problem, in which children are shown five cows and two horses and asked, "Are there more cows or more animals?". Here, the salient but inappropriate gist of the problem as a comparison between two subclasses (cows vs. horses) overrides the appropriate gist of a comparison between a superordinate class and one of its subclasses (animals vs. cows), leading children to answer that there are more cows, a case known as competing gist interference. Beyond forming an appropriate representation of the task information, many reasoning tasks also require retrieving the appropriate reasoning principle to apply to that representation. Retrieval interference occurs when contextual cues misdirect this retrieval. In this problem, the word more cues retrieval of a relative-numerosity principle (which class has a greater number of members?) rather than the cardinal ordering principle (a superordinate class necessarily includes, and therefore cannot be smaller than, any of its subclasses). Finally, even when the appropriate representation and reasoning principles are retrieved, accurate reasoning requires applying the principle coherently to the representation. In class inclusion, this is difficult because the classes overlap: the cows belong both to the subclass and to the superordinate class, so the reasoner must keep track of the same items as members of two classes at once. FTT posits that failure to track these overlapping relations produces processing interference, and we will argue that it is at this stage that demands on WM are likely to be greatest. Reasoning errors can therefore arise at any step of representation, retrieval, or processing, either because the reasoner fails to detect interference or because the interference is detected but cannot be successfully resolved or inhibited.

These assumptions about gist-based reasoning have implications for the role of WM in reasoning. Because gist-based processing operates on coarse-grained rather than precise representations, FTT predicts that reasoning should place minimal demands on WM and processing time, such that providing more of either should not necessarily improve performance (Reyna et al., 2017). On the one hand, reasoning accuracy is often independent of verbatim recall of problem information, as people use verbatim memories to answer memory questions but gist memories to solve reasoning problems. On the other hand, FTT's account of processing interference suggests that at least some functions of WM, namely active maintenance and manipulation of information, are implicated in reasoning performance, given that reasoning errors can sometimes arise from a failure to coherently track overlapping classes (Brainerd & Reyna, 1990 , 2002; Reyna & Brainerd, 1995; Reyna & Mills, 2007). Thus, the independence of reasoning from verbatim memory speaks mainly to the storage of problem information and may not extend to the independence of reasoning from the broad definition of WM, which includes the active maintenance and manipulation of information.

These forms of interference imply that competing representations or reasoning principles can come into conflict during reasoning. FTT therefore posits a third process, monitoring and inhibition, that operates on verbatim and gist representations (Reyna & Mills, 2007). Reasoning errors can then be attributed in part to a failure to inhibit a salient but taskinappropriate representation in favour of the appropriate one. However, the theory does not specify at length how a reasoner comes to recognize that inhibition is needed in the first place. In other words, the monitoring mechanism that detects when interference is present and triggers its resolution receives little attention in the theory itself.

The Meta-Reasoning Framework

Both default-interventionist and fuzzy-trace accounts converge on an unresolved question: what determines whether and when conflicting representations or thought processes are detected and resolved? The meta-reasoning framework addresses this question directly by providing a unified account of how reasoners monitor the progress of their thought processes and make control decisions about time and effort allocation (Ackerman & Thompson, 2017).

Based on the cue-utilization approach, monitoring judgements are formed from heuristic cues such as subjective experiences during the reasoning process (Koriat, 1997). For example, retrieval fluency, or the subjective ease with which a response comes to mind, can be one such cue for certainty. Studies have shown that answers that come to mind quickly elicit a strong feeling of rightness (FOR) and confidence, regardless of the actual accuracy of the response (Shynkaruk & Thompson, 2006; Thompson et al., 2011 Thompson et al., , 2013)). Extant research using the two-response paradigm developed by Thompson et al. (2011), in which reasoners first give a fast, intuitive response and then a final response in free time, show an important predictor of whether one attempts to override an erroneous first response by engaging in further deliberation is FOR, or confidence, in the initial response (Prowse Turner & Thompson, 2009; Thompson et al., 2011).

The meta-reasoning framework thus offers a switching mechanism that DI and FTT leave unspecified: a low FOR or low confidence signals that the initial response may be inadequate and prompts further analytic engagement, whereas a high FOR or high confidence prompts the reasoner to stop, regardless of whether the response is correct (Thompson et al., 2011). Because monitoring judgements drive these control decisions, their accuracy matters.

When confidence is poorly calibrated to accuracy or fails to discriminate correct from incorrect responses (i.e., low resolution), reasoners may prematurely stop thinking about wrong answers. This offers one explanation for why errors persist even among reasoners who have the capacity to correct them: the problem may not always lie in the inability to reason analytically but in the monitoring signal that determines whether analytic reasoning would engage at all. It is therefore important to understand the cues that inform confidence and the conditions under which monitoring is systematically miscalibrated.

Conflict problems in which an intuitively cued response conflicts with the normative response, offer a useful test case for these ideas. A classic example is the categorical syllogisms in which the logical validity and the believability of the conclusion are incongruent. Consider the following syllogism, where the conclusion is believable but logically invalid: All flowers (A) need light (B); Roses (C) need light (B); Therefore, roses (C) are flowers (A). Extending FTT's account of representational interference (Brainerd & Reyna, 1990; Reyna & Mills, 2007), we propose that this conflict can be understood as competition between two gist representations extracted from the same premises. One is a structural gist, corresponding to the logical form of the premises independent of their content: All A are B, and all C are B. Correctly represented, this gist preserves the possibility that B includes members that are not A, so the inclusion of C in B does not entail the inclusion of C in A. The other is a thematic gist, corresponding to real-world class relations retrieved from long-term memory: roses are nested within flowers, which are in turn nested within the class of things that need light. Because the thematic gist is more salient, it can override the structural gist, producing belief bias, the tendency to accept or reject conclusions based on their consistency with prior beliefs rather than their logical validity (Evans et al., 1983). In this sense, belief-bias conflict reflects representational interference that FTT identifies in class-inclusion reasoning.

Importantly, conflict itself can serve as a monitoring cue. Reasoners show sensitivity to conflict, taking longer to respond (Franssens & De Neys, 2009) and reporting lower confidence (Bago & De Neys, 2017; Thompson & Johnson, 2014) on conflict than on no-conflict problems, even when they ultimately give the erroneous, belief-based response. This suggests that conflict is often detected even when it is not successfully resolved, and that confidence is at least partly sensitive to the reduced likelihood of accuracy on conflict problems. In FTT terms, detection and resolution correspond to the monitoring and inhibition functions described above.

Working Memory and Meta-Reasoning

While the role of WM in reasoning has been studied extensively, its role in metareasoning remains unexplored. Does constraining WM resources impair both reasoning performance and reasoners' ability to detect that impairment, or can monitoring proceed relatively unimpaired even when performance itself suffers? The cue-utilization approach suggests the latter, since monitoring judgments are thought to be based on experiential cues rather than on the same resource-demanding computations that support the reasoning process itself (Koriat, 1997). Consistent with this, Franssens and De Neys (2009), building on De Neys and Glumicic (2008), found that although a concurrent WM load impaired reasoning accuracy, it did not impair participants' implicit conflict detection, as indexed by participants' recall of conflict-relevant problem information. Similarly, Bago and De Neys (2017) found that conflict detection, as indexed by confidence reduction on conflict problems, remained significant both under concurrent WM load and under a combined load and time pressure.

However, both studies have limitations. Franssens and De Neys (2009) inferred conflict detection from recall of problem features rather than from a direct measure of monitoring. Their index therefore confounds verbatim memory for problem content with metacognitive sensitivity to conflict and recall differences may reflect differences in encoding rather than in monitoring. They also did not report whether load interacted with problem type, leaving open the question of whether load reduced the size of the conflictdetection effect even if it did not eliminate it. In Bago and De Neys (2017), reasoning accuracy increased rather than decreased under WM load, a counterintuitive pattern that is difficult to reconcile with a resource-depletion account (Quayle & Ball, 2000) and makes the effect of the load manipulation difficulty to interpret. Together, these limitations suggest that the evidence for resource-independent monitoring is less conclusive than it is often presented as being (e.g., De Neys, 2012).

Importantly, a reasoner could report lower confidence on conflict than on non-conflict problems, which reflects sensitivity to conflict, yet still fail to discriminate between their correct and incorrect responses on a trial-by-trial basis, reflecting low resolution. Resolution is arguably more consequential, because it determines whether confidence accurately guides control decisions such as deliberation and answer revision. To our knowledge, however, no study has examined whether WM load affects monitoring resolution in reasoning. Evidence from the metamemory literature is mixed. On a resource-competition account, monitoring resolution and WM draw on shared cognitive resources, such that burdening one impairs the other (Bryce et al., 2023). Other studies, however, have found no such effect (Conte et al., 2023). Whether WM load affects resolution in reasoning therefore remains an open question.

Finally, even if monitoring and monitoring resolution proves robust to WM load, this does not guarantee that monitoring translates into adaptive control decisions: a reasoner could accurately detect that something is wrong yet fail to act on that signal. It is therefore equally important to consider the effect of WM load on control sensitivity, or the correspondence between monitoring judgements and subsequent control decisions such as answer revision (Koriat & Goldsmith, 1996). To our knowledge no study has directly manipulated WM load and measured its effect on control sensitivity. The closest evidence comes from the metamemory research on aging where older adults showed reduced control sensitivity relative to younger adults, and this reduction was associated with lower scores on neuropsychological measures of executive functioning (Pansky et al., 2009). While informative, this finding is correlational and age differences in control sensitivity are confounded with other cognitive and neural changes beyond WM. An experimental manipulation of WM load is needed to establish whether control sensitivity depends on WM resources.

The Current Research

The goal of the current study was to investigate the role of WM in reasoning and meta-reasoning. Participants solved categorical syllogisms in which the logical validity and the believability of the conclusion were either congruent (non-conflict problems) or incongruent (conflict problems). We used a two-response paradigm (Thompson et al., 2011): participants gave an initial response, rated their confidence in that response, and then gave a final response, which could differ from the initial one. Following previous research (De Neys, 2006; Evans & Curtis-Holmes, 2005), we constrained WM during the initial response with a response deadline, a concurrent WM load (a dot-pattern task or an auditory tone-monitoring task), or a combination of load and deadline; in one combined condition, the constraints also applied during the final response. We refer to these manipulations collectively as WM constraints. As outlined below, belief-bias syllogisms involve two distinct sources of interference identified by FTT, making them well suited to examining how interference is monitored and resolved, and what role WM plays in each.

We derived competing predictions about the effect of WM constraints on reasoning accuracy. According to DI theories, WM constraints should impair performance by preventing the analytic override of intuitive responses and should therefore selectively impair performance on conflict problems (De Neys, 2006; Evans & Curtis-Holmes, 2005). We derived a different prediction from FTT. Categorical syllogisms involve overlapping class relations, and reasoners may fail to keep track of these relations, producing processing interference (Reyna, 1990). As our WM constraint manipulations were expected to tax the same resources required to maintain and manipulate relations between classes when solving syllogisms, we reasoned that WM constraints should impair performance on all problems by exacerbating processing interference, regardless of problem type. We also expected accuracy to be lower on conflict than on non-conflict problems (De Neys, 2006; Evans et al., 1983), because conflict problems are additionally subject to representational interference, in which a salient thematic (belief-based) gist overrides the structural (logical) gist, producing belief bias. Belief-bias syllogisms thus involve both processing interference, which is common to all problems, and representational interference, which is specific to conflict problems.

Because FTT does not discuss in detail whether resolving representational interference draws on WM, we made no directional prediction about whether WM constraints would additionally impair performance on conflict problems. A WM Constraint × Problem Type interaction would instead be consistent with the DI account.

Our predictions about the effect of WM constraints on meta-reasoning are threefold.

First, we expected confidence to be sensitive to conflict between competing gist representations, such that confidence would be lower on conflict than on non-conflict problems (Bago & De Neys, 2017; De Neys et al., 2011; Thompson & Johnson, 2014). Our design provides a more rigorous test of existing findings on whether this sensitivity depends on WM (Bago & De Neys, 2017; Franssens & De Neys, 2009), because we measured monitoring directly through confidence, and tested whether WM constraints interacted with problem type. Nevertheless, based on these previous findings, we expected conflict sensitivity to be robust to WM constraints.

Second, we tested two competing accounts of monitoring resolution. If monitoring competes with reasoning for the same limited WM resources, WM constraints should reduce resolution alongside accuracy. If instead monitoring depends primarily on cues available at the point of judgement (Koriat, 1997), resolution should remain comparatively stable even as WM constraints impair accuracy.

Third, we expected control sensitivity overall, such that lower initial confidence would be associated with a higher probability of answer change and longer rethinking times (Thompson et al., 2011). Given the absence of previous work on WM and control sensitivity, we made no directional prediction about whether WM constraints would affect it; instead, the study provides a first direct test of whether control sensitivity depends on WM resources.

Finally, because confidence may integrate cues other than conflict (Koriat, 1997), we examined in exploratory analyses whether confidence predicted control decisions over and above conflict.

Method Participants

A total of 1137 participants were recruited from Prolific and randomly assigned to one of the 6 experimental conditions. Participants received 0.92 GBP upon completion of a 10-minute experiment for which they were required to be native English speakers residing in the US or Canada. To ensure data quality, participants (n = 139) were excluded if they met either of the following a-priori exclusion criteria designed to exclude people who were not engaged in the task: (a) their total task completion time fell within the bottom 5% of the sample distribution, if (b) their accuracy on easy trials (i.e. non-conflict problems) fell below chance level (50%) or (c) if they showed no variability in their confidence ratings across trials (unless their overall accuracy was 100%). Design

The study featured a 6 (WM constraint) x 2 (Problem type) mixed design with WM constraint as the between-subjects and problem type as the within-subjects factor. We used a two-response paradigm (Thompson et al., 2011), in which participants first gave a fast, intuitive response to each syllogism (Time 1) and were subsequently given unlimited time to reconsider and provide a final response (Time 2). Across the six between-subjects conditions, we varied both the type of WM constraint imposed on participants (response deadline, a concurrent WM load, or both) and its duration (restricted to the Time 1 only, versus extended across both Time 1 and Time 2), resulting in the following conditions: Control, Load (Time1), Deadline, Load (Time 1) + Deadline, Load (Time 1-2) + Deadline, Auditory load (Time 1).

Our primary dependent variables were response accuracy at both Time 1 and Time 2, confidence (0 = not confident to100 = very confident) at Time 1, and response time (RT) at Time 2. Working memory task accuracy (dot-pattern recognition or tone-monitoring accuracy, depending on condition) was also recorded to confirm that the WM task was successfully engaging working memory resources as intended.

Procedure

The experiment was administered online using Gorilla Experiment Builder (www.gorilla.sc; Anwyl-Irvine et al., 2020). Participants first received general instructions emphasizing that their first response to each problem should be the fast and intuitive answer that came to mind quickly, and that they would subsequently be given unlimited time to reconsider. Instructions for the syllogism task asked participants to assume that premises are true, and indicate whether the conclusion follow logically from the given premises by either choosing Yes or No. Participants then were asked to report how confident they are in their answer by moving slider from 0 to 100; these instructions were followed by the practice problems and were presented prior to the start of experimental trials. The number of practice trials varied slightly by condition and will be specified below.

On each trial, the first syllogism premise was presented for 2000 ms, followed by the second premise (presented below the first) for a further 2000 ms. The conclusion, a question "Does the conclusion follow logically?", and response options then appeared below the premises. Text and response options were coloured green during the intuitive (Time 1) response and blue during the free-time (Time 2) response, accompanied by the on-screen labels "[INTUITIVE RESPONSE]" and "[THINK CAREFULLY]", respectively, to remind participants which type of response they were completing. After the Time 1 response, participants rated their confidence in that response. The same problem was then re-presented for the Time 2 response, followed by a second confidence rating. Participants completed all eight items in randomized order.

Working memory conditions.

Six between-subjects conditions varied the type and duration of working memory constraint imposed during the two-response task, following the general trial structure described above unless otherwise noted.

Control.

Participants completed the task as described above, with no additional WM manipulation, relying solely on instructions to respond quickly and intuitively at Time 1.

Load (Time 1). Prior to each syllogism, a dot-matrix pattern (see Materials) was presented for 2000 ms; participants were instructed to memorize the pattern while solving the syllogism. Following the Time 1 confidence rating, participants selected the studied pattern from among four alternatives and received accuracy feedback. No load was imposed during the Time 2 response. After familiarizing themselves with instructions for the syllogism task, and completing the practice trials, participants read instructions for the dot-matrix task and completed one practice trial, followed by one full practice trial, which contained both the syllogism and dot-matrix tasks before starting the experiment.

Load (Time 1) + Deadline. In addition to the concurrent dot-matrix task described above, a strict response deadline was imposed on the Time 1 response only: following the 2000 ms reading time for each premise, participants had 3000 ms, signalled by an on-screen timer, to respond once the conclusion and question appeared. Failure to respond within the deadline produced feedback instructing participants to respond faster on subsequent trials. No deadline was imposed on the Time 2 response. Participants completed two practice trials for the syllogism task, one practice trial for the dot-matrix task, and two full practice trials which contained the timed syllogism and dot-matrix tasks before starting the experiment.

Load (Time 1-2) + Deadline. This condition was identical to the Load (Time 1) + Deadline condition, except that the concurrent dot-matrix task was extended across both the Time 1 and Time 2 responses: participants were required to retain the pattern until after the Time 2 confidence rating, at which point they completed the recognition test. The response deadline applied to Time 1 only, as in the preceding conditions.

Deadline. The timing procedure was identical to the deadline conditions above: following the 2000 ms reading time for each premise, an on-screen timer signalled the final 3000 ms of the response window once the conclusion and question appeared, with no concurrent memory load imposed at any stage. The deadline was imposed on the Time 1 response only. Participants completed three practice trails before starting the experiment.

Auditory Load (Time 1). This condition used the tone-monitoring task (see Materials)

in place of the dot-matrix task. Presentation of the syllogism was identical to the two preceding conditions. Participants were instructed to press "f" as soon as they heard the highpitched tone and "v" for the low-pitched tone, while continuing to attend to and reason about the syllogism. Before beginning experimental trials, participants completed a familiarization phase in which the high and low tones were played individually and could be replayed as many times as needed until participants felt confident distinguishing them, followed by two full practice trials using the complete tone-monitoring procedure. In each experimental trial, the audio portion of each trial at Time 1 began with 300ms of silence and was followed by the unique tone sequence which would follow for the remainder of Time 1 reasoning. Five tones were presented in random order during the presentation of the premises and conclusion in Time 1. Because this task required continuous responding throughout the Time 1 window rather than a single post-hoc judgment, it was not paired with a fixed 3000 ms deadline as in the conditions above. Instead, Time 1 responses were capped at a maximum of 11,580 ms, calculated as the mean Time 1 response latency plus one standard deviation observed in the Control condition. This cap was not intended to impose the same time pressure as the deadline manipulations; rather, it kept trial length finite and prevented participants from withholding their reasoning response until a self-paced end to the tone sequence, since the sequence itself had no fixed endpoint. As in the deadline conditions, an on-screen timer signalled the final 3000 ms of the response window, beginning when the last tone ended.

Failure to respond within this time produced feedback instructing participants to respond faster on subsequent trials. Participants were, however, permitted to respond to the syllogism before the five-tone sequence had finished playing.

Results

Across the full sample, participants' conclusion acceptance rate was 63.34% of trials at Time 1 (T1) and 60.88% of trials at Time 2 (T2). Overall accuracy on the syllogistic reasoning task (i.e. accepting logically valid conclusions and rejecting logically invalid ones), was 66.31% at Time 1 and 66.36% at T2, and participants revised their answer between Time 1 and T2 on 14.6% of trials. Accuracy on the concurrent dot-matrix and tone monitoring tasks was above chance (all WM load conditions at or above 75%), confirming sustained engagement with the WM load manipulations throughout the reasoning period.

Analysis strategy

As the WM constraints primarily occurred at Time 1 (except for Load (Time 1-2) + Deadline condition), to examine the effect of WM on reasoning performance and monitoring, we only report Time 1 analyses for accuracy and confidence. To examine the relationship between monitoring and control, we report the relationship between Time 1 confidence and both T2 rethinking time and the probability of answer change. To ensure that observed effects reflected reasoning and metacognitive processes under WM constraint, trials on which the participants' failed the secondary task (dot-matrix recognition or tone-monitoring, as applicable) were excluded from all subsequent analyses. All analyses were conducted in R, version 4.6.1 (R Core Team, 2026), using RStudio (Posit team, 2026). Analyses of variance (ANOVA) were conducted using the afex (Singmann et al., 2025) and emmeans (Lenth & Piaskowski, 2026) packages. Goodman-Kruskal gamma correlations were computed using the DescTools package (Signorell, 2025), and Spearman correlations were computed using the base R cor() function. Mixed-effects models were fit using the lme4 (Bates et al., 2015) and lmerTest (Kuznetsova et al., 2017) packages.

Confidence

We conducted 6 (WM constraint) x 2 (Problem type) mixed factorial ANOVA on confidence ratings. There was a significant main effect of WM constraint, F(5, 975) = 6.90, MSE = 334.95, p < .001, η²p = .03. Confidence was also significantly higher in the Load condition than in the Load (Time 1-2) + Deadline condition, t(975) = 2.92, p = .041. No other pairwise comparisons between conditions were significant (all ps > .21; see Table 2 for marginal means).

Control Sensitivity

Confidence and Answer Change.

To examine the relationship between confidence and the decision to change a response, we computed within-participant gamma correlations between initial confidence and whether the response changed between the initial and final responses (0 = unchanged,1 = changed), for each participant with at least six trials and no missing data.

In contrast to monitoring resolution, control sensitivity was robust. One-sample t tests indicated that gamma coefficients were significantly negative in every condition (all ps < .001), including the load (Time 1-2) + deadline condition, in which WM constraints were also present during the final response. Participants were thus more likely to change their response when their initial confidence was low. A one-way ANOVA indicated that these coefficients did not differ across conditions, F(5, 368) = 0.54, p = .74.

Confidence and Rethinking Time. Our second index of control sensitivity was the withinparticipant Spearman correlation between initial confidence and log-transformed finalresponse latency (rethinking time), computed for each participant with at least six trials and no missing data. One-sample t tests indicated that these correlations were significantly negative in every condition (all ps < .001; see Table 3), indicating that participants spent longer rethinking their answers when their initial confidence was low. Both indices therefore replicate the well-established relationship between monitoring and control (Ackerman & Thompson, 2017; Thompson et al., 2011 Thompson et al., , 2013)), and indicate that this relationship did not differ reliably across WM constraint conditions.

Confidence, Conflict, and Metacognitive Control

So far, we have treated conflict as the primary cue informing confidence. According to the cue-utilization framework, however, monitoring judgements draw on several kinds of cues, including experience-based cues, such as fluency and conflict, and information-based, or theory-driven, cues grounded in beliefs about the task or one's own performance (Ackerman, 2019; Koriat, 1997 Koriat, , 2007)). If confidence integrates cues beyond conflict, it should predict control decisions even when conflict is held constant. To test this exploratory prediction, we fit a generalized linear mixed-effects model predicting answer change and a linear mixed-effects model predicting (log-transformed) rethinking time. Each model included standardized initial confidence, WM constraint condition, their interaction, and problem type as fixed effects, with random intercepts and random slopes for confidence by participant. Random slopes for confidence were included because participants were expected to differ not only in their baseline tendency to change responses or rethink, but also in how strongly their confidence governed these decisions (Barr et al., 2013). Both models converged without singular fits. Both models replicated the pattern observed in the correlation analyses: confidence remained a robust predictor of both control decisions, and control sensitivity was largely stable across WM constraint conditions.

Confidence and Answer Change.

Controlling for problem type, confidence remained a strong predictor of answer change, b = -1.01, SE = 0.11, z = -9.11, p < .001, OR = 0.36, such that a 1-SD increase in confidence was associated with approximately 64% lower odds of changing the initial response. Conflict problems were independently associated with higher odds of answer change, b = 0.27, SE = 0.09, z = 3.11, p = .002, OR = 1.31, although this effect was smaller than that of a 1-SD change in confidence. None of the WM constraint conditions differed significantly from the control condition in the strength of the confidenceanswer change relationship (all ps > .05).

Confidence and Rethinking Time. Controlling for problem type, confidence also remained a strong predictor of rethinking time, b = -0.23, SE = 0.03, t(466.8) = -8.26, p < .001, such that a 1-SD increase in confidence was associated with approximately 21% shorter rethinking time. Conflict problems were independently associated with longer rethinking time, b = 0.08, SE = 0.02, t(5520.8) = 4.93, p < .001, although this effect was again smaller than that of confidence. The confidence-rethinking time relationship was significantly weaker in the load (Time 1) + deadline condition than in the control condition, b = 0.08, SE = 0.04, t(388.9) = 1.99, p = .048, although it remained reliably negative within that condition. No other condition differed significantly from the control condition (all ps > .05). Overall, the relationship between monitoring and control was largely robust to WM constraints.

Control sensitivity was largely preserved across WM conditions. A significant confidence × WM constraint interaction emerged for the Load + Deadline condition, b = 0.08, SE = 0.04, t(388.9) = 1.99, p = .048, indicating a modest attenuation of the confidencerethinking relationship in this condition relative to Control; critically, the relationship remained reliably negative within Load + Deadline itself. No other condition differed significantly from Control in the strength of this coupling (all p> 0.05), indicating that the monitoring-control link mostly robust to working-memory constraint.

Discussion

In the present research, we examined the role of WM in reasoning and meta-reasoning using a two-response paradigm (Thompson et al., 2011), in which WM was constrained by a response deadline, a concurrent WM load (a dot-matrix or auditory tone-monitoring task), or both. Our findings can be grouped into three broad areas: reasoning accuracy, monitoring, and control. With respect to reasoning accuracy, neither constraint alone significantly impaired performance, but accuracy declined when a concurrent WM load and a response deadline were combined. This decline was similar for conflict and non-conflict problems, indicating that WM constraints impaired reasoning generally rather than impairing conflict processing specifically. With respect to monitoring, confidence was sensitive to conflict, and this sensitivity was largely unaffected by WM constraints, as indicated by the absence of a WM constraint × problem type interaction, although confidence was lower overall under the auditory load. Monitoring resolution, however, was uniformly low across conditions, which limits our ability to adjudicate between resource-competition and cue-utilization accounts of monitoring resolution. Finally, with respect to control, control sensitivity, the strength of the relationship between initial confidence and subsequent control decisions, was not attenuated by WM constraints. Notably, although participants changed their answers between the initial and final responses, these changes did not improve accuracy, possibly because of low resolution. We discuss each set of findings in detail below.

The Role of WM in Reasoning Accuracy

WM constraints and overall accuracy. DI theories predict that resource constraints should selectively impair performance on conflict problems, where override is required, whereas our interpretation of FTT predicts a general effect of resource constraints across problem types through processing interference, and leaves open whether constraints additionally affect conflict problems. Our finding that neither WM load nor a response deadline alone impaired reasoning performance, while the combination did, is broadly compatible with both accounts, but for different reasons. Under DI, load and deadline constrain different aspects of the same override process: load limits the resources available for effortful analytic processing, while a deadline limits the opportunity to engage in analytic processing at all (Evans & Curtis-Holmes, 2005). Neither constraint alone may have been sufficient to prevent successful override if reasoners can compensate, for instance, by taking more time under load alone, or by relying on otherwise-unconstrained resources under a deadline alone, but the conjunction of both should leave insufficient time and insufficient capacity to complete override. We note that although the auditory load condition also had a time limit, this was not meant to put the same level of pressure as the deadline conditions did.

Thus, this task, like other WM task, may not have been sufficiently demanding on its own to disrupt reasoning performance. Under our interpretation of FTT, the same pattern reflects processing interference: keeping track of overlapping class relations requires sustained attention, and while a WM load alone may be manageable given sufficient time, adding a deadline reduces the time available and exacerbates interference between competing class representations.

An alternative explanation for the absence of a main effect of WM constraint worth noting is that our load manipulation may not have been demanding or engaging enough to produce measurable interference on its own, with impairment emerging only once the added time constraint increased overall task difficulty. This seems only partially likely, however, since participants performed well above chance on both WM tasks, suggesting they were in fact engaging with the load manipulation. Still, above-chance performance does not rule out the possibility that the manipulation taxed a form or level of executive demand insufficient to interfere with reasoning, and we return to this question in the Limitations section.

WM constraints and conflict processing.

More interesting, however, is our finding that WM constraint did not interact with conflict status: reasoning was reliably poorer on conflict than non-conflict problems, but our load manipulation did not selectively exacerbate this conflict effect. This pattern is difficult to reconcile with traditional DI accounts, which locate the role of WM primarily in overriding intuitive responses on conflict problems. If WM resources were consumed principally by override, constraints should have impaired performance most on conflict problems, and they did not. Previous studies have taken a load by problem type interaction as evidence that resource constraints specifically disrupt the resolution of conflict between logic-and belief-based responses (De Neys, 2006), and our results do not support that interpretation. The pattern is more compatible with logical intuition accounts as we discuss below.

The pattern is consistent with our interpretation of FTT, in which resource constraints impair reasoning generally through processing interference, whereas the cost of conflict reflects representational interference. It also bears on a question FTT leaves open: whether resolving representational interference draws on WM. The absence of an interaction suggests that it does not, at least under the constraints imposed here. One possibility, consistent with FTT's claim that gist processing is low in effort, is that representational interference is resolved largely by the relative salience of the competing gists rather than by resourcedemanding inhibition. On this view, whether the thematic gist overrides the structural gist would be determined independently of available resources, and resource constraints would affect reasoning primarily through processing interference. This account converges with recent hybrid dual-process models, in which competing intuitions are generated in parallel and the response is determined by their relative strength rather than by effortful override (Pennycook et al., 2015; Bago & De Neys, 2017; De Neys, 2023). These findings raise the possibility that processing and representational interference contribute to reasoning errors in different ways, and future work should aim to disentangle their respective roles and their dependence on WM.

The role of WM in Monitoring WM constraints and conflict sensitivity. Cue-utilization accounts (Koriat, 1997) and the conflict-detection literature suggest that conflict monitoring is implicit and therefore relatively unaffected by resource constraints (Bago & De Neys, 2017; Franssens & De Neys, 2009). FTT does not directly address this question, although gist-level competition would plausibly produce such cues. Our findings support this view with a more rigorous test: confidence was lower on conflict than on non-conflict problems in all conditions, resource constraints did not interact with problem type in predicting confidence, and conflict sensitivity was preserved even in the combined condition, where accuracy was reliably impaired. However, confidence was lower overall in the auditory load condition. We consider two non-mutually exclusive explanations for this pattern. First, the tone-monitoring task may impose greater continuous attentional demands than the dot-matrix load; on this account, the lowered confidence reflects a genuine, implicit consequence of divided attention on the monitoring process, distinct from its effect (or lack thereof) on accuracy. Second, participants may have engaged in a more explicit form of monitoring, drawing on beliefs about the perceived difficulty of multitasking to consciously lower their confidence ratings, independent of how well they performed the reasoning task itself. This aligns with theoretical accounts that distinguish multiple, dissociable cues underlying confidence judgments: some implicit and experience-based, others explicit and theory-driven (Koriat, 2007).

The finding that reasoners often detect conflict yet still produce the incorrect response has typically been interpreted as evidence that reasoning errors reflect a failure of inhibition, rather than a failure of monitoring (Franssens & De Neys, 2009). FTT's account of representational interference makes a similar claim: errors on conflict problems arise when reasoners fail to inhibit a salient but incorrect gist in favour of the correct one (Reyna & Mills, 2007). Our findings complicate this picture. Although participants were sensitive to conflict at the level of problem type, this sensitivity did not translate into accurate monitoring at the level of individual responses: participants were largely unable to discriminate their own correct from incorrect answers. Errors may therefore reflect not only a failure to inhibit an inappropriate gist but also a failure to monitor which specific responses are wrong.

WM constraints and resolution.

There are theoretical reasons to expect that monitoring resolution could depend on working memory, and reasons to expect that it need not. On the resource-competition account, monitoring draws on working memory, or on the same limited cognitive resources that working memory relies on; when those resources are already committed to maintaining information in working memory, too little capacity remains available to support monitoring. This idea follows from direct-access accounts of monitoring (e.g., Nelson, 1990), which characterize monitoring as a process of continuously observing one's own ongoing task performance in real time. Thus, it follows that monitoring should be vulnerable when WM resources are taxed. On the cue-utilization view, confidence is generated largely automatically from cues produced during the reasoning process itself (Koriat, 1997; Thompson et al., 2011). If so, it should place few demands on WM, and resolution should be insensitive to WM constraints. Our results showed that monitoring resolution did not differ reliably as a function of WM load. However, this null effect should be interpreted with caution: resolution was near zero across all conditions, including the control condition. That is, participants were largely unable to discriminate their correct from incorrect answers via confidence even in the absence of any WM constraint. Because resolution had no room to decline, our design could not detect a load-related decrement, and the data therefore cannot adjudicate between the resource-competition and cue-utilization accounts. Instead, our findings are consistent with the generally poor resolution based on previous meta-reasoning research (Shynkaruk & Thompson, 2006), which suggests a limitation of monitoring that exists independently of resource constraints.

The role of WM in Control

Our findings indicate that control sensitivity remained robust across resource constraints. Lower initial confidence reliably predicted both a higher probability of changing the initial answer and longer rethinking times, regardless of condition and even after controlling for problem type. To our knowledge, this provides the first direct experimental evidence on whether control sensitivity depends on WM as previous evidence has come from correlational comparisons of age groups (Pansky et al., 2009). Our results suggest that acting on a monitoring signal does not depend heavily on WM resources, at least under the constraints imposed here. Preserved control sensitivity even in the Load (Time 1-2) + Deadline condition, where WM load was applied at both the initial and final responses, strengthens this interpretation, as resources were constrained during the control stage itself.

However, preserved control sensitivity does not imply effective control. Because resolution was near zero, participants were acting on confidence signals that carried little information about accuracy, which may explain why answer changes between the initial and final responses did not improve accuracy.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. First, the WM load was fixed rather than calibrated to individual WM capacity. Because the same load is unlikely to be equally demanding for individuals who differ in capacity, our group-level results may average over quite different levels of subjective demand. Relatedly, WM load alone did not impair accuracy, and although participants performed well above chance on the load task, the load may have imposed a level of demand insufficient to interfere with reasoning on its own.

Future studies could address both issues by measuring individual WM capacity and adjusting the load accordingly. Second, resolution was near zero even in the no-constraint condition, suggesting that participants could not reliably discriminate their correct from incorrect responses on this task under any circumstances. This floor effect prevented us from testing whether resource constraints reduce resolution. Future studies could use tasks on which resolution is higher at baseline. Finally, several of our central conclusion's rest on null effects. With a sample of 998 participants, the study was well powered to detect even modest effects, which gives the null interactions considerably more weight than they would carry in a smaller study. Statistical power cannot, however, compensate for the floor effect on resolution, so conclusions about resolution remain limited.

Integrating Fuzzy-Trace Theory and Meta-Reasoning

A recurring theme across the findings discussed above is that reasoning errors, and their correction, depend not only on what representation a reasoner holds, but on whether and when that representation is monitored and acted upon. FTT is explicit that reasoners do not rely on a single fixed level of representation: gist exists at several levels of complexity, and reasoners prefer the simplest gist trace available, escalating to a more precise gist or to verbatim processing only when the simpler representation fails to resolve the problem, or when gist and verbatim representations conflict (Reyna et al., 2021; Thompson et al., 2021).

This gist hierarchy implies some mechanism for recognizing that the currently held representation is insufficient, whether consciously or implicitly. As Thompson et al. (2021) note, both FTT and other dual-process accounts implicitly call for a monitoring mechanism: reasoners must detect a conflict between gist and verbatim interpretations, but neither theory specifies where this monitoring function sits in its architecture. Our findings illustrate why this specification is needed: reasoners' confidence was sensitive to conflict between competing gists yet did not discriminate correct from incorrect responses, and although confidence reliably guided control, that control did not improve accuracy. Next, we propose three ways to integrate the fuzzy-trace account of reasoning and the meta-reasoning framework. Reyna and Mills (2007) describe memory and reasoning tasks alike as proceeding through representation, retrieval, and application, followed by metacognitive judgments to withhold or output the resulting response. In the dual-recall and conjoint-recognition models, this stage is formalized as a metacognitive review parameter, described as an inhibitory mechanism, which can be estimated as more stringent or more lenient across developmental groups and clinical populations (Reyna & Mills, 2007). A separate line of work incorporates metacognition from an individual-differences component: reasoners higher in need for cognition are more likely to detect conflict between gist and verbatim interpretations and to inhibit the resulting incoherent response (Reyna, 2012 , as discussed in Thompson et al., 2021). However, both discussions of metacognitive monitoring describe an outcome -how often a response is withheld, and who is more likely to withhold it -without specifying the process that produces it. It therefore remains unclear what the metacognitive monitoring process is actually sensitive to when it decides whether to withhold or output a response: Is it set by the strength or fluency of the reconstructed gist trace itself? Does it operate differently depending on whether the competing representations are two gist traces, or a gist trace against a verbatim one? A more mechanistic account of this stage, specifying what, representationally, is being evaluated when a response is inhibited or output would be a valuable next step.

This connects to a second, more basic question: what representational information feeds into the monitoring signal in the first place? One possibility, consistent with a directaccess view, is that confidence is read directly off the strength of the currently active trace.

On an inferential view, consistent with Koriat's (1997) cue-utilization framework, confidence is inferred from experiential cues that accompany processing, such as fluency. As Thompson et al. (2021) propose, a natural extension of this to FTT is that the fluency of gist processing may explain why reasoners default to gist-based answers even when verbatim traces are available. However, FTT does not discuss in detail what generates a sense of trust in a gist trace, and there is correspondingly little direct empirical work on FTT and confidence specifically. Our finding that confidence did not discriminate correct from incorrect responses at the trial level, even in the absence of resource constraints, bears directly on this question. It suggests that whatever feeds the monitoring signal does not track accuracy well in this task, independently of available resources.

Finally, integrating two frameworks also calls for distinguishing monitoring from control or what a reasoner does in response to a monitoring signal. Withholding a response is the form of control most often discussed in FTT. The meta-reasoning literature, however, has documented a wider range of control operations, including revising or replacing an answer, seeking external input, and moving to a more precise representation. FTT's gist hierarchy arguably already describes one such operation, since escalating from a simpler to a more precise gist is a form of control, but it is not framed as a response triggered by a monitoring signal. Making this link explicit, by treating escalation within the gist hierarchy as a control response to a specific monitoring signal, would allow FTT to specify when and why reasoners move between levels of representation. Our finding that low confidence reliably prompted answer changes that did not improve accuracy suggests that the link between monitoring and control was intact, but that the control operations reasoners engaged, whether or not they involved moving to a more precise representation, were not effective.

Fuzzy-Trace Theory and Meta-Reasoning in Real-World Decisions

The processes studied by theories of reasoning and meta-reasoning underlie everyday judgements and decisions, such as whether a symptom warrants a visit to the doctor or whether a news headline is from a trustworthy source. Few theories of reasoning have been translated into applied settings as extensively as FTT. Through a sustained programme of research, Reyna, Brainerd, and colleagues have extended the theory from its origins in memory and cognitive development to risk perception, medical decision-making, and public health (Reyna, 2008; Reyna & Brainerd, 2011 , 2023). The finding that people rely on the gist of information rather than verbatim details explains why precise risk information is not necessarily effective in encouraging preventive behaviour or supporting medical decisions in domains including HIV prevention, cardiovascular disease, surgical risk, genetic risk, and cancer prevention (Reyna, 2008). This work has also informed theoretically grounded interventions, such as gist-based programmes to reduce risk-taking among adolescents (Reyna & Mills, 2014). Notably, research with experts such as physicians and intelligence agents has shown that reliance on gist strengthens with expertise (Corbin et al., 2015; Reyna & Brainerd, 2023). Gist-based reasoning also bears on misinformation. Online content that conveys a clear, simple gist is shared more widely, and corrections that explain the gist of why a claim is false reduce both the sharing and the endorsement of misinformation more effectively than presenting the same evidence in verbatim form (Broniatowski et al., 2024).

Relatedly, gist knowledge is associated with lower acceptance of misinformation (Hwang & Jeong, 2024). The COVID-19 pandemic offered a vivid test case at scale. A nonsignificant result from a randomized trial of masks, stripped of its context, was widely shared online as evidence that masks do not work (Broniatowski et al., 2024; Reyna et al., 2021). This illustrates that accurate facts alone do not protect against misinformation, because people remember and share the gist rather than the verbatim details.

However, as we have argued throughout this paper, no theory of reasoning is complete without a theory of meta-reasoning. Metacognition matters in the same applied domains, independently of the accuracy or type of representation a person relies on. The willingness to question intuitive responses predicts a wide range of everyday beliefs, including scepticism toward conspiracy theories, paranormal claims, and alternative medicine (Pennycook et al., 2015). Relevant knowledge and skills are also not sufficient on their own: numeracy predicts normative judgements on probabilistic reasoning tasks only among people who also have strong reflective thinking dispositions and sufficient cognitive ability (Klaczynski, 2014). In health decision-making, resolution, that is, how well confidence discriminates accurate from inaccurate beliefs, predicts health behaviour above and beyond the accuracy of the beliefs themselves (Fischer et al., 2023). Among experts, radiologists' confidence remains inappropriately high as diagnostic difficulty increases, producing substantial overconfidence on difficult cases regardless of training or experience, and longer deliberation predicts more errors and greater overconfidence rather than better judgement (Bercovich & Ackerman, 2026). In the domain of misinformation, poor discernment between true and false news is more closely linked to a failure to reflect than to partisanship, and overconfidence may contribute by discouraging people from slowing down to reason (Pennycook & Rand, 2021). People also share content that they would recognize as inaccurate if asked and simply prompting them to consider accuracy reduces the sharing of false headlines. Notably, FTT anticipates this role for metacognition: reasoners who are willing to monitor and edit their own thinking are expected to attend more to cues from alternative representations, and accuracy prompts may work by increasing this willingness (Broniatowski et al., 2024). Confidently held misinformation has also been linked to reduced information-seeking and resistance to correction, and brief exposure to misinformation can reduce confidence in accurate knowledge people already hold (Rapp & Withall, 2024). The COVID-19 pandemic again offered a test case at scale. Resolution predicted compliance with public health measures and willingness to be vaccinated (Fischer et al., 2023), and across Canada, the United Kingdom, and the United States, cognitive sophistication was consistently associated with fewer misperceptions and stronger vaccination intentions but was largely unrelated to mitigation behaviours such as social distancing (Pennycook et al., 2022). While findings from applied research using FTT has shown that effective risk communication depends on conveying the right gist, these findings suggest that effective decision-making also depends on knowing when that gist can be trusted. Integrating FTT's account of how people represent risk with an account of how they monitor those representations may therefore offer a more complete basis for improving real-world decisions.

Conclusion

The present research examined whether WM is required not only for reasoning but also for monitoring and controlling one's own reasoning. Combining a concurrent WM load with a response deadline impaired reasoning accuracy, but the impairment was similar for conflict and non-conflict problems, suggesting that WM constraints disrupt reasoning generally rather than increasing belief bias specifically. By contrast, reasoners' sensitivity to conflict and their use of confidence to guide subsequent control decisions were largely robust to WM constraints, even when accuracy was impaired. However, effective reasoning was limited by poor resolution: reasoners acted on their confidence, but their confidence did not reliably distinguish correct from incorrect responses, even in the absence of constraints.

Together, these findings suggest that cognitive constraints affect how well people reason more than whether they detect interference or act on their confidence.

Theoretically, these findings highlight the value of integrating FTT with the metareasoning framework. FTT explains how competing gist representations give rise to interference, but it does not specify how reasoners detect that interference or decide whether to act on it. The meta-reasoning framework addresses precisely this gap by distinguishing monitoring from control and our results suggest that both processes can operate under WM constraints. FTT proposes that good decisions depend on extracting the appropriate gist; our findings suggest that they may also depend on judging when that gist can be trusted.

Consequently, identifying the cues that underlie these judgments, and how to make them more diagnostic of accuracy, is an important direction for both traditions.
