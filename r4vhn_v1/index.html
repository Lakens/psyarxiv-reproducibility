Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

In an increasingly digitalized world, digital inclusion has become a key societal concern (Kargapolova et al., 2020). However, neurodivergent youth, including those with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), attentiondeficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), mild intellectual disabilities (MID), and dyslexia (Layinka et al., 2024), remain at risk of digital exclusion (Groot et al., 2022). Digital technologies can enhance social participation, provide access to information, and support active citizenship, they may also expose these young people to distinct risks and vulnerabilities. Many professionals and caregivers find it difficult to guide children in their digital lives (Gür & Türel, 2022; Nikken et al., 2024). The pace of change in the digital world makes it difficult to keep up, and caregivers who lack digital skills find it difficult to connect with and guide young people online (Gür & Türel, 2022). While children tend to adapt quickly to these changes and develop strong digital skills, they often fail to anticipate the consequences of their online actions (Vale et al., 2018). It has therefore been suggested that improving caregivers' digital literacy could help them become better guides of young people's digital media use (Banic & Orehovacki, 2024; Nikken & Opree, 2018). However, digital literacy needs to be transferred into practice, which makes parenting style and digital support practices at least as important (Vossen et al., 2024).

Children need support in both online and offline settings, for example in navigating social interactions, managing risk-taking behaviors, and respecting boundaries. Online and offline environments are not fundamentally different in this regard and often; youth might even consider them to be one and the same. From this perspective, parental style, professional support and digital support practices may matter more than adults' digital literacy in managing children's digital behavior (Vossen et al., 2024).

As online and offline environments are not fundamentally different, understanding effective digital support may benefit from insights into established parenting styles in offline contexts. These styles are commonly characterized along two dimensions: demandingness and responsiveness (Baumrind, 2013). A parenting style that is high on demandingness and low on responsiveness is referred to as an 'authoritarian' parenting style. When both demandingness and responsiveness are high, this is considered 'authoritative' parenting. A combination of low demandingness and high responsiveness is called 'permissive' parenting.

Finally, a parenting style low on both demandingness and responsiveness is considered a 'neglectful' parenting style (Baumrind 2013).

In the digital field offline parenting styles were not directly incorporated. Instead, much of the research on digital support styles builds on the three parental-mediation strategies originally identified by Bybee et al. (1982) for television and later refined by Valkenburg et al. (1999): restrictive mediation (setting rules and boundaries), instructive or active mediation (discussing media content with the child), and social co-viewing or co-use (watching/using media together without critically discussing it). Building on this, a large cross-national study in eight countries by Livingstone et al. (2017) identified two broader support styles: 'enabling parenting', which combines active mediation together with control and monitoring creating a safe environment to encourage positive internet use, and 'restrictive parenting', which focusses on restrictions. Although this framework was originally developed in the context of parental support, Mendoza (2009) argued that these mediation styles also apply to teachers and other professionals working with children.

Various studies show that support style is generally associated with children's wellbeing and their engagement in risky behavior, although effects are often small and the resulting picture is nuanced. For the offline world, Azman et al. (2021) found that both authoritative and permissive parenting styles were associated with better mental health scores in children and adolescents, while demanding-controlling and emotionally distancing styles showed the least favorable outcomes. In the digital domain, this would translate to support that combines support and trust with clear rules about media use. Geurts et al. (2023) found that a tolerant and supportive parenting profile (characterized by high responsiveness and autonomy-granting rather than strict rulesetting) predicted lower levels of problematic social media use over time than profiles combining high restrictions with either low or high support, with high restrictions and low support predicting the highest levels of problematic social media use, suggesting that a supportive general parenting context may matter more than internet-specific restrictions. Flamant et al. (2022) found that overprotective support was linked to maladjustment, aggression, and internalizing distress, though these effects were modest and depend partly on how adolescents cope with such parenting. Some degree of parental control does appear to play a protective role.

Low levels of parental control were associated with excessive screen exposure (Coyne et al., 2017), and permissive or laissez-faire parenting was associated with greater involvement in cyberbullying as both victim and perpetrator (Vale et al., 2018). Parental co-use and active involvement related to online risk-taking, particularly among boys (Notten & Nikken, 2016). In addition, Young and Tully (2022) found that autonomysupportive parenting, when combined with active mediation, had the most favorable outcomes: parents could best protect children from risky online experiences by supporting adolescent autonomy through open communication rather than through control alone.

Beyond individual caregivers, institutional settings may also shape children's digital behavior. Schools in several countries have recently introduced smartphone bans, though the evidence on their effects remains inconclusive. A meta-analysis of five studies found a significant but modest overall effect for social wellbeing (Böttger & Zierer, 2024). A large observational study found that restrictive school phone policies were followed by reductions in phone and social media use during school hours but found no evidence of changes in use on weekdays or weekends, and no changes in overall mental wellbeing (Goodyear et al., 2025). This suggests that school-based restrictions alone might be insufficient and that interventions should address both in-school and out-of-school screen use simultaneously, including effects on related behaviors such as sleep and physical activity.

All in all, these findings suggest that neither strict control nor unrestricted freedom is optimal. Both responsiveness and appropriate demandingness contribute to reducing risky online behavior and promoting healthy digital engagement. Children themselves recognize the value of parental rules and boundaries. In a qualitative study, children across age groups agreed that rules were necessary and good for their safety and wellbeing, though older adolescents emphasized that rules should be negotiated rather than imposed (Nixon et al., 2010).

The effects of support might also not be one-directional. The way children respond to support may shape the support they receive. Digital support is often described in a one-sided manner, focusing on the actions of caregivers whilst paying less attention to children's coping strategies and self-disclosure (Modecki et al., 2022). However, support is inherently interactive: a caregiver's approach will shape the child's reaction, and vice versa. Bidirectional effects were identified by De Groot et al. (2023) among professionals working with youth with mild intellectual disabilities, in which support was only provided when youth actively asked for help, making the child's initiative the starting point for support. Active and open communication may encourage adolescent self-disclosure, while rule setting can lead to compliance but also to aggression, internalizing distress (Flamant et al. 2022) or technical workarounds such as moving to alternative platforms when certain apps are restricted (Modecki et al., 2022). The benefits of youth involvement in rule-setting is age-dependent. should not be overstated, however. Geurts et al. (2025) found age-dependent effect of rule-setting on the onset of problematic social media use. Effect of rules were strongly age-dependent: for younger adolescents, stricter rules were associated with a lower likelihood of developing problematic use, while for older adolescents -from around age 16 -stricter rules were associated with a higher likelihood. This underscores the importance of age-appropriate support strategies. Finally, caregivers should bear in mind that their own media use serves as a direct model for children's digital behavior (Geurts et al., 2022; Nikken, 2017).

Teachers, professionals and caregivers might find it particularly challenging to find the right balance in digital support for neurodivergent children. Digital support involves a complex balance between communicating trust, offering support and digital opportunities, while also setting boundaries and monitoring to prevent harm (Modecki et al. 2022). Parents of children with ASD, for example, more frequently offered digital devices as a strategy to help their child self-regulate during moments of distress compared to parents of typically developing children (Pliska et al., 2025). At the same time, these neurodivergent children are more vulnerable to online risks such as addiction (Alper et al., 2023), requiring caregivers to navigate the tension between the potential benefits of digital media and its associated risks. Several studies support the need for a dual approach combining protection with engagement. For children with ADHD, whose impulsivity makes them more prone to risk-taking behavior, monitoring online behavior is found to have a protective effect (Pollak et al. 2020). For autistic children, who tend to use screens more frequently and for longer periods, increasing their risk of screen addiction, limiting screen time is found to be particularly important (Yuan et al. 2024). Additionally, Verhagen et al. (2025) found that resilience-oriented parenting was associated with increased externalizing symptoms in children with ADHD and ASD, suggesting limit-setting is important for caregivers of neurodivergent children.

However, an overly restrictive approach might increase the risk of digital exclusion (Livingstone et al. 2017).

This underscores the need for a careful balance between protection and participation, a core principle of social work practice in offline settings (Veselinova, 2014). As digital technologies become more integrated into everyday life, this tension is increasingly reflected in the online world as well.

To better understand how a balance can be achieved between protection and participation, it is essential to consider children's own perspectives. Despite a growing body of research on digital inclusion and online safety, relatively little is known about how children themselves experience and evaluate the digital support they receive. Research on digital guiding styles has predominantly focused on caregivers' perceptions and less on the children's own views in which parents describe their children's media use and their own parenting styles (Alper et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2022; Livingstone et al. 2017). This is a noticeable gap, as children have experiential views on what forms of support they find helpful or unhelpful in navigating their digital lives. Children might not merely seek fewer boundaries, they are also aware that restrictions are sometimes necessary (Nixon et al., 2010). This is particularly relevant for neurodivergent children, whose specific vulnerabilities and needs may shape how they experience and evaluate the support they receive. Hence, this study explored support styles applied by caregivers (parents and teachers) as experienced by neurodivergent children and forms of support they found most helpful. This leads to the following research questions: Which digital support styles did youth perceive from their parents and teachers? How effective did neurodivergent youth perceive digital support styles? Did support styles differ by type of neurodivergence and self-perceived digital capabilities?

Methods

Participants and procedure

This study employed a cross-sectional quantitative design. Participants were neurodivergent young people aged 11 to 23 years, recruited through multiple channels: advertisements on websites targeting neurodivergent groups, social media such as LinkedIn, the professional and personal networks of the researchers including contacts at residential facilities, and student-led recruitment as part of a course project at Leiden University of Applied Sciences. Inclusion criteria were age between 11 and 23 and having a diagnosis of one or more types of neurodiversity or self-identifying as neurodivergent. These criteria were verified through screening questions included in the online questionnaire.

Data collection took place between October 2024 and January 2025 using two methods. Participants aged 16 years and older filled in an online questionnaire in Qualtrics. Participants younger than 16 and those with MID filled in a similar questionnaire in a face-to-face setting with the support of a trained interviewer. The interviews took place either at the participant's home or at the residential facility where the adolescent lived. Data collection was conducted in accordance with scientific ethical regulations. All participants and/or their parents or legal guardians were informed about the aim of the study, its voluntary nature, and the activities involved. Participants aged 16 years and older provided consent through the questionnaire. For participants younger than 16 years, informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians. For participants with MID, healthcare professionals at the relevant facilities informed parents or guardians about the study and obtained consent on behalf of the research team. Data collection commenced only after consent had been secured. The study was approved by the Leiden University of Applied Sciences Research Ethics Committee (Reference: CEO 10/2024).

Material

The questionnaire included questions about demographics, online activities, perceived need for support in online activities, support capabilities of parents, perceived internet self-esteem, digital capabilities, and parenting and support styles. For participants younger than 16 years and those with MID some questions were removed and others were reworded to reduce cognitive load. Below, only the measures relevant to the study aims are described.

Digital support style

To assess digital support styles, 13 items on guiding behaviors were developed by the first two authors (Table 1). These were based on items used in a previous exploratory study (Nikken & Büttner, 2021) and adapted considering findings from further exploratory research by the second author (De Groot et al., 2023). No priori dimensions were defined. The items were for this study categorized according to the theoretical frameworks of Baumrind (2013) and Glatz et al. (2018). Face validity and feasibility were assessed by the senior researchers of the research project group with expertise on the topic.

Frequency of digital support style

Participants indicated how often parents showed each of the 13 guiding behaviors, using a 4-point Likert-scale ranging from 'never' to 'very often'. An example item was: "How often does your parent set limits (e.g., saying how long or how often you are allowed to use a screen)?" Participants aged 16 and older answered the same questions twice: once with reference to their parents and once with reference to their teachers.

Perceived effective support behavior

Participants aged 16 and older ranked the same 13 guiding behaviors from most to least effective, where a rank of 1 indicated the behavior perceived as the most effective and 13 the least effective.

Analyses

To answer the research questions, we first examined the factor structure of the 13 digital support behaviors, after checking the distribution of items and inter-item correlations. Items with consistently low correlations with all other items (r < .30) were considered for exclusion. An exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with varimax rotation was conducted on the frequency of support behaviors as reported for parents. The number of factors to retain was determined by inspecting eigenvalues greater than one and the scree plot, with theoretical considerations serving as the final criterion in case of ambiguity. Items with low loadings on both factors were considered for exclusion. Items with negative loadings were recoded prior to further analyses. Based on the factor structure identified in the EFA for support by parents, we then assessed whether the same factor structure was applicable to support by teacher by examining the internal consistency of the resulting support style dimensions using McDonald's omega (Hayes & Coutts, 2020).

For each participant, mean scores were calculated for the two identified support style dimensions.

Differences in the reported frequency of support styles between parents and teachers were examined using paired samples t-tests. Differences in reported frequencies between groups based on type of neurodiversity were examined using independent samples t-tests, with each neurodiversity type compared separately against participants without that diagnosis. To address the research questions on perceived effectiveness, analyses were conducted at two levels. At the item level, descriptive statistics were calculated for each of the 13 support behaviors based on their rank-order scores. At the support style level, differences in perceived effectiveness between the two support styles were examined using a Wilcoxon signed-rank test, given the ordinal nature of the ranking data and the paired structure of the comparisons. All effects were teste on a significant p-level of p < 0.05.

Check for assumptions.

The items of the digital support frequency scale as answered for parents did not follow a normal distribution. Some items showed positive skewedness, while others were negatively skewed. This deviation from normality was taken into account when interpreting the results. Although violations of normality may reduce the quality of the solution, factor analysis under these conditions is still considered informative (Tabachnick & Fidell, 2001). Due to skewness, univariate outliers were identified but not excluded.

Inter-item correlation analyses revealed that the item "My parents do not know how to help me" showed consistently low correlations with all other items (r < .23) and was therefore excluded from further analyses, reducing the scale to 12 items. Screening for multivariate outliers at the respondent level identified three multivariate outliers (χ²(12) > 32.91, p < .001). Since excluding these respondents did not substantially alter the results, they were retained. All reported results are therefore based on the full sample (N = 58).

Based on these results, all items were retained for the responses on parent support scale.

Participants aged 16 and older (N = 47) also reported how often their teachers applied the same support behaviors. For the responses on teacher support, internal consistency for Factor 1 could not be computed when item 3 was included, as McDonald's omega assumes a unidimensional structure, an assumption that is likely violated when item 3 (which loaded negatively on Factor 1) is included. When item 3 was deleted, internal consistency for Factor 1 was acceptable (ω = .79). For Factor 2, internal consistency was questionable (ω = .63) and did not meaningfully improve when items were deleted.

Based on these findings and on item content, item 3 was deleted from all further analyses. Although item 3 -"They let me arrange it myself; I am allowed to try it myself." -taps into the degree of autonomy children experience in their digital lives, it is conceptually ambiguous: a high score on this item does not necessarily reflect the opposite of restrictive parenting, and its inclusion undermined the unidimensionality of Factor 1.

It should be noted that the non-normal distribution of items may have affected the reliability estimates. Skewed items tend to show reduced variance due to ceiling and floor effects, which can artificially lower McDonald's omega values. The reliability estimates reported here should therefore be interpreted with this limitation in mind.

Prevalence of digital support styles

For parents, the enabling and authoritative support style was reported significantly more frequently (M = 1.96, SD = 0.61) than the restrictive and authoritarian support style (M = 1.38, SD = 0.49; t(57) = -6.90, p < .001). A similar pattern was found for teachers: the enabling and authoritative support style occurred significantly more frequently (M = 1.61, SD = 0.52) than the restrictive and authoritarian support style (M = 1.36, SD = 0.43; t(46) = -3.75, p < .01).

Differences between parents and teachers.

Participants aged 16 and older (N = 47) answered questions about both parents and teachers, allowing for a direct comparison. Due to listwise deletion -not all participants completed the teacher questions -these analyses are based on a slightly reduced subsample, which accounts for the small difference in means compared to the analyses above. No significant difference was found in the frequency of restrictive and authoritarian support between parents and teachers (M=1.36, SD = 0.43 vs. M=1.22, SD =0.24). However, parents were reported to apply an enabling and authoritative support style significantly more frequently than teachers (M = 1.89, SD = 0.58 vs. M = 1.61, SD = 0.52; t(46) = 2.67, p < .05).

Type of neurodivergence and support style

Parents. To examine whether the frequency of support styles differed by type of neurodivergence, independent samples t-tests were conducted for each neurodivergence type separately. Children with MID reported that their parents applied a restrictive and authoritarian support style significantly more frequently than children without MID (M = 1.88, SD = 0.60 vs. M = 1.31, SD = 0.44; t(56) = 3.08, p < .01). In contrast, children with ADHD reported receiving a restrictive and authoritarian support style significantly less frequently than children without ADHD (M = 1.28, SD = 0.45 vs. M = 1.49, SD = 0.53; t(56) = 1.68, p < .05). A similar pattern was found for children with dyslexia, who also reported receiving a restrictive and authoritarian support style less frequently than children without dyslexia (M = 1.22, SD = 0.20 vs. M = 1.43, SD = 0.55; t(56) = -2.16, p < .05). No significant differences were found for autism or high intellectual ability.

Teachers.

Children with dyslexia reported receiving an enabling and authoritative support style from their teachers significantly less frequently than children without dyslexia (M = 1.40, SD = 0.32 vs. M = 1.68, SD = 0.57; t(45) = -2.14, p < .05). No significant differences were found for autism, ADHD, or high intellectual ability. Youth with MID did not answer these questions.

Perceived effectiveness of digital support Support style level With respect to perceived effectiveness, an enabling and authoritative support style was rated as significantly more effective than a restrictive and authoritarian support style. For 43 of the 47 respondents, the restrictive and authoritarian support style received a higher rank-order value, indicating lower perceived effectiveness, compared to the enabling and authoritative support style (z = -5.75, p < .001).

Item level

Examining perceived effectiveness at the level of individual support behaviors, a consistent pattern emerged. Prohibiting access to digital content (item 12, M rank = 11.04), monitoring media use (item 10, M rank = 9.77), and removing media devices (item 9, M rank = 9.83) were perceived as least effective. Setting limits on screen time (item 1, M rank = 6.11) and unsolicited explanation (item 6, M rank = 6.11) were perceived as moderately effective. By contrast, allowing children to arrange things themselves (item 3, M rank = 2.68), helping when asked (item 5, M rank = 3.62), and doing online activities together (item 2, M rank = 3.72) were perceived as most effective, see Table 1 for the mean rankings.

Discussion

This study examined digital support styles as perceived by neurodivergent young people. Support behaviors differed between an 'enabling and authoritative style' and a 'restrictive and authoritarian style'. Both parents and teachers applied the enabling and authoritative style more frequently than the restrictive and authoritarian style. When parents and teachers were compared directly, parents were reported to apply the enabling and authoritative style more frequently than teachers, while no significant difference was found for the restrictive and authoritarian style. Some differences were found between neurodivergence types. According to neurodivergent youth, parents of young people with MID applied a restrictive authoritarian style more frequently than parents of young people without MID. In contrast, responses of the youth indicated that parents of young people with ADHD and dyslexia applied a restrictive authoritarian style less frequently than parents of young people without these conditions; a finding that warrants further interpretation and is discussed below. Teachers applied an enabling authoritative style less frequently to young people with dyslexia than to those without dyslexia, as perceived by these young people.

With respect to perceived effectiveness, neurodivergent young people rated the enabling authoritative support style as significantly more effective than the restrictive authoritarian style. At the item level, monitoring, removing media devices, and prohibiting digital content were perceived as least effective, while setting limits was considered moderately helpful. Helping when asked, doing online activities together, and allowing young people to manage their own digital lives were perceived as most effective. Among neurotypical youth evidence indicates that such a more autonomy-supportive style does benefit children's development (De Clercq et al., 2019).

The finding that parents applied an enabling and authoritative support style more frequently than teachers, while no significant difference was found for the restrictive and authoritarian style, is consistent with theoretical distinctions between enabling and restrictive mediation strategies (Livingstone et al., 2017). In educational settings, teachers' digital support tends to be organized around risk prevention, managing online behavior, and meeting institutional responsibilities -which may make rule-based and restrictive practices comparatively prominent (Berger, 2020; Chiner et al., 2025). Parents, by contrast, can engage in more continuous day-to-day interaction with their children, which may create more opportunities for supportive, dialogical support and make such behaviors more visible to adolescents. It is important to acknowledge, however, that the parent-teacher comparison in this study is only based on participants aged 16 and older. There is a possibility that teachers use enabling and authoritative support more frequently in this younger group as these children still learn how to use and cope with digital media. Several differences were found between neurodivergence types, although the small group sizes warrant cautious interpretation. Parents of young people with MID were reported to apply a restrictive authoritarian support style more frequently than parents of young people without MID. This finding is perhaps unsurprising given the cognitive vulnerabilities associated with MID, which may lead caregivers to prioritise protection and limit-setting. While such an approach may offer short-term protection, overly restrictive support risks long-term digital exclusion (Livingstone et al., 2017). If young people with MID are only controlled and protected, they might not have the opportunity to try things for themselves and learn from their own experiences and mistakes (Nowogrodzki, 2025; Sharman, 2014). Finding the right balance between protection and participation therefore takes courage but is particularly important for this group.

In contrast, parents of young people with ADHD and dyslexia applied a restrictive authoritarian support style less frequently than parents of young people without these conditions. This is somewhat counterintuitive, given that both ADHD and dyslexia are associated with increased vulnerability to online risks. One possible explanation is that parents of children with ADHD and dyslexia are more focused on supporting their child's autonomy and self-regulation. In these conditions enabling support may be seen as more appropriate than restriction.

Finally, this study relied entirely on children's self-reported perceptions of the support they receive. No caregiver data were collected to compare the perceptions of the children with those of the caregivers. Future research would benefit from including both child and caregiver perspectives to obtain a more complete picture of digital support practices.

Conclusion

This study provides a first exploratory overview of digital support styles as perceived by neurodivergent young people, addressing a gap in the literature that has predominantly focused on caregivers' perspectives. The findings show that neurodivergent young people experience enabling and authoritative support (combining support, open communication, and opportunities for autonomy) as more effective than restrictive and authoritarian approaches. Monitoring, removing devices, and prohibiting digital content were perceived as least effective, while being helped when asked and having freedom to navigate digital life independently were valued most. These findings are nuanced by differences between neurodivergence types and between parents and teachers and underscore the importance of incorporating children's own perspectives in research on and guidelines for digital support of neurodivergent young people.

Practical implications & thoughts about future research

This study demonstrates that neurodivergent young people's perceptions of digital support can be meaningfully studied by asking them to evaluate specific support behaviors. Given that today's caregivers did not grow up navigating the same digital landscape as their children, incorporating young people's perspectives into guidelines and training materials is both feasible and valuable.

The findings might lead to direct implications for practice if replicated. Neurodivergent young people perceive enabling and authoritative support as most effective. At the same time, they recognise that some degree of boundary-setting is helpful. These perceptions should inform guidelines for neurodivergent young people. In particular, teachers appear to apply enabling and authoritative support less frequently than parents, suggesting that professional development in educational settings should explicitly encourage this approach. By offering enabling rather than solely protective support, professionals, parents, and teachers can strengthen the digital participation of neurodivergent youth. Increased participation may, in turn, promote access to information, social connectedness, and societal citizenship, contributing to greater digital inclusion.

Especially for young people with MID, the findings highlight the importance of moving beyond purely protective and restrictive approaches. While the vulnerabilities of this group make caution understandable, overly restrictive support risks digital exclusion and limits opportunities for learning through experience.

Caregivers of young people with MID need to be supported in how to balance protection with participation.

Several directions for future research follow from these findings. First, studies with larger and more diverse samples are needed to replicate and extend these findings, particularly for specific neurodiversity types where subgroup sizes were small. Second, including a neurotypical comparison group would allow for a more direct examination of whether and how the support preferences of neurodivergent young people differ from those of their neurotypical peers. Third, future research could collect data from both children and caregivers to examine the degree of alignment or misalignment between children's perceptions of support received and caregivers' accounts of support given. Finally, longitudinal designs would allow researchers to examine whether support style predicts digital wellbeing and online risk-taking over time in neurodivergent populations.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, not all participants received the same questionnaire or data collection procedure. Participants aged 16 and older completed an online survey independently, while younger participants and those with MID completed a simplified questionnaire in a supported face-to-face interview. These different modes of data collection may have influenced responses. Faceto-face interviews can be susceptible to social desirability effects, potentially leading participants to present support as more positive than it was. At the same time, the supported interview format may have enabled participants with MID to report their experiences more accurately than they could have in an unsupported online survey. The extent to which mode effects influenced the findings is difficult to determine.

Second, the subgroup sizes for specific neurodiversity types were particularly limited and confounding factors such as gender or age could not be taken into account. This reduces statistical power and means that null findings, such as the absence of significant differences for autism and high intellectual ability, should not be interpreted as evidence of no effect. Conversely, significant findings based on small subgroups should be interpreted with caution.

Third, neurodivergent children's perceptions were not compared with those of neurotypical children.

While meaningful conclusions can be drawn about the experiences of neurodivergent young people themselves, no comparative statements about differences between neurodivergent and neurotypical children can be made on the basis of these data.

Fourth, the frequency of support behaviors showed skewed distributions with ceiling and floor effects, limiting variance. While factor analysis is considered relatively robust to violations of normality (Tabachnick & Fidell, 2001), the reduced variance may have lowered statistical power in the group comparison analyses, potentially obscuring differences that exist in the broader population.
