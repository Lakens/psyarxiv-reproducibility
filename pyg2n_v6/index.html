What we found

We found 6 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Objective This systematic review synthesizes prospective evidence on associations between obesity assessed in young or middle adulthood and subsequent cognitive performance before age 65. Methods A comprehensive search of PubMed, PsycINFO, and Web of Science identified prospective studies comparing adults with obesity to those of normal weight and assessing cognitive performance, using validated cognitive tests. Risk of bias was evaluated using ROBINS-E, and findings were synthesized narratively. Results Among 4,871 records screened, four publications representing three cohorts met the inclusion criteria. Two publications from Whitehall II reported small negative associations between obesity assessed in adulthood and global cognitive performance, whereas the MIDUS analysis found no longitudinal association for BMI. A modestly steeper 10-year decline in global cognition was reported among metabolically unhealthy adults with obesity compared with adults of normal weight in Whitehall II. Findings for executive function and memory were inconsistent and generally small. One study was judged at high risk of bias and the other three raised some concerns. Conclusions The available evidence is too sparse, heterogeneous, and methodologically uncertain to establish a meaningful independent association between obesity and cognitive performance before age 65. Future prospective studies should include cognitive assessment at baseline and repeated follow-up, report standardized effect estimates, use domain-specific cognitive outcomes, address confounding a priori, and assess central adiposity and metabolic health alongside BMI, particularly in younger adults.

Introduction

Obesity is a major global public health problem, driven by a complex interplay of genetic, behavioral, social, and environmental factors (Silventoinen et al., 2010; Choquet & Meyre, 2011; Swinburn et al., 2011; Thaker, 2017; Phelps et al., 2024). In 2022, it was estimated that 878 million adults worldwide were living with obesity, and its prevalence continues to rise (Phelps et al., 2024). Typically, in epidemiological studies, obesity is assessed by the body mass index (BMI), which is an individual's weight (in kilograms) divided by the square of their height (in meters). A chronic and multifactorial disease, obesity is characterized by excessive adipose tissue accumulation, increasing the risk of numerous health complications (Bray, 2004). Chronic low-grade neuroinflammation and alterations in gut microbiota have been identified as key mechanisms by which obesity may affect brain health and function (Monteiro & Azevedo, 2010; Heneka et al., 2015; Miller & Raison, 2016; Chen & Devaraj, 2018; Kacířová et al., 2020; Amabebe et al., 2020; Ly et al., 2023). Over the past few decades, lifestyles in many societies have changed dramatically, with major shifts in dietary habits, modes of transportation and work environments. Easy access to energy-dense, palatable foods, combined with increasingly sedentary routines, has led to a marked decrease in overall energy expenditure. This transition is further exacerbated by the abundance of food marketing that exploits dietary cues and targets neural pathways associated with cognitive functions (Berthoud, 2012).

Cognition involves the processes of integrating, storing and utilizing information, with key domains including memory, attention and executive functions. Executive functions are higher-order cognitive skills that enable individuals to engage in goal-directed behaviors (Welsh & Pennington, 1988). Among these, three core executive functions-inhibition (the voluntary suppression of dominant responses), mental flexibility (switching between different cognitive tasks), and updating (managing new information and modifying items in working memory)-have been widely studied, particularly in the field of cognitive psychology (Morris & Jones, 1990; Monsell, 1996; Miyake et al., 2000). In individuals with obesity, impairments in these cognitive functions, particularly executive functions, may undermine their ability to adopt and maintain health-promoting behaviors (Smith et al., 2011; Lowe et al., 2019). Given that the age of onset of obesity is decreasing, determining whether specific cognitive domains might already be impaired in young to middleaged adults is crucial (Phelps et al., 2024). In this review, we focused on two age groups: young adults (18-45 years) and middle-aged adults (46-64 years).

Although previous systematic reviews have examined associations between obesity and cognition, many have focused on cross-sectional data, limiting consideration of causal and temporal associations. These reviews report associations between obesity and poorer cognitive performance, affecting both global cognitive function and specific domains such as executive functions and memory (Van den Berg et al., 2009; Fitzpatrick et al., 2013; Hendrikse et al., 2015; Prickett et al., 2015; Favieri et al., 2019). Moreover, most included older populations, providing limited evidence on obesity exposure earlier in adulthood and cognitive outcomes before age 65. We therefore aimed to synthesize prospective evidence on associations between obesity assessed in young or middle adulthood and subsequent global and domain-specific cognitive performance before age 65.

Materials and methods

Protocol registration

The protocol was developed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 guidelines (Shamseer et al., 2015). This protocol was registered in the "International prospective register of systematic reviews" PROSPERO (registration number CRD42024573250) and deposited on the HAL-an open archives websitein July 2024. Furthermore, to ensure transparency and rigor in the reporting of this systematic literature review, we followed the PRISMA guidelines (Page et al., 2021), with the checklist available as supplementary material (Table S1 and Table S2). Two deviations from the published Population  Adult women and men aged 18 to 64 years at the time of anthropometric measurements and assessment of cognitive functions.  At the start of the study, participants should: have no history or current cognitive impairment, mental disorders (including addiction), chronic illnesses or bariatric surgery; not be taking regular medication that could affect their cognition; not be pregnant; not be on a weight-loss diet.  Individuals drawn from the general population.

Exposure

 Adults with obesity according to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m 2 ). Overweight individuals (25.0 ≤ BMI ≤ 29.9) were excluded.

Comparison group  Compared with adults with normal weight status (18.5 ≤ BMI ≤ 24.9).

Outcomes measures

 Studies assessing cognitive performance as an outcome using validated cognitive tests. Both self-reported and examiner-reported assessments of cognitive functions were included.

Study design and type of publication 

Longitudinal studies with a follow-up of at least two years after exposure, or metaanalyses of such studies.  Articles published in English or French after 1996, following the WHO's 1997 recommendation to use BMI for diagnosing obesity.  Grey literature, conference abstracts and narrative reviews were excluded.

Information sources, search strategy

The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH terms in PubMed) and freetext keywords covering the population, obesity exposure, global and domain-specific cognitive outcomes, and longitudinal study design. Full strategies for all databases are provided in the supplementary materials (Table S3). PubMed, PsycINFO, and Web of Science were searched through August 2024. Updates were conducted in January 2026 and October 2026 in PubMed only; these updates identified no additional eligible publication. Elicit was used as a supplementary AI-assisted search tool rather than as a substitute for database searching. Reference lists of included publications were also screened, and study authors were contacted when information required for eligibility or extraction was missing or unclear.

Selection process

All search results were imported into Rayyan, a platform designed for collaborative systematic literature reviews. After removing duplicates, three authors (CS, MCB and SC) screened the articles based on titles and abstracts. The full text of potentially eligible articles was then assessed to determine eligibility. When there was disagreement among the authors, consensus was reached through discussion and, where necessary, a fourth author was consulted.

Data extraction

Three authors (CS, MCB and SC) extracted data from each included study into a standardized data extraction table. In parallel, one of these authors (CS) used Elicit to extract the same data and compared it with the manually extracted data. The extracted information included: first author's name, year of publication, country of origin of the cohort, years of data collection, duration of followup, sample size, percentage of women, mean age or age range, mean BMI, cognitive tests used, cognitive functions assessed, and results (estimates of association) relevant to the research objectives. For the qualitative synthesis, we grouped the results according to the cognitive functions assessed (e.g., global cognitive functions, memory, or executive functions). Given the heterogeneity of the cognitive performance assessed, the variety of cognitive tests used in the included articles and the differences in the adjustment for confounding variables, we did not perform a meta-analysis, but instead provide a structured narrative synthesis of the results. The results were organized by cognitive domain, with additional attention to study design and risk of bias, to ensure a structured and systematic narrative synthesis.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using the ROBINS-E tool (Higgins et al., 2024) which evaluates seven domains, including confounding factors, exposure measurement, participant selection, postexposure interventions, missing data, outcome measurement, and the selection of reported outcomes.

Results

Study characteristics

A summary of our findings is presented in Table 1. Using our predefined search equations, we retrieved 5,348 articles (Figure ). After removing duplicates, 4,871 articles remained. During the initial screening based on titles and abstracts, 133 articles were selected for full-text evaluation. After full-text review, 129 articles were excluded for the following reasons: ineligible publication type (n=20), ineligible outcome (n=19), ineligible study design (n=49), ineligible population characteristics (n=35), and absence of a group with obesity at baseline (n=6). Finally, 4 articles were included in the qualitative analysis. A list of the articles selected for full-text screening is provided as supplementary material (Table S4).

The four studies published between 2009 and 2019 analyzed three cohorts from the general populations of the UK and the US, with sample sizes ranging from 2,083 to 6,401 adults. These studies used three cohorts: Sabia et al. (2009) (Sabia et al., 2009; Albanese et al., 2012; Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a; Hartanto et al., 2019). Most of the evidence came from a small number of cohorts, particularly Whitehall II. The anthropometric measurements used to calculate BMI followed the WHO categories (Sabia et al., 2009; Albanese et al., 2012; Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a; Hartanto et al., 2019). Hartanto et al. (2019) also examined waist-to-hip ratio (WHR); because this review focuses on obesity defined by BMI, the results below concern BMI unless otherwise stated (Hartanto et al., 2019). To categorize obesity, two studies used a combination of clinical and self-reported height and weight measurements (Sabia et al., 2009; Albanese et al., 2012), one study relied exclusively on selfreported data (Hartanto et al., 2019) and another used clinically measured weight and height (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a).

Cognitive performance was assessed twice in the study by Albanese et al. (2012) at 43 and 53 years; twice in the study by Hartanto et al. (2019), a first time at a mean age of 55 years, followed by a second time at a mean age of 64 years; once in the study by Sabia et al. (2009) at a mean age of 61 years; and three times in the study by Singh-Manoux et al. (2012), the first time at a mean age of 56 years, the second time at a mean age of 61 years and the last time between 3 and 7 years later (Sabia et al., 2009; Albanese et al., 2012; Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a; Hartanto et al., 2019). Throughout the results, reported ages indicate the timing of obesity exposure or cognitive assessment. The original models also included age as a covariate, but the publications did not consistently explain the rationale for this adjustment; it should therefore be interpreted as statistical control for age variation within the analytic sample rather than as a second exposure. The four studies reported effect sizes on different scales. For each estimate in the Results, we therefore state the unit and, when possible, give the approximate equivalent in standard-deviation (SD) units. One study assessed global cognitive performance using the 30-item Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) (Sabia et al., 2009). Sabia et al. standardized all cognitive outcomes, including the MMSE, to T-scores (mean 50, SD 10), so the reported mean differences are in T-score points rather than raw MMSE points (maximum score of 30), with 1 T-score point equivalent to 0.10 SD (e.g., -1.56 T-score points ≈ -0.16 SD) (Sabia et al., 2009). The executive function score was the mean of the reasoning, phonemic, and semantic fluency T-scores, rescaled to an SD of 10, and is expressed in the same unit (Sabia et al., 2009). On the other hand, Singh-Manoux et al. (2012) created a global cognitive score by averaging the z-scores (mean 0, SD 1) of the following four tests: the Alice Heim 4-I (to assess reasoning), the 20-word free recall test (to assess short-term verbal memory), the "S" words test (to assess phonemic fluency), and the animal words test (to assess semantic fluency) (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). In this study, mean differences are expressed in z-score units, the unit used in the original article (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). Albanese et al. (2012) standardized all cognitive outcomes (mean 0, SD 1) and reported regression coefficients (β) per 1-SD increase in BMI in the continuous analyses. β coefficients from the categorical analyses are reported as published; the article does not explicitly state their unit (Albanese et al., 2012). In the study by Hartanto et al. (2019), global cognitive function was modeled as a single latent factor indicated by the following cognitive tests: categorical fluency, number series, backward counting, stop and go switch task, backward digit span, immediate word list recall, and delayed word list recall (Hartanto et al., 2019). Longitudinal associations were reported as standardized path coefficients, i.e., the change in SD of the Time 2 outcome associated with a 1-SD change in the Time 1 predictor (Hartanto et al., 2019). SD equivalents are approximate, because the reference SD differs across cohorts and because group contrasts are not strictly comparable with estimates per 1-SD change in the exposure (Sabia et al., 2009; Albanese et al., 2012; Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a; Hartanto et al., 2019). All four studies assessed memory-related outcomes (Sabia et al., 2009; Albanese et al., 2012; Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a; Hartanto et al., 2019) including episodic memory, short-term verbal memory, or verbal memory. Albanese et al. (2012) additionally measured general verbal abilities, verbal fluency, attention, processing speed, and concentration (Albanese et al., 2012). Executive function was assessed in the studies by Hartanto et al. (2019 2009) aggregated them into an overall executive function score (Sabia et al., 2009; Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a).

Association between obesity and global cognitive performance

Three studies (Sabia et al., 2009; Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a; Hartanto et al., 2019) investigated the association between obesity in adulthood and global cognitive performance. However, two of them were based on the same (Whitehall II) cohort (Sabia et al., 2009; Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). Thus, much of the evidence came from a single cohort, which limits the independence of the available data. In the Whitehall II cohort, the authors selected different cognitive assessment waves for their analyses, with Sabia et al. (2009) using cognition measured at a mean age of 61 years and Singh-Manoux et al. (2012a) using repeated assessments at mean ages of 56 and 61 years, as well as an additional follow-up 3 to 7 years later (Sabia et al., 2009; Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). Sabia et al. (2009) reported that adults with obesity at age 25 years (self-reported weight) or at a mean age of 44 years did not differ significantly from adults of normal weight on global cognition (MMSE) measured at a mean age of 61 years (Sabia et al., 2009). The mean differences were -1.17 T-score points (95% CI: -3.33 to 0.99; ≈ -0.12 SD) for obesity at age 25 and -0.98 T-score points (95% CI: -2.25 to 0.30; ≈ -0.10 SD) for obesity at a mean age of 44 years (Sabia et al., 2009). Models were adjusted for age at the cognitive assessment (mean age 61 years; used here as a continuous covariate to account for within-assessment age variation), sex, and education (Sabia et al., 2009). In contrast, in the cross-sectional analysis, adults with obesity at a mean age of 61 years had significantly lower MMSE scores than adults of normal weight, with a mean difference of -0.99 T-score points (95% CI: -1.78 to -0.21; ≈ -0.10 SD; p < 0.05) (Sabia et al., 2009). The authors also examined cumulative obesity (obesity on two or three of the three assessments, at age 25 and at mean ages of 44 and 61 years). After adjustment for age at the cognitive assessment, sex, education, and health behaviors (alcohol intake, fruit and vegetable consumption, physical activity, and smoking status) and health measures (cholesterol, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, diabetes, coronary heart disease, and stroke) at the mean age of 44 years, cumulative obesity was associated with lower MMSE scores at a mean age of 61 years than never having obesity: mean difference of -1.56 T-score points (95% CI: -2.84 to -0.28; ≈ -0.16 SD; p < 0.05) (Sabia et al., 2009). In the same cohort, Singh-Manoux et al. (2012) classified adults as metabolically unhealthy when they had two or more of four components: high triglycerides or lipid-lowering medication; high blood pressure or antihypertensive medication; high fasting glucose or diabetes medication; and low HDL cholesterol (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). The authors reported that adults with obesity at a mean age of 49 years had lower global cognition scores at a mean age of 56 years than their normal-weight counterparts, with a mean difference of -0.12 z-score units (95% CI: -0.20, -0.04) (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). These results were adjusted for within-assessment age, sex, and education. When they separated metabolically healthy and unhealthy adults, adults with obesity (at a mean age of 49 years) in both metabolic states had lower global cognition scores (at a mean age of 56 years) than metabolically healthy adults of normal weight, with mean differences of -0.16 z-score units (95% CI: -0.28, -0.04) and -0.14 z-score units (95% CI: -0.24, -0.04), respectively (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). These results were adjusted for within-assessment age, sex, and education (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). The authors found no significant difference in global cognition between metabolically healthy and unhealthy adults with obesity (p = 0.82) (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). Examining cognitive performance over 10 years, global cognition declined by -0.40 z-score units (95% CI: -0.42, -0.38) in metabolically healthy adults of normal weight, -0.42 z-score units (95% CI: -0.50, -0.34) in metabolically healthy adults with obesity, and -0.49 z-score units (95% CI: -0.55, -0.42) in metabolically unhealthy adults with obesity (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). Among metabolically unhealthy adults, the decline increased across BMI categories (p for trend = 0.03) (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). Within the MIDUS cohort, Hartanto et al. (2019) analyzed bidirectional longitudinal relationships between obesity at Time 1 (mean age of 55 years) and global cognitive performance at Time 2 (mean age of 64 years), approximately 9 years later (Hartanto et al., 2019). The authors found no bidirectional longitudinal relationships between BMI and global cognitive performance after controlling for age at Time 1, gender, education and household income, smoking history, alcohol problems, hypertension, diabetes and stroke (p > 0.05). Associations were reported as standardized path coefficients, i.e., the change in SD of the Time 2 variable per 1-SD higher Time 1 variable (Hartanto et al., 2019). Overall, the available studies provided limited and heterogeneous evidence.

Associations between obesity and poorer performance in specific cognitive domains

Executive functions

In the Whitehall II cohort, Sabia et al. (2009) found that adults with obesity at a mean age of 44 years and at a mean age of 61 years had significantly lower executive function scores (derived from tests of reasoning, phonemic fluency, and semantic fluency) at a mean age of 61 years than adults of normal weight (Sabia et al., 2009). The mean differences were -1.45 T-score points (95% CI: -2.55 to -0.34; ≈ -0.15 SD; p < 0.05) for obesity at a mean age of 44 years and -0.80 T-score points (95% CI: -1.49 to -0.12; ≈ -0.08 SD; p < 0.05) for obesity at a mean age of 61 years, after adjustment for within-assessment age, sex, and education (Sabia et al., 2009). No significant difference was found when obesity was measured at age 25 years (0.47 T-score points; 95% CI: -1.46 to 2.39; ≈ 0.05 SD) (Sabia et al., 2009). Cumulative obesity was associated with lower executive function at a mean age of 61 years than never having obesity (Sabia et al., 2009). The mean difference was -1.33 T-score points (95% CI: -2.43 to -0.23; ≈ -0.13 SD; p < 0.05) when adjusting for within-assessment age, sex, education, and health behaviors (alcohol intake, fruit and vegetable consumption, physical activity, and smoking status), and -1.16 T-score points (95% CI: -2.28 to -0.04; ≈ -0.12 SD; p < 0.05) when adjusting for within-assessment age, sex, education, and health measures (cholesterol, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, diabetes, coronary heart disease, and stroke) instead (Sabia et al., 2009). When all these covariates were included simultaneously, the association was no longer significant (-1.09 T-score points; 95% CI: -2.20 to 0.02; ≈ -0.11 SD) (Sabia et al., 2009). In the MIDUS cohort, Hartanto et al. (2019) found no association between obesity at a mean age of 55 years and changes in executive performance at a mean age of 64 years (Hartanto et al., 2019). In the Whitehall II cohort, Singh-Manoux et al. (2012) reported that adults with obesity at a mean age of 49 years had lower reasoning scores at a mean age of 56 years than their normal-weight counterparts, with a mean difference of -0.10 zscore units (95% CI: -0.18, -0.02) (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). These results were adjusted for within-assessment age, sex, and education (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). For verbal fluency, the estimate was smaller and its confidence interval reached zero (-0.08 z-score units; 95% CI: -0.17, 0.00), adjusted for the same variables (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). The authors found that adults with obesity and an unhealthy metabolic profile at baseline (mean age of 49 years) had significantly lower reasoning scores at a mean age of 56 years than metabolically healthy adults of normal weight, with a mean difference of -0.14 z-score units (95% CI: -0.23, -0.04), whereas the estimate for metabolically healthy adults with obesity was -0.11 z-score units (95% CI: -0.23, 0.01) (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). For verbal fluency, neither group with obesity differed significantly from metabolically healthy adults of normal weight (-0.12 z-score units; 95% CI: -0.24, 0.01 for metabolically healthy adults and -0.11 z-score units; 95% CI: -0.21, 0.00 for metabolically unhealthy adults) (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). The authors did not observe any difference between metabolic states among adults with obesity for reasoning (p = 0.71) or verbal fluency (p = 0.90) (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). These results were also adjusted for within-assessment age, sex, and education (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). In the 1946 British birth cohort, Albanese et al. (2012) observed lower verbal fluency scores at 53 years in women with obesity at age 20 (β = -0.50; 95% CI: -0.99, -0.05), age 26 (β = -0.65; 95% CI: -1.08, -0.23), and age 36 (β = -0.40; 95% CI: -0.67, -0.13) compared to normal weight women (Albanese et al., 2012). These β coefficients are reported as published; the article does not explicitly state their unit or the covariates included in this categorical analysis. These findings concern obesity exposure in young adulthood and a cognitive outcome measured in middle adulthood, not cognitive performance assessed in young adulthood. Overall, the findings were derived from heterogeneous tasks and overlapping cohorts.

Memory and other cognitive domains

In the 1946 British birth cohort, Albanese et al. (2012) found that women with obesity at age 53 years had lower memory scores at the same age than women of normal weight in unadjusted analyses (β = -0.30), but this difference was no longer significant after adjusting for BMI at age 43 years (p = 0.46) (Albanese et al., 2012). In the Whitehall II cohort, Sabia et al. (2009) reported that adults with obesity at age 25 years or at a mean age of 44 years did not differ significantly from adults of normal weight in memory scores at a mean age of 61 years (Sabia et al., 2009). The mean differences were 0.56 T-score points (95% CI: -1.53 to 2.65; ≈ 0.06 SD) for obesity at age 25 and -1.11 T-score points (95% CI: -2.31 to 0.10; ≈ -0.11 SD) for obesity at a mean age of 44 years (Sabia et al., 2009). In the cross-sectional analysis, adults with obesity at a mean age of 61 years had significantly lower memory scores than adults of normal weight (-0.82 T-score points; 95% CI: -1.57 to -0.08; ≈ -0.08 SD; p < 0.05) (Sabia et al., 2009). These results were adjusted for withinassessment age, sex, and education (Sabia et al., 2009). Cumulative obesity was also associated with lower memory scores at a mean age of 61 years than never having obesity (Sabia et al., 2009). The mean difference was -1.22 T-score points (95% CI: -2.42 to -0.02; ≈ -0.12 SD; p < 0.05) when adjusting for within-assessment age, sex, education, and health behaviors (alcohol intake, fruit and vegetable consumption, physical activity, and smoking status), and -1.23 T-score points (95% CI: -2.43 to -0.02; ≈ -0.12 SD; p < 0.05) when adjusting for within-assessment age, sex, education, and health measures (cholesterol, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, diabetes, coronary heart disease, and stroke) instead (Sabia et al., 2009). When all these covariates were included simultaneously, the association was no longer significant (-1.16 T-score points; 95% CI: -2.37 to 0.05; ≈ -0.12 SD) (Sabia et al., 2009). In the same cohort, Singh-Manoux et al. (2012) studied the association between obesity at baseline (mean age of 49 years) and memory alteration (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). These authors found that adults with obesity at an average age of 49 years (at baseline) and a metabolically healthy status had lower memory scores at a mean age of 56 years than their counterparts of normal weight, with a difference in mean of -0.20 z-score units (95% CI: -0.32, -0.07) (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). However, no difference was observed in the metabolically unhealthy group. The authors also reported that adults with obesity at a mean age of 49 years had lower memory scores at a mean age of 56 years compared to their normal weight counterparts, with mean difference scores of -0.13 z-score units (95% CI: -0.22, -0.05) (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). These results were adjusted for within-assessment age, sex, and education (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). No differences in cognitive decline related to memory over 10 years were observed between groups based on weight and metabolic status (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). Hartanto et al. (2019) found in the MIDUS cohort that BMI measured at Time 1 (mean age of 55 years) was not significantly associated with episodic memory at Time 2 (mean age of 64 years) (β = 0.005, SE = 0.019; 95% CI: -0.026, 0.035; p = 0.805) (Hartanto et al., 2019). These results were adjusted for age at Time 1, gender, education, household income, and health-related covariates (smoking history, alcohol problems, hypertension, diabetes and stroke) (Hartanto et al., 2019). However, a higher WHR at Time 1 predicted a decline in episodic memory over 9 years after adjustment for demographic variables, socioeconomic status and health-related covariates (Hartanto et al., 2019). In the 1946 British birth cohort, after adjusting for BMI at age 43 years and childhood cognitive ability, women with obesity at age 53 years did not display significant associations with verbal ability, also called "cognitive reserve" (National Adult Reading Test), at the same age (Albanese et al., 2012). Considered together, the effect estimates for global cognition, executive function, and memory in the studies reporting T-score or z-score units (Sabia et al., 2009; Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a) or standardized path coefficients (Hartanto et al., 2019) were generally modest. The coefficients from the exploratory categorical analyses of verbal fluency in Albanese et al. (2012) could not be compared in magnitude, as their unit is not explicitly stated and their confidence intervals were wide (Albanese et al., 2012).

Risk of bias assessment

We assessed the risk of bias in the included studies using the modified ROBINS-E (Higgins et al., 2024). The overall risk-of-bias judgments and detailed risk assessments for each study are presented in Table 2. Among the four studies analyzed, three raised some concerns regarding bias (Sabia et al., 2009; Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a; Hartanto et al., 2019), while the other one was classified as having a high risk of bias (Albanese et al., 2012). Domain 1 (confounding) was of particular concern, as confounding factors are especially influential in this type of assessment. In addition, none of the studies explicitly stated whether cognitive test results were assessed by blinded investigators. Furthermore, some studies relied on self-reported weight and height data (Hartanto et al., 2019) or a combination of self-reported and clinically measured data (Sabia et al., 2009; Albanese et al., 2012), which may introduce inaccuracies due to reporting bias. The overall rating for Sabia et al. (2009) was changed from high risk to some concerns, mainly after reassessment of Domain 5 (missing data) (Sabia et al., 2009).

Discussion

Overall, the available prospective evidence does not support a clear, clinically meaningful independent association between obesity in young or middle adulthood and cognitive performance before age 65.

Obesity and global cognition

Across the three studies examining global cognition, the association with obesity appeared specific to obesity assessed in middle, rather than young, adulthood, and was not replicated in the MIDUS cohort (Sabia et al., 2009; Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a; Hartanto et al., 2019). This inconsistency, occurring even across cohorts with broadly similar designs, suggests that any true association is not robust across measurement contexts. We might have expected poorer performance in global cognition in adults with obesity who also had unhealthy metabolic parameters, but the association did not change with metabolic status, suggesting a more complex interplay between metabolic status and cognition (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). This separation of different metabolic states takes into account the fact that people with obesity do not always have an unfavorable metabolic profile (Karelis et al., 2004; Wildman et al., 2008). Singh-Manoux et al. (2012a) also found poorer overall cognitive performance over 10 years in metabolically unhealthy adults with obesity than in normal weight individuals. In addition, in the study by Sabia et al. (2009), cumulative obesity in young and middle-aged adults was significantly associated with poorer global cognitive performance in middle-aged adults (Sabia et al., 2009). However, another systematic review did not find any association between obesity and cognitive impairment (Prickett et al., 2015). These inconsistencies highlight the complexity of the relationship between obesity and cognitive outcomes and suggest that factors such as metabolic health, duration of obesity, and vulnerabilities in specific cognitive domains might play a critical role in cognitive decline. At the same time, the current evidence does not allow firm conclusions that obesity itself meaningfully predicts clinically defined cognitive impairment in non-elderly adults. Other studies report poorer cognition in later midlife among adults with obesity (Elias et al., 2005; Cournot et al., 2006; Wolf et al., 2007), whereas obesity in older adults has been associated with a possible protective effect (Kuo et al., 2006; Fitzpatrick et al., 2009), which suggests that associations depend on age at assessment. No prospective studies have examined global cognition in young adults (between 18 and 45 years of age) with obesity. While much of the existing literature focuses on older populations, where cognitive decline is more commonly observed, young adults are increasingly affected by rising obesity rates. The inconsistent pattern may reflect differences in exposure measurement, cognitive tests, confounding control, sample composition, or risk of bias; it is also compatible with the absence of a meaningful independent association. Evidence directly concerning cognitive outcomes in young adulthood was absent, because obesity was sometimes measured at younger ages but cognition was assessed primarily in middle adulthood.

Obesity and specific cognitive functions

Executive functions

Evidence for executive functioning was sparse and inconsistent. Lower executive performance in midlife was reported in adults with obesity assessed in middle adulthood or cumulatively over the life course, but this association was no longer significant after joint adjustment for health behaviors and health measures, which suggests that it may partly reflect shared cardiometabolic and behavioral factors (Sabia et al., 2009). Findings for reasoning and verbal fluency, which rely on executive processes, were not consistent across studies or metabolic subgroups (Albanese et al., 2012; Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). In the MIDUS cohort, obesity did not predict later executive performance, whereas better executive performance predicted a lower WHR, which points to a possible reverse direction of association (Hartanto et al., 2019). The agespecific associations with WHR in that study were exploratory, and effect sizes were small, so these results should be interpreted with caution (Hartanto et al., 2019). A systematic review by Favieri et al. (2019) highlighted the relationship between obesity and executive functions (Favieri et al., 2019). However, that review included studies across a wide age range, from childhood to adolescence, and did not identify any specific executive functions (Favieri et al., 2019). Understanding which executive functions are particularly affected is crucial, as impairments-in inhibition, mental flexibility and decision-making-may contribute to poor food choices and overeating, ultimately leading to weight gain. These results highlight the potential bidirectional relationship between obesity and cognition, a relationship that has also been shown between working memory and adiposity (Shields et al., 2021). Furthermore, impairment of executive functions may be an early indicator of Alzheimer's disease (Chen et al., 2000). Some studies also show that executive function retraining improves obesity outcomes in people with poorer performance in executive functions (Cortese et al., 2013). There is also evidence that weight loss is associated with small improvements in executive functions and memory in adults with obesity (Siervo et al., 2011). Furthermore, bariatric surgery has been associated with better performance or less decline in memory, executive functions and cognitive control (Gunstad et al., 2011; Handley et al., 2016; Li et al., 2022). However, at least one other study did not find such improvements (Custers et al., 2023). The available evidence is therefore insufficient to identify a specific executive domain that is independently associated with obesity. Future studies should use repeated, domain-specific measures and explicitly test bidirectional associations, because executive performance may influence health behavior and adiposity as well as potentially being associated with them.

Memory and other cognitive functions

Research into the association between obesity and memory has yielded mixed findings. While some studies suggest that the relationship depends on age, metabolic health and lifestyle factors, others report conflicting or null results. Associations with memory appeared for obesity assessed in later midlife but not in young adulthood (Sabia et al., 2009; Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a), and were not found in two other cohorts (Albanese et al., 2012; Hartanto et al., 2019). The association with cumulative obesity was no longer significant after joint adjustment for health behaviors and health measures (Sabia et al., 2009), and memory decline over 10 years did not differ by weight or metabolic status (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a), which suggests that obesity may not be independently related to memory in this age range. By contrast, a higher WHR predicted a decline in episodic memory in the MIDUS cohort (Hartanto et al., 2019), consistent with other studies (Rothman, 2008; Hartanto & Yong, 2018). This suggests that fat distribution may play a more critical role in memory than BMI alone, but this conclusion relies on a very limited evidence base of longitudinal studies. These findings are somewhat similar to the cross-sectional results of a study of verbal memory in young adults, which found poorer performance in those with obesity (Gunstad et al., 2006). A systematic review also indicated that the cognitive profile of adults with obesity was not highly specific, with the most consistent findings pertaining to memory (Van den Berg et al., 2009). The scarcity of longitudinal data on memory in young to middle-aged adults with obesity highlights the need for further research in non-elderly populations, particularly given that memory impairment may signal the onset of broader cognitive decline, including dementia and Alzheimer's disease (Chen et al., 2000). Taken together, the longitudinal evidence does not establish an independent association between obesity and poorer memory performance before age 65.

Limitations of the included studies and the overall review

It is important to critically examine the limitations of the included studies and the overall review, including potential biases, methodological limitations, and concerns about the generalizability of the findings. First, the restrictive nature of our inclusion criteria resulted in cohorts exclusively from the United Kingdom and the United States. In addition, the Whitehall II cohort-used in Sabia et al. (2009) and Singh-Manoux et al. (2012)-consists primarily of civil servants working in government offices, making the sample not fully representative of the UK population (Sabia et al., 2009; Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). Moreover, we focused primarily on the influence of obesity on cognitive performance, but the relationship may be bidirectional (Shields et al., 2021). Furthermore, publication bias must be considered, as studies with null or negative findings are less likely to be published (Stanley, 2005). The heterogeneity of the methods used in the included studies must be taken into account when interpreting the results. For example, the included studies calculated BMI using clinically measured weight and height (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a), self-reported data (Hartanto et al., 2019), or a combination of both (Sabia et al., 2009; Albanese et al., 2012). However, while self-reported height and weight have been shown to be reliable approximations of actual measurements (Jeffery, 1996; Lassale et al., 2013), it remains impossible to determine whether adults with obesity tend to underestimate their weight (Boström & Diderichsen, 1997). As BMI is a widely used measure in studies, we chose to make it a restrictive inclusion criterion. However, it is important to consider the limitations of this measure. This measure does not distinguish between adipose tissue and lean body mass, leading to an underestimation of the population with obesity (Romero-Corral et al., 2008). Importantly, BMI is a simple and widely used adiposity indicator, but it may not fully capture the obesity-related features most relevant to cognition, such as central fat distribution and metabolic heterogeneity; this is consistent with evidence showing that WHR, rather than BMI, can better predict certain cognitive outcomes (Deckers et al., 2017; Hartanto & Yong, 2018; Hartanto et al., 2019). The observed associations cannot be interpreted as evidence that BMI itself affects cognition, as the relationship is more plausibly explained by metabolic and vascular factors, including central adiposity, chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and cardiometabolic disease. Indeed, the use of BMI or waist circumference to define obesity may result in different strengths of association with cognitive domains (Deckers et al., 2017). For example, the WHR in the Hartanto et al. (2019) study predicted episodic memory performance 10 years later, in contrast to BMI (Hartanto et al., 2019). In addition, Hartanto et al. (2019) modeled BMI and WHR as continuous variables to examine the association between obesity and cognitive performance (Hartanto et al., 2019). Furthermore, as obesity thresholds for Asian populations are considered to vary, but there is no consensus, we excluded any definition with a threshold below 30 when BMI was used as a categorical variable (Pacific, 2000; WHO Expert Consultation, 2004; Pan & Yeh, 2008). When effect magnitude is considered alongside risk of bias, the pattern that emerges further supports caution in interpreting the obesitycognition association. Across the three studies with lower risk of bias (Sabia et al., 2009; Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a; Hartanto et al., 2019), effect estimates were consistently modest or null. In contrast, the associations between obesity and verbal fluency reported by Albanese et al. (2012) originated from the study judged at high risk of bias, were derived from an exploratory categorical analysis of BMI, carried wide confidence intervals, and were expressed in a unit that is not explicitly stated in the original article, which prevents a direct comparison of their magnitude with the other studies (Albanese et al., 2012). This suggests that the most pronounced apparent signal in the available evidence is also the least methodologically robust one, whereas the studies at lower risk of bias point toward associations of limited magnitude. This pattern is consistent with the possibility that statistically significant findings in this literature reflect modest or biased effects rather than a clinically meaningful independent association.

There are also differences in the tools used to assess cognitive performance. To assess global cognitive performance, the three articles used different tests (Sabia et al., 2009; Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a; Hartanto et al., 2019). In the Whitehall II cohort, Sabia et al. (2009) evaluated overall cognitive performance using the MMSE (Sabia et al., 2009). It is possible that smaller differences between adults with obesity and normal weight adults were not detected, as the MMSE is known to be poor at detecting mild cognitive impairment (Tombaugh & McIntyre, 1992; McDowell et al., 1997). In addition, age-independent fixed thresholds may indeed underestimate poorer cognitive performance in younger adults (Tombaugh & McIntyre, 1992). To overcome MMSE detection sensitivity problems, Singh-Manoux et al. (2012) and Hartanto et al. (2019) used a combination of cognitive tests (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a; Hartanto et al., 2019). In addition, the included studies evaluated memory and executive performance using different methods and tests, making crossstudy comparisons difficult and potentially affecting the consistency of observed results. It is important to consider that it is difficult to dissociate specific cognitive domains, as most cognitive tests assess multiple functions simultaneously. Establishing an independent association between obesity and cognitive functions is challenging. Potential confounders associated with both obesity and cognitive functions include alcohol and tobacco use (Kalmijn et al., 2002), type 2 diabetes (Stewart & Liolitsa, 1999), hypertension or hypotension (Qiu et al., 2005), age (Gustafson, 2006), educational level (Hackman et al., 2010 ), depression (McDermott & Ebmeier, 2009), cardiovascular disease (Harrison et al., 2014), and stroke (Knopman et al., 2009). In the included studies, some key variables, such as medication use that may affect cognition, were not accounted for. As two of the included studies did not assess cognitive performance at baseline, it is difficult to establish a clear temporal relationship between obesity and cognitive functions (Sabia et al., 2009; Singh-Manoux et al., 2012a). Moreover, among the four included studies, only one assessed cognitive function in young adults, and no differences were found. The studies included had no conflicts of interest, and the funder was not involved in the study.

Strengths of the review

Despite the aforementioned constraints, this review also has some notable strengths that deserve to be highlighted. Prior to conducting this review, we performed a systematic literature search in the PROSPERO, PubMed and Cochrane Library databases, which allowed us to identify five systematic reviews on the same topic. However, these reviews differed in the age range of the populations included, ranging from no restrictions (Van den Berg et al., 2009; Hendrikse et al., 2015) to specific age ranges, such as 5 to 70 years (Favieri et al., 2019) or 18 to 65 years (Fitzpatrick et al., 2013; Prickett et al., 2015). Instead, in our review, we included articles that focused only on adult women and men aged between 18 and 64 at the time of anthropometric measurements and assessment of cognitive functions. This age range was chosen because development and maturation of the prefrontal cortex is complete by the age of 25 (Arain et al., 2013). The prefrontal cortex is a critical region for cognitive functions, particularly executive functions (Arain et al., 2013). Furthermore, cognitive decline begins as early as 45-49 years of age (Singh-Manoux et al., 2012b). It is important to consider that early detection of cognitive changes in this age group could serve as a basis for preventive measures.

In addition, we chose to include only longitudinal studies in our review to establish a temporal relationship between exposure and outcomes, whereas other reviews included different study designs, ranging from no design limitations (Fitzpatrick et al., 2013; Prickett et al., 2015) to any empirical studies (Hendrikse et al., 2015), population-based studies, case-control designs (Van den Berg et al., 2009), or pooled longitudinal and cross-sectional studies (Favieri et al., 2019). We also required a minimum follow-up period of two years to minimize the risk of missing the effects of interest, with the included studies having longer follow-up periods ranging from 7 to 45 years. In addition, we specifically focused on obesity (BMI ≥ 30) rather than overweight (25 ≤ BMI < 30) to reduce heterogeneity and ensure that we captured more severe cases of excess adiposity. By following the PRISMA guidelines, we have further improved the transparency and reproducibility of our methods. The review's principal contribution is therefore not to confirm an obesity-cognition association, but to demonstrate how little high-quality prospective evidence currently supports such a conclusion in young and middle-aged adults.

Conclusion

This systematic review highlights that although several studies have found poorer cognitive performance in middle-aged adults with obesity, an independent association remains inconclusive, particularly in young to middle-aged adults. Given that one of the four included studies was judged at high risk of bias and that findings were inconsistent across cohorts, the current evidence remains too weak to support a robust independent association between obesity and poorer cognitive performance, and the possibility that no true link exists cannot be excluded. As obesity rates increase at earlier ages, it is crucial to determine whether cognitive functions are impaired and whether such impairment occurs before the onset of typical age-related cognitive decline. Further research is needed to identify which cognitive domains are most vulnerable to obesity over time, particularly in light of emerging evidence that obesity-related neurodegeneration may mirror processes observed in Alzheimer's disease. Identifying these high-risk domains may allow the development of targeted interventions-such as dietary changes, physical activity or bariatric surgery-to preserve or improve cognition in adults with obesity. Ultimately, this may help prevent cognitive impairment from preventing individuals with obesity from adopting and maintaining healthy behaviors, while potentially reducing the risk of dementia in later life. Future research should identify which cognitive domains are particularly vulnerable to obesity in young adults, helping to guide early, targeted interventions. To achieve this, prospective studies should incorporate relevant control variables such as age, education, and depression, use appropriate control groups, and consider both BMI and WHR. Nevertheless, these design improvements are necessary before implications for prevention or intervention can be justified.
