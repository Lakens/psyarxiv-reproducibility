How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Evidence on the associations between social media use (SMU) and youth affect remains inconsistent, partly because it remains unclear how active versus passive engagement differs in daily life, whether these relationships are bidirectional, and how individual characteristics such as gender and mental health symptoms moderate them. We utilized ecological momentary assessment (EMA) via a smartphone app to sample real-world affect and SMU (active [posting/messaging] vs. passive [browsing]) among 80 young people (Mean age=20.1 years) with varying depression and anhedonia, yielding 2,316 observations across six consecutive days. Multilevel regressions revealed that, concurrently, SMU was associated with lower positive affect (PA; β=-0.35, p<.001) and higher negative affect (NA; β=0.22, p=.002). Crucially, this negative affect association was weaker among individuals with higher depression (β=-0.02, p=.04) but stronger among those with higher anhedonia (β=0.04, p=.02). Conversely, active (vs. passive) use was concurrently linked to higher PA (β=4.12, p<.001) and lower NA (β=-2.01, p=.05), with these favourable associations stronger among males than females and among individuals with lower depression symptoms (β=0.06, p=.03). In time-lagged analyses, higher PA predicted a lower subsequent likelihood of active versus passive use (β=-1.23, p=.01), while SMU itself did not predict subsequent affect. These findings emphasize that while general SMU coincides with less favourable affect, active engagement might offer situational benefits over passive browsing. Crucially, these dynamic relationships depend heavily on gender and distinct psychological vulnerabilities, demonstrating the necessity of distinguishing depression from anhedonia in digital mental health research.

1. Introduction

>Introduction

1.1. Prevalence and Impact of Social Media Use Among Youth

Social media use (SMU) has become an integral daily-life component for most adolescents and young adults, who use it for establishing and maintaining social connections, information-seeking, inspiration, and entertainment (van der Wal et al., 2024). Recent findings suggest that 32% of adolescents meet the criteria of intensive or problematic SMU (World Health Organization, 2024). Additionally, population-level investigation has highlighted that more than half of adolescents, who use at least one social media platform, report an average usage of more than four hours per day (McAlister et al., 2024). Given the widespread adoption and intensive engagement with social media among young people, considerable research has sought to understand its potential effects on mental health and wellbeing, but the findings are inconsistent.

Evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicates that problematic SMU is associated with a range of adverse mental health outcomes, including depression, anxiety, stress, and loneliness (Huang, 2022; Shannon et al., 2022; Ulvi et al., 2022). Conversely other studies find that social media use may confer benefits, including facilitating social connectedness, enabling authentic self-expression, and reducing stress (Grieve & Watkinson, 2016; Karim et al., 2020; Reinecke & Trepte, 2014; Ulvi et al., 2022), and other studies report no relationships between social media use and mental health outcomes (Berryman et al., 2018; Coyne et al., 2020).

1.2. Active vs. Passive Social Media Use

One reason for inconsistencies across studies could be that the relationship between SMU and mental health depends on how SMU is being used. Recent research has categorised social media use into active (e.g., messaging, posting, commenting) and passive (e.g., browsing, scrolling) forms, with passive use hypothesised to be more detrimental to mental health than active use (Valkenburg, van Driel, et al., 2021). Consistent with this view, some studies have found that greater passive use is associated with higher levels of depression and anxiety in both retrospective (Thorisdottir et al., 2019) and real-time assessments (Nereim et al., 2020). However, other studies have failed to replicate these findings (Smout et al., 2026; Valkenburg, Beyens, et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2020). Consequently, it remains unclear whether active forms of social media use, such as messaging and posting, are more beneficial for mental health and wellbeing than passive activities, such as scrolling and browsing.

1.3. Individual differences and moderating factors

Individual differences in internalising symptoms, such as depression and anxiety may also confer susceptibility to the negative effects of social media use (Beyens et al., 2020). For example, some studies find adolescents with depression and anxiety, spend more time on social media but report lower satisfaction with their online friendships than those without such symptoms (Fassi et al., 2025). In contrast, adolescents with higher levels of well-being are more likely to engage actively on social media, for example through group chats and keeping up with updates, supporting the hypothesis that active forms of social media use may be associated with more favourable mental health outcomes (Reiten Finserås et al., 2025).

Gender may also moderate these associations, as studies show social media use is associated with lower well-being and higher levels of depression symptoms in females than in males (Kelly et al., 2018; Twenge & Martin, 2020). Together, these findings suggest that the relationship between social media use and wellbeing may vary according to individual characteristics. However, most studies to date have not yet examined how individual characteristics influence the outcomes of social media use, therefore further research is needed to determine how factors such as gender and mental health status moderate the associations between social media use and affective wellbeing in daily life.

1.4. Capturing Momentary Affective Dynamics in real-life

Momentary affective states, including positive affect (PA) and negative affect (NA), may be particularly sensitive to the effects of social media use (Dienlin & Johannes, 2020). For example, studies that assessed the experimental effect of social media use upon affective states in college students found that PA decreased by approximately 20% following social media use (Lepp & Barkley, 2022), and depressive symptoms were associated with students feeling worse (increased NA or decreased PA) (Tuck et al., 2025). However, findings remain inconsistent. Das-Friebel et al. (2020) found that social media use before sleep did not predict next-day affect, whereas Reinecke and Trepte (2014) reported that authentic selfrepresentation on social media longitudinally predicted increases in PA and decreases in NA.

The inconsistencies in these findings might be attributed to different study designs.

Experimental studies establish causality but rely on researcher-imposed exposure that might not fully reflect real-life situations, while cross-sectional and conventional longitudinal designs typically rely on retrospective self-reports or widely spaced assessments (e.g., weeks or months), which are less sensitive to momentary changes and more susceptible to recall bias.

These limitations can be addressed by Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA) via repeatedly sampling participants' experiences and behaviour within their natural environment multiple times a day for several days usually via a smartphone device, enabling the capture of moment-to-moment temporal dynamics while reducing recall bias and enhancing ecological validity (Shiffman et al., 2008). EMA studies have begun to reveal complex bidirectional associations between social media use and affect. For example, negative perceptions of social media chatting predicted subsequent increases in negative affect (NA), while higher NA also predicted more negative perceptions of chatting, although this reverse association was weaker (Brimmel et al., 2025). Similarly, sustained NA has been linked to negative social media interactions compared with in-person interactions (Hamilton et al., 2021). Findings for positive affect (PA), however, remain inconsistent. Brimmel et al. (2025) reported that positive perceptions of viewing social media predicted subsequent increases in PA, whereas higher PA also predicted more positive perceptions of chatting. In contrast, Hamilton et al. (2021) found that even positive social media interactions were associated with lower sustained PA than comparable in-person interactions. Furthermore, Politte-Corn et al. (2024) found that social media use predicted both immediate and subsequent decreases in PA among adolescents with and without depression, while also predicting subsequent decreases in NA among adolescents with depression. Collectively, these findings highlight the value of EMA for investigating the dynamic relationship between social media use and affect in daily life. Nevertheless, evidence remains limited regarding how specific patterns of social media use, particularly active versus passive use, relate to momentary affect and whether these associations differ according to individual characteristics, such as gender and depression.

1.5. Distinguishing the Roles of Depression and Anhedonia

Although individual characteristics may explain some of the heterogeneity in findings, it remains unclear how mental health symptoms and gender moderate the relationship between social media use (SMU) and affect. Depression is among the most extensively studied mental health conditions in this context and is often operationalised primarily in terms of low mood, one of the two core symptoms of Major Depressive Disorder (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). However, considerably less attention has been paid to the other core symptom, anhedonia. Characterised by diminished interest and pleasure, anhedonia is closely linked to positive affect and reward processing (Kaya & McCabe, 2019; Ma et al., 2026; McCabe, 2018; Rizvi et al., 2016), and may therefore influence affective experiences during social media use by altering the extent to which its rewarding aspects are experienced. EMA studies suggest that individuals with higher anhedonia engage more passively with social media (Zhan & Wang, 2026) and anticipate less pleasure from checking social media, although their experienced pleasure during use may not differ (Dreier et al., 2026). Depression and anhedonia may therefore be associated with distinct patterns of affect during social media use. For example, people with higher depression symptoms may already experience elevated negative affect, potentially limiting the additional increase associated with social media use. In contrast, people with higher anhedonia may experience less reward from social media engagement, potentially contributing to less favourable affective experiences. These possibilities remain tentative, and to our knowledge no study has examined whether depression and anhedonia moderate the momentary associations between social media use and affect in daily life.

1.6. The present study

Therefore, the present study used ecological momentary assessment (EMA) to examine concurrent and time-lagged associations between momentary positive and negative affect and social media use in young people. We distinguished social media use from non-use and, among assessments involving social media use, active engagement (posting or messaging) from passive engagement (browsing). We also examined whether these associations varied according to gender, depression symptoms and anhedonia. By considering depression and anhedonia separately, the study aimed to clarify whether the affective correlates of social media use differ according to engagement type and individual psychological characteristics.

Finally, we examined whether affect predicted subsequent social media engagement and whether social media use predicted subsequent affect.

2. Method

>Method

2.1. Power analysis

We conducted a priori power analysis using PowerAnalysisIL, designed for EMA (Lafit et al., 2021) using data provided by Li et al. (2019) (supplementary Table S1, Figure S1). Analysis suggested that 80% power could be achieved with a sample size of 50, consistent with estimates from multilevel logistic (Eeske Van Roekel et al., 2019) and linear models (Eisele et al., 2022) using our design.

2.2. Participants

Young people (N=95), aged 16-25 years with varying depression symptoms, were recruited from local schools and universities as part of a larger EMA study examining predictors of leisure and social activities (Sahni & McCabe, 2025). We excluded participants who completed <30% of assessments (N =15) (Edwards et al., 2016; Oorschot et al., 2013), resulting in a final N = 80 with mean compliance rate of 69%. The study adhered to the Revised Helsinki declaration in 2008 and was approved by the University of Reading Psychology Department Ethics Committee (REC no: 2021-120-CM). All participants provided written informed consent after reviewing the information sheets. To preserve participant privacy, no identifiable features were included in the manuscript and supplementary materials. After completion, participants were compensated by SONA credits.

2.3. Questionnaires

Participants completed the Mood and Feelings Questionnaire (MFQ) (Angold & Costello, 1987) designed for measuring depression in young people, with each item rated on a 3-point Likert scale "not true", "sometimes", and "true". To disentangle the unique effects of depression vs anhedonia symptoms, we used an adjusted MFQ to remove the 3 anhedonia items: "I did not enjoy anything at all", "I didn't want to see my friends", and "I didn't have any fun in any of my activities" similar to previous studies (Demir-Kassem et al., 2025), in line with anhedonia conceptualized as a lack of interest and pleasure (Insel et al., 2010), this leaves the scores of the MFQ ranging from 0 to 60. Higher scores indicate increased depression symptoms.

They also completed the Anhedonia Scale for Adolescents (ASA) designed for measuring anhedonia in adolescents (Watson et al., 2021), with each item rated on a 4-point Likert scale "never", "sometimes", "often", "always". Scores can range from 0 to 42. Higher scores indicate increased anhedonia.

2.4. EMA assessments

At beginning of each assessment, participants first rated their affect (Table 1). Positive affect (PA) was measured by 4 items (cheerful, enthusiastic, relaxed, satisfied) and negative affect (NA) by 7 items (ashamed, annoyed, anxious, lonely, insecure, down, guilty). PA and NA were calculated as the mean of the corresponding items, which were rated on a Likert scale from 1 (not at all) to 7 (very much).

They then indicated whether they were using social media by selecting their current activity from a selection of categories, one of which was "social media" coded as 0 (not using social media) and 1 (using social media). If they selected social media as their current activity, they would next indicate if it was passive use (browsing) =0 or active (posting/messaging) =1.

After this, participants proceeded to indicate whether their next activity would be using social media and their future usage patterns.

2.5. Procedure

The experimenter met with each participant to brief them on the app. Participants were then required to log on and fill in their age, gender, ethnicity, the MFQ, and the ASA. EMA assessments began the next day. Participants were asked to respond to each assessment as soon as possible; otherwise, it would expire.

We collected data 7 times a day, between 8:30 AM and 10 PM, for 6 days, during the period July 2022 to October 2023. There was at least a 45-minute delay between each semirandom assessment, which took approximately 1 minute to complete and expired after 20 minutes.

This sampling frequency and questionnaire design has been shown to encourage compliance and reduce burden in young people and those with mood disorders (Eisele et al., 2022; Myin-Germeys et al., 2018; E. van Roekel et al., 2019; Wrzus & Neubauer, 2023). We contacted participants on days 2 and 5 to check that they were receiving notifications and to troubleshoot any problems.

At the end of the study, we collected app user experiences (supplementary Table S2), and participants were debriefed and advised to contact their general practitioner or the mental health charity, the Samaritans, if they were concerned about their mood.

2.6. Data analysis

We prepared the EMA data using esmpack in R (version 4.4.1; R Core Team, 2024) (Viechtbauer & esmpack, 2025). The data was arranged hierarchically in three levels; 1) level 1 variables are nested within each day including momentary affect (PA and NA), SMU (0 = not using social media;1 = using social media), active vs. passive usage patterns (0 = passive browsing;1 = active posting or messaging), and assessment time (1-7; time of assessment in the day), 2) level 2 variables are nested within the 6-day assessment period including assessment day (1-6), and 3) level 3 variables are nested within each participant including gender (0 = male;1 = female), depression, and anhedonia symptoms, which are timeinvariant.

To check for autoregression, we predicted PA, NA, and SMU at assessment time (t) with themselves at (t-1) using mixed effect regression models with the lme4 package in R (supplementary Table S3). To check for potential confounds, we ran multilevel mixed-effect regression models with assessment time and assessment day as predictors of PA, NA, SMU, and active/passive use, controlling for the corresponding autoregression (supplementary Table S4). All mixed-effect models included a subject-level random intercept, with assessment day included as a covariate where relevant, and their predictors were subject-mean centred.

To control for autoregression, PA (t-1) and NA (t-1) were added as covariates in all multilevel models predicting PA (t) and NA (t), respectively (supplementary Table S4). Additionally, assessment time covaried with PA, and assessment day covaried with SMU (supplementary Table S4). Therefore, these variables were also added as covariates in models predicting their respective outcomes.

Next, we ran multilevel models with SMU and active/passive use as predictors of PA and NA, respectively. We applied an iterative model build-up strategy to test the interactive effects of SMU and active/passive use with, depression, anhedonia and gender on momentary PA and NA. For each outcome (PA and NA), in step 1 we introduced fixed effects of level 1 predictors SMU and active/passive use separately, and we introduced fixed effects of the time-invariant level 3 predictors (depression, anhedonia and gender). In step 2, we examined the moderating effects of the time-invariant predictors by adding cross-level interactions between level 1 predictors (SMU and active/passive use) and level 3 predictors.

We also investigated the bidirectional temporal predictions between affect, SMU, and active/passive use using time-lagged analyses. To examine whether SMU and active/passive use predicted PA and NA at next assessment, we modelled PA and NA as outcomes at (t) using multilevel linear models with SMU and active/passive use separately as predictors at (t-1). Similarly, to examine whether affect predicted SMU and active/passive use at next assessment, we modelled SMU and active/passive use at (t) using multilevel logistic models with PA and NA as predictors at (t-1). We also applied the iterative model build-up strategy to these models, introducing only fixed effects in step 1 and cross-level interactions in step 2 to examine the moderating effects of the time-invariant predictors (depression, anhedonia and gender).

4. Discussion

This study examined how social media use and active versus passive engagement were associated with momentary affect in young people, and whether these associations varied according to depression symptoms, anhedonia and gender. Three findings are particularly noteworthy. First, social media use was concurrently associated with lower positive affect and higher negative affect, whereas active engagement was associated with more favourable affect than passive browsing. Second, the association between social media use and negative affect varied in opposite directions according to depression and anhedonia symptoms: it was weaker among participants with higher depression symptoms but stronger among those with higher anhedonia. Third, higher positive affect predicted a lower likelihood of active relative to passive engagement at the subsequent assessment, although social media use did not predict subsequent affect. Together, these findings suggest that the concurrent association between social media use and affect depends on both engagement type and individual psychological characteristics, while providing limited evidence about temporal directionality.

4.1 Explanations for Main Findings

We found that SMU was associated with decreased momentary PA and increased NA in young people, consistent with most previous EMA (Hamilton et al., 2021; Politte-Corn et al., 2024) and non-EMA findings (Tuck et al., 2025). However, Politte-Corn et al. (2024) did not find a positive association between SMU and momentary NA, suggesting that SMU's detrimental effects may primarily manifest as reductions in PA, with the NA increase potentially reflecting an outcome of reduced PA (affective bipolarity) (Dejonckheere et al., 2018; Reich et al., 2003). Such PA dampening may partly reflect social comparison (Nesi & Prinstein, 2015), co-rumination (Ohannessian et al., 2021), or reduced positive perceptions of social media contents, as shown in a prior EMA study (Brimmel et al., 2025). Even when social media interactions are themselves positive, time spent on social media may come at the expense of in-person interactions, which could be protective against mental disorders linked to decreased PA and increased NA, consistent with displacement behavioural theory (Coyne et al., 2020).

Active (compared with passive) social media use was associated with higher momentary positive affect and lower negative affect after controlling for depression, anhedonia and gender and their interactions. This finding adds to the limited real-life EMA evidence (Nereim et al., 2020) and retrospective findings (Thorisdottir et al., 2019). It is consistent with the possibility that active use facilitates self-disclosure, feedback and social connection, whereas passive browsing may involve fewer direct social exchanges or greater exposure to social comparison (Clark et al., 2017; Dienlin & Johannes, 2020; Karsay et al., 2022). However, these observational associations do not establish that active use improves affect. They could also reflect the type of interaction, the situations in which young people choose to engage actively, or pre-existing differences in affect.

A particularly important finding was that depression and anhedonia moderated the association between social media use and negative affect in opposite directions, despite both being core features of depressive symptomatology. Consistent with Politte-Corn et al.

(2024), this association was weaker among participants with higher depression symptoms, whereas it was stronger among those with higher anhedonia. As Figure 1 shows, participants with higher depression symptoms reported elevated negative affect regardless of social media use. The weaker association may therefore reflect this already elevated level of negative affect, leaving less room for a further increase during social media use. An alternative or complementary explanation is emotion context insensitivity (ECI), whereby depression is associated with blunted emotional reactivity to both positive and negative stimuli (Bylsma et al., 2011; Rottenberg et al., 2005). The present data cannot distinguish these explanations. In contrast, the stronger association among participants with higher anhedonia suggests that anhedonia may be relevant not only to how young people engage with social media, but also to their momentary affect during use. One possibility is that individuals experiencing reduced interest and pleasure derive less reward from their social media experiences, potentially contributing to less favourable affect. However, because the study did not directly measure the rewarding qualities of social media content or participants' motives for using it, this interpretation remains tentative. These contrasting associations highlight the value of examining depression and anhedonia separately rather than treating depressive symptomatology as a unitary construct.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to show that depression and gender also moderate the association between active (vs. passive) SMU and affect. Consistent with the ECI account, individuals with higher depression may derive fewer affective benefits from active engagement due to reduced reward responsiveness (Berry et al., 2019; Foti & Hajcak, 2009; Halahakoon et al., 2020). We further found that active (vs. passive) use was more strongly associated with increased PA and decreased NA among males than females, potentially reflecting females' greater sensitivity to both positive and negative social interactions indicated by both retrospective (Frison & Eggermont, 2015) and EMA findings (Hamilton et al., 2021). Therefore, young females may experience more mixed emotional outcomes from active SMU, while young males derive more consistent affective benefits. This moderating effect of gender was not observed in all prior studies (Politte-Corn et al., 2024), highlighting the need for further research on how gender shapes the affective correlates of SMU.

This study also examined time-lagged associations between affect and active versus passive social media use. Higher positive affect predicted a lower likelihood of active (rather than passive) use at the subsequent assessment. However, social media use did not predict subsequent positive or negative affect, and some time-lagged interaction models did not converge. The single prospective association should therefore be interpreted cautiously: it does not establish why participants were less likely to engage actively after reporting higher positive affect, and it provides limited evidence for a general mood-regulation account.

Further research with more frequent observations and larger numbers of social media use assessments is needed to clarify the temporal relationship between affect and engagement type.

4.2. Limitations

This study has several limitations. Social media use was recorded when it was the main current activity, which likely underestimated use and yielded relatively few social media use assessments (121 in total, including 32 active-use assessments). Estimates involving active versus passive use, particularly the moderation effects, should therefore be interpreted cautiously and replicated in larger samples. The sample was also small and included relatively few male participants, so gender differences require replication in larger, more gender-balanced samples. Some time-lagged interaction models did not converge, and analyses were not corrected for multiple comparisons; moderation findings, particularly those with p values close to .05, should be considered provisional. Future studies combining EMA with passive sensing of smartphone and app use could provide more precise measures of social media engagement.

5. Conclusion

Overall, this study suggests that the association between social media use and momentary affect in young people is not uniform. Social media use was associated with lower positive and higher negative affect, while active engagement was associated with more favourable affect than passive browsing. Crucially, the association between social media use and negative affect was stronger at higher levels of anhedonia but weaker at higher levels of depression symptoms; the latter pattern may reflect the already elevated negative affect reported by participants with higher depression. Higher positive affect also predicted a lower likelihood of subsequent active relative to passive engagement, although social media use did not predict subsequent affect. These findings highlight the importance of considering both engagement type and individual psychological characteristics, particularly the distinction between depression and anhedonia. Given the observational design, relatively few social media use assessments and multiple exploratory moderation analyses, replication in larger samples is needed before drawing firm conclusions about the mechanisms underlying these associations or their implications for intervention.
