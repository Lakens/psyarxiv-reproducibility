How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Informative pre-cues can guide visual search, but it remains unclear how precisely cue features must match the features they specify. Across two experiments, we examined how cue-feature mismatch affected target-and distractor-based guidance. Experiment 1 compared precise cues (0° deviation) with imprecise cues deviating by 15°, 35°, or 45° in separate groups. Positive cues, indicating upcoming target features, reliably facilitated search, whereas negative cues, indicating upcoming distractor features, produced little benefit. There was limited evidence that cueing effects changed specifically with cue precision. Experiment 2 varied cue deviation from 3° to 60° within participants and manipulated cue direction, deviating toward or away from the competing feature (i.e. target or distractor), between two groups. Positive cueing effects were direction-dependent, with large toward deviations reducing cueing benefits whereas away deviations left them unaffected. However, away-shifted cues did not outperform the 3° reference, providing no direct evidence for optimally shifted attentional templates. Negative cueing effects showed no reliable modulation by deviation magnitude or direction. These findings indicate that target-based guidance can tolerate substantial cue-feature mismatch but is constrained by the cue's position relative to competing distractor features. All data generated or analyzed during this research is available on the OSF repository: https://osf.io/fd6t3/.

Introduction

Visual search relies on the ability to direct attention toward task-relevant information while ignoring irrelevant distractors. One key mechanism underlying this process involves setting up an attentional template (Duncan & Humphreys, 1989), a representation of task-relevant features that biases attentional selection (Desimone & Duncan, 1995; Wolfe, 1994 Wolfe, , 2021)). Accumulating evidence indicates that such templates are not necessarily fixed but can be adjusted according to current search demands (Harris et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2022 Yu et al., , 2024)). Moreover, feature information provided before search is not always perfectly precise. The present study therefore asks how attentional guidance changes as the mismatch between an informative pre-cue and the feature it specifies increases.

Informative pre-cues can provide advance information about either a target or a distractor feature. Positive cues provide information about the upcoming target and reliably facilitate visual search relative to non-informative or neutral cues (Arita et al., 2012; Beck et al., 2018; Beck & Hollingworth, 2015; Carlisle et al., 2011; Conci et al., 2019; Woodman et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2020 Zhang et al., , 2022;; Zhang & Carlisle, 2023). This benefit is attributed to target-based guidance, in which attention is biased toward items matching the target feature. Negative cues provide information about features to be ignored and can also facilitate search. This benefit is often attributed to distractor-based guidance, in which attention is biased away from items sharing the distractor feature. However, the benefits of negative cues are typically smaller (Arita et al., 2012; Conci et al., 2019; Kerzel & Huynh Cong, 2022; Zhang et al., 2022; Zhang & Carlisle, 2023). This difference raises the question of whether the two forms of guidance also differ in their sensitivity to the precision of advance feature information.

For target-based guidance, two theoretical accounts make different predictions about how such feature mismatch should affect attentional selection. According to the feature-similarity gain account (Treue & Trujillo, 1999), attentional gain is maximal for features matching the currently relevant feature and decreases as feature similarity declines (Anderson & Folk, 2010; Ansorge & Becker, 2014). Applied to informative pre-cues, this account predicts that guidance should become progressively less effective as the cue differs more strongly from the feature it specifies. A different prediction can be derived from the optimal tuning account (Navalpakkam & Itti, 2007). This account was originally proposed for situations in which targets are difficult to discriminate from competing nontargets, predicting that attentional selection may be shifted away from distractor features rather than being centered on the veridical target feature (e.g., Becker, 2010; Geng et al., 2017; Navalpakkam & Itti, 2007; Yu & Geng, 2019). For instance, when searching for an orange target among yellower nontargets, attentional selectivity may be tuned toward a redder value because this increases target-distractor discriminability. Such asymmetric tuning has been observed in some visual search studies, although evidence remains mixed and appears to depend on trial history (Becker, 2010; Becker et al., 2013 Becker et al., , 2014;; Chapman et al., 2023; Grössle et al., 2023; Hamblin-Frohman & Becker, 2021; Kerzel & Huynh Cong, 2024; Scolari & Serences, 2009; Scolari et al., 2012). Extending this logic to informative pre-cues, attentional guidance may depend not only on the magnitude of cue-target mismatch, but also on whether the cued feature is displaced toward or away from the distractor. Specifically, optimal tuning predicts that a cue shifted away from the distractor should outperform a cue that exactly matches the target feature, because such a cue allows observers to set up a template with greater target-distractor discriminability. Conversely, a cue shifted toward the distractor should reduce the discriminability and thus impair performance. Consequently, a deviation of the same magnitude should be less costly when it shifts the cued feature away from the distractor than when it shifts it toward the distractor.

Previous studies have used the contingent capture paradigm (Folk et al., 1992; Folk & Remington, 1998) to test predictions relevant to these two accounts. In such paradigms, spatially non-predictive peripheral cues serve as probes of the currently active attentional set and reveal which features are capable of triggering shifts of spatial attention (Anderson & Folk, 2010; Ansorge & Becker, 2014; Kerzel & Huynh Cong, 2024). The present study tested these predictions in a different cue-use context, in which feature cues were informative about the upcoming search and could therefore be used to guide subsequent selection. Specifically, we asked whether guidance based on informative pre-cues varied primarily with the magnitude of cue-feature mismatch or also with its direction relative to competing features.

The Present Study

To resolve the question, we manipulated cue deviation across a broad range in a continuous color space. Cue deviation was defined as the angular distance between the cue color and the feature specified by that cue. For positive cues, this was the distance between the cue color and the upcoming target color; for negative cues, it was the distance between the cue color and the upcoming distractor color. Smaller angular deviations therefore indicated more precise cue information. Neutral cues indicated a color absent from the search display and were manipulated in parallel to provide deviationspecific baselines for measuring positive and negative cueing effects. Cue direction was defined relative to the competing feature. Positive-cue deviations were directed either toward or away from the distractor color, whereas negative-cue deviations were directed either toward or away from the target color. Target-distractor color pairings in search displays varied across trials to discourage reliance on a single, consistently repeated target-distractor relation (Becker, 2010; Becker et al., 2013; Kerzel & Huynh Cong, 2024), and participants were instructed to use pre-cues to guide search.

The "feature-similarity gain" account (Treue & Trujillo, 1999) predicts that positive cueing benefits should decrease as cue-target deviation increases, with comparable reductions for toward and away deviations of the same magnitude. In contrast, the optimal tuning account (Navalpakkam & Itti, 2007) predicts that equally large deviations should produce better guidance when they shift the cue away from rather than toward the distractor, with an away-shifted cue outperforming the exact targetmatching reference providing stronger evidence for this account. The competing predictions of feature-similarity gain and optimal tuning primarily concern target-based guidance. Negative cues provide a complementary test of whether distractor-based guidance is similarly sensitive to the magnitude and direction of cue-feature mismatch.

Experiment 1A and 1B

Experiment 1 examined whether cue-feature mismatch affected positive and negative cueing effects. Within each deviation group, participants received both precise (0°) cues and cues that deviated from the feature they specified, allowing the 0° condition to serve as a within-participant reference.

Experiment 1A tested 15° and 35° deviations in separate participant groups, whereas Experiment 1B tested a 45° deviation in an independent sample. This design allowed us to assess the effect of cue imprecision across different degrees of cue-feature mismatch.

Method

Participants. An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 (Faul et al., 2009) for the cue type × deviation group interaction in a 3 × 2 mixed ANOVA, with cue type (positive, negative, neutral) as a within-subject factor and deviation group (15°, 35°) as a between-subject factor. Assuming a medium effect size (Cohen's f = 0.25), an alpha level of .05, and a statistical power of .80, a correlation of r = 0.5 among the three repeated measures, and a nonsphericity correction of ε = 1, the analysis indicated that a minimum total sample size of 28 participants (14 per group) was required. In Experiment 1A, we included 40 participants (28 female, 12 male, mean age =22.38 years, SD = 2.60) who were randomly assigned in equal numbers to the 15° and 35° groups (20 participants per group). Experiment 1B included an independent sample of 20 new participants (13 female, 7 male, mean age = 22.80, SD = 3.53), recruited from the same participant pool using the same eligibility criteria, matching the sample size of each deviation group in Experiment 1A. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision; visual acuity and color vision were assured using an OCULUS Binoptometer 3 (OCULUS Optikgeräte GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany). Participants provided written informed consent before starting the experiment and they were compensated with either course credits or payment of 9€/hour. The study was approved by the ethics committee of the Department of Psychology at Philipps-Universität Marburg (No. 2021-56k) and conducted in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki.

Apparatus and stimuli.

The experiment was conducted in a dimly lit room with attenuated sound. Participants were seated in front of a 22-inch VPixx VIEWPixx monitor (Vpixx Technologies Inc.) at a viewing distance of approximately 100 cm. The experiment was controlled by a Windows 7 operating system (1680 x 1050 pixels, 60 Hz refresh rate) using E-Prime 2.0 software. All stimuli were presented on a black (0.28cd/m²) background. Each search display contained 24 Gabor patches, with patch radius of 0.81° of visual angle, defined by a cosine envelope with a spatial frequency of 0.05 and a standard deviation of 12 pixels. All patches were generated using the online Gabor-patch generator (https://www.cogsci.nl/gabor-generator). The 24 Gabor patches were arranged on three imaginary circles (see Fig. 1A): 4 items on an inner circle (radius = 2.42°), 8 on a middle circle (radius = 4.84°) and 12 on an outer circle (radius = 7.26°). Items were evenly spaced along each circle.

The colors used in the experiments were matched for luminance and defined in the CIELAB color space. This color space is approximately perceptually uniform, such that equal distances in the space correspond to similar perceptual differences. Each search display consisted of 16 task-irrelevant gray Gabor patches (L* = 70, a* = 0, b* = 0, where L* represents perceived lightness, a* indexes the redgreen axis, and b* indexes the yellow-blue axis) and 8 chromatic Gabor patches. The gray patches were randomly assigned one of four orientations (horizontal/0°, vertical/90°, or ± 15° relative to vertical midline). The 8 chromatic patches were task-relevant, consisting of 4 patches in the target color and 4 in a distractor color. These colors were pseudo-randomly sampled from three equidistant hues (separated by 120°): red (L* = 70, a* = 40, b* = 0), green (L* = 70, a* = -20, b* = 34.60), and blue (L* = 70, a* = -20, b* = -34.60), with the constraint that each color was used equally often across trials. To prevent a fixed target-distractor relation, the distractor hue varied relative to the target across trials (e.g., green or blue distractors for a red target). The target was defined as the chromatic Gabor patch tilted either left (-15°) or right (+15°) relative to the vertical midline. To prevent orientation-based pop-out, the achromatic nontarget patches were presented in heterogeneous orientations, substantially reducing the uniqueness of the target's orientation. Variation in the orientations of the chromatic nontarget patches further increased orientation heterogeneity. The target was never placed on the outer circle but appeared with equal probability at any of the remaining candidate locations on the inner and middle circles, thereby limiting the range of target eccentricities.

As shown in Fig. 1B, the cue display consisted of two circles (radius 0.69°) presented 1.62° to the left and right from the central fixation (see also Conci et al., 2019). On each trial, one of the circles was gray and the other was filled with a chromatic color (e.g., red, green or blue) that served as the cue. The symbol presented at the circle center indicated the cue type: positive (+), negative (-), or neutral (o). A positive (+) cue indicated the upcoming target color; a negative (-) cue color indicated the to-be-ignored distractor color, and a neutral cue (o) showed a color that did not appear in the subsequent search display. Cue precision (see Fig. 1C) was defined as the angular deviation of the cue color from the relevant reference color (i.e., the actual target color for positive cues, the actual distractor color for negative cues, and the "absent" color for neutral cues) in the CIELAB a-b plane. Within each cue type, one-third of the trials were precise (0° deviation), and the remaining two-thirds were imprecise, with cue colors evenly deviating by ±15° in the high precision group (Experiment 1A), ±35° in the medium precision group (Experiment 1A), or ±45° in the low precision group (Experiment 1B). The three deviations were selected on an exploratory basis. All deviations stayed below 60°, corresponding to the midpoint between adjacent reference hues, at which the shifted cue would be equally compatible with either reference hue. The selected values were intended to provide an initial sampling of different levels of cue imprecision rather than to test an independently established perceptual boundary. Cue colors appeared equally often in each cue type, with the constraint that the color category (i.e., red, green, blue) did not repeat on consecutive trials.

Procedure and design. As illustrated in Fig. 1D, each trial began with the presentation of a central fixation point accompanied by two arrow cues (length: 1.2°; positioned 0.8° above and below fixation), indicating the hemifield in which the task-relevant color would appear. The arrows were retained from the paradigm used in previous studies (Conci et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2022) and allowed attention to be oriented to the position at which the chromatic cue would subsequently appear. Following a randomized delay of 300-500 ms, one of three cue types (positive, negative, or neutral) was presented for another 250 ms. The left/right position of chromatic cue was counterbalanced and randomly intermixed, and the arrows identified that position in advance. After another jittered delay of 800-1000 ms, the search display appeared. Participants were instructed to search for a left-or right-tilted target embedded among seven chromatic distractors. Responses were by pressing the left button with their right index finger for left-tilted targets, and the right button with their right middle finger for right-tilted targets on a gamepad as quickly and accurately as possible. Incorrect or slow responses exceeding 4000 ms were followed by a feedback screen ("incorrect" or "too slow" in German) presented for 500 ms. Each trial concluded with a 1000 ms blank interval. After each block, feedback on mean accuracy and mean response time was shown for 3000 ms.

Cue types were presented in separate blocks to minimize trial-by-trial switching between different cue-use strategies. This allowed participants to maintain the general strategy associated with each cue type. Their order followed a Latin-square design and was counterbalanced across participants. The experiment consisted of 12 blocks of 72 trials, resulting in a total of 864 trials. Prior to the main experiment, participants completed three practice blocks, one for each cue type, comprising 36 trials each.

Statistical analysis

When calculating mean RTs, trials with responses faster than 200 ms or slower than 4000 ms were excluded. Incorrect trials (which accounted for 2.40%-3.70% of trials) and trials with RTs exceeding ±2.5 SD from the condition-specific mean were also removed. Accuracy, by contrast, was calculated across both correct and incorrect trials. To account for potential speed-accuracy trade-offs, we evaluated overall performance using the Balanced Integration Score (BIS; Liesefeld et al., 2015; Liesefeld & Janczyk, 2019), which integrates RT and accuracy into a single score so that both measures are weighted equally in the assessment of performance. Higher (more positive) BIS values represent better performance. To ensure results were comparable across groups, BIS values were calculated on the pooled data of all participants. Statistical analyses were performed using JASP 0.18.3 (JASP Team, 2024). Mean BIS values were submitted to a mixed ANOVA with cue type (positive, negative, neutral) and cue precision (precise, imprecise) as withinsubject factors and cue deviation group (15°, 35°) as a between-subject factor. Statistical significance was defined as p < .05. When the assumption of sphericity was violated, the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied. Follow-up contrasts were conducted using paired-samples t-tests on participant-level condition means, with degrees of freedom determined as N -1. p values were Holm-adjusted for multiple comparisons, with the exception of the planned contrasts, which were not corrected. Cohen's dz was calculated from the corresponding paired difference scores. For all comparisons of theoretical interest, we additionally report BF01 values from Bayesian paired-samples t-tests, using a Cauchy prior with a default scale of r = 0.707. Bayes factors were interpreted following Lee and Wagenmakers (2013): BF values of 1-3 were considered ₀₁ anecdotal evidence for the null hypothesis, 3-10 moderate, 10-30 strong, 30-100 very strong, and values above 100 extreme evidence. Correspondingly, BF values between 1/3 and 1 were considered anecdotal ₀₁ evidence for the alternative hypothesis, 1/10 -1/3 moderate, 1/30-1/10 strong, 1/100-1/30 very strong, and < 1/100 extreme evidence.

Discussion

Experiment 1 examined whether the effectiveness of positive and negative cues depended on how precisely the cue color matched its reference feature. Across Experiments 1A and 1B, positive cues produced a robust benefit relative to neutral cues, whereas negative cues yielded little or no reliable benefit.

The precise cue condition provided a within-participant baseline for assessing the effect of cue precision. In Experiment 1A, the precision-related change in the positive cueing effects differed between the 15°-and 35°-deviation groups. Importantly, however, this interaction cannot be attributed straightforwardly to changes in target-based guidance. In the 35° group, where the positive cueing effects differed between precise and imprecise cues, performance following positive cues themselves did not differ reliably with cue precision. Instead, the change in the cueing effect was largely associated with variation in the neutral condition (see Table 1). Thus, Experiment 1A provides little evidence that increasing cue-target mismatch up to 35° directly altered performance following positive cues. In Experiment 1B, performance was generally better for precise than imprecise cues, but neither the positive nor the negative cueing effects showed a significant precision-related change. Thus, the present findings provided limited evidence that target-or distractor-based guidance was specifically modulated by cue precision.

However, because the imprecise cue deviations were compared partly across independent samples, Experiment 1 provides limited evidence about how cueing effects change across these deviations. Experiment 2 therefore manipulated cue deviation within participants over a broader range.

Experiment 2

Experiment 1 provided robust positive cueing benefits but limited evidence that cue precision specifically modulated target-based attentional guidance. One possibility is that the effect of cue precision depends on its direction relative to the competing feature. That is, deviations toward and away from the distractor may have different consequences, which could be obscured when direction is not distinguished. Experiment 2 therefore manipulated both cue deviation and cue direction. Cue deviation was manipulated within participants, allowing the effect of deviation magnitude to be assessed within the same observers. Cue direction was manipulated between participants so that each participant experienced a single, consistent mapping between cue deviation and the competing search feature throughout the experiment. Intermixing toward and away cues within participants could have prevented template adjustment to a consistent deviation direction and introduced potential carryover effects between opposing cue-feature relations. Because direction would be undefined at 0°, the smallest deviation was set at 3° to preserve formally distinct but closely matched toward and away conditions. As the closest approximation to the reference color in the present design, the 3° condition was treated as a near-match reference, although its perceptual equivalence to an exact match was not directly tested.

The "feature-similarity gain" account (Treue & Trujillo, 1999) predicts that positive cueing benefits should decline relative to the 3° reference as cue-target deviation increases, with comparable reductions for toward and away deviations of the same magnitude. In contrast, the "optimal tuning" account (Navalpakkam & Itti, 2007) proposes that attentional templates are shifted away from distractor features to maximize target-distractor discriminability. It therefore predicts greater positive cueing benefits for cues that deviate away from, rather than toward, the distractor. More critically, an a way cue outperforming the 3° reference would provide stronger evidence for optimal tuning than a directional asymmetry alone. Negative cues were additionally included to assess whether distractorbased guidance exhibits similar sensitivity to deviation magnitude or direction.

Discussion

In Experiment 2, positive cues again reliably facilitated search performance, whereas negative cues did not yield benefits relative to neutral cues, replicating the findings observed in Experiment 1. Crucially, positive cueing effects varied as a function of both the magnitude and direction of cue deviation. In the toward group, positive cueing effects remained comparable with the 3° reference at deviations up to 36° but was reduced at 48° and 60°. Analyses of the constituent conditions indicated that these reductions were associated with poorer performance following positive cues rather than corresponding changes in the neutral baseline, consistent with the interpretation that large deviations toward the distractor impaired target-based guidance. In contrast, positive cueing effects in the away group did not differ significantly from the 3° reference at any deviation. Thus, the interaction was primarily attributable to reduced guidance for large toward deviations rather than enhanced guidance for away deviations.

This directional asymmetry is difficult to reconcile with a strict feature-similarity gain account (Treue & Trujillo, 1999), which would predict that performance is determined primarily by the magnitude of the cue-target difference, irrespective of its direction. The findings are broadly consistent with the idea that target-distractor relations constrain the effective range of target-based attentional guidance, as indicated by the optimal tuning account (Navalpakkam & Itti, 2007). However, because cues deviating away from the distractor did not outperform the 3° reference, the results do not provide direct evidence that attentional templates were optimally shifted away from the distractor. Rather, they show that large cue deviations were especially detrimental when they shifted the cued feature toward the distractor.

General Discussion

Across two experiments, the present study examined how cue-feature mismatch constrains attentional guidance based on informative target and distractor information. Positive cues consistently facilitated search performance relative to neutral cues, whereas negative cues did not produce reliable benefits. The effects of cue precision, however, were less straightforward than a simple decline in performance with increasing deviations. Experiment 1 provided limited evidence that cue precision specifically altered either positive or negative cueing effects. Experiment 2 showed that larger deviations toward the distractor reduced positive cueing effects, whereas deviations away from the distractor did not produce a corresponding reduction. Importantly, away-shifted cues also did not outperform the nearmatch reference condition. Collectively, these results indicate that target-based guidance depends on the relation between the cued feature and competing distractor information, but they do not provide direct evidence that attentional templates are shifted to an optimally tuned feature value.

Target-based guidance tolerates moderate cue-feature mismatch

Experiment 1 showed robust positive cueing benefits across all deviation groups, but provided little evidence that these benefits were determined by cue precision. In Experiment 1A, the precisionrelated change in the positive cueing effects differed between the 15°-and 35°-deviation groups, but the effect in the 35° group was largely attributable to variation in the neutral condition rather than to a reliable change in performance following positive cues themselves. Experiment 1B likewise showed an overall advantage for precise over imprecise cues, but this effect did not reliably differ across cue types.

Experiment 2 leads to a similar conclusion for the smaller cue deviations. Positive cueing effects did not show a systematic decline from 3° through 36°, and the numerical values fluctuated across these conditions rather than decreasing monotonically. These results suggest that moderate discrepancies between the cue and target can still support effective attentional guidance. This interpretation is consistent with proposals that the representation used to guide search need not preserve the target feature with the same fidelity required for subsequent target identification (Wilschut et al., 2014; Wolfe, 2021; Yu et al., 2022). Coarse or category-consistent feature information may therefore remain useful even when the cue is not an exact physical match to the target (Daoutis et al., 2006; Wolfe, 2021).

We thank an anonymous reviewer for suggesting a discussion of the feature-based surround suppression (Störmer & Alvarez, 2014; see also Chapman et al., 2023) in Experiment 1 with exactmatch trials included. Such accounts predict enhanced processing of the attended feature itself but suppression of nearby features in feature space, with this suppression weakening as feature distance increases. Accordingly, if feature-based attention was centered on the positive cue color, the target would coincide with this center at 0° but fall away from it under cue-target mismatch. A small mismatch could therefore place the target within a suppressive surround, resulting in poorer performance than in the exact-match condition. At larger cue-target distances, the target may move beyond the region of strongest suppression, in which case some recovery in performance would be expected. We found no clear evidence for such a non-monotonic pattern. Positive-cue performance at the 15° deviation was similar to that in the corresponding 0° condition, and no subsequent recovery was evident at the larger tested deviations. However, because the present deviation levels were not selected to map the width of the suppressive surround and sampled feature space only sparsely, we cannot determine whether any of the tested deviations fell within the region of strongest suppression. The present findings therefore neither provide clear support for nor a strong test of feature-based surround suppression.

Cue direction constrains target-based guidance, but does not demonstrate optimal tuning

Experiment 2 was designed to distinguish predictions based solely on cue-target similarity from predictions that additionally depend on the relation between target and distractor features. A strict feature-similarity gain account predicts that performance should deteriorate as cue-target distance increases and that equally large deviations should have comparable consequences regardless of direction (Treue & Trujillo, 1999). The observed pattern did not conform to this simple prediction. At large deviations (48° and 60°), positive cueing effects were reduced when the cue shifted toward the distractor, whereas away-shifted cues did not show a comparable reduction. Thus, the consequences of cue-target mismatch appear to depend on the competing distractor feature, rather than on cuetarget similarity alone.

The 60° condition imposes an additional constraint on this interpretation. At this deviation, positive and negative cues were identical in color in the toward group, whereas negative and neutral cues were identical in the away group. Because this introduced a systematic difference between the two direction groups beyond cue direction itself, the 60° condition could confound the interpretation of directiondependent effects. To assess whether the direction-dependent modulation of cueing effects depended on the ambiguous 60° condition, we repeated the analyses of positive and negative cueing effects after excluding this deviation (see Supplementary Materials). The cue deviation × cue direction interaction for positive cueing effects remained significant in both BIS and RT, whereas the corresponding interaction for negative cueing effects remained non-significant. This indicates that the directional modulation of positive cueing effects was not solely driven by the cue-color overlap at 60°, although the 60° condition itself remains difficult to interpret because of its structural ambiguity. Importantly, a reduced positive cueing effect was already evident at the 48° toward deviation, where cue colors remained physically distinct. This indicates that detrimental effects of shifting the cue toward the distractor can emerge before the structural ambiguity introduced at 60° is reached.

However, this directional asymmetry should not be equated with direct evidence for optimal tuning (Navalpakkam & Itti, 2007). The optimal tuning account predicts that shifting the guiding representation away from distractors can increase target-distractor discriminability. Deviations shifting the cued feature away from the distractor should therefore be less detrimental than those shifting it toward the distractor. A particularly diagnostic prediction is that an away-shifted cue should outperform a cue that (nearly) matches the target. In Experiment 2, however, none of the away conditions reliably exceeded the 3° near-match reference. The present results therefore showed that large deviations toward the distractor are especially detrimental, but not that attentional templates were optimally shifted away from the distractor. Nonetheless, target-based guidance can tolerate substantial deviations from the target feature without additional costs relative to the near-match reference, provided that the deviation shifts the cue away from the distractor. This pattern is broadly compatible with a key premise of optimal tuning, whereby the influence of an imprecise template depends on its position relative to the distractor, rather than solely on its distance from the target.

A related consideration is that the behavioral patterns predicted by "optimal tuning" (Navalpakkam & Itti, 2007) are often mirrored by "relational" (Becker, 2010) accounts, despite their mechanistic divergence. For instance, when searching for an orange target among yellower distractors, the target is consistently redder than the distractors; consequently, nontargets (e.g., red) sharing this relational direction can capture attention, mimicking the appearance of an adaptive shift away from distractor features. This convergence has led these accounts to be frequently considered together in the literature (Becker et al., 2014; Kerzel, 2020; Yu et al., 2022), especially in designs with consistent targetdistractor relations (Becker, 2010; Becker et al., 2013). The relational account (Becker, 2010) assumes that attentional guidance can rely on relations that generalize across trials. Because target-distractor direction varied across trials in the present study, participants could not rely on a fixed directional rule. However, all target-distractor pairs were separated by the same 120° distance in color space, leaving open a more abstract relational strategy. For example, participants could select the search color closer to a positive cue, a relation that becomes less discriminative as a toward cue approaches the midpoint between target and distractor but remains informative for away cues. Such a strategy could therefore produce the observed direction-dependent modulation without requiring an optimally shifted feature template. The present design thus cannot fully distinguish feature-specific tuning from more abstract relational guidance.

What can be concluded from the negative cues?

Across both experiments, negative cues produced little or no reliable benefit relative to neutral cues, and we found no reliable evidence that negative cueing effects varied systematically with cue deviation or direction. These findings contrast with studies in which negative cues have facilitated search (Arita et al., 2012; Conci et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2022; Zhang & Carlisle, 2023).

One possibility is that the null effects simply reflect insufficient statistical power or inadequate sensitivity to detect precision-related changes. However, this explanation is unlikely for several reasons. First, the feature-based search task was comparable to those used in studies that robustly demonstrated negative cueing effects (Arita et al., 2012; Conci et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2022). Both experiments included substantial trial counts per condition and sample sizes similar to those in which optimal tuning has been observed (Yu et al., 2022; Yu & Geng, 2019). Moreover, negative cues were structurally equivalent to positive cues and provided the same amount of information; variability in negative cue trials was not appreciably higher than in positive cue trials. These considerations suggest that the design had sufficient power to detect precision-related effects, had they been present. Nevertheless, the associated Bayes factors were generally inconclusive, providing at most anecdotal evidence and no consistent support for either the presence or absence of precision-related modulation. We therefore cannot draw firm conclusions regarding negative cueing effects from the present data.

Future studies in which negative cues provide stronger or more consistent utility will be needed to determine whether distractor-based representations themselves can be flexibly tuned.

Another possibility is that the present task favored target-based guidance. The observer's ultimate task was always to identify a predefined target, and positive cues mapped directly onto the feature that could guide selection of that target. Negative cues instead specified a feature to be rejected and therefore required observers to use the cue indirectly to constrain the remaining candidate features, which may simply be a less efficient strategy. Besides, studies reporting reliable negative cueing effects (Arita et al., 2012; Conci et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2022; Zhang & Carlisle, 2023) typically employed cues that perfectly matched the distractor feature, thereby maintaining high utility. In the present study, however, cue-feature mismatch was manipulated such that imprecise cues constituted the majority of trials. This reduced reliability may have discouraged participants from investing in maintenance of a distractor-based attentional template. Furthermore, since negative cueing benefits have been shown to emerge only under high task demands (Conci et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2022), the current task may have been too easy for negative cues to serve as an effective strategy. If the effort required to maintain a distractor representation outweighs its expected benefit, observers may use negative cues inconsistently or disregard them altogether. This interpretation is compatible with evidence that strategic cue use depends strongly on both exact and expected task demands (Conci et al., 2019; Kerzel & Huynh Cong, 2022; Han et al., 2025).

Informative cue use and attentional selection

The present task differs from paradigms such as contingent capture (Folk et al., 1992; Folk & Remington, 1998; Kerzel, 2019 Kerzel, , 2020 Kerzel, , 2025)), in which cues are brief and explicitly non-predictive. These cues serve as diagnostic probes that reveal which features are capable of automatically attracting attention given the current attentional set. In contrast, the present study was designed to examine how observers use informative pre-cues to prepare for search. Participants were encouraged to use the cues, and target-distractor pairings varied across trials so that a fixed feature relation could not reliably be learned. These design features increase the relevance of cue information for search, although they do not imply that the cue use was exclusively voluntary. The present findings should therefore be understood as characterizing attentional guidance based on informative pre-cues rather than as establishing a strict dichotomy between voluntary and involuntary attention. Besides, because cue types were blocked in the present study, participants may have maintained a general target-or distractor-based guidance strategy while updating the specific color information provided by each cue. Whether comparable effects emerge when cue types are intermixed remains an open question.

Conclusion

In sum, the present study shows that the benefits of positive cues depend not only on how closely they match the target, but also on how the cued feature is positioned relative to competing distractor features. This directional dependence is not readily explained by cue-target similarity alone, although the present results do not uniquely identify an optimally shifted attentional template. Negative cues, in contrast, produced little reliable benefit under the present task conditions and showed no reliable modulation by cue deviation or direction.

Sensitivity analysis excluding the 60° deviation.

At the 60° deviation, positive and negative cues were identical in color in the toward group, whereas negative and neutral cues were identical in the away group. Because this introduced a systematic difference between the two direction groups beyond cue direction itself, the 60° condition could confound the interpretation of direction-dependent effects. We therefore excluded this condition and repeated the analyses of positive and negative cueing effects to determine whether their modulation by cue direction remained evident across the deviation range unaffected by cue color overlap.

Baseline-corrected cueing effects (ΔBIS: positive/negative minus neutral) were each submitted to separate mixed ANOVAs with cue deviation (3°, 12°, 24°, 36°, 48°) as a within-subject factor and cue direction (toward, away) as a between-subject factor. For positive cueing effects, the cue deviation × cue direction interaction was significant, F(4, 160) = 2.50, p =.045, ηp² = 0.06. Followup contrasts among the remaining deviation levels relative to the 3° reference yielded the same results as those reported in the main text. For negative cueing effects, the corresponding interaction was not significant, F(4, 160) = 0.39, p =.814, ηp² = 0.01.

The same analyses were conducted on RT-and error-rate-based positive and negative cueing effects. For RTs, cue deviation × cue direction interaction was significant for positive cueing effects, F(2.64, 105.65) = 3.47, p =.023, ηp² = 0.08, but not for negative cueing effects, F(3.15, 125.98) = 0.54, p =.662, ηp² = 0.01. The corresponding interaction in error rates was not significant for either positive cueing effects, F(4, 160) = 1.64, p =.166, ηp² = 0.04, or negative cueing effects, F(4, 160) = 0.39, p =.814, ηp² = 0.01.
