Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Information-seeking behavior is often interpreted as evidence of metacognitive monitoring, but nonmetacognitive factors may also explain such behavior. We examined this issue in mice using a target-cueing procedure in which an information-seeking response produced a hint indicating the location of the rewarded stimulus. In Experiment 1, mice performed an orientation discrimination. In Experiment 2, the same mice performed a grating discrimination in which grating coarseness varied across trials. During training, some aspects of performance were compatible with a metacognitive account. In Experiment 1, information seeking did not increase reliably as the discrimination became more difficult, although hint use improved accuracy most clearly at smaller orientation differences. In Experiment 2, mice sought information more often at lower grating coarseness, and hint use was associated with the greatest accuracy benefit under those more difficult conditions. However, the critical test results did not support the prediction that information seeking would track the need for additional information. Mice frequently sought information in target-only trials, in which no additional information was required, and showed relatively low rates of information seeking in trials in which the initial display consisted of two identical, and therefore nondiagnostic, stimuli. These findings indicate that information seeking in the present task was multiply determined. The observed pattern was consistent with contributions from response competition, reinforcement history, and the behavioral utility of the hint. Although some aspects of training remained compatible with confidence-based control, the critical test conditions showed that information seeking was not governed by confidence monitoring alone.

One influential approach to studying information seeking in nonhuman animals has been the tube task (Call & Carpenter, 2001). In this task, subjects may inspect potential baited locations before choosing, and they often do so selectively when they have not seen where the food was hidden. Although this pattern has been interpreted as evidence that animals monitor their own knowledge states and seek information accordingly, that interpretation remains debated. Information seeking in the tube task may allow alternative explanations that are more parsimonious than a metacognitive account (Carruthers, 2008; Crystal & Foote, 2011; Marsh, 2019). For example, looking into a tube may result from response competition rather than monitoring of one's own knowledge state. More broadly, even when subjects seek information adaptively, that behavior need not be tailored to the specific missing information. For example, Jordan et al. (2025) reported that capuchin monkeys searched more when information was occluded, but their search did not reliably track the specific location of the missing information. In addition, the task depends on sensitivity to the experimenter's baiting action and on memory for the baited location, which limits its applicability in some species. Although Call (2010) addressed this issue by manipulating reward quality (see also Gazes et al., 2023), information seeking in apes is not limited to locating hidden food. Language-trained chimpanzees also sought information about the identity of a food item before reporting its identity using lexigrams (Beran et al., 2013), suggesting that information seeking can extend beyond simple spatial search for food. These considerations highlight the need for additional paradigms that allow tighter control over stimuli, contingencies, and response options while also permitting stronger evaluation of alternative explanations.

Computer-based paradigms offer such an alternative. They are especially useful in rodents, for which evidence consistent with metacognitive monitoring or uncertaintysensitive responding has begun to emerge (Foote & Crystal, 2007; Templer et al., 2017; Yuki & Okanoya, 2017), but progress depends on tasks that can distinguish metacognitive interpretations from nonmetacognitive accounts based on first-order stimulus control or reinforcement history (Foote & Crystal, 2012; Hampton, 2009; Templer, 2019 Templer, , 2022)). Kirk et al. (2014) demonstrated information-seeking behavior in rats using a T-maze and an eight-arm radial maze, in which rats could make an additional response to obtain a cue indicating the correct goal location. Because the informational conditions were associated with different maze structures rather than varied randomly from trial to trial, however, their procedure did not directly test whether information seeking was adjusted to the immediate difficulty of each trial.

However, information-seeking behavior does not necessarily imply metacognitive monitoring. Castro and Wasserman (2013) illustrated this problem in pigeons. In their task, choosing an information response could acquire greater reinforcement value on difficult trials because obtaining the cue increased the probability of a correct choice, whereas this advantage was smaller on easy trials. Thus, difficultydependent information seeking could arise from differential reinforcement histories associated with the available responses without requiring the assumption that pigeons monitored their own uncertainty.

In a previous study, Hataji and Goto (2024) examined information seeking in mice using a touchscreen visual discrimination task in which selecting the information option eliminated the distractor stimulus. During training, mice used the information option more often when the discrimination was more difficult, a pattern compatible with a metacognitive interpretation. However, the critical test results suggested that this behavior was not explained by confidence monitoring alone and may also have reflected response competition and reinforcement history associated with target-directed responding. Thus, the earlier study showed that mice can acquire information-seeking behavior in a computerized visual discrimination task, while leaving unresolved whether such behavior is better explained by monitoring of the primary discrimination or by nonmetacognitive influences.

The present study addressed that issue using a targetcueing procedure. In the present task, selecting the information-seeking option did not remove one of the alternatives but instead produced a cue indicating the location of the S+. This procedure is closer to hint-seeking paradigms in which an optional response yields information about the correct action (e.g., Kornell et al., 2007), and, more broadly, to cue-producing response procedures in which an additional response generates discriminative information to guide subsequent choice (e.g., D'Amato & Fazzaro, 1966). Unlike the earlier distractor-elimination paradigm, the target-cueing procedure preserved the primary discrimination after the information-seeking response. This feature made it possible to assess separately whether mice requested the hint and whether they used it effectively once it was obtained. The procedure also provided a stricter test of the metacognitive account. If information seeking is guided by monitoring of confidence in the primary discrimination, mice should seek information more often under more difficult discriminations, reduce information seeking when additional information is unnecessary, and increase information seeking when the initial display is nondiagnostic. This logic is consistent with Foote and Crystal's (2012) aim of developing paradigms that can distinguish metacognitive from nonmetacognitive accounts.

Experiment 1 applied this target-cueing procedure to an orientation discrimination in which difficulty was manip-ulated by varying the orientation difference between the target and the distractor. The critical test phase included Target-Only (TO) trials, in which only the S+ was presented together with the information-seeking option, and Equiorientation (EO) trials, in which the two side stimuli were physically identical and the correct choice could not be determined from the initial display alone. Experiment 2 examined whether the same overall pattern would generalize to a grating discrimination in which difficulty was manipulated by grating coarseness. Across the two experiments, the aim was to clarify the extent to which information seeking in mice reflects confidence monitoring rather than task-specific influences such as response competition, reinforcement history, and the behavioral utility of the hint.

Experiment 1: Orientation Discrimination

The stimuli used in Experiment 1 and the informationseeking option are shown in Figure 1. During the initial training phase, the mice were trained to discriminate between a horizontal line (0° orientation) and a vertical line (90° orientation), each 120 pixels (3 cm) long. After the mice acquired this discrimination, four additional stimulus pairs were introduced: 10° and 80°, 20° and 70°, 30° and 60°, and 40° and 50°. Initially, the line stimuli were presented in white on a black background. When the information-seeking option was introduced, the line stimuli were changed to gray to increase task difficulty and promote information-seeking behavior; this gray corresponded approximately to RGB (64, 64, 64). The information-seeking option was displayed as a 180 × 180-pixel (4.5 × 4.5 cm) white dot-array icon on a black background. The icon comprised a 5 × 5 matrix of white circles whose diameters gradually increased from the upper left to the lower right.

Procedure

Pretraining. Mice first underwent habituation and magazine training in the operant conditioning chamber. They were then trained to nose touch a white circle (2.5 cm in diameter) presented centrally at the bottom of the monitor. After this response was acquired, the circle was presented at varying horizontal positions across trials. A trial-start signal was then introduced, and each trial began with the magazine light flashing at 250-ms intervals. The stimulus was presented when the mouse inserted its head into the magazine. This pretraining procedure was the same as that used in previous studies (Goto & Hataji, 2020; Goto & Watanabe, 2021).

Orientation Discrimination. The mice were divided into two groups. Half of the mice (AE01, AE03, and AE05) were trained with the more vertical stimulus as the positive stimulus (S+), whereas the remaining mice (AE02, AE04, and AE06) were trained with the more horizontal stimulus as the S+. During the orientation discrimination phase, each trial began with the magazine light flashing. The light turned off when the mouse inserted its head into the magazine. Two line stimuli with different orientations were then presented side by side on the monitor, separated by 12.5 cm. Mice received a food pellet for choosing the S+, whereas choosing the negative stimulus (S-) resulted in a 5-s timeout. Incorrect choices triggered a correction trial in which the same trial was repeated. This procedure was used to reduce the development of side biases. Correction trials were excluded from the analysis. The intertrial interval was 8 s, and each daily session consisted of 60 randomly inter-mixed trials, equally divided among the five stimulus pairs. Mice first received 20 sessions of discrimination training with horizontal and vertical lines, followed by 40 additional sessions in which the four new stimulus pairs with orientation differences of 10°, 30°, 50° and 70° were introduced. All training and testing sessions were conducted 5 days per week throughout the experiment.

Hint-Option Training. After completion of the orientation discrimination phase, an information-seeking option was introduced. This phase lasted 21 sessions, each consisting of 60 trials. Each trial began with the magazine light flashing. The light turned off when the mouse inserted its head into the magazine. An information-seeking option, shown in Figure 1, was then presented at the bottom center of the screen. When the mouse responded to this option, a flashing white frame was presented on either the left or right side of the information-seeking option. No orientation stimuli were presented during this phase. Mice were reinforced for a nose touch to within the flashing white frame, whereas a nose touch to the corresponding location on the opposite side resulted in a 5-s timeout. This procedure was used to establish an association between the information-seeking option and the subsequent appearance of the hint. Incorrect choices triggered a correction trial, which was excluded from the analysis. The intertrial interval was 8 s.

Information-Seeking Training. Following hint-option training, mice proceeded to the information-seeking (IS) training phase. From this phase onward, the S+ and S-stimuli were presented in gray rather than white (Figure 1; after darkening). Each session included three types of trials: baseline trials, forced IS trials, and optional-IS trials (Figure 2). Each daily session consisted of 20 baseline trials, 20 forced-IS trials, and 20 optional IS trials, for a total of 60 trials, excluding correction trials. Within each trial type, trials were equally divided among the five stimulus pairs with orientation differences of 10°, 30°, 50°, 70°, and 90°. In baseline trials, mice performed the standard orientation discrimination task without the IS option. In forced-IS trials, the magazine light flashed and turned off when the mouse inserted its head into the magazine. The IS option then appeared on the monitor. After the mouse responded to this option, the S+ and S-stimuli were presented 6.25 cm to the left and right of the IS option, and the S+ was surrounded by a flashing white frame that served as a hint indicating the correct choice. In optional IS trials, the magazine light again turned off after the mouse inserted its head into the magazine, and the IS option was presented together with the orientation discrimination stimuli. Mice could either respond directly to one of the side stimuli without touching the central IS option or first select the IS option and then make a side choice. Selecting the IS option caused a flashing white frame to appear around the S+, and the hint remained visible until the mouse chose one of the side stimuli. Selection of the IS option required an additional nose touch to the cen-trally located option, followed by a response to one of the peripheral choice stimuli. If the mouse responded directly to a side stimulus, the trial was completed without presentation of the hint. In all three trial types, correct choices were reinforced with a food pellet, a chime, and illumination of the magazine light for 5 s, whereas incorrect choices were followed by a buzzer and a 5-s timeout without pellet delivery. The 60 trials in each session were randomly ordered without additional constraints, with trials equally divided among the stimulus pairs. Each mouse completed 20 sessions.

Test. Following completion of IS training, testing began. This test was designed to evaluate whether IS behavior was controlled primarily by discrimination difficulty or by stimulus factors associated with the S+. Test sessions included baseline trials, IS trials, and two types of novel trials: Target-Only (TO) trials and Equiorientation (EO) trials. In baseline trials, mice performed the standard orientation discrimination without the IS option. In TO trials, only the S+ was presented on either the left or right side of the IS option. In EO trials, two identical copies of the S+ from the corresponding training pair were presented to the left and right of the IS option; therefore, the correct choice could not be determined from the initial display. Instead, the rewarded side was assigned randomly across trials, with the rewarded side assigned equally often to the left and right within each session. Each session consisted of 10 TO trials, 20 IS trials, 10 EO trials, and 20 baseline trials, for a total of 60 trials. Trial order within each session was randomized without additional constraints, and the five orientation-difference conditions were represented equally often. This phase lasted 20 sessions.

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted in R (R Core Team, 2025). Trial-level binary responses were analyzed using generalized linear mixed-effects models with a binomial error distribution and logit link, as implemented in the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015). Subject was included as a random intercept to account for repeated observations within individuals. Fixed effects were specified according to the question addressed in each analysis and included variables such as session, trial type, orientation difference, hint use, and their interactions, where appropriate.

Omnibus tests of fixed effects were evaluated using likelihood-ratio tests comparing nested models. Where appropriate, follow-up comparisons of estimated marginal means were conducted using the emmeans package (Lenth & Piaskowski, 2025), with Holm adjustment for multiple comparisons. Model diagnostics were evaluated using the DHARMa package (Hartig, 2024).

Effect sizes were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals for binomial generalized linear mixedeffects models. Discussion

Experiment 1 examined information-seeking behavior in an orientation discrimination task adapted from Hataji and Goto (2024). Mice acquired the discrimination, baseline accuracy increased with orientation difference, and, during information-seeking training, the accuracy advantage associated with hint use was greatest at smaller orientation differences. Considered in isolation, this pattern is compatible with a metacognitive account because the hint was most useful under more difficult discriminations. At the same time, the proportion of information-seeking responses did not vary reliably with orientation difference during training. Thus, although mice benefited from the hint particularly when the discrimination was more difficult, information seeking did not systematically track objective discrimination performance. Baseline accuracy increased with orientation difference, whereas information seeking did not vary reliably across orientation difference, providing little evidence that mice sought information more often when the discrimination was difficult or confidence was presumably low.

The more diagnostic evidence came from the test phase. If mice used the information-seeking response because they monitored confidence and sought additional information when confidence was low, information seeking should have decreased in TO trials, in which no additional information was required, and increased in EO trials, in which the target could not be identified from the initial display alone. This prediction was not supported. Instead, mice showed the highest proportion of information-seeking responses in TO trials, whereas EO trials produced the lowest proportion. This pattern indicates that the decision to seek information did not track informational demand in the straightforward manner predicted by a metacognitive account.

Importantly, EO-trial accuracy was higher when the hint was used, indicating that mice often exploited the obtained hint effectively once it was available. Although mice did not seek information in the pattern predicted by a metacognitive account, they often used the hint effectively when they obtained it. Therefore, the discrepancy cannot be attributed to a lack of hint value. Instead, the task appears to distinguish between choosing to seek information and using that information successfully after it is provided.

The especially high proportion of information-seeking responses in TO trials nevertheless suggests that response competition contributed to performance. Because TO trials reduced the number of available response alternatives, selection of the information-seeking option may have faced less competition from peripheral choice responses, thereby increasing the likelihood that it would be chosen. Importantly, the two stimuli presented on each EO trial were identical copies of the S+ from the corresponding discrimination pair and therefore had a history of reinforced responding as S+ stimuli. This reinforcement history may have made both peripheral stimuli relatively attractive compared with the central information-seeking option. The decline in information seeking across the corresponding orientation-difference conditions may likewise reflect differences in this reinforcement history: S+ stimuli from the easier discrimination conditions had been associated with higher rates of successful responding during training and may therefore have exerted stronger control over responding. From this perspective, information seeking was influenced not only by the informational value of the central option but also by the response context in which that option was embedded.

Taken together, Experiment 1 provides only limited support for a straightforward metacognitive interpretation. During training, hint use improved performance most clearly under more difficult discriminations, indicating that the hint could be behaviorally useful. However, mice did not seek the hint more often as orientation difference decreased, and the critical test yielded the opposite of the simple informational-demand prediction: Information seeking was highest in TO trials, in which additional information was unnecessary, and lowest in EO trials, in which the initial display was nondiagnostic. The most conservative conclusion, therefore, is that information seeking was not governed solely by objective discrimination performance and did not show the pattern expected if mice were monitoring confidence in the primary discrimination. The TO pattern is consistent with a contribution of response competition because the central information-seeking option may have faced less competition when only one peripheral target was present, but that mechanism was not isolated directly in the present experiment.

Experiment 2: Grating Discrimination

Experiment 2 examined whether the pattern observed in Experiment 1 would generalize to a different visual discrimination task. The procedure was the same as in Experiment 1, except that the orientation discrimination was replaced with a grating discrimination in which the S+ was a grating stimulus and the S-was a uniform gray stimulus. This manipulation served two purposes. First, it tested whether the pattern of information-seeking behavior observed in the target-cueing procedure would replicate in a different visual discrimination task. Second, it allowed us to evaluate whether the pattern was also consistent with that reported previously by Hataji and Goto (2024), despite differences in paradigm and stimulus structure. As in Experiment 1, selecting the information-seeking option produced a cue indicating the S+, thereby providing information that reliably predicted reward regardless of task difficulty. In this task, discrimination difficulty was determined by the spatial frequency of the grating stimulus. If information seeking is primarily governed by metacognitive monitoring, it should again vary with discrimination difficulty. By contrast, if the pattern observed in Experiment 1 depended on properties specific to the orientation discrimination task, it should not necessarily be reproduced in the present grating discrimination. Experiment 2 therefore provided a test of the robustness and generality of the pattern observed in Experiment 1.

Method

Stimuli

The stimuli used in Experiment 2 are shown in Figure 8. The S+ was a circular sinusoidal grating, and the S-was a uniform gray circle of the same size. Both stimuli were 120 pixels in diameter (3.0 cm). The cue stimulus was presented at the lower center of the screen and measured 180 × 180 pixels (4.5 × 4.5 cm). The grating appeared at either 45° or 135°, and its phase was randomized on each presentation. Grating orientation was not predictive of reward.

In the initial acquisition phase, the grating was presented at full contrast as a black-and-white sinusoidal pattern. During this phase, grating coarseness was fixed at 60 pix-els/cycle. In the subsequent phase, grating coarseness varied across trials among 1, 5, 10, 20, and 40 pixels/cycle, while the grating remained a full-contrast black-and-white pattern. Finally, when the information-seeking option was introduced, the stimuli were darkened by superimposing semiopaque black rectangles (opacity = .75) over the S+ and S-locations. This manipulation was intended to reduce stimulus visibility and thereby increase the likelihood of information-seeking responses.

Procedure Grating Discrimination

Experiment 2 was conducted after completion of Experiment 1 using the same six mice. The procedure was the same as in Experiment 1, except that the orientation discrimination was replaced with a grating discrimination in which the S+ was a grating stimulus and the S-was a uniform gray stimulus. The mice were first trained on a discrimination with a fixed grating coarseness. This initial training phase lasted 20 sessions of 60 trials each, and no individual acquisition criterion was used before progression to the next phase. After completion of the 20-session initial training phase, training proceeded to a second phase in which grating coarseness varied across trials. This phase also lasted 20 sessions, each consisting of 60 trials. Grating coarseness varied among 1, 5, 10, 20, and 40 pixels/cycle. This phase was introduced to establish discrimination performance across multiple grating stimuli before adding the information-seeking option.

Information-Seeking Training

Next, the mice received information-seeking (IS) training with darkened stimuli. As in Experiment 1, each mouse completed 20 sessions. Each session consisted of 20 baseline trials, 20 forced-IS trials, and 20 optional IS trials, for a total of 60 trials. In baseline trials, the two discrimination stimuli were presented simultaneously, and the mice responded directly to one of them. In forced-IS trials, the same IS option as in Experiment 1 was presented initially at the bottom center of the screen. A response to this option produced two discrimination stimuli, with the S+ surrounded by a flashing white frame that served as a cue indicating correct choice. In optional-IS trials, the IS option and both discrimination stimuli were presented simultaneously. Mice could either respond directly to one of the discrimination stimuli or first select the IS option and then make a side choice. If the IS option was selected, a flashing white frame appeared around the S+ and remained visible until the mouse chose one of the side stimuli. If the mouse responded directly to a side stimulus, the trial was completed without presentation of the cue. Trial order within each session was randomized without additional constraints, and the gratingcoarseness conditions were represented equally often.

Test

After IS training, the mice received test sessions. As in Experiment 1, each test session consisted of 20 baseline trials, 20 optional-IS trials, 10 Target-Only (TO) trials, and 10 Equicoarseness (EC) trials, for a total of 60 trials. In TO trials, only the S+ was presented together with the IS option. In EC trials, two identical copies of the S+ from the corresponding grating-coarseness conditions were presented to the left and right of the IS option; therefore, the correct choice could not be determined from the initial display. The rewarded side was therefore assigned randomly across trials, with the rewarded side balanced equally across the left and right positions within each session. Trial order within each session was randomized without additional constraints, and the grating-coarseness conditions were represented equally often across trials. Each mouse completed 20 test sessions.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted as in Experiment 1. For the initial acquisition phase, trial-level accuracy was analyzed as a function of session. For the variable grating-coarseness phase and the information-seeking training phase, grating coarseness was included as a fixed effect. For analyses of accuracy on IS trials, hint use and the Hint Use × Grating Coarseness interaction were included as fixed effects. In the test phase, hint-use responses were analyzed as a function of trial type, grating coarseness, and their interaction. Significant interactions were followed by Holm-adjusted pairwise comparisons of estimated marginal means.

Finally, we examined accuracy on the critical EC trials as a function of hint use (Figure 13B; individual-subject data are shown in Figure S8B). The Grating Coarseness × Hint Use interaction was not significant, χ²(4) = 1.37, p = .849. In an additive model, there was no significant main effect of grating coarseness, χ²(4) = 1.21, p = .877, whereas the main effect of hint use was significant, χ²(1) = 47.13, p < .001. Accuracy was higher on trials in which the hint was used than on trials in which it was not used at all five grating coarseness values (ORs for Hint used relative to Hint not used = 2.21-3.68, Holm-adjusted ps ≤ .024). Thus, although the association between hint use and accuracy did not vary reliably across the EC trial conditions, EC-trial accuracy was consistently higher when mice used the hint.

Discussion

Experiment 2 extended the findings of Experiment 1 by examining information-seeking behavior in a grating discrimination task. Mice acquired the grating discrimination, baseline accuracy increased with grating coarseness, and, during information-seeking training, mice sought the hint more often at lower grating coarseness. In addition, the accuracy advantage associated with hint use was greatest at lower grating coarseness. Considered on their own, these training results are compatible with a metacognitive account because both hint seeking and hint benefit were greatest when the primary discrimination was more difficult.

However, the more diagnostic evidence came from the test phase. If mice used the information-seeking response mainly because they monitored low confidence and sought additional information when needed, information seeking should have decreased in TO trials, in which no additional information was required, and increased in EC trials, in which the target could not be identified from the initial display alone. That pattern was not obtained. Instead, mice showed the highest level of information seeking in TO trials, even though those trials did not require additional information, whereas EC trials did not consistently elicit the highest level of seeking. Thus, as in Experiment 1, the critical test results indicate that information seeking did not track informational demand alone.

At the same time, EC-trial accuracy was consistently higher when the hint was used, indicating that mice often exploited the obtained hint effectively once it was available. The mismatch between hint-seeking frequency and the metacognitive prediction therefore cannot be explained simply by the hint having little behavioral value. Rather, the present task again separated two components of performance: the decision to request the hint and the ability to use it effectively once obtained.

The test pattern is also informative about possible nonmetacognitive influences on behavior. The especially high level of information seeking in TO trials is consistent with a contribution of response competition because the central information-seeking option may have faced less competition when only one peripheral target was available. In addition, information seeking remained more frequent at lower grating coarseness across trial types, including TO trials, in which additional information was not required. This pattern is consistent with the possibility that lower grating coarseness had acquired a weaker history of successful target-directed responding, thereby increasing the probability that the information-seeking option would be selected instead. These interpretations remain tentative because the present experiment did not isolate these mechanisms directly. Accordingly, the present results support the narrower conclusion that information seeking was influenced by factors other than confidence monitoring, while remaining compatible with contributions from response competition and reinforcement history.

Taken together, Experiment 2 strengthens the interpretation from Experiment 1. Although the training results were compatible with a metacognitive account, the critical test results did not support the claim that mice sought information primarily when additional information was needed. The broader conclusion is therefore not that metacognitive control was demonstrated and then qualified, but that information seeking in the present target-cueing procedure was influenced by multiple determinants, including the behavioral utility of the hint and nonmetacognitive factors such as response competition and reinforcement history.

General Discussion

The present study examined the extent to which information seeking in a target-cueing procedure reflects confidence monitoring and nonmetacognitive factors associated with target-directed responding. Across two visual discrimination tasks, the most informative pattern came from the contrast between training and test. During training, several aspects of performance were compatible with a metacognitive account. In Experiment 1, the association between hint use and higher accuracy was clearest at smaller orientation dif-ferences, although the frequency of information seeking did not vary reliably with difficulty. In Experiment 2, mice sought the hint more often when the grating discrimination was more difficult, and the accuracy advantage associated with hint use was greatest for finer gratings. Thus, across both tasks, the hint was behaviorally useful, and, in Experiment 2, hint seeking also covaried with difficulty. On their own, however, such training patterns are not decisive evidence for metacognitive monitoring because behavior can covary adaptively with task difficulty for reasons other than monitoring one's own confidence. Although obtaining the hint was not explicitly penalized, information seeking carried a small response and movement cost. Selecting the hint required an additional nose touch to the centrally located information-seeking option, followed by movement to and a response on one of the peripheral choice stimuli. Such costs may have discouraged indiscriminate use of the hint.

More diagnostic evidence came from the test conditions because those conditions dissociated informational demand from other features of the response context. If mice sought information primarily because they monitored low confidence in the discrimination, information seeking should have been reduced in Target-Only (TO) trials, in which no additional information was needed, and elevated in Equiorientation (EO) or Equicoarseness (EC) trials, in which the target could not be identified from the initial display. That pattern was not obtained. In both experiments, TO trials elicited particularly high levels of information seeking, whereas EO and EC trials did not consistently elicit the highest levels of seeking. The critical implication is that information seeking in the present task did not track informational demand alone. The strongest conclusion from the present data is therefore not that metacognitive control was demonstrated imperfectly, but that hint seeking was influenced by multiple sources of control.

At the same time, the EO and EC accuracy analyses clarify an important methodological advantage of the present target-cueing procedure. In both experiments, accuracy in the critical EO or EC trials was higher overall when the hint was used than when it was not used. Thus, the mice did not merely emit an information-seeking response but often used the requested hint effectively once it was obtained. This point does not establish that the decision to seek the hint was itself metacognitive. It does, however, strengthen the methodological value of the present procedure because it permits separate assessment of requesting information and using that information successfully to guide choice. Relative to the earlier distractor-elimination paradigm (Hataji & Goto, 2024), the target-cueing task therefore allows a more direct assessment of how obtained hints guide subsequent choices even when the determinants of hint seeking remain mixed.

This overall behavioral organization resembles that reported by Hataji and Goto (2024), despite the procedural change from distractor elimination to target cueing. In both studies, the critical tests showed that mice often sought information in TO trials even when information seeking was redundant. Considered together, the two studies therefore make a simple informational-demand account difficult to maintain and suggest that first-order task variables also contributed to performance. This concern parallels the account proposed by Castro and Wasserman (2013), who noted that information seeking can covary with task difficulty because the information response acquires greater relative reinforcement value on difficult trials. From this perspective, the difficulty-dependent information seeking observed in Experiment 2 does not uniquely imply monitoring of confidence, because a similar pattern could arise from differential reinforcement histories associated with the available responses. A separate but complementary possibility is response competition. In the present task, TO trials contained only two available responses, the target and the information-seeking option, whereas standard information-seeking and EO or EC trials contained three. This reduction may have increased the probability of selecting the information-seeking option by reducing competition from peripheral choice responses. Because the number of available response alternatives differed between TO trials and the other test conditions, the present design does not allow the effects of informational demand and response competition to be fully dissociated. Future studies could address this response-competition account by equating the number of available response alternatives across test conditions. One straightforward approach would be to add a dummy distractor to Target-Only trials so that mice choose among three response options, as in the standard information-seeking conditions, while the target itself remains directly identifiable. This manipulation would allow the effect of informational demand to be assessed without confounding it with the number of competing responses.

A second source of first-order control concerns the reinforcement history associated with responding to the S+ stimuli themselves, and the different effects of difficulty across the two experiments may reflect how difficulty was implemented. In Experiment 2, grating coarseness affected information seeking even in TO and EC trials, although grating coarseness should not have changed whether additional information was needed in those test conditions. This pattern is compatible with the possibility that harder gratings had acquired a weaker history of successful targetdirected responding. Such an effect may have been more evident in Experiment 2 because the grating manipulation primarily changed the S+, whereas the S-remained a uniform gray circle. Finer gratings, especially after the darkening manipulation, may have exerted weaker excitatory control over target-directed responding or may have become more functionally similar to the S-. In Experiment 1, by contrast, increasing difficulty moved both the S+ and S-toward the middle of the orientation continuum, which may have reduced discriminability without selectively weakening targetdirected responding to the S+. These interpretations were not isolated experimentally here. Accordingly, the present data do not identify the separate causal contributions of response competition and reinforcement history. Rather, they constrain interpretation by showing that hint seeking was not governed by informational demand alone and by identifying patterns that are consistent with these alternative sources of control.

The training regimen itself may also have contributed to the relatively high level of information seeking observed in the test phase. During forced-information-seeking trials, selection of the information-seeking option was repeatedly followed by a valid cue and, when followed by the correct choice, reinforcement. This history may have strengthened responding to the information-seeking option and promoted its use even when additional information was unnecessary. However, information seeking was not completely indiscriminate, particularly in Experiment 2, where it varied systematically with discrimination difficulty. Thus, training history may have contributed to the overall tendency to seek information without fully accounting for the trial-by-trial pattern of information seeking.

A further limitation concerns the interpretation of behavior after the hint was obtained. Because responding to the flashing white frame was repeatedly reinforced during training, the present design cannot determine whether the frame was used as an informative cue indicating the correct stimulus or whether it had itself acquired control over responding through reinforcement history. Future studies could address this issue by comparing conditions in which the white frame validly indicates the correct stimulus with conditions in which it is presented independently of the correct choice. If mice respond to the frame because of its informational value rather than merely because of a learned tendency to approach it, responding should decrease when the frame is no longer predictive of the correct choice.

Comparative studies also suggest species differences in the flexibility of information seeking. Pigeons showed little evidence of spontaneously seeking access to an unseen sample before making a matching response (Roberts et al., 2009). In contrast, both rhesus macaques and capuchin monkeys succeeded on a similar basic information-seeking test, but only rhesus macaques subsequently showed flexible adjustment of information seeking across changing task demands (Beran & Smith, 2011). More generally, evidence for flexible metacognitive control appears to be less consistent in capuchin monkeys than in rhesus macaques and apes, despite successful information seeking by capuchins in some tasks. Together with findings from other species and paradigms, these results indicate substantial variation in the flexibility and control of information-seeking behavior across species, tasks, and testing conditions (Basile et al., 2015; Brady & Hampton, 2021; Call & Carpenter, 2001; Castro & Wasserman, 2013; Hampton et al., 2004; Iwasaki et al., 2013; Kornell et al., 2007; Subias et al., 2025; Templer, 2019; Templer et al., 2017; Yuki & Okanoya, 2017).

The present findings should therefore not be taken to mean that metacognitive control was absent. A more cautious conclusion is that information seeking in the present task was multiply determined. The present results are informative not because they yield a simple positive or negative answer but because they specify more clearly which variables appear to govern information seeking in a controlled touchscreen procedure. The main contribution of the present study is therefore methodological as well as theoretical. The target-cueing procedure made it possible to separate requesting the hint from using it effectively once it was obtained, and the present results show that these two components need not be governed by the same determinants.

Several points define the scope of the present conclusions. The use of the same six mice across both experiments is a strength because it shows that the main behavioral pattern was reproduced within the same individuals across two different visual discriminations. At the same time, because Experiment 2 followed Experiment 1 in the same animals, transfer, task familiarity, and procedural carryover cannot be fully separated from generalization across stimulus domains. Moreover, although information seeking was not expressed indiscriminately, the key remaining question is whether the present behavior reflects task-general monitoring of confidence or task-specific stimulus control (Basile & Hampton, 2014). Because both experiments used the same basic touchscreen task and the same information-seeking response, an important next test will be whether information seeking transfers adaptively to a novel task with different stimulus dimensions or response requirements. Such transfer would provide stronger evidence against an account based solely on task-specific learning and reinforcement history (e.g., Kornell et al., 2007; Templer et al., 2017), but would not by itself establish metacognitive control, because apparently adaptive transfer may also arise from stimulusindependent, low-level response mechanisms (Crystal & Foote, 2009).

In conclusion, the present target-cueing experiments indicate that information seeking in mice was not governed by confidence alone. Training performance showed adaptive use of the hint, especially under more difficult discriminations, whereas the critical test phases revealed systematic effects of trial structure that were inconsistent with a straightforward metacognitive account. Some aspects of performance remained compatible with processes related to confidence monitoring, but the most defensible conclusion is that information seeking in the present task was multiply determined. This conclusion does not weaken the value of the mouse model. Rather, it clarifies what future paradigms must control if they are to isolate confidence-based informa-tion seeking more convincingly.
