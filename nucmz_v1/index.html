Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Mental health problems are increasingly recognised as a major challenge within higher education (Auerbach et al., 2016; 2018). Recent international population-based studies utilizing screening instruments indicate a high prevalence of probable mental health conditions and clinically relevant symptom levels among university students (ibidem).

Specifically, published data from 2016 by the World Mental Health Survey Initiative conducted across 21 countries indicate that approximately one in five university students (20.3%) meets the diagnostic criteria for a DSM-IV mental disorder within a 12-month period (Auerbach et al., 2016). Within this population, anxiety disorders represent the most prevalent class of conditions, followed by mood, substance use, and behavioral disorders.

Importantly, the vast majority of students reporting a 12-month disorder (83.1%) indicated symptom onset prior to university enrollment. This suggests that higher education institutions host a substantial number of individuals who are already vulnerable to psychological difficulties before entering campus (ibidem).

Recent empirical evidence suggests that overall levels of psychological distress among young adults may also be significant. According to a landmark global umbrella review published in 2025, 13.42% and 16.78% of university students worldwide experienced severe depressive and anxiety symptoms, respectively, while prevalence estimates rose to approximately one in three when milder or overall symptom levels were also considered (Paiva et al., 2025). Additionally, Brunette et al. (2023) documented a substantial rise in past-year Major Depressive Episodes (MDE) with functional impairment among US young adults (aged 18-24), with rates more than doubling from 5.2% in 2011 to 13.3% in 2021.

Collectively, these figures point to a substantial and persistent burden of psychological distress within recent cohorts of young adults, highlighting the widespread presence of clinically significant symptoms.

Whitin the Italian national context, Nosè and colleagues (2025) published one of the most recent and largest cross-sectional study to date. They investigated mental health disorders among 1766 Italian students aged 18-25 years from Verona and found substantial levels of psychological distress: moderate and severe depressive symptoms were reported by 9.0% and 3.2% of participants, while moderate and severe anxiety symptoms were reported by 25.4% and 10.7% of students (Nosè et al., 2025). It is worth pointing out this study also included high school students (11%) which may limit its generalizability to university populations. Interestingly, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 27 studies representing a total of 22,928 Italian college students (Gambolò et al., 2025) reported pooled prevalence rates of 46% for depressive symptoms and 65% for anxiety. While these rates are higher than the clinical thresholds reported in localized studies like Nosè et al. (2025), the authors observed a high heterogeneity in the collected data. They partly attributed this variance to the different psychometric instruments and scales used in the studies (Gambolò et al., 2025).

1.1.2 University students as a vulnerable population

The high burden of mental health problems among university students may partly reflect the convergence of academic pressures, social challenges, and major developmental transitions that characterize this stage of life (Mosconi et al., 2026). To critically analyze the psychological vulnerabilities of university students, it may be useful to situate this demographic within Jeffrey Arnett's developmental theory of emerging adulthood (Arnett, 2000). Spanning the late teens through the twenties, more specifically between the ages 18 to 25, emerging adulthood is conceptualized by Arnett as a developmental stage that is theoretically and structurally distinct from both adolescence and young adulthood. This period is uniquely characterized by relative independence from traditional social roles and normative expectations. Having left behind the structured dependencies of childhood and adolescence, but not yet having entered the enduring, stabilizing responsibilities of full adulthood (such as marriage, parenthood, or long-term career commitments), emerging adults operate in a transitional "roleless role" (Arnett, 2000, p.471).

This unstructured developmental space, while full of opportunity, introduces distinct psychological vulnerabilities that can compromise students' mental well-being (Arnett, 2000): • Systemic Instability and Residential Mobility: Unlike adolescence, emerging adulthood is characterized by less stable and less normatively structured living arrangements, particularly regarding residence, education, work, and family roles.

The residential status of emerging adults is highly volatile. This segment of the population exhibits the highest rates of residential mobility of any age group, peaking in the mid-twenties. For college students, this often involves negotiating semiautonomous living arrangements (such as dormitories or temporary shared apartments) and frequently moving back and forth between parental and independent homes.

• Subjective Ambiguity: Subjectively, the vast majority of university-aged individuals do not feel they have fully reached adulthood, typically reporting that they feel adult "in some respects yes, in some respects no". This sense of ambiguity arises because the criteria they value most for achieving adulthood are not demographic milestones (like marriage or graduation) but demanding, individualistic qualities of character. Specifically, accepting responsibility for oneself, making independent decisions, and attaining financial independence are viewed as the essential markers of adulthood.

• Identity Exploration: Although emerging adulthood is that period of life that offers unparalleled opportunities to explore diverse paths in love, work, and worldviews. These identity exploration opportunities can reasonably be expected to be stressful and isolating experiences. Trying on different life directions often results in romantic disappointment, academic disillusionment, or the unsettling deconstruction of childhood beliefs without a clear replacement. Furthermore, emerging adults tend to navigate these difficult transitions in isolation. Young people in this age bracket spend significantly more of their productive and leisure time alone than almost any other demographic under forty, leaving them highly vulnerable to loneliness and depressive symptoms.

• Peak Vulnerability for High-Risk Behaviors: Driven by a combination of low parental surveillance, high sensation-seeking drives, and the absence of stabilizing adult roles, emerging adulthood represents the developmental peak for several dangerous behaviors. Rates of substance abuse, binge drinking, unprotected sex, and reckless driving all reach their lifetime highs between the ages of 18 and 25. Arnett theorizes that these behaviors are often explored as a way to gain novel experiences before settling down. Neurobiological evidence (Steinberg, 2008) suggests that the higher prevalence of risk-taking behavior between the ages of 18 to 25 is potentially explained by structural and functional changes in the brain's socio emotional system, particularly the remodeling of the dopaminergic system, leading to increased reward-seeking. As prefrontal cortical connectivity continues to mature during emerging adulthood, the regulatory processes involved in impulse control and risktaking may remain under development throughout this period (Steinberg, 2008). It can't be ignored that risky behaviors can also act as maladaptive coping mechanisms for underlying psychological distress, exacerbating the clinical risks this population already faces.

Ultimately, recognizing university students as emerging adults highlights that their psychological distress is not merely a collection of isolated clinical symptoms.

Instead, it is deeply rooted in a highly volatile, unstructured, and often lonely developmental transition, which may help explain the heightened vulnerability to mental health difficulties during the university years.

In addition to these shared developmental challenges, individuals in higher education must contend with a unique set of student-specific demands that distinguish their experience from the broader non-student demographic. University students are exposed to sustained cognitive demands, performance pressure, and psychosocial stressors that have been associated with elevated rates of anxiety and depressive symptoms (Wunsch et al., 2021).

In a qualitative review, Hurst et al. (2012) identify seven categories amongst the most commonly reported stressors. These include: academic demands, interpersonal relationships (mostly family-related stressors), expectations, financial and material resources, environmental factors, diversity-related challenges and life transitions (Hurst et al., 2012).

1.1.3 Consequences for functioning and quality of life

The consequences of this mental health burden extend across multiple areas of students' lives, including quality of life, social functioning, academic performance, and the risk of self-harm and suicidal behaviours (Wilks et al., 2020). The World Health Organization (2016) places suicide as the second leading cause of death worldwide for individuals aged 15-29. Empirical evidence from a global meta-analysis indicates that suicidal thoughts and behaviors (STB) are alarmingly common in this demographic (Mortier et al., 2018).

Specifically, pooled lifetime prevalence estimates show that nearly one in four college students (22.3%) experience suicidal ideation, while 6.1% formulate a suicide plan, and 3.2% make a suicide attempt. It must be said that prevalence estimates of STB amongst college students vary widely (Mortier et al., 2018). Consequently, the exact magnitude of suicide thoughts and behavior among college students around the world is currently unknown (ibidem). In the Italian university system, a systematic review estimated suicide risk among students at 6.1% (Gambolò et al., 2025).

Furthermore, the progression from thought to action within this population is highly critical: approximately 40% to 45% of students who experience suicidal ideation also report formulating a plan, and about 20% progress to an active suicide attempt. (Mortier et al., 2018).

To contextualize this data, Rory O'Connor's integrated motivational-volition model of suicidal behavior (O'Connor & Kirtley, 2018) provides a "ideation to action" framework by splitting the suicidal process into two distinct stages. First, the motivational phase explains how suicidal thoughts develop. Under intense stress, students can experience severe feelings of defeat. If they perceive no escape from this psychological pain, they enter a state of inescapable entrapment, which acts as the direct cognitive bridge to suicidal ideation (ibidem). Crucially, research has shown that entrapment is a robust predictor of depression, and this association seems to be partly mediated by hopelessness (Choi & Shin, 2023).

Second, the volitional phase governs the critical transition from thoughts to actual behavior. This transition is moderated by volitional moderators such as access to methods and specific personological traits (e.g. impulsivity, fearlessness about death). Distinguishing between these two phases is clinically essential, as it explains the stark epidemiological discrepancy between the large percentage of students who experience lifetime suicidal thoughts and the much smaller, distinct subgroup who progress to active suicide attempts (Mortier et al., 2018).

Even though suicide is the most extreme outcome of mental health struggles, its risk must be carefully evaluated and managed by mental health professionals, especially in university settings. To this day, suicide remains a heavily stigmatized topic, often due to the fear that discussing it openly might trigger copycat behaviors (Romaniello, 2021). However, the impact of suicide goes far beyond the individual, deeply affecting families, friends, and entire communities. As a result, identifying risk and protective factors that may inform effective public health action represents an important priority.

1.2 Physical Activity, Sedentariness and Mental Health

Against this background, physical activity has emerged as a promising factor associated with mental health and well-being. There is an increasing amount of evidence documenting the beneficial impacts of physical activity (PA) on mental health (MH) (Biddle, 2016; Ahsan & Abualait, 2025). Sedentary behaviour has also emerged as an important correlate of mental health, with systematic reviews and meta-analyses consistently showing associations between greater sedentary time and poorer psychological well-being, higher levels of depressive and anxiety symptoms, and an increased risk of other common mental disorders (Firth et al., 2020; Hallgren et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2016; Zhai et al., 2015).

1.2.1 Evidence for preventative and therapeutic effects

Physical activity and sedentariness have been extensively investigated in relation to mental health and psychological well-being. The current state of research seems to suggest that physical activity may serve both therapeutic and preventive functions. A substantial body of evidence indicates that physically active individuals experience lower levels of depression, anxiety, and psychological distress, while exercise interventions have been shown to improve symptoms of depression and other common mental disorders (Kang et al., 2020; Ross et al., 2023; Singh et al., 2023). Remarkably, some evidence even suggests that the effectiveness of exercise may be comparable to that of established treatments for depression, including psychotherapy and pharmacological interventions, while potentially carrying less stigma (Babyak et al., 2000; Thomas et al., 2020). Specifically, strength training has antidepressant effects in people with depression or depressive symptoms, above and beyond psychosocial aspects alone (Rossi et al., 2024). PA is also highly beneficial for improving symptoms of anxiety and distress across a wide range of adult populations, including the general population, people with diagnosed mental health disorders and people with chronic disease (Singh et al., 2023). These findings support the inclusion of physical activity as a component in the management of depression and anxiety. Exercise interventions may also provide effective treatment for stress-related disorders, psychotic disorders, and ADHD (Firth et al., 2020). Additionally, it can be an effective supplement in the fight against use disorder by managing cravings (Mahindru et al., 2023).

In addition to its therapeutic role, regular physical activity has also been associated with a lower risk of developing depression and anxiety disorders altogether. This protective effect exhibits a distinct dose-response relationship, whereby higher volumes of physical activity are associated with a progressively lower risk of developing depressive symptoms (Dishman et al., 2021; McDowell et al., 2019). Interestingly, this is observed regardless of global region, gender, age, follow-up period, and timing or measures (Dishman et al., 2021). The same holds true for anxiety disorders. Engaging in physical activity protects against anxiety symptoms and disorders. (McDowell et al., 2019). Assuming causality, 1 in 9 cases of depression might have been prevented if everybody in the population was active at the level of current health recommendations. (Pearce et al., 2022). Current recommendations pertain largely to aerobic activity and cardiorespiratory fitness as the focus of exercise interventions. It should be noted that there is now some evidence that muscular strength and resistance training are just as protective against mental illness (Firth et al., 2020). Against this background, the relevance of physical activity and sedentariness may be particularly pronounced in higher education settings because they represent modifiable behaviours that can be targeted through large-scale prevention and health promotion initiatives.

1.2.2 Heterogeneity and moderators

The relationship between physical activity and mental health appears more complex than a simple linear association. Evidence suggests that the magnitude and direction of associations may vary according to certain moderators which may play a role in psychological outcomes: intensity (low, moderate, vigorous), type (yoga, strength training, cardio, stretching), contact with nature, frequency and context (Eather et al., 2023; Lubans et al., 2016; Mahindru et al., 2023; Rossi et al., 2024).

For example, Klussman et al. (2021) reported inconsistent relationships between physical activity, health, and well-being, highlighting the importance of considering activity intensity and type. Similarly, Bell et al. (2019) found no strong evidence that physical activity was directly associated with better overall mental well-being or reduced symptoms of mental disorder among adolescents, although a protective association emerged for emotional problems. These findings suggest that the relationship between physical activity and mental health may be more nuanced than commonly assumed, with benefits potentially emerging for specific psychological domains rather than across all indicators of mental health.

Additional evidence suggests that the context in which physical activity occurs may also be relevant, with leisure-time physical activity appearing particularly beneficial for promoting mental health and reducing mental ill-health (Teychenne et al., 2026). Conversely, when physical activity occurs in poorly designed or negative environments, it can actively undermine psychological wellbeing.

Compulsive exercise provides an interesting example of this potential tension. Compulsive exercise is a concept first described in the 1970s. It is defined by an intense urge to train, which leads to uncontrollable and excessive workouts (Lichtenstein et al., 2017). As the authors explain, this behavior often has negative effects, including physical injuries and strained social relationships. Although it is widely discussed, compulsive exercise is not yet officially recognized as a mental disorder in diagnostic manuals like the DSM or the ICD.

Interestingly, according to Weinstein et al (2015), as cited in Lichtenstein et al., 2017, a positive link between compulsive exercise and depressive symptoms has been observed in both professional and amateur athletes.

Thus, the relationship between physical activity and mental health cannot simply be reduced to a "more is better" model.

However, understanding the relationship between physical activity and mental health requires consideration of how mental health itself is conceptualized and measured. Much of the literature has focused on psychopathological symptoms and diagnostic outcomes, which provide important information about the presence and severity of mental disorders but may not fully capture individuals' broader psychological experience.

1.3 Diagnosis, symptoms and the lived experience of mental disorder

In clinical psychology and psychiatry, there is a growing debate about how we should understand mental health conditions. Traditional psychiatric approaches, heavily influenced by biological reductionism, often view mental disorders as simple brain dysfunctions or chemical imbalances (Liccione. 2013). In this view, diagnosing a patient means matching their behaviors with a static category in manuals like the DSM. Clinical psychology defines this diagnostic process as "nomothetic" which stands in stark contrast to the alternative, the "idiographic" approach. While an idiographic focus concentrates on the irreplaceable specificity and uniqueness of the individual, it risks producing clinical formulations that are unscientific, non-verifiable, and impossible to generalize; conversely, a purely nomothetic approach seeks universal laws and recurrent patterns but risks equating the patient to an inanimate object (Dazzi et al., 2009).

In doing so, as Liccione (2013) argues, this nosographical categorization fails to see the unique person (a "Who") and instead treats the individual as a mere physical "thing" (Körper). When a clinical diagnosis like depression is applied, the patient's individual history is often lost in favor of a general label. This risk of turning a person into a static category connects directly with the clinical perspective of Vittorio Lingiardi in his work Diagnosi e destino (2018). Lingiardi is an Italian psychiatrist, psychoanalyst, and Full Professor of Clinical Psychology at Sapienza University of Rome. He argues that a diagnosis should never be a deterministic "destiny" or a label that defines a person's entire identity. Instead, it must serve as a dynamic map within the therapeutic relationship, helping clinicians understand the patient's unique way of suffering. Therefore, to truly understand mental illness, we must look beyond objective symptom severity and focus on the patient's lived experience. In this context, integrating subjectivity into diagnostic assessment does not imply an extreme, unscientific subjectivism, but rather serves to "give meaning to the diagnosis (Dazzi et al., 2009). It's this very same philosophy that has helped shape the Psychodiagnostic Manual (PDM-2), of which he and Nancy McWilliams are authors. The PDM-2 stands in direct contrast to the DSM's descriptive nosology. Unlike the DSM, which groups disorders based on checklists of observable symptoms, the PDM adopts a psychodynamically oriented, dimensional framework. It evaluates the depth of personality organization, mental capacities, and the subjective experience of symptoms. The clinical philosophy behind this manual stands in contrast to the traditional medical-positivist model -which relies on generalizable theoretical constructs and views subjectivity as redundant or even detrimental -as well as classical psychoanalysis, which historically rejected descriptive categorization to the point of treating "diagnosis" as a dirty word (Dazzi et al., 2009).

Against this background, the symptom-reduction focus of the previous literature reviewed above reveals a significant conceptual and clinical limitation in how the relationship between movement and mental health has been approached. Research in university settings has predominantly examined how physical activity and sedentary behavior relate to symptoms of depression, anxiety, and psychological distress in student populations as a whole. In other words, conceptually most studies have operated under a reductionist model that treats the student's body merely as a physical object (Körper). In this view, physical activity is analyzed almost mechanically, focusing on how exercise can reduce score severity on standard symptom checklists of anxiety and depression. This symptom-centered approach mirrors the descriptive, category-based models of the DSM, which have been criticized by clinicians like Vittorio Lingiardi for ignoring the patient's subjective experience. By focusing only on biological and chemical mechanisms, past research has conceptually detached physical activity from the lived body (Leib), the actual vehicle of our actions, personal meanings, and relationship with the world (Liccione, 2013).

Comparatively less attention has been devoted to students experiencing mental disorders, for whom the relevant question may concern not only whether physical activity and reduced sedentary time are associated with lower condition-specific symptom levels, but also whether they relate to broader aspects of well-being. This reductionist blind spot may also explain many of the inconsistencies and contradictory findings observed in the existing literature, we discussed previously. A simple count of active minutes or step counts cannot distinguish between a student engaging in joyful, autonomous leisure-time exercise which is consistently linked to better mental health (Teychenne et al., 2026) and a student engaging in rigid, compulsive, or compensatory movement driven by psychological distress (Lichtenstein et al., 2017). Because the reductionist approach aggregates these vastly different subjective experiences into the same quantitative data, the underlying clinical realities might be obscured, leading to mixed and confusing statistical outcomes. Furthermore, by focusing strictly on symptom-reduction checklists, past studies might miss the broader, positive changes in how active students evaluate their lives (life satisfaction) or view their future (hopelessness), even when their baseline clinical symptoms remain unchanged. Therefore, shifting the focus from objective symptom severity to the subjective experience of functioning is not just a theoretical preference; but it might be a methodological necessity to resolve the contradictions in current psychiatric and sports science literature.

1.3.1 From Psychopathology to subjective functioning

To address this issue, contemporary research increasingly recognizes that standardized measures of symptom severity often fail to capture subjective experience of living with a mental disorder, as comparable scores may correspond to markedly different personal circumstances and perceptions (Correll et al., 2025). As Correll and colleagues (2025) point out, relying strictly on categorical, symptom-based checklists carries the risk of dehumanizing the patient by reducing their complex existence to a mere checklist of deficits. To address this limitation, contemporary clinical psychiatry is shifting its focus toward patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) that capture dimensions beyond simple symptom reduction.

Within this framework, a crucial distinction must be made between "functional recovery" defined as the observable ability to perform daily, social, and academic activities, and "personal recovery" (Correll et al., 2025). As Correll and colleagues explain, personal recovery represents a deeply subjective, unique process of building a satisfying, hopeful, and contributing life, even within the limitations potentially caused by a mental health condition. For university students experiencing psychological difficulties, clinical recovery is about much more than just lowering a symptom score on a questionnaire. The most relevant question is whether movement behaviors relate not only to functional performance but also to these deeper, subjective aspects of personal recovery.

By failing to investigate how physical activity and sedentary behavior relate to these broader, subjective dimensions, such as self-rated mental health, life satisfaction, and hopelessness, the existing literature misses the opportunity to evaluate movement as a transdiagnostic resource that supports the student's overall sense of agency and future outlook.

1.3.2 Self rated mental health

Self-rated mental health represents an individual's subjective evaluation of their own mental health status and has been shown to correlate with objective measures of mental health such as symptom severity, functional impairment, and broader health outcomes (McAlpine et al., 2018). A debate persists in the literature regarding the scientific validity of this construct. Although self-rated mental health is moderately correlated with standardized measures of distress and depression, a significant proportion of people who meet the clinical criteria for a mental disorder still evaluate their own mental health positively. Specifically, evidence shows that despite experiencing active symptoms or having a formal medical diagnosis for conditions such as major depression or anxiety disorders, about half of these individuals rate their overall mental health as good, very good, or excellent (Jang et al., 2015; Mawani & Gilmour, 2010; as cited in McAlpine et al., 2018).

Some researchers view SRMH as an indicator of how well individuals recognize their own symptoms as signs of a mental illness. From this perspective, individuals who meet the criteria for a mental disorder but still rate their mental health positively are often described as "mismatched" or incongruent (ibidem). From a traditional, symptom-based psychiatric perspective, this gap is seen as a cognitive mismatch or a lack of clinical insight. The assumption is that these individuals simply fail to recognize their symptoms as signs of a mental illness. In other words, they are indeed rating their mental health "better than it actually is" because they are either in denial or lack the literacy to understand their clinical condition. Traditional frameworks treat standardized DSM criteria and symptom scales as the objective 'gold standard' for mental illness. From this perspective, because self-rated mental health (SRMH) does not always align strongly with these clinical measures, it is often dismissed as an invalid indicator. However, McAlpine et al. (2018) offer a compelling alternative explanation. They argue that these individuals are not wrong or in denial about their symptoms. Rather, it could also be they do not define their overall mental health solely by the presence of clinical symptoms. An individual experiencing sadness or sleep issues after a breakup or an academic challenge may acknowledge these struggles but view them as normal and not symptoms of a psychiatric disease. Because they factor in these temporary life circumstances, they still evaluate their overall mental health positively. A student might actively screen positive for generalized anxiety but also have strong social support and excellent coping strategies. They recognize their anxiety, but because they are successfully navigating their life, they still rate their overall mental health as "good". In other words, this perspective challenges the assumption that standardized, symptom-based scales are completely objective measures of psychiatric conditions, highlighting instead that the boundaries between health and clinical disorder are highly amorphous. Many common symptoms of psychological distress represent expected, natural human reactions to challenging life events rather than signs of permanent pathology. Because individuals naturally incorporate these personal life circumstances into their emotional selfassessments, subjective appraisals of mental health (SRMH) may carry the same validity and clinical relevance as categorical clinical measures that rely on arbitrary scale cut-off points.

Ultimately, self-rated mental health emerges not as an imperfect proxy for objective pathology, but as a highly valid, context-sensitive appraisal of an individual's emotional status (McAlpine et al., 2018). This demonstrates that psychological well-being is defined by much more than the mere absence of clinical symptoms, as individuals actively evaluate their mental state in relation to their unique life circumstances. Shifting the focus to this subjective appraisal of functioning paves the way for examining broader cognitive dimensions of subjective well-being, most notably how students evaluate their overall satisfaction with life.

1.3.3 Life Satisfaction

Life satisfaction or satisfaction with life constitutes the cognitive-judgmental component of subjective well-being, distinguishing itself from the positive and negative affect that represent the emotional, or affective, dimensions of psychological well-being.

Conceptually, life satisfaction is defined as a global assessment of an individual's quality of life according to their own self-selected standards (Diener et al., 1985). Rather than relying on externally imposed criteria chosen by a researcher, such as objective health markers or material wealth, this evaluative process centers entirely on the individual's subjective judgment. An individual assesses their satisfaction by comparing their unique life circumstances against a standard that they set for themselves (ibidem). A key strength of this conceptualization is captured by the Satisfaction With Life Scale (SWLS), designed by Ed Diener and colleagues (1985), which operates as a "narrow-band" instrument. Unlike other scales that include affective elements like zest, apathy, or loneliness, the SWLS is strictly focused on cognitive appraisal.

Consequently, evaluating life satisfaction provides a vital, subjective window into student functioning and personal recovery, capturing a vital dimension of well-being that symptom-based clinical checklists cannot measure

1.3.4 Hopelessness

Another construct of clinical relevance is hopelessness, commonly defined as the presence of negative expectations regarding the future and perceived inability to influence desired outcomes (Abramson, Metalsky, & Alloy, 1989). In their reformulated hopelessness theory of depression, Abramson and colleagues (1989) conceptualize hopelessness not merely as a symptom of psychological distress, but as a proximal sufficient cause that directly triggers a specific subtype of depressive symptoms. Specifically, this cognitive construct is defined by the coexistence of two core expectations: a negative outcome expectancy, where an individual believes that highly desired outcomes will not occur or that highly aversive ones will occur, and a helplessness expectancy, where the individual believes that no response in their personal repertoire can change the likelihood of these outcomes. The theory distinguishes between generalized hopelessness, which occurs when these negative expectations span many areas of life, and circumscribed pessimism, which is restricted to a limited domain.

1.3.5 Why these dimensions matter

Taken together -self-rated mental health, satisfaction with life, and hopelessness -provide complementary perspectives on individuals' overall appraisal of their current mental state, their global evaluation of their lives, and their outlook on the future (Kjell & Diener, 2021; McAlpine et al., 2018; Yip & Cheung, 2006). Hopelessness has long been recognized as a central feature of depression (Abramson & Metalsky, 1989) and represents one of the strongest predictors of suicidal ideation and behavior (Ribeiro et al., 2018; Choi & Shin, 2023). Furthermore, the presence of mental health problems has consistently been associated with lower levels of life satisfaction (Fergusson et al., 2015).

These dimensions may be particularly salient during the university years, a period often marked by substantial personal, academic, and social change (Vigezzi et al., 2026).

Examining them may therefore provide a more comprehensive understanding of the potential benefits of physical activity and reduced sedentary behaviour among students living with mental disorders.

1.4 The Health Mode On Project

The present study was conducted as part of the Health Mode On project, conducted within the World Mental Health International College Student (WMH-ICS) framework at the University of Pavia, Northern Italy (Vigezzi et al., 2026).

Health Mode On is a PRO-BEN project funded by the Italian Ministry of University and Research (MUR) and coordinated by the University of Pavia. It involves a wide network of universities and AFAM institutions (Higher Education in Art, Music and Dance) working to promote the well-being of everyone who populates these environments (Mosconi et al., 2026). To transform the university environment requires a fundamental shift in its narrative.

The Health Mode On framework is built upon seven core guiding principles designed to redefine educational spaces while actively seeking to mitigate the systemic stressors historically associated with higher education (HMO, 2025): 1.

People, Not Users: Rather than viewing students as mere metrics, targets, or matriculation numbers, this principle recognizes them as unique individuals with distinct aspirations, challenges, and needs. Educational institutions must prioritize active listening and welcoming spaces to ensure that the university experience fosters both personal and social growth.

2. Continuous Experiences, Not Isolated Projects: Fostering student well-being requires a continuous, harmonious, and long-term institutional commitment rather than temporary, short-term initiatives. The focus must remain on cultivating sustained and meaningful relationships between educators and students.

3. Relational Quality, Not Bureaucratic Procedures: In every institutional touchpoint, relational depth must take precedence over rigid administrative procedures. This human-centric approach is essential to honor the unique nuances of each individual, supporting them holistically through their developmental journey. 4. Holistic Growth, Not Performance-Driven Metrics: Education is a developmental process designed to enrich and shape personal and social growth. Academic achievement should not be reduced to numeric grades; rather, true success is defined by a student's capacity to navigate challenges with emotional balance, resilience, and conscious self-determination.

5. Autonomy and Choice, Not Forced Paths: Rather than imposing highly constrained, predetermined paths that fuel anxiety and uncertainty, the university must provide students with the space to identify their own needs, encouraging autonomous and deliberate life choices. By combining different tracking tools, this project looks at both students' mental health symptoms and their physical lifestyle habits, which help design personalized prevention plans. To bring together typically disconnected campus services like counseling, sports centers, and local clinics, the Health Mode On (HMO) project and its expansion, Health Mode On Plus (HMO+), combine rigorous screening with a step-by-step, multi-team support model (Vigezzi et al., 2026).

Therefore, the current study belongs to the first phase of the HMO project. This phase has three main goals (ibidem): • Measure distress: Find out how common psychological distress and risky habits are among students and identify what causes them.

• Connect services: Design a flexible counseling model that links campus support directly with local specialist clinics.

• Train staff: Set up a Virtual Academy to train university staff and student tutors.

The second phase, HMO+, expands this work by: • Following up with individual students over a 12-month period.

• Improving the online counseling and triage (student sorting) platform.

• Using objective tracking tools for physical activity and diet.

• Testing a short, online Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT) program specifically for anxiety.

Ultimately, both projects aim to create practical, fair, and scalable mental health support systems on campus. Within this framework, assessing students is not just about gathering statistics. It is a tool used to constantly improve campus services and target help directly to those who need it most (Vigezzi, 2026).

1.4.1 Rationale of the present study

The current study describes patterns of physical activity and sedentary behaviour and examines their associations with self-rated mental health, life satisfaction, and hopelessness among nearly 4,800 university students who screened positive for at least one mental disorder.

It also analyzes whether these associations vary across major categories of mental disorders. The broader aim is to extend current evidence beyond symptom-based outcomes and provide a more comprehensive understanding of how movement behaviours relate to students' subjective experience of living with a mental disorder.

2. METHODS

This study used data from Benessere in Campus, a university-wide mental health survey conducted at the University of Pavia (Italy) as part of the Health Mode On research project. The study design can be described as a cross-sectional study. As opposed to a longitudinal study that follows the participants over time, a cross-sectional study collects data from participants at one single point in time. Details regarding study design, recruitment procedures, survey administration, and ethical approval have been reported on Vigezzi et al. (2026).

Study population and eligibility

Eligibility requires current enrollment, age ≥18 years, and provision of electronic informed consent; there are no exclusions related to health status or field of study (Vigezzi et al., 2026). Recruitment leverages institutional mailing lists and learning platforms, amplified by student associations and institutional channels. To mitigate non-response and attrition, the survey is modular and concise, staged reminders are deployed, and communications emphasize benefits and confidentiality. Mitigation of non-response bias therefore relies not only on weighting at the analysis stage, but also on recruitment design, institutional dissemination, student engagement, and a questionnaire structure intended to support participation and completion (ibidem).

Measures and data collection

All enrolled undergraduate and postgraduate students were invited to complete an anonymous online questionnaire between July 2025 and February 2026. The survey instrument comprised the standard World Mental Health International College Student (WMH-ICS) questionnaire and additional modules selected through a Delphi study conducted within the Health Mode On project (Mosconi et al., 2026).

• The WMH-ICS: The WMH-ICS questionnaire was originally designed in 2016 by the World Health Organization (WHO) and Harvard University in collaboration with a global network of higher education institutions, to address the rising global demand for student psychological support (Mosconi et al., 2026). The goal of this initiative was to gather highquality epidemiological data on student mental health, test the efficacy of scalable webbased prevention and intervention programs, and distribute evidence-backed strategies that can be tailored to various university environments (ibidem). The foundation of this project is a detailed survey distributed to first-year university students. This comprehensive questionnaire assesses a broad range of areas. Our version encompasses 11 sections including demographics (your background), presence of disorders (emotional problems), stressful life experiences that might be risk factors for these disorders (childhood background and recent experiences), history of obtaining treatment for these disorders and barriers to treatment (seeking treatment).

The questionnaire included short validated self-report screening scales for lifetime and 12month prevalence of six common DSM-IV mood (major depressive disorder, mania/hypomania), anxiety (generalized anxiety disorder, panic disorder), and substance (alcohol abuse or dependence [AUD], drug abuse or dependence, involving either cannabis, cocaine, any other street drug, or a prescription drug either used without a prescription or used more than prescribed to get high, buzzed, or numbed out) (Auerbach et al., 2018).

Furthermore, the instrument also generates reliable estimates of a range of eating disorders (anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder) and suicidal thoughts and behaviors. In addition, it collected estimates of self-report health and use of services for emotional or mental health problems (Harvard Medical School, 2025; Mosconi et al., 2026; Auerbach et al., 2018). Socio-demographic data was collected in the following way. Gender was assessed by asking respondents whether they identified themselves as male, female, transgender (male-tofemale, female-to-male), or "other." Parental educational level was assessed for father and mother separately (none, elementary, secondary, some post-secondary, college graduate, doctoral degree), and was categorized into high (college graduate or more), medium (some post-secondary education), and low (secondary school or less) based on the highest-of-both parents' educational level (Auerbach et al., 2018). Parental marital status was dichotomized into "parents not married or parent(s) deceased" versus "parents married and both alive." Sexual orientation was classified into heterosexual, gay or lesbian, bisexual, asexual, not sure, and other. Additional questions were asked about the extent to which respondents were attracted to men and women and the gender(s) of people they had sex with (if any) in the past 5 years. Respondents were categorized into the following categories: heterosexual with no same-sex attraction, heterosexual with same-sex attraction, non-heterosexual without same-sex sexual intercourse, and non-heterosexual with same-sex sexual intercourse (ibidem).

By utilizing the validated self-report instruments of the WMH-ICS, we ensured international compatibility while expanding the survey's reach beyond beyond first-year cohorts to include all academic years and programmes (Auerbach et al., 2018; Mosconi et al., 2026).

• Additional modules (IPAQ, Lifestyle and contextual factors): The additional modules were developed by Giansanto Mosconi and Giacomo Pietro Vigezzi at the University of Pavia (Mosconi et al., 2026). The researchers aimed to address certain blind spots inherent to the WMH-ICS instrument, considered to be epidemiologically relevant. These previously not included domain include lifestyle, psychological and contextual factors. The new section includes anthropometric measures, socioeconomic factors, housing conditions, commuting habits (i.e., home-to-university travel), physical activity, diet, sleep, social media use, video game use, gambling, use of nicotine-based products, loneliness, hopelessness, life satisfaction, academic stress, use, and expectations regarding student support services, discrimination, and general well-being. (Mosconi et al., 2026). Sleep is measured with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI); physical activity is assessed with the International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Short Form (IPAQ-SF) (Vigezzi et al., 2026). Tobacco and cannabis use, problematic internet and social media use, gaming and gambling are captured with brief, internationally used screeners such as the IGDS9-SF for gaming disorder, the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale and the Problem Gambling Severity Index, leveraging available Italian adaptations (ibidem). Additional measures were selected on the basis of validation status and the availability of Italian versions or adaptations where available. The resulting supplementary questionnaire comprises 18 sections and 64 items (Mosconi et al., 2026).

The survey was available in both English and Italian. A full copy of the English version is included in the supplement section of this text.

Outcomes

For the present study, we restricted the sample to students who screened positive for the presence of at least one mental disorder in the 12 months before taking part in the survey, according to standard WMH-ICS algorithms based on validated screening instruments derived from the Composite International Diagnostic Interview Screening Scales (CIDI-SC).

As exposures, we considered physical activity and sedentary behaviour. Physical Mental disorders were identified using WMH-ICS scoring algorithms and grouped into major diagnostic categories, including mood disorders, anxiety disorders, traumarelated disorders, eating disorders, substance-related and addictive disorders, and other mental disorders (Vigezzi et al., 2026). Diagnostic groups were not mutually exclusive because participants could screen positive for multiple conditions.

The outcomes of interest were self-rated mental health, life satisfaction, and hopelessness. Self-rated mental health was assessed using a single-item question asking participants to rate their overall mental health on a five-point scale (poor, fair, good, very good, and excellent). Life satisfaction was measured using the three-item Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS-3) and analysed in quartiles. Hopelessness was assessed using the fouritem version of the Beck Hopelessness Scale and dichotomised using a cut-off score of ≥6.

Physical activity and sedentary behaviour patterns were described across 12-month diagnostic groups, including comorbidity (i.e., two or more disorders), mood disorders, anxiety disorders, Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) (brief screening), suicidal dimension, and eating disorders.

Statistical analysis

Differences between groups were verified through a Chi-square test. Associations between each exposure (i.e., physical activity levels and sedentary behaviour) and each outcome were examined using separate logistic regression models, according to the outcomes distribution. Reference category was "low level" for physical activity, and "<4 hours/day" for sedentary behaviour. Binary logistic regression was used for hopelessness, whereas ordinal logistic regression models were used for self-rated mental health and life satisfaction. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were estimated through three sequential models: i) an unadjusted model; ii) further adjusted for sociodemographic, academic characteristics, and family contextual factors (sex, age, foreign student status, year of study, academic macro-area, academic delay, parental education, and family economic situation); iii) further fully adjusted for body mass index (BMI), self-rated physical health, and psychiatric comorbidity (presence of two or more mental disorders). To explore potential heterogeneity across the considered groups of mental conditions during the past 12 months, stratified fully adjusted models were performed for each major diagnostic category according to presence or absence.

All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4.

4. DISCUSSION

In this large sample of university students who screened positive for at least one mental disorder in the 12 months before the survey, higher physical activity and lower sedentary behaviour were associated with more favourable profiles across the mental health outcomes assessed. Specifically, higher physical activity was associated with lower hopelessness and greater life satisfaction, whereas prolonged sedentary behaviour was associated with poorer self-rated mental health, greater hopelessness, and lower life satisfaction. These associations remained after adjustment for several confounders, including sociodemographic, academic, behavioural, and clinical factors, suggesting that movement behaviours may represent relevant correlates of specific aspects of well-being among students screening positive for mental health conditions.

Our findings are broadly consistent with the extensive literature linking physical activity to better mental health outcomes. Previous reviews, systematic reviews, and metaanalyses have shown that physically active individuals report lower levels of depression, anxiety, and psychological distress, while exercise interventions can improve symptoms across a range of common mental disorders (Firth et al., 2020; Ross et al., 2023; Singh et al., 2023). However, unlike previous research, which has largely focused on symptom-based measures, our findings suggest that physical activity may also be associated with a more favourable subjective experience of living with a mental disorder.

Notably, physical activity was not associated uniformly with the three subjective outcomes examined among students who had already screened positive for at least one mental disorder. After full adjustment, higher physical activity remained associated with lower hopelessness and greater life satisfaction, whereas the association with self-rated mental health was attenuated to the null. This differential pattern suggests that, within a population already experiencing a substantial mental health burden, physical activity is not simply associated with a more favourable global appraisal of mental health. Rather, its association appears to be more evident for specific dimensions of subjective experience, particularly how individuals evaluate their lives and future prospects. These findings support the distinction between the presence of a probable mental disorder and the broader subjective experience of living with it, and highlight the value of considering life satisfaction and hopelessness alongside global self-rated mental health. (Correll et al., 2025; Kjell & Diener, 2021; McAlpine et al., 2018; Yip & Cheung, 2006).

4.1 Potential mechanisms

Evidence from research on young people and sports participation suggests that movementbased activities could influence such outcomes through multiple mechanisms (Eather et al., 2023; Lubans et al., 2016). To conceptualize these effects, we adopt the landmark framework proposed by Lubans and colleagues (2016), which delineates how physical activity and sport, a specific subset of PA, enhances cognitive and mental health across three distinct pathways: neurobiological, psychosocial and behavioral mechanisms.

4.1.1 Neurobiological Mechanisms

The neurobiological hypothesis proposes that physical activity enhances cognitive function and mental health by inducing structural and functional changes in the brain (Lubans et al., 2016). Montgomery and Grant (2026) provided a comprehensive synthesis of the neurobiological, molecular and systemic mechanisms of exercise in treating mental health disorders. Their work offers a crucial bridge between physiological changes and psychological outcomes (such as hopelessness and life satisfaction), reinforcing a holistic biopsychosocial perspective (Montgomery & Grant. 2026). The key neurobiological pathways outlined by the authors are the following: 1.

Neural Plasticity, Growth Factors, and Brain Structure Exercise acts as a major catalyst for structural and functional brain remodeling, reversing the cell atrophy and limbic structure degradation linked to mental disorders (ibidem): • Hippocampal and Grey Matter Growth: Montgomery and Grant report that daily physical activity can modify up to 82% of the total grey matter in the brain, with aerobic exercise shown to actively prevent volume decline in the hippocampus over a single year.

It also strengthens neural connectivity between the prefrontal cortex (PFC), amygdala (AMY), and hippocampus, which helps blunt the fear/arousal response to stressful stimuli. Aerobic training also increases grey and white matter volume in the anterior cingulate cortex (ibidem).

• Neurotrophic Signaling (BDNF and NGF): As explained by the authors, brainderived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and nerve growth factor (NGF) support synaptic plasticity, cell genesis, and neuronal survival. Individuals with mood disorders display low baseline levels of these factors, which elevate with chronic exercise (ibidem).

• Long-Term Potentiation (LTP) and Angiogenesis (IGF-1 and VEGF): Physical exercise stimulates the peripheral release of insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) from the liver, muscles, and blood cells (ibidem). These proteins cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) to enhance neurogenesis, blood vessel formation (angiogenesis), and LTP, the primary synaptic mechanism for learning and memory (ibidem).

• Anti-Aging Protein Expression (Klotho): according to the authors exercise also stimulates the expression of Klotho, a pleiotropic protein that improves cognitive function, enhances stem-cell-driven regenerative responses, and confers resistance to oxidative stress (ibidem).

2. Myokines and Muscle-Brain Crosstalk

Skeletal muscle acts as a secretory endocrine organ, releasing proteins (myokines) and metabolites that cross the blood-brain barrier to trigger neuroplasticity (ibidem).

• Cathepsin B (CTSB): The authors explain that protease is upregulated in skeletal muscle and brain following exercise, crossing the BBB to directly stimulate BDNF mRNA, BDNF protein, and doublecortin (a neuroprotective protein promoting neuronal migration).

• Irisin and the PGC-1α Pathway: Endurance exercise triggers PGC-1α (a co-activator of mitochondrial biogenesis), which upregulates FNDC5 gene expression in the hippocampus. This FNDC5/irisin cascade directly stimulates BDNF synthesis.

Boosting brain FNDC5 has been shown to rescue synaptic plasticity and memory impairments in models of Alzheimer's disease (ibidem).

• Lactate and Ketones (b-HB): Prolonged aerobic exercise elevates bhydroxybutyrate b-HB), a ketone body that crosses the BBB via monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs) to stimulate hippocampal BDNF expression (ibidem).

Similarly, lactate accumulates during acute exercise and crosses the BBB via MCTs.

Astrocytic lactate is transported to neurons, acting as a vital signaling molecule that supports memory formation, induces the PGC-1α/FNDC5/BDNF pathway, and enhances synaptic plasticity by potentiating NMDA glutamate receptor activity (ibidem).

3. Monoamines

Rather than supporting a simplistic, single-neurotransmitter model of mental health (such as the now-challenged serotonin theory of depression), exercise works through a dynamic, multi-system recalibration of several modulatory networks (ibidem).

• Serotonin (5-HT): Essential for emotional processing and hippocampal memory.

During sustained aerobic exercise, skeletal muscle uptake of branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) frees up tryptophan to cross the BBB, boosting central 5-HT synthesis in the hippocampus, striatum, and midbrain. paired with BDNF, this supports hippocampal neurogenesis and mimics SSRI mechanisms (ibidem).

• Dopamine (DA): DA governs motivation, effort allocation, positive anticipation, and reward learning. Acute aerobic activity lifts mood by enhancing DA synthesis and release in mesolimbic regions (nucleus accumbens and PFC), while chronic training increases D2/D3 receptor availability and density, actively repairing the brain's reward circuitry to counter anhedonia (ibidem).

• Norepinephrine (NE): Originating in the locus coeruleus, moderate exercise-induced NE elevations improve alertness and executive control in the PFC, while training recalibrates adrenergic receptor sensitivity, buffering against the hyperarousal seen in anxiety and PTSD (ibidem).

4. Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal (HPA) Axis Regulation

HPA axis hyperactivity (such as abnormal cortisol secretion and glucocorticoid receptor insensitivity) is a hallmark of stress-related and mood disorders (ibidem).

• Acute vs. Chronic Adaptive Responses: While acute bouts of moderate-to-highintensity exercise trigger healthy, transient cortisol spikes to mobilize energy, chronic moderate exercise reduces resting basal cortisol, enhances glucocorticoid receptor sensitivity, and improves HPA negative-feedback efficiency. This shifts the body toward a highly flexible, resilient stress-response system (ibidem).

5. Inflammatory and Autonomic Systems

Chronic low-grade inflammation (driven by pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6, TNF-a, and IL-1b) impairs neuroplasticity, reduces serotonin availability, and drives oxidative stress (ibidem).

• Systemic Anti-Inflammatory Effects: Montgomery and Grant report that regular moderate exercise has profound anti-inflammatory properties. It downregulates Toll-like receptor expression on immune cells, increases anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10 and IL-1ra), and reduces metabolically active visceral body fat, which otherwise secretes pro-inflammatory markers (ibidem).

• The IL-6 Myokine Cascade: During acute, vigorous exercise, IL-6 is acutely synthesized and released by skeletal muscle to act as a myokine. This acute spike initiates downstream anti-inflammatory cascades and directly inhibits TNF-a, ultimately lowering long-term baseline plasma levels of IL-6 (ibidem).

• Vagal Tone and Heart Rate Variability (HRV): Exercise improves vagal parasympathetic modulation and enhances HRV. Crucially, an increased high frequency/low frequency ratio in HRV is positively correlated with reductions in depressive symptom severity, providing a direct physiological marker of emotional regulation (ibidem).

6. The Endocannabinoid System (ECS)

The endocannabinoid system, specifically the neuromodulators anandamide (AEA) and 2arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG), acts as a critical biological interface regulating stress, reward, and anxiety (ibidem): • Acute Aerobic Exercise and Runner's High: The authors report that moderateintensity aerobic exercise reliably elevates circulating AEA (but not 2-AG).

This rise corresponds with reductions in pain (analgesia), diminished anxiety (anxiolysis), and elevated mood. This is hypothesized to activate mesolimbic dopamine pathways, reinforcing exercise behavior (ibidem).

• HPA-ECS Interplay and Synaptic Plasticity: CB1 receptors on sympathetic terminals inhibit NE and CRH release, constraining excess cortisol. Acute exerciseinduced glucocorticoid bursts upregulate 2-AG signaling in the amygdala, helping buffer emotional circuits against stress overload. Sustained exercise also increases hippocampal CB1 receptor expression and AEA content, directly driving BDNF release and neurogenesis (ibidem).

7. Mitochondrial Health and Bioenergetics

While chronic psychological stress disrupts mitochondrial dynamics, bioenergetics, and redox balance, promoting high reactive oxygen species (ROS) and elevating psychiatric risk, exercise directly targets mitochondrial dysfunction (ibidem): • Mitochondrial Biogenesis: Aerobic exercise increases citrate synthase activity and decreases damaging ROS (ibidem).

Combining aerobic and resistance exercise amplifies skeletal muscle mitochondrial biogenesis compared to single modalities alone, enhancing ATP production and supporting overall neuroprotection (ibidem).

4.1.2 Psychosocial Mechanisms

The psychosocial hypothesis posits that physical activity supports mental health by satisfying fundamental human psychological needs (Lubans et al., 2016). Lubans and colleagues identify these following needs: social interaction (relatedness), mastery in the physical domain (self-efficacy and perceived competence), improvements in appearance self-perception (body image) and independence (autonomy). To fully unpack these psychosocial pathways, it is essential to situate them within the broader literature of sport and exercise psychology.

Self-Determination Theory (SDT) and Motivational Dynamics

The psychological outcomes of movement may be heavily influenced by the motivational forces that drive behavior (Lubans et al., 2016). Motivation is defined as the internal or external forces that determine the initiation, direction, intensity, and persistence of behavior (Cei, 2021). Within sport and exercise psychology, a traditional distinction is made between intrinsic motivation, where an individual engages in an activity purely for the inherent pleasure and satisfaction of the task itself and extrinsic motivation, which refers to engagement driven by instrumental goals, such as obtaining external rewards or avoiding negative consequences (Vallerand, 2007).

To unify these motivational elements, Deci and Ryan (1985, 2000) formulated the Self-Determination Theory (SDT), which assumes that human beings possess an innate, proactive tendency toward psychological growth and autonomous development. According to this framework, healthy psychological functioning and subjective well-being depend on the satisfaction of three basic psychological needs: 1.

Competence: The need to feel effective in interacting with one's social environment and expressing one's capabilities.

2. Autonomy: The need to perceive oneself as the origin and author of one's own behaviors and choices.

3. Relatedness: The need to feel connected to others, to care and be cared for, and to experience a sense of belonging within a community.

SDT moves beyond a simple intrinsic-extrinsic dichotomy by introducing the concept of internalization, which describes a continuum of behavioral regulation ranging from nonself-determined to fully self-determined states. This continuum spans amotivation (lack of any intent to act), externally regulated behavior (driven entirely by external rewards or punishments), introjected regulation of behavior (guided by internal pressures such as avoiding guilt, shame, or seeking approval), regulation through identification (acting out of a personal valuation of the activity's utility), integrated regulation (where the behavior is fully aligned with one's values and self-identity. It still remains classified as extrinsic because actions are driven by personal utility rather than inherent enjoyment of the task) and finally, intrinsic motivation (Cei, 2021). Deeper internalization leads to a greater sense of autonomy, which is strongly associated with enhanced well-being, high-quality performance, and long-term behavioral persistence (Moè, 2010). In the context of our study, this motivational internalization provides a possible explanatory mechanism for the observed associations. Students screening positive for mental disorders who autonomously engage in physical activity satisfy their basic psychological needs for autonomy (by choosing their active habits) and competence (by experiencing physical progress).

According to SDT, the satisfaction of these core psychological needs directly drives eudaimonic well-being. This theoretical pathway could explain why physical activity in our cohort was strongly and independently associated with higher global life satisfaction.

Self-Efficacy Theory and Cognitive Appraisals

A cornerstone of cognitive-behavioral functioning within sport is Albert Bandura's theory of self-efficacy, situated within the broader framework of Social Cognitive Theory (Felts, 1988). Self-efficacy is defined as an individual's subjective belief in their capability to successfully execute a specific task to achieve a desired outcome (Bandura, 1997).

Crucially, it must be distinguished from global self-esteem; while self-esteem represents a broad evaluative judgment of self-worth, self-efficacy is task-specific, though the two constructs are highly correlated and mutually reinforcing (Cei, 2021). Bandura (1997) conceptualizes self-efficacy along three essential dimensions: level (the difficulty of the tasks an individual believes they can perform), strength (the depth of conviction in one's capability), and generality (the number of life domains to which these efficacy beliefs extend) (Cei, 2021).

According to Bandura (1997), self-efficacy is constructed from four primary sources of information: 1.

Performance Accomplishments (Mastery Experiences): The most influential source, where personal successes directly build robust efficacy beliefs, while failures undermine them. Repeated mastery experiences can also generalize, boosting confidence in other areas where the individual previously felt inadequate.

2. Vicarious Experiences: Observing a competent model succeed, which is particularly effective when the model shares similar characteristics, such as gender, with the observer.

3. Verbal Persuasion: Encouragement or feedback from others, such as coaches, though research indicates its effects are typically modest and temporary, fading quickly after a subsequent failure.

4. Physiological and Affective States: The subjective interpretation of bodily arousal and somatic symptoms. Rather than the objective level of physiological activation itself, it is the cognitive appraisal of this activation that dictates efficacy (Cei, 1998); individuals with high self-efficacy interpret somatic symptoms of arousal (such as a rapid heart rate or fatigue) as facilitative energy, whereas those with low selfefficacy interpret them as indicators of impending failure (Ibidem).

Self-efficacy acts as a powerful cognitive mediator that shapes subsequent behavior, goal choices, emotional reactions, and cognitive appraisals (Bandura, 1977; 1997). Efficacy beliefs also influence how reality is subjectively filtered directly impacting emotional states and subsequent athletic and psychological outcomes (Caselli, 2024). Furthermore, changes in self-efficacy influence causal attributions; following a success, an increase in self-efficacy is positively correlated with internal and stable attributions, reinforcing a sense of personal agency (Ntoumanis & Biddle, 1999). Within this psychosocial framework, perceived self-efficacy may act as a cognitive mediator that links physical activity to subjective well-being. Physical activity represents a structured environment where students can experience immediate performance accomplishments (mastery experiences), which Bandura (1977; 1997) identifies as the most powerful source of self-efficacy. By achieving self-selected physical goals, students rebuild their task-specific confidence and physical self-worth, which can then generalize into a broader sense of global self-esteem, personal agency, and existential empowerment (Fox, 1999). As Fox (1999) argues, taking deliberate command over one's body and movement habits triggers a broader sense of autonomy, where the primary psychological benefit is the subjective feeling of empowerment that achieving personal change provides. This restored sense of control could weaken the cognitive foundations of hopelessness, explaining why active students in our Pavia cohort had significantly lower odds of experiencing high hopelessness. This proposed mechanism is strongly supported by the empirical findings of Doğruyol and Tayınmak (2023), who observed that general self-efficacy is significantly and negatively correlated with hopelessness, acting as a critical cognitive mediator that buffers against psychological distress. Crucially, higher self-efficacy was found to protect individuals across all three core sub-dimensions of hopelessness: reducing negative feelings about the future, mitigating the loss of motivation, and raising positive future expectations (Doğruyol & Tayınmak, 2023).

By illustrating that self-efficacy may serve as a vital cognitive anchor to preserve motivation and future-oriented goals, our findings are consistent with the hypothesis that movementinduced mastery experiences could help buffer the cognitive architecture of hopelessness in university students experiencing psychiatric distress.

Locke's Goal-Setting Theory

To fully understand the psychological mechanisms behind physical activity must be conceptualized as an act of personal goal setting. Physical activity is not merely a mechanical action. It also represents a conscious cognitive target that a student sets for themselves (Cei, 2021). According to Edwin A. Locke's Goal-Setting Theory (1968), cognitive intentions act as a direct regulator of human action, mobilizing effort, persistence and focus. In our Pavia cohort, higher physical activity levels were consistently associated with lower hopelessness and greater life satisfaction. Locke's framework could provide a precise cognitive-behavioral explanation for this relationship through several key pathways, either directly or indirectly: 1.

Focus of Attention: Goals direct attention toward task-relevant activities and away from irrelevant distractions (ibidem). For example, in distressed students, this means diverting cognitive resources away from depressive rumination or academic worry and onto concrete physical targets.

2. Effort: High, challenging goals mobilize greater physical and mental effort. This process establishes a highly beneficial cyclic relationship between cognitive and motivational systems: setting clear yet challenging goals (such as performing physical activity) increases focus, which enhances task-specific performance and mastery; this achievement, in turn, boosts self-efficacy and self-esteem, motivating the individual to set further progressive goals and maintain behavioral commitment (Cei, 2021). This, in turn, could have a cascading protective effect by reducing the sense of hopelessness experience by individuals (Doğruyol and Tayınmak, 2023). Furthermore, as highlighted by Emmons (2003), the specific content and cognitive orientation of a person's goals are the true determinants of subjective well-being. Personal goals serve as the core element of psychological organization, lending vital structure, order, and future-oriented purpose to daily life, which directly dictates how individuals evaluate their lives as meaningful and worthwhile (ibidem). In our study, the significant associations between physical activity and elevated global life satisfaction strongly support this goal-based model of well-being.

Engaging in regular physical activity or sports represents an active commitment to intrinsic, self-selected goals, which Emmons (2003) demonstrates are robust predictors of life satisfaction and positive affect.

Ultimately, integrating Emmons' framework with Locke's theory demonstrates that physical activity does not merely serve as a physiological distraction; rather, it may reorganize a student's psychological space around meaningful, attainable, and approach-oriented goals that systematically rebuild future optimism and global life satisfaction.

Social support and interpersonal dynamics

Sport practice can provide more potent and additional benefits through social connections and support. Eather and colleagues (2023) have identified seven mechanisms by which social support may influence mental health: -Social influence/social comparison: Group dynamics, social influence, and peer comparison can have either protective or harmful effects on an individual's mental health.

Whether participating in individual or team sports, athletes are constantly influenced and guided by the behaviors, expectations, and norms of other players and teams. Comparing one's own capabilities, attitudes, and values to those of other participants can directly affect an individual's behaviors and subsequent health outcomes; -Social control: This environment also involves social control, which occurs when others attempt to encourage or discourage specific health practices.

Such dynamics often manifest through strategies discussed and implemented among players, or between players and coaches. Similarly, teammates may motivate one another to exert more effort during a match or to engage in off-field routines and events, such as fitness programs, post-game celebrations, and club gatherings, which can impact both current and future physical and mental health.

-Behavioral guidance, purpose and meaning: As seen within Locke's goal setting theory, participating in sports can offer individuals a sense of purpose, meaning, and behavioral guidance. Through the social connections built in sports, players often develop role identities, which are defined as positions within a social structure that carry mutual duties and obligations.

In team sports especially, athletes realize they are an essential part of a larger group, giving them a shared responsibility for the team's success. This includes concrete commitments to train, play, and communicate with the team, alongside responsibilities to maintain their own health, perform to the best of their abilities, and support their teammates. Ultimately, because these role identities provide both direction and a sense of meaning in life, they are highly likely to impact the mental health of sports participants.

-Self-esteem: An individual's self-esteem can be deeply shaped by the social connections and support they experience through sports. When athletes feel more capable or valued within a team environment, this localized confidence often boosts their overall self-esteem and global sense of worth.

-Sense of control: Additionally, sports offer unique opportunities to build new skills, face challenges, and develop self-control or a sense of mastery. Actively working through and finding creative solutions to sports-related difficulties helps participants build this inner competence. Crucially, this sense of mastery can carry over into other areas of daily life, giving individuals the confidence to handle various realworld challenges. For instance, learning how to quickly design creative strategies against an opponent on the field can translate into greater confidence when solving creative problems in the workplace.

-Belonging and companionship: The social support and relationships built through sports can offer participants a key source of companionship and belonging.

Developing these connections, both on and off the field, with peers who share similar interests helps foster a sense of community. Consequently, this enhanced sense of belonging may act as a mediator that leads to improved mental health outcomes.

-Perceived support availability: Social support is often provided through expressions of trust and care; instrumentally via tangible assistance; through information such as advice and suggestions; or as appraisal such feedback. All forms of social support provided on and off the field contribute to a more generalized sense of perceived support that may mediate the effect of social interaction on mental health outcomes.

These social support mechanisms are highly relevant to the developmental stage of our sample. Emerging adulthood is characterized by acute social and academic challenges, where students often experience isolation and a loss of structured role identities (Arnett, 2000). Our findings show that physical activity is a key correlate of high life satisfaction.

Through the lens of Eather and colleagues' model ( 2021), sports participation provides crucial psychosocial resources, such as role identities, behavioral guidance, and a sense of belonging, that could directly buffer against this developmental vulnerability. By participating in sports, students move from feeling like isolated individuals to becoming essential parts of a social structure, transforming physical movement into a vital vehicle for interpersonal connection and global life satisfaction.

4.1.3 Behavioral Mechanisms

The behavioral hypothesis suggests that physical activity does not always affect mental health directly; instead, its benefits are often mediated by the adoption or optimization of other positive health behaviors (Lubans et al., 2016). Lubans and colleagues identify these behaviors as the following: -Sleep Optimization: Physical activity plays a critical role in regulating sleep patterns by improving sleep duration, sleep efficiency, and sleep onset latency, while reducing daytime sleepiness and fatigue.

This is crucial for mental health, as insufficient sleep is heavily associated with behavioral problems and deficits in complex cognitive functioning (ibidem). Furthermore, Lenneis and colleagues (2024) observed that a more optimized sleep pattern influence subjective well-being, which includes life satisfaction.

-Coping Skills and Self-Regulation: Certain types of physical activity provide unique opportunities to develop cognitive, affective, and physical self-regulation. For example, martial arts and holistic mind-body systems like yoga which integrate relaxation, mindfulness, and meditation, directly teach youth coping strategies to manage anxiety, stress, and emotional difficulties (ibidem).

Ultimately, while Eather and colleagues (2023) assert that sports participation shapes mental health through a complex, non-uniform interplay of neurobiological, psychosocial, and behavioral mechanisms, our empirical findings directly reflect this pathway heterogeneity.

Rather than producing a uniform, global shift across all health indicators, physical activity in our university cohort demonstrated more pronounced, domain-specific associations. This is evidenced by the significant reduction in hopelessness and corresponding increases in life satisfaction, contrasted against a non-significant relationship with general self-rated mental health after full adjustment.

The observed dose-response relationship further supports the plausibility of these associations. These findings are particularly relevant given the central role of hopelessness in the development and maintenance of mental disorders, as well as the established associations of both hopelessness and life dissatisfaction with suicidal thoughts and behaviours (Koivumaa-Honkanen et al., 2001; Yip & Cheung, 2006). Interventions aimed at increasing physical activity among university students with mental health problems may therefore contribute not only to symptom reduction but also to fostering more positive expectations about the future.

Sedentary behaviour showed a broader pattern of association, primarily because, unlike physical activity, it remained directly related to worse self-rated mental health after adjustment. Students reporting more than eight hours of sitting per day also had poorer outcomes across the other dimensions examined. Our findings complement previous evidence linking sedentary behaviour to depressive and anxiety symptoms by suggesting that its associations may extend to individuals' overall appraisal of their mental health (Hallgren et al., 2018; Stanczykiewicz et al., 2019; Teychenne et al., 2015; Zhai et al., 2015).

Importantly, the associations observed in our study remained significant even after accounting for physical activity levels and other relevant covariates, supporting the notion that sedentary behaviour may exert detrimental effects beyond simply reflecting a lack of exercise. Several mechanisms may help explain these results, including reduced social interaction, greater exposure to passive screen-based activities and the resulting disruption of sleep quality, as well as adverse neurobiological effects of prolonged sitting (Firth et al., 2020). At the same time, the association with self-rated mental health, which was not observed for physical activity after adjustment, suggests that the relationship may also include a component operating in the opposite direction, whereby poorer perceived mental health, greater symptom severity, or functional impairment may contribute to increased sedentary time.

Stratified analyses suggested that some associations of movement behaviours may extend across diagnostic profiles. High physical activity was consistently associated with lower hopelessness across strata defined by screening status, and prolonged sedentary behaviour showed broadly adverse associations across the same strata, supporting a possible transdiagnostic relevance of these behaviours for psychological functioning. However, this pattern was not uniform across outcomes: associations with self-rated mental health were weaker, whereas those with life satisfaction varied more substantially in magnitude and direction. This may indicate that broader evaluations of life are more sensitive to the clinical and contextual characteristics accompanying different diagnostic profiles.

4.2 Public Health Implications and Intervention design

The university setting provides a particularly relevant context for these findings.

Emerging adulthood is a developmental period characterised by substantial academic, social, and psychological challenges, while mental disorders frequently emerge or become more severe during these years (Arnett, 2000; Auerbach et al., 2018; Solmi et al., 2022). At the same time, physical activity and sedentary behaviour are modifiable behaviours that can be targeted through campus-wide health promotion initiatives (Peng et al., 2022; Plotnikoff et al., 2015). University sport provision can encompass different forms of participation, ranging from highly competitive sport to recreational and informal physical activity (García-Álvarez & Faubel, 2020). While competitive sport can provide important social and psychological benefits (Eather et al., 2021; Cei, 2021), performance-oriented environments may also expose participants to additional pressures, including training demands and competitive stress (Rice et al., 2016). Conversely, research on campus recreation suggests that participation is influenced by a range of interpersonal and structural barriers, including accessibility, social connections and familiarity with available opportunities (Sim et al., 2026; Selvaratnam et al., 2021). This suggests that simply providing sporting opportunities may not be sufficient to engage less-active students, particularly those who do not identify with competitive sport. Universities may therefore benefit from adopting a broader approach to physical-activity promotion, alongside competitive sport, that emphasizes accessible and inclusive opportunities for students with different levels of ability, motivation and experience.

To encourage broader student participation in physical activity, institutions could leverage the existing academic credit system. Research has examined the use of academic incentives to promote physical activity among students, suggesting that such incentives may increase participation (DeVahl et al., 2005). Moreover, greater academic incentives were associated with greater intervention effectiveness, as reflected in both higher exercise adherence and improved health outcomes (ibidem). Whitin the Italian university system, this could be operationalized by awarding university credits (CFUs) for sports participation, or alternatively, by establishing a mandatory physical activity CFU quota within degree requirements. Additionally, for universities lacking dedicated on-campus fitness facilities, institutions could forge private-sector partnerships with local gyms to offer students subsidized, reduced-rate training options (Sim et al., 2026).

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions regarding causality or directionality. Although early models conceptualized the association as primarily unidirectional (PA → MH) (Biddle, 2016), more recent research suggests a bidirectional relationship. While exercise can improve mental wellbeing, individuals with better mental health are more likely to engage in regular physical activity (Ahsan & Abualait, 2025). Second, all measures were self-reported and may therefore be subject to recall bias and reporting inaccuracies. Third, mental disorders were identified using validated screening instruments rather than clinical diagnoses. Finally, the study was conducted within a single Italian university, which may limit the generalisability of findings to other educational and cultural contexts.

Despite these limitations, the study has several important strengths, including the large sample size, the use of validated instruments within the WMH-ICS framework, the simultaneous consideration of both physical activity and sedentary behaviour, the focus on subjective outcomes that are highly relevant to the everyday experience of students living with mental disorders, and the capability of taking into account several potential confounders in the regression models. To our knowledge, few studies have examined how movement behaviours relate to self-rated mental health, life satisfaction, and hopelessness specifically among university students screening positive for mental disorders.

In conclusion, physical activity and sedentary behaviour were differentially associated with how students experienced living with a mental disorder. Higher physical activity was most consistently associated with lower hopelessness and greater life satisfaction, whereas prolonged sedentary time was associated with poorer outcomes across all dimensions, including self-rated mental health. Although a component operating in the opposite direction cannot be excluded, these findings identify movement behaviours as potentially modifiable targets and accessible markers of students experiencing less favourable outcomes. Longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to establish temporal direction and determine whether increasing physical activity and reducing sedentary time can improve these outcomes.
