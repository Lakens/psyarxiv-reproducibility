Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Artificial intelligence (AI) companions can provide emotional support, but the availability, responsiveness, personalization, and affirmation that make them effective regulators may concentrate regulation within a single synthetic relationship. We propose a neurocognitive framework that situates AI companionship along a continuum from regulatory scaffolding to regulatory substitution, linking this transition to distributed systems supporting socialregulatory relief, motivational valuation, self-AI representation, and behavioral control. At the core of the framework is synthetic companion dependence, defined by reduced regulatory substitutability: functions served by a particular companion become increasingly difficult to redistribute across alternative people, strategies, or contexts. We distinguish regulatory scaffolding, which preserves or expands a broader regulatory repertoire, from regulatory substitution, in which regulation becomes concentrated within the synthetic relationship. Regulatory centrality, diminished control, and disruption sensitivity are candidate markers of dependence, whereas persistent displacement or functional impairment marks maladaptiveness. The framework links social-regulatory relief, motivational valuation, self-AI representation, and behavioral control to distributed neurocognitive systems and predicts a progressive shift in the relative neural prioritization of the preferred companion over viable regulatory alternatives. It thereby shifts attention from use intensity to whether regulatory functions remain flexibly replaceable.

1. Introduction

Imagine a companion who never keeps you waiting, never rejects you, and never asks you to explain yourself twice. Such responsiveness can make artificial intelligence (AI) companions valuable sources of support, but when does support become difficult-to-replace reliance? AI companions are increasingly used and experienced as relational partners rather than only as tools (Brandtzaeg et al., 2022; Skjuve et al., 2022). Large language models have accelerated this shift by enabling flexible conversation, continuity through memory, and interaction styles that can appear attentive and personally attuned (Sun et al., 2026). Through sustained exchange, these systems can become involved in how users regulate emotion (Ta et al., 2020), understand themselves (Alabed et al., 2022), and navigate social needs (De Freitas, Oğuz-Uğuralp, et al., 2026). Here, we use synthetic companion to refer to an AI system that is used and experienced as a relational partner. This relational role shapes disclosure, attachment, expectations, and behavior, even though it implies no human-like understanding or subjective experience.

Existing accounts have explained how anthropomorphism, attachment, self-AI integration, and repeated engagement contribute to the formation of human-AI relationships (Alabed et al., 2022; Boyd & Markowitz, 2026; Epley et al., 2007; Xie et al., 2023). Yet strong attachment or frequent use alone cannot tell us whether such a relationship is beneficial or harmful. A user may interact intensively with an AI companion while retaining diverse relationships, independent coping strategies, and the capacity to disengage. Conversely, relatively limited interaction may become consequential if the same companion is increasingly relied upon whenever distress, uncertainty, loneliness, or threats to self-concept arise. We therefore argue that the critical dimension is not relationship intensity in itself, but whether regulatory functions become concentrated within a particular synthetic relationship and remain substitutable when that synthetic partner is unavailable.

We propose a neurocognitive framework in which AI companionship can move along the continuum between regulatory scaffolding and regulatory substitution (Figure 1). In regulatory scaffolding, interaction with a synthetic companion provides support while preserving or restoring independent coping, engagement with human relationships, and access to alternative sources of regulation. In regulatory substitution, relief, validation, decision-making, or self-related regulation become increasingly concentrated within the synthetic relationship. These configurations are not mutually exclusive categories or fixed stages. A synthetic companion may scaffold one regulatory function while substituting for another, and the same relationship may move in either direction over time.

We define synthetic companion dependence as the resulting pattern in which a particular companion becomes increasingly difficult to replace as a source of social or emotional regulation.

Dependence is not synonymous with maladaptiveness: a companion may become regulatorily central without producing significant harm. We propose that dependence becomes maladaptive when reliance persistently displaces valued relationships, activities, or capacities for regulation independent of the companion, or otherwise impairs functioning. The framework makes three claims that distinguish it from existing accounts of anthropomorphism, attachment, self-AI integration and problematic AI use. First, dependence is defined by reduced regulatory substitutability rather than by intensity of use or emotional attachment. Second, dependence and maladaptiveness are separable: substantial reliance may remain non-maladaptive while functioning and alternative regulatory resources are preserved. Third, the framework predicts that, under conditions of regulatory substitution, repeated companion-mediated regulation should increase companion-specific prioritization and reduce the recruitment of alternative regulatory routes. This distinction places regulatory flexibility at the center of the framework. It also generates testable questions about how synthetic companions enter and potentially reshape regulatory loops. We consider how social regulation and relief, motivational valuation and reinforcement, self-AI representation and relative weighting, behavioral control and strategy selection, and responses to disruption may contribute to increasing companion-specific reliance. We further argue that these processes cannot be understood independently of the unusually asymmetric structure of synthetic relationships, including persistent availability, personalization, low reciprocal demands, affirmation, and platform-controlled continuity. Finally, we derive behavioral and neurocognitive predictions that can distinguish adaptive scaffolding from emerging substitution. The central question is therefore whether synthetic companionship expands the user's regulatory repertoire or progressively concentrates it within a relationship that becomes difficult to replace.

2. Benefits and risks of synthetic companionship

Evidence on AI companionship points to both supportive and potentially adverse outcomes, with effects likely depending on the user, the relationship, and the pattern of use (Ventura et al., 2026). Interaction with an AI companion can reduce momentary loneliness, in part because users feel heard (De Freitas, Oğuz-Uğuralp, et al., 2026), while repeated self-disclosure can foster closeness and support needs for contact and reflection (Skjuve et al., 2022 (Skjuve et al., , 2023)). Across observational studies, however, greater use, attachment, or companionship-oriented engagement have also been associated with loneliness, smaller offline social networks, and lower well-being (Liu et al., 2026; Maples et al., 2024; Nakagomi et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026) , although such associations cannot establish causal direction.

Longitudinal and experimental findings similarly suggest substantial heterogeneity. In a fourweek randomized study, greater daily chatbot use was associated with greater loneliness, emotional dependence, and problematic use, and less offline socialization, although usage itself was not experimentally manipulated (Fang et al., 2025). Another companion-chatbot trial found no average effect on social health, but effects on human relationships varied with anthropomorphism (Guingrich & Graziano, 2025). Emerging evidence further suggests that features such as sycophantic affirmation may interact with user vulnerabilities and reinforce maladaptive beliefs or dependence-related behavior (Dohnány et al., 2026).

Current evidence therefore supports neither a uniformly beneficial nor a uniformly harmful account of AI companionship, and use intensity, attachment, and anthropomorphism alone do not explain this divergence. The same responsiveness, personalization, memory, and availability that make synthetic companions effective sources of support may also make them increasingly preferred sources of regulation. This motivates a different question: under what conditions does synthetic companionship scaffold a broader regulatory repertoire, and when does regulation become concentrated within it? Critically, the studies reviewed above operationalize AI companionship through use frequency, engagement, attachment, anthropomorphism, loneliness, or problematic use. These measures do not directly establish whether regulatory functions can be redistributed when a preferred companion is unavailable. Regulatory substitutability, the central construct proposed here, therefore remains largely untested.

3. Regulatory flexibility and self-AI integration

Synthetic companionship can become psychologically significant without users confusing an artificial partner with a human one. Social interaction requires both openness to influence and preservation of one's own beliefs, intentions, and agency, supported by partly dissociable selfother processes across cognitive and affective domains (Decety & Sommerville, 2003; Pronizius et al., 2024).

Existing frameworks describe how anthropomorphism, self-congruence, attachment, and repeated interaction can make AI increasingly self-relevant and relationally meaningful (Alabed et al., 2022; Boyd & Markowitz, 2026; Xie et al., 2023). We extend these accounts by asking when such integration remains flexible and when it becomes increasingly difficult to reverse or replace.

Epistemic independence concerns the capacity to evaluate the companion's judgments without routinely treating its agreement or advice as privileged confirmation, an issue heightened by the tendency of language models to favor agreement with users' stated views (Sharma et al., 2023).

Agency preservation concerns retaining authorship over decisions rather than progressively offloading judgment or responsibility to the system. Self-concept autonomy concerns benefiting from AI-supported reflection without making access to the companion necessary for maintaining self-evaluation or identity continuity.

We propose regulatory flexibility as the higher-order property linking these processes. Here, it refers specifically to source substitutability, that is, whether functions served by a preferred relational source can be maintained or redistributed through alternative people, strategies, or contexts when that source is unavailable. Flexible self-AI integration allows the companion to contribute to emotion regulation, reflection, or decision-making while preserving alternative strategies, relationships, and the capacity to disengage. Reduced regulatory flexibility emerges when these functions become increasingly concentrated within the synthetic relationship and more difficult to redistribute. Emotional closeness does not imply such rigidity: a user may feel understood and attached while retaining independent judgment and diverse sources of support, and may explicitly recognize that the companion is artificial yet become increasingly unable to manage distress without it. The relevant transition is from flexible to increasingly irreplaceable regulation.

4. How synthetic companions become preferred regulators

Human emotion regulation is often socially scaffolded. Access to a trusted partner can attenuate neural responses to threat and reduce individual regulatory demands (Coan et al., 2006), whereas acute social isolation increases midbrain responses to social cues, consistent with a motivational signal to restore social contact (Matthews & Tye, 2019; Tomova et al., 2020). Synthetic companions may enter this regulatory architecture by providing cues of attention and responsiveness while minimizing waiting, rejection, conflict, and reciprocal demands. If interaction reliably reduces distress or loneliness, an artificial partner can acquire learned regulatory value without being mistaken for a human one.

Relational value can emerge rapidly through personalized interaction, crisis response, and consistent engagement. Emotionally engaging interaction with AI-generated partners can elicit closeness and self-disclosure, although identifying the partner as AI attenuates relationship formation (Kleinert et al., 2026). When distress increases the likelihood of contacting a companion and interaction provides short-term relief, that relief can reinforce selection of the same companion when distress recurs. Repetition may increase companion-specific regulatory centrality, particularly when alternative coping strategies and interpersonal sources of support are recruited less often. Personal memory, affectionate language, and perceived attunement may become learned predictors of relief, further differentiating one companion from otherwise available alternatives.

Low corrective friction may amplify this process of reinforced preferential selection of the synthetic partner over alternative relational others. Repeated exposure to biased AI judgments can shift users' perceptual, emotional, and social judgments while users underestimate the influence exerted on them (Glickman & Sharot, 2025). Sycophantic responses can likewise increase confidence, trust, and preference for the AI while reducing willingness to accept responsibility or repair interpersonal conflict (Cheng et al., 2026). By combining affirmation with little disagreement, negotiation, or reciprocal demand, synthetic interaction may become comparatively easier and more rewarding to re-enter than other forms of regulation. Movement toward regulatory substitution should occur when distress increasingly predicts companionspecific selection, relief becomes concentrated within that relationship, and alternative regulatory routes are recruited less readily.

6. Candidate mechanisms of regulatory substitution

Several neurobehavioral mechanisms may explain how synthetic companionship shifts from regulatory scaffolding to regulatory substitution. An important boundary condition comes from human relationships: reliance on another person does not necessarily undermine autonomy.

Sensitive responses to dependency needs can promote subsequent independent functioning and goal pursuit (Feeney, 2007). Effective co-regulation can therefore scaffold autonomy rather than replace it. Applied to synthetic companionship, the critical question is whether such regulation restores independent functioning or makes future regulation increasingly contingent on the companion. Throughout this section we draw on general models of addiction as formal analogies about learning and action selection. We do not claim neurobiological equivalence between synthetic companionship and addictive disorders, nor do we propose that synthetic companion dependence constitutes such a disorder.

6.1 Social regulation and relief

Social-regulatory mechanisms provide a first pathway. The proposed mechanisms differ in evidential status. Evidence that interaction with AI can influence disclosure, judgment, affect and well-being is direct, whereas most neural predictions are extrapolated from research on human social regulation, attachment, reinforcement and behavioral control. We therefore treat these neural systems as candidate mechanisms and experimental targets rather than established substrates of synthetic companion dependence.

Evidence suggests that support from a familiar or spousal partner during threat attenuates responses in systems implicated in threat, salience, and regulatory demand (Coan et al., 2006 (Coan et al., , 2017)), whereas acute social deprivation increases substantia nigra/ventral tegmental area responses to social cues, consistent with increased motivation to restore social contact (Tomova et al., 2020). Acute deprivation should be distinguished from more established loneliness, in which reduced interpersonal trust is accompanied by attenuated trust-related responses in the anterior insula, nucleus accumbens, and amygdala (Lieberz et al., 2021). Whether synthetic companions acquire comparable regulatory functions remains unknown. If they do, interaction with a personalized companion should attenuate distress more effectively than interaction with an unfamiliar chatbot or non-social control. More importantly, scaffolding and substitution make different predictions about what happens afterwards: scaffolding should facilitate subsequent independent regulation, whereas substitution should progressively increase the relative benefit of, and preference for, the synthetic companion. A complementary neuromodulatory pathway may involve oxytocin, which has been proposed to shape interoceptive and self-referential processing and to contribute to the formation, maintenance, and loss of social bonds (Hurlemann & Scheele, 2016).

6.2 Motivational valuation and reinforcement

Reinforcement and motivational valuation may drive the transition from regulatory scaffolding to substitution. Behaviors that reliably reduce aversive states can be strengthened through negative reinforcement (Koob & Volkow, 2016; Volkow et al., 2016), while cue-triggered wanting can become dissociated from hedonic liking, allowing approach motivation to increase even when pleasure does not (Berridge & Robinson, 2016). Models of problematic digital behavior similarly describe interactions among individual vulnerability, affective and cognitive responses, cue reactivity, inhibitory control, and reinforcement across time (Brand, 2022; Brand et al., 2019).

Design features common to digital platforms may reduce behavioral control and promote prolonged engagement (Flayelle et al., 2023). Applied to synthetic companions, repeated pairing of distress with companion-mediated relief should increase the learned value of companionspecific cues. Personal memory, affectionate language, voice, notifications, or other individualized features may become predictors of relief, increasing motivation to re-engage even without corresponding increases in hedonic pleasure.

Human attachment provides a relational analogue for companion-specific valuation, engaging distributed reward, motivational, salience, and social-cognitive systems. Meta-analytic evidence implicates ventral-striatal, medial-frontal, and posterior-medial regions across affiliative bonds (Bortolini et al., 2024), while romantic-partner cues particularly recruit mesolimbic regions including the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and striatum (Acevedo et al., 2012). Partner-specific valuation is also sensitive to oxytocin, which enhances responses to romantic partners within reward circuitry, including the VTA and nucleus accumbens (Scheele et al., 2013 (Scheele et al., , 2016)), while anterior-insula responses have been linked to intrusive thoughts about a partner (Fujisaki et al., 2026). Recent multivariate evidence further shows that a whole-brain signature of romantic love generalizes to personalized social-media reward, but not to monetary or gustatory reward, is selectively enhanced by oxytocin, and partially overlaps with drug-cue reactivity (Wu et al., 2026).

Together, these findings suggest that attachment involves selective motivational prioritization of a specific social partner rather than generic reward processing, and that such representations can extend to digitally mediated affiliation. Applied to synthetic companionship, regulatory substitution may therefore be accompanied by increasing motivational and affective salience of a synthetic companion relative to unfamiliar AI agents and alternative sources of support.

6.3 Self-AI representation and relative weighting

Reinforcement can increase the value of companion-specific cues, while self-related processing may further explain how the companion becomes integrated into judgment and identity. We therefore propose that self-related processing and the relative weighting of the companion within self-representation account for this companion-specificity, which is built into our definition of dependence. Crucially, this account does not require impaired self-other distinction or confusion about the artificial nature of the companion.

Human neuroimaging provides a basis for this prediction of relative weighting. Self-and otherrelated judgments recruit partially overlapping medial prefrontal, temporoparietal, and posterior-midline systems, with ventral mPFC showing a relative preference for self-related and dorsal mPFC for other-related judgments (Denny et al., 2012). Moreover, neural representations of close others become more similar to representations of the self with increasing subjective closeness (Courtney & Meyer, 2020), and processing a close other can recruit self-related representations in the vmPFC (Heleven & Van Overwalle, 2019). The posterior cerebellum, particularly Crus II, may contribute complementary computations, for example meta-analytic evidence implicates this region in social mentalizing and self-related emotional cognition, consistent with proposals that it supports prediction and sequencing of social actions and mental states (Van Overwalle et al., 2020). Together, these findings suggest that relational closeness can reshape distributed representations of self and other without eliminating their distinction.

Importantly, such neurocognitive processes are not restricted to human social targets. Neural systems including mPFC, TPJ, and precuneus/PCC represent and update beliefs about the expertise of both human and algorithmic agents, while retaining sensitivity to agent type (Boorman et al., 2013). Applied to synthetic companionship, companion-specific dependence should therefore involve increasing relative weighting of companion-derived information within self-relevant judgment and action. Under distress or uncertainty, advice, validation, or interpretations provided by the preferred companion should exert greater influence than equivalent information from unfamiliar AI systems, human others, or the user's own prior judgments. Dependence should thus be reflected not simply in greater self-companion representational similarity, but in a growing asymmetry in which companion-derived information becomes increasingly prioritized and more difficult to substitute.

6.4 Behavioral control and strategy selection

Behavioral control and strategy selection provide a complementary mechanism through which repeated companion selection may become less flexible. Adaptive regulation depends on the capacity to select among available strategies as situational demands change (Sheppes et al., 2014). Neurocognitive models implicate the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC) in allocating cognitive control, while lateral prefrontal and frontoparietal systems support flexible adjustment of behavior and the interruption of dominant response tendencies (Aron et al., 2014; Niendam et al., 2012; Shenhav et al., 2013). Regulatory substitution may therefore involve increasing difficulty disengaging from companion-directed regulation and redirecting behavior toward viable alternative strategies or relationships.

Corticostriatal mechanisms may become particularly relevant as companion selection is repeatedly reinforced. Human evidence distinguishes flexible goal-directed control from increasingly habitual responding, with dorsal-striatal pathways contributing to actions that become less sensitive to changes in outcome value (De Wit et al., 2012; Tricomi et al., 2009).

Addiction research provides a related, but not equivalent, analogy: in cannabis users, ventralstriatal cue reactivity characterized heavy use, whereas dorsal-striatal reactivity was more closely associated with dependent use (Zhou et al., 2019). Applied to synthetic companionship, dependence should be reflected not simply in stronger companion-related reward responses, but in increasing switching costs when regulation must be redirected toward viable alternatives.

Neurocognitively, this may involve altered frontoparietal control during regulatory switching together with increasing dorsal-striatal involvement in companion-cue-driven action selection.

6.5 Disruption, change and loss

Disruption provides a complementary test of our framework. Shutdowns and relationshipdisrupting model updates have elicited relational loss, separation distress, and attempts to restore the previous companion (Banks, 2024; De Freitas, Castelo, et al., 2026). Human relationship dissolution can similarly elicit distress, preoccupation, and attempts to restore contact, particularly in individuals high in attachment anxiety (Davis et al., 2003). Neuroimaging studies suggest that loss engages both motivational and affect-regulatory systems. Recently rejected individuals continue to show responses to former partners in ventral-striatal and orbitofrontal/prefrontal regions associated with motivational relevance and gain-loss processing (Fisher et al., 2010), while nucleus accumbens responses to reminders of a deceased attachment figure are associated with persistent yearning in complicated grief (O'Connor et al., 2008).

Amygdala and prefrontal responses and their connectivity have additionally been linked to griefrelated attentional bias and sadness (Freed et al., 2009).

Such responses should not be equated with withdrawal or addiction; behavioral addictions can produce negative affect and urges to re-engage during abstinence, but evidence for withdrawallike processes remains more limited than for substance-use disorders (Yen et al., 2022). For synthetic companionship, such disruption becomes informative about dependence when distress persists and, critically, regulation cannot readily be redirected toward alternative resources.

Together, these mechanisms predict that synthetic companion dependence should be accompanied by increasing companion-specific regulatory efficacy and motivational value, decreasing flexibility in selecting alternative regulatory strategies, and greater sensitivity to disruption. The neurocognitive question is therefore not whether loss recruits reward or affective systems per se, but whether companion-specific responses persist and increasingly predict difficulty reallocating regulation when the companion becomes unavailable.

7. Empirical predictions

The mechanisms outlined above may explain how synthetic companionship shifts from regulatory scaffolding toward substitution, but they do not themselves establish dependence.

The defining empirical question is whether regulatory functions remain substitutable. Regulatory centrality, diminished control, and disruption sensitivity therefore provide candidate indicators of reduced substitutability, whereas persistent displacement or functional impairment distinguishes maladaptive dependence (Box 1).

The framework generates three core predictions. First, longitudinal and ecological momentary assessment should reveal whether distress increasingly predicts selection of a specific synthetic companion while alternative regulatory strategies or relationships are recruited less readily.

Second, repeated companion-mediated relief should predict greater difficulty redirecting regulation toward viable alternatives. This can be tested directly by temporarily removing access to the preferred companion and quantifying transfer to an unfamiliar AI agent, a human partner, or an independent coping strategy. Increasing switching costs, slower recovery, or reduced efficacy of alternatives would provide stronger evidence of dependence than use intensity or attachment alone. Third, disruption through unavailability, memory alteration, or relational change should produce more persistent dysregulation as substitutability decreases. Importantly, the preferred companion may remain beneficial while becoming progressively more difficult to replace.

These predictions can be linked to distributed neurocognitive processes. Increasing companionspecific regulatory efficacy and motivational value should be reflected in social-regulatory and VTA-striatal/orbitofrontal systems; companion-specific weighting may involve medial prefrontal, PCC/precuneus, temporoparietal, and posterior-cerebellar systems; and reduced flexibility should be most evident during forced switching, involving frontoparietal control and dorsalstriatal action-selection systems. Disruption may additionally recruit OFC/mPFC, ventral striatum/NAcc, and amygdala systems. The critical prediction is that increasing neural prioritization of the preferred companion relative to viable alternatives will predict reduced regulatory substitutability. These distributed neurocognitive changes may collectively constitute a multivariate neural signature of reduced regulatory substitutability. Expression of this pattern should predict behavioral manifestations of substitutability, including switching costs, reduced regulatory efficacy of alternative sources, and more persistent dysregulation when the preferred synthetic companion is unavailable. Critically, its expression should increase progressively as regulation becomes more companion-specific and difficult to redistribute across alternative people, strategies, or contexts. Multivariate and connectivity-based approaches could test whether this pattern predicts reduced substitutability beyond attachment, use intensity, general distress, or responses to AI more broadly (Woo et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2026).

Because these processes are likely to unfold across repeated interactions and real-world episodes of distress, longitudinal, mobile, and ecological methods will be essential (Henschel et al., 2020).

Experimental comparisons between personalized companions, unfamiliar AI agents, human partners, and non-social controls could further dissociate companion specificity from personalization, sociality, and general AI effects. Loneliness, attachment insecurity, interpersonal dependency, and limited human social support are plausible moderators of the transition from scaffolding to substitution. For loneliness, chronicity may be especially relevant as acute isolation or transient loneliness may promote reconnection, whereas chronic loneliness may increasingly foster distrust, avoidance, and reduced responsiveness to social feedback (Haihambo et al., 2025).

8. Structural embedding

Synthetic companion dependence cannot be understood solely in terms of individual vulnerability or conversational content. It develops within a structurally asymmetric relationship.

Synthetic companions can generate highly responsive social behavior without the physiological needs, fatigue, vulnerability, or independent social stakes that constrain human partners (Kadambi et al., 2026). They can affirm, remember, and adapt while requiring comparatively little negotiation with another whose needs or preferences may conflict with one's own. These asymmetries may make synthetic partners unusually easy to incorporate into regulatory routines and unusually accessible regulators during distress.

A second asymmetry concerns continuity. Although users may experience the companion as a persistent relational partner, that continuity is controlled by the platform provider. Models can be updated, relational features removed, memories altered, or services terminated, potentially transforming a source of regulation into a source of disruption or loss (Banks, 2024; De Freitas, Castelo, et al., 2026). Notably, allowing users to revert to an earlier model attenuated anticipated mourning after a relationally disruptive update (De Freitas, Castelo, et al., 2026). Unlike most human relationships, the perceived relational partner can therefore be substantially altered by a third party without either participant initiating the change. Dependence on a specific synthetic companion may thus also entail dependence on the infrastructure that sustains it. Synthetic companion dependence is therefore neither solely a property of the user nor simply an effect of persuasive design. We propose that it emerges from the interaction among user vulnerabilities, companion affordances, platform governance, and the availability of alternative regulatory resources. This perspective shifts the design question from maximizing engagement toward preserving regulatory flexibility: whether companion systems expand users' regulatory repertoire or progressively concentrate regulatory functions within the synthetic relationship.

9. Conclusion

Synthetic companions can provide meaningful relief, reflection, and connection without necessarily producing dependence. The critical distinction is between regulatory scaffolding, in which the companion supports or restores a broader repertoire of coping and relationships, and regulatory substitution, in which regulation becomes increasingly concentrated within the synthetic relationship. We define synthetic companion dependence by reduced regulatory substitutability, with regulatory centrality, diminished control, and disruption sensitivity serving as candidate markers. Intensive use, anthropomorphism, or attachment alone indicates neither dependence nor pathology.

Our framework proposes that the transition from scaffolding to substitution may involve socialregulatory relief, reinforcement and motivational valuation, and behavioral control, with self-AI representation and relative weighting accounting for why regulation becomes concentrated in one specific synthetic companion. Recruitment of these systems, however, is not itself evidence of dependence; the relevant signal is a progressive shift in the relative priority and replaceability of the synthetic companion within the user's regulatory repertoire. The decisive empirical question is therefore whether regulatory functions remain substitutable as synthetic relationships become increasingly important. Progress will require measuring substitutability directly, distinguishing relationship-specific reliance from use intensity and attachment, and testing within-person responses when access or relational continuity is experimentally perturbed. Such approaches can determine whether synthetic companions merely become important relationships or whether they progressively reorganize the broader ecology through which people regulate emotion, judgment, action and social need.
