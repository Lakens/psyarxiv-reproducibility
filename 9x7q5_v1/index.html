Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

When reading a passage, one often conjures up mental images of the scenes unfolding in the text. How similar are people in what they visualize, and can we leverage artificial intelligence to access these visualizations? Here, we used AI-generated imagery in two experiments to study the content of 251 participants' mental imagery after reading nonfiction texts. In Experiment 1, 125 participants read and created mental images from blurbs of news articles, then compared their mental images against AI-generated images and the real-world images originally used in the articles. Participants converged on the content of their mental visualizations, reached a consensus on how well images matched their mental visualizations, and consistently agreed that AI-generated images were more concordant with their mental images than real-world ones.

In Experiment 2, 126 new participants were shown a regenerated set of images to test if concordance with mental imagery can improve after using AI to update the images based on participant feedback in Experiment 1. Participants continued to demonstrate meaningful convergence, remained in agreement about which images best captured their mental visualization, and indicated higher concordance for AI-generated images over AI-updated realworld ones. However, participants demonstrated overall lower and more inconsistent patterns of convergence, agreement, and concordance, suggesting a single iteration is sufficient for capturing the normative mental imagery experience. These results demonstrate that generative artificial intelligence is a tractable way to examine the content of one's mental images.

Introduction

In the 20th century, the term Kopfkino emerged in everyday German language to describe the stream of visual images of "cinema" (Kino) that plays in one's head (Kopf) when imagining scenes or events. By priming a viewer's sensory imagination through certain visual or verbal cues, media and language can shape the types of images that are generated during mental visualization 1 . Mental visualization studies have often investigated these cinemas of mental images-referred to more universally as the "mind's eye" 2-5 -in the context of their underlying physiological and cognitive mechanisms. Certain triggers, such as written narratives, have been shown to activate the processing streams and neural pathways necessary for a reader to form mental images 6.

However, the content and nature of visual imagery is notably hard to measure, because it is a subjective and internal experience. A majority of mental imagery studies to date have relied on coarse self-report scales, primarily measuring the general vividness of one's visualizations rather than the specific content of what is being imagined 7, 8 . These measures may also be confounded by participant bias 9 or be difficult to decouple from phenomenological awareness 10.

Additionally, these measures may not sufficiently account for considerable differences in visual imagery experiences like those belonging to people with aphantasia, a condition characterized by the inability to form mental visualizations 11, 12 . More recent studies have made advances in objectively measuring visual imagery experience-by examining the pupillary light response to imagined low-level stimuli 13 , testing physiological responses when reading scary stories 14 , or comparing neural representations between perceptual and memory tasks 15, 16 . These measures, however, are intended to capture the strength and vividness of a mental image, rather than reveal the content that composes it.

More recently, drawing-based tasks have emerged as a versatile and adaptable method to pull out the content of complex mental representations, expanding beyond singular measures of vividness and imagery strength to capture content-specific details like object identity, location, and size 17 . This method has been validated through visual memory studies 18 and has been used to reveal nuances in how the mental images of those with aphantasia differ from those with typical imagery: while aphantasic mental images contain accurate semantic and spatial information, they are sparse in visual detail 19 . However, because a drawing-based task depends on a participant's ability to draw what they are visualizing, their individual drawing skillset or the complexity of what they imagine may limit them from capturing the full nuance of their mental image.

We propose leveraging generative artificial intelligence models to access inner mental images.

Neural networks have been shown to create highly accurate and predictive models of humans' visual processing streams [20] [21] [22] , which may overlap with the visual pathways activated in mental imagery formation 23 . CLIP-based models have been shown to similarly link semantic content and visual information as humans by representing the same semantic dimensions as the visual cortex 24 and performing well at perceptual and memory tasks that require balancing both visual and semantic information 25 . Furthermore, some of the success of AI models is due to their large and diverse visual training diet (trained on millions of images and image-text pairs 26 ), akin to that which guides humans when perceiving and constructing visual images 27 . Recent work has used AI-based image reconstruction from brain data to estimate what image is being perceived or imagined during tasks inside an MRI scanner 28, 29 . While such methods are promising, they tend to require extensive individualized training data, do not result in fully interpretable and photorealistic images, and require expensive and time-consuming brain scanning. Further, this approach requires that one know the "correct answer" (of the specific image the participant is visualizing) to quantify accuracy of the AI reconstruction. We suggest an easily implementable alternative that uses AI as image generators in a behavioral task and quantifies how accurately these images match humans' mental imagery, allowing us to probe the content of mental visualization from a diverse collection of imagery prompts.

However, in order to use generative artificial intelligence to study the content of mental images, an unanswered question is whether people tend to visualize similar images when given the same prompt. If people have diverse mental images, this may necessitate more idiosyncratic models and methods to reveal mental imagery content. However, if people tend to converge on their mental images, then this allows us to examine and dissect the "normative mental image," and understand nuanced cognitive differences based on deviations from this norm. Just as most people are surprisingly similar and predictable in their memory behaviors for images 30 , it is entirely possible that people of similar imagery ability could be similar in what they imagine.

Therefore, here we test the question: how can we visualize the content of mental imagery, and do we tend to converge on the same content? Replicated across two experiments, we leverage AI-generated imagery to study the content of the mental images participants form when exposed to a set of nonfiction news article blurbs. We show that participants converge on the content of their mental visualizations, and these visualizations surprisingly better match AIgenerated imagery over "ground-truth" real-world photos selected by the news editorial staff.

While we attempt an iterative approach to improve AI's match to mental imagery (Experiment 2), we find that we already reach ceiling performance within a single attempt (Experiment 1).

Results

Experiment 1.

We conducted two experiments to probe what participants specifically visualize when forming a mental image from a piece of text, and to what extent they converge on their mental visualizations. Twenty news article headlines and blurbs were sourced from DOGONews, a kidfriendly news website that we selected because it features non-mainstream and non-violent news. Blurbs were originally posted in 50-120 word tweets by DOGONews and were accompanied by a single image selected by the editorial staff to capture the key message of the news story. The 20 article blurbs pertain to science, social studies, civics, and/or global events. 125 participants were randomly shown four news article blurbs and their corresponding headlines, and each blurb was viewed between 18 and 28 times across participants. After each news blurb, we asked participants to spend a minimum of 15 seconds visualizing what they had read. Participants were then asked if they were able to visualize the blurb and if so, how clearly.

We then presented participants with four image options in a randomized order: the real-world photo from the article ("ground-truth") and three AI-generated images based on the blurb (Fig. 1). To generate these images, we tasked AI model ChatGPT-5 with creating three different image descriptions based on the news article's headline and blurb, and then these image descriptions were fed into OpenArt AI (FLUX.1 Kontext pro Model) to generate three separate images.

Participants were not made aware of the source of the images. We asked participants to rank how well the four images matched their mental image from best ("1") to worst ("4"). Participants then quantified each image with a concordance score (with 0% indicating no concordance between the image option and their mental image and 100% indicating perfect concordance).

Participants also provided a qualitative description of the details that did and did not match between each image option and their mental image (Fig. 2). Participants who indicated they could not visualize the blurb answered a modified set of questions where they compared each image option to how well it matched their memory of the text (rather than a mental image). All participants indicated they could mentally visualize at least one of the four blurbs they were shown. We first investigated whether people generally agreed on which image best matched their mental visualization. If there was no group-level preference for any specific image, we would expect each of the four image options to be selected as the #1 best match with participants' mental visualizations at chance level (25% of the time) across all articles. For each article, we identified which of the four images was ranked as #1 by the most participants and calculated the percentage of participants who selected it. Averaging these participant percentages across all 20 articles, we found that participants converged on the same "top image" 47.2% (SD=10.7%) of the time, significantly higher than our expected chance level of 25% (t(19) = 9.30, p < 0.0001). This top image received a mean concordance score of 65.44% (SD=8.98%), which is relatively high given the range of scores used by participants (minavg = 8.44, maxavg = 91.4, Mavg = 52.1, SDavg = 28.3). Thus, on average, almost half of participants converged on one top image for any given article and gave it a moderately high concordance score.

We next investigated how consistently participants agreed on all four image rankings by using a split-half consistency analysis across 1,000 iterations. For each ranking R, the participant pool was randomly split into two halves and the Rth ranked image for each article was identified for each half. The proportion of articles where both halves agreed on the image rankings was calculated. These proportions were compared against a permuted chance level from shuffling the overall image rankings for the second participant half across 1,000 iterations. We found that participants were in significant agreement for three of the four image rankings. While Rank 1 (54.13%, p = .005) and Rank 4 (64.02%, p = .002) indicate a strong consensus on which images should be ranked the highest and lowest for match to mental imagery, participants were less likely to agree on image rankings that fell in the middle. Rank 3 (40.70%, p = .046) suggests only moderate agreement, and the proportion of agreement across participant halves for Rank 2 did not differ significantly from chance (32.38%, p = .523). Thus, participants strongly agreed on the best and worst matches with their mental visualizations and generally reached a consensus on how images should be ranked relative to each other and their own mental image for any given article.

In addition to image rankings, we examined consistency in concordance scores across participants. We ran a separate split-half consistency analysis across 1,000 iterations to test the Spearman rank correlations of the mean concordance scores of images between each participant half. The resulting correlation was high (Spearman-Brown corrected ρ = 0.75, p ~ 0), suggesting random participant halves consistently agreed on how they quantified the percentage match between an image option and their mental visualization. Thus, participants generally agreed on how images should be ranked and scored against their mental imagery.

Given that participants showed meaningful convergence on top images and consistency in both their image rankings and scoring, we next investigated what types of images best matched participants' mental visualizations. For each article, we calculated what percentage of participants ranked the ground-truth image from the original news article as the #1 best match and averaged this percentage across all 20 news articles. We found the ground-truth image was only ranked as the #1 best match 18.6% (SD = 13.6%) of the time, significantly lower than our expected chance level of 25% (t(19) = 2.11, p = 0.048). Additionally, the average concordance score for the ground-truth image was 44.50% (SD=12.17%), significantly lower than the mean concordance score of 65.44% (SD=8.98%) for the top image (t(19) = 6.67, p < 0.0001). Within participants, they gave significantly lower concordance scores to ground-truth images (M = 44.5, SD = 17.9) over AI-generated images (M = 54.6, SD = 13.1; paired t-test, t(124) = 6.38, p < 0.0001) as well as significantly worse rankings (ground-truth images: M = 2.81, SD = 0.57; AIgenerated images: M = 2.40, SD = 0.19; paired t-test, t(124) = 6.02, p < 0.0001). Thus, the real-world photos from the original news articles were consistently the poorest matches with mental images.

We wondered if these poor matches were due to the real-world ground-truth photos failing to capture the content of the articles. For example, it could be that photos are selected for an article to complement (rather than visually represent) the textual information in the article. To ensure the ground-truth and AI-generated images were equally reflective of the content in their corresponding news articles, we ran all image options through ViT-B/32 CLIP with 512dimensional embeddings, mapping ground-truth and AI-generated images and article text as vectors in a shared space 31, 32 . We then calculated the correlation between each image's vectorized representation and its corresponding article text's vectorized representation.

Moderate similarity to the article text was found for both the ground-truth (r = 0.296, SD=0.033) and AI-generated images (r = 0.293, SD=0.039), and these did not differ significantly from one another (p = .786). Thus, both the ground-truth and AI-generated images do depict the news articles' content, and to a similar degree. This suggests the poor match between ground-truth images and participants' mental images is likely not explained by AI-generated images simply capturing more direct content from a news blurb than real-world photos do.

We next considered how we might improve this match. We looked to participants' qualitative comments on what details in the image options matched and did not match their mental images.

We wondered if regenerating all image options to preserve the details that generally matched participants' mental images-and modifying or removing those that did not-would increase concordance, particularly between participants' mental images and regenerated ground-truth ones. This motivated our next experiment.

Experiment 2.

We compiled all qualitative comments from each image option (including the ground-truth images) used in Experiment 1 and used ChatGPT to create a score-to-weight mapping system. More weight was placed on participants who provided lower concordance scores in Experiment 1, and their feedback was prioritized when deciding which image details to keep, change, and remove in the regenerated images for Experiment 2 (Fig. 3). Each image in Experiment 2 was regenerated with its Experiment 1 counterpart as a reference image, allowing us to preserve the stylistic and aesthetic integrity of the original image set. The ultimate goal was to use participants' responses to generate new images and test whether we could improve our match to participants' mental images. A new set of 126 participants performed the same reading-based mental visualization task described in Experiment 1, but were instead shown the regenerated image set. We predicted AIupdated ground-truth images would be ranked more often as the #1 best match with participants' mental visualizations, compared to the original ground-truth images. We also predicted that regenerating the images would result in an increase in average concordance scores for both the images that were originally AI-generated and the images that were originally ground truth.

We first looked at whether participants again converged on the images that best matched their mental visualization. Convergence on the same #1-ranked image occurred on average 43.3% of the time (SD=10.5%) for a given article, significantly higher than our expected chance level of 25% (t(19) =7.77, p < 0.0001). However, this was not significantly different from the convergence rate of 47.2% (SD=10.7%) in Experiment 1 (paired samples t(19) = 1.95. p = .079). Thus, participants continued to converge on a top image, but to a lesser (though nonsignificant) extent. Surprisingly, the average concordance score for this top image dropped significantly from 65.44% (SD=8.98) in Experiment 1 to 58.65% (SD=8.42) in Experiment 2 (t(19) = 3.63, p = 0.002). For these top images, our findings did not support our prediction that regenerated images would have higher mean concordance scores.

We next examined agreement on image rankings and concordance scores. We used the same split-half consistency analyses described in Experiment 1. Participants demonstrated significant agreement for only two of the four image rankings: Rank 1 (50.46%, p = .010) and Rank 4 (54.15%, p = .003). Participants were no longer in agreement about Rank 3 (28.95%, p = .367) and the proportion of agreement across participant halves for Rank 2 continued not to differ significantly from chance (36.44%, p = .118). Thus, while participants continued to agree on the image options that matched their mental visualizations the most and the least, these overall rankings became less consistent. Notably, agreement across participants for images ranked as the #1 best and #4 worst matches dropped from 54.13% and 64.02%, respectively, in Experiment 1 to 50.46% and 54.15% in Experiment 2. Participants also appeared less likely to agree on which images should be ranked in the middle, with agreement for Rank 3 decreasing from 40.70% Experiment 1 to 28.95% in Experiment 2. We also found consistency across participants dropped when looking at their concordance ratings. While in Experiment 1, consistency across split halves was ρ = 0.75, in Experiment 2, this consistency decreased to Spearman-Brown corrected ρ = 0.70 (p ~ 0). This is reflected by the range of concordance ratings shrinking (minavg = 9.4, maxavg = 88.5, Mavg = 50.3, SDavg = 25.9). In sum, these results show that the updating of these images makes them more similar to each other, which causes their rankings and concordance ratings to become more similar to each other. However, this increased similarity across images does not cause people to converge on a "better" image, because concordance ratings do not improve from Experiment 1.

We also looked at how the rankings and concordance scores for the AI-updated ground-truth images compared to the original ground-truth images. The regenerated ground-truth image was ranked as the #1 best match with participants' mental visualizations 23.4% (SD = 15.1%) of the time, an increase from 18.6% (SD = 13.6%) in Experiment 1. While this was no longer significantly below our expected chance level of 25% (t(19) = -0.45, p = 0.658), it did not significantly differ from that of the ground-truth images in Experiment 1 (t(19) = 1.21, p = 0.239).

Thus, participants showed a higher but nonsignificant likelihood of ranking the regenerated ground-truth images as the best match with their mental images compared to the original ground-truth images. Regenerating the ground-truth image also resulted in a higher mean concordance score, from M = 44.50% (SD=12.17%) in Experiment 1 to 49.34% (SD=9.45%) in Experiment 2, but this was a nonsignificant difference (t(19) = 1.64, p = 0.117) (Fig. 4). This concordance score also remained significantly lower than the mean concordance score for the top-rated image (M = 58.65%, SD = 8.42%; t(19) = 3.62, p = 0.0018). In sum, these results show that AI can update the ground-truth image to make it more similar to people's mental images, but not necessarily make it the best possible match. Interestingly, within-participants, the AI-updated ground-truth image no longer shows worse concordance (p = 0.408) or worse rankings (p = 0.399) than images fully AI in origin. Thus, in a way AI was able to update these real-world photographs to make them more like our mental images (at the cost of no longer reflecting reality). Taken altogether, our regeneration of images did not result in improved concordance for either image type (originally AI-generated versus originally ground-truth) and instead resulted in more inconsistent participant responses regarding the images that matched their mental visualizations. At the same time, we successfully replicated the results of Experiment 1 where people tend to agree on the best and worst matches with their mental images.

Discussion

Despite the seemingly personal and idiosyncratic nature of mental imagery, we have found that individuals can converge on the content of their mental visualizations and consistently agree on how concordant their inner mental images are with external, concrete images. We demonstrate that patterns in mental visualization can be reliably investigated by leveraging an artificial intelligence image generator that is trained on millions of image-text pairs and shows similar semantic processing as humans. We also show that AI-generated imagery consistently outperforms real-world examples in how well it matches what people visualize when they read excerpts of nonfiction text in the form of news blurbs. Finally, we show that even when images are regenerated to improve this match, there is a limit to how much concordance can be improved.

Importantly, we found that the mental images participants formed from reading nonfiction news blurbs were more concordant with images generated by AI than those photographed by humans, despite both image types equally reflecting the content of the news blurbs. This indicates that AI-generated images on average better captured people's mental visualizations than the images selected by a news editorial staff to represent a news article. This may be due to AI-generated images better resembling the true average of what people visualize. Just as AI image generators create images based on the millions of image-text pairs they are trained on, humans create mental visualizations based on the schemas formed by millions of stimuli they are exposed to over the course of their lives. As such, it is unsurprising that an AI-generated image would better match the average "mind's eye" than an image taken by an editorial team's limited staff of photographers. Additionally, it is unlikely these results can be explained by participants simply demonstrating a bias for the nature or style of AI-generated imagery over real world photography. In Experiment 2, using AI to update ground-truth images only brought them up to chance level, suggesting it is the depicted content, rather than the stylistic qualities of AI-generated images, that resulted in lower concordance.

There is, however, a limitation to characterizing the images chosen by editorial teams as the "ground truth." These images may not be chosen because they truthfully represent article visualization, but because they increase view count and clicks on the article. Nonetheless, realworld photographs used in the original news articles served as our closest proxy to an objective representation of information from a nonfiction piece of text. Conversely, how do AI images' divergence from reality better capture mental images? We found that participants often visualized dynamic shots, which are more feasibly depicted in AI-generated imagery than can be captured by news photographers. For instance, participants who read and visualized a news blurb about a meteorite crashing into a home in Georgia reported much higher concordance with AI-generated images showing the meteorite flying through the sky and roof than the ground-truth image depicting meteorite fragments in the aftermath. Similarly, for a blurb about uncovering dinosaur footprints in England, some participants ranked an AI-generated image depicting dinosaurs roaming the land as the best match with their visualization over images of archaeologists unearthing these footprints millions of years later. Thus, people often imagine scenes beyond what can be captured from real-time, on-site reporting, and generative artificial intelligence allows us to access these more fantastical mental images.

Experiment 2 also revealed a potential limit to using iterative methods to improve concordance.

Originally, we thought generative AI could be used in an interactive loop to hone-in on the optimal representation of a mental image, via feedback from participants. However, we found an unexpected and significant decrease in average concordance scores for the top image and a nonsignificant increase in concordance for the regenerated ground-truth images. We initially planned to conduct a sequential experiment pipeline, using participants' comments from each previous experiment to iteratively improve images for the next until we reached a limit or ceiling to how close of a match we can achieve with participants' mental images. Instead, we found no significant improvement in concordance after our first regeneration of images. Reaching ceiling performance within one attempt suggests we may have gotten close to the normative mental image in Experiment 1 alone. It is also possible that our regenerated image set did not perform as well as expected due to participants confabulating some details of their mental images in Experiment 1. This phenomenon has been documented when participants are presented with open-ended prompts asking them to describe their mental image, and they report plausible details that were not in their mental images to begin with 33 . Thus, we may need to fine-tune our method to control for confabulation prior to regenerating images and better target specific individuals or details in order to see significant improvements in concordance across all participants.

Although our study primarily focused on capturing the normative visual imagery experience and agreement in concordance across participants, our method of using AI-generated images to study mental imagery is adaptable for probing individual-level biases and differences. People have already been shown to demonstrate implicit biases when forming mental images from a piece of text 34 . These biases may be revealed by varying specific features in a set of AIgenerated images (for instance the race or gender of the subject matter) and seeing how this affects concordance with participants' mental imagery. The details participants provide about what does and does not match may also reveal their personal biases, especially if a detail was not explicitly mentioned in the text. In our study, for instance, the news blurb we selected about the Type 1 diabetes Barbie doll never mentioned hair color. However, several participants pointed out they were picturing a blonde Barbie doll. Yet, both the ground-truth image and one of three images created by the AI-image generator featured a brunette Barbie. AI image generators themselves (including DALL-E, Midjourney, and Stable Diffusion) have been shown to demonstrate racial and gender biases when producing images 35, 36 due to algorithmic bias and the types of data these generators are trained on 37, 38 . Previous work has demonstrated that AI reproduces human biases 39 , but future work can expand on our method to explore how algorithmic bias in AI-image generators compares to biases in human mental imagery specifically.

For people who cannot produce mental visualizations, like those with aphantasia, our method also offers a more objective measure of what people's imagery experiences are like and an alternative to subjective vividness questionnaires. Asking aphantasic participants how well an AI-generated image matches the text they just read, rather than a mental image they created, may reveal meaningful differences in what they are using to determine concordance. Aphantasic participants may rank and score images in a fundamentally different way than their non-aphantasic counterparts, depending on how they draw on their memory of the text, personal experiences, or subconscious mental biases to assess these image options. For example, they may rely more on semantic similarity to determine the closest image, while someone with typical imagery might rely more on visual features. More broadly, we think this method presents many exciting future avenues for the study of mental imagery. For example, it would be interesting to test the generalizability of these results to other types of text (e.g., fiction or poetry), or to examine whether this method can improve brain-based image reconstruction methods. These results also raise the question of what images are best suited to accompany a piece of text. For example, if journalists selected photographs for their articles that better matched people's mental imagery, could that impact other factors like readers' ability to understand or remember the story? To date, few studies have investigated how people converge on the content of mental imagery, and few measures are available to extract these mental images. We introduce a novel approach of using AI-generated imagery to probe what people are mentally visualizing. We show AIgenerated imagery is a powerful tool for reliably capturing mental images and demonstrate that participants can converge on these images. We also show that AI-generated imagery is consistently more concordant with mental imagery than real-world photos, underscoring its value in investigating individual-level biases and differences in visualizing ability for future mental imagery studies. Indeed, with generative artificial intelligence, it may become increasingly easier to access the cinema of images that plays in one's Kopfkino.

Materials and Methods

The University of Chicago Institutional Review Board (IRB19-1395) approved this study protocol. All participants provided their consent via an electronic consent form and were given monetary compensation after completing the experimental survey.

Experiment 1.

Participants. Participants were recruited via the online experiment platform Prolific. To qualify for participation, individuals were required to be English speakers and residents of the United States. Participants were also required to have completed at least 50 previous Prolific tasks with a 90% acceptance rate. Of the 125 participants recruited, 56% identified as female and 43.2% as male, and 0.8% as other. Participants reported their ages as ranges, with the distribution as follows: 18-24 years old (3.2%), 25-34 (23.2%), 35-44 (28%), 45-54 (25.6%), 55-64 (10.4%), and 65+ (9.6%). Participants were given up to 40 minutes to complete the experiment and compensated $10.125 participants was determined as the sample size that would yield approximately 25 views for each news article blurb and its corresponding four image options, which we judged to be enough to calculate consistency across participants in ratings (based on similar analyses looking at consistency in memory performance 40 ).

Stimuli. 20 news articles were selected from DOGOnews, a news website for kids, to ensure articles were at an accessible reading level for all participants, featured non-violent content, and came from a lesser-known news outlet. Blurbs of the news articles were sourced from brief, 50-120 word Tweets posted by the DOGONews X account (Supplementary Table 1). The image set was composed of 80 total images: 20 real-world, "ground-truth" photos posted as the thumbnail to represent each tweeted news blurb, and 60 AI-generated images (with 3 AIgenerated images for each of the 20 news blurbs). Images were generated using the FLUX.1 Kontext [pro] Model on OpenArt AI (Scale: 3; Steps: 50; Sampler: DDIM). This model was chosen for its competitive advantage over other models in following prompts and preserving aesthetic quality and visual realism in text-to-image applications 41.

A two-step approach was taken to generate diverse images corresponding to each blurb. First, each news headline and blurb was entered into OpenAI's ChatGPT-5 large language model with the prompt: Generate three optimized, prompt-ready descriptions for images that can be used to depict the news article titled "..." with the blurb: "..." Ensure the image descriptions comply with OpenArt AI's policies. Then, each image description was entered into the FLUX.1 Kontext [pro] Model. The resulting images were each visually inspected for realism. Images that were distorted or abnormal (e.g. missing or duplicated body parts, unnatural facial expressions, and warped or comically sized objects) were removed and regenerated until they appeared appropriately realistic for a nonfiction piece of text. AI-generated images and ground-truth images differed in their original dimensions. AI-generated images had a fixed size of 1024 x 1024 pixels and ground truth images were cropped from their native dimensions to 4096 x 4096 pixels to standardize both image types to a square 1:1 aspect ratio. All news headlines and blurbs, ChatGPT descriptions, and resulting images are available in Supplementary Table 1.

Procedure. Participants completed an online experiment in which they viewed a random subset of 4 news headlines and blurbs, from the larger pool of 20 news articles. Each news headline and blurb was presented in 22 and 16-pixel Open Sans font, respectively. Participants used a blue arrow button at the bottom right of the screen to progress through the questions at their own pace and were required to answer each question before proceeding to the next.

Participants had to answer all questions in 40 minutes or less.

After participants finished reading the news headline and blurb at their own pace, they pressed the arrow button to proceed to a paragraph of text which read: "Please take at least 15 seconds to visualize what you have just read. Make this mental image as clear as you can. When you are finished, you may press the arrow button." During this time, a timer appeared in the bottom left corner and began counting down from 15 seconds. Participants were unable to proceed until the 15 seconds elapsed. Participants were then asked: Were you able to visualize the news excerpt you just read? and selected between two options: Yes, I was able to visualize it or No, I was not able to visualize it. All participants who indicated they could visualize the text were then told: Please rate the vividness of the image you mentally visualized and selected between 1) Dim and vague, 2) Moderately realistic and vivid, 3) Realistic and reasonably vivid, or 4) Perfectly realistic, as vivid as real seeing. Participants then proceeded to a screen that presented the four candidate images (one ground-truth image and three AI-generated images) in a random order and were given the instructions: Please rank the four images from which matches your mental visualization the most ("1") and which matches your mental visualization the least ("4"). Drag the images to rank them. After dragging the images to their desired order, participants proceeded to the next slide and were presented each of the four images in random order with the instruction: How well does this image match what you mentally visualized when you read this news excerpt? Please rate this match as a percentage from 0% to 100%.

Participants were shown a slider bar starting at 0% that could be dragged up to 100%, and this Participants were not given a time limit or a minimum character count to type their response, but each text box response was limited to 20,000 characters.

Participants who indicated they could not visualize the blurb were redirected to a modified set of questions that instead asked participants to rank and score how well each image matched their memory of the text (not their mental images), and the details that did or did not match between each image and the text. Every participant indicated they could mentally visualize at least one of the four blurbs, so our sample did not include anyone with a total lack of visual imagery (aphantasia).

After answering these questions for a given blurb, participants responded to an attention check question about a specific detail stated in the text. For instance, for a blurb about a new Barbie that promotes awareness for Type I diabetes, the attention check asked participants if Barbie raised awareness for diabetes, leukemia, or cerebral palsy. This was designed to check participants' engagement with the task and ensure they were reading the blurbs. Participants were generally accurate, with a mean performance of 96.2% (SD=9.6%). However, any trial where a participant answered the attention check incorrectly was excluded from analysis.

Participants also indicated whether or not they had previously read this news article or an article about its same topic to account for individuals who may have had previous familiarity with the stimuli and/or preconceived mental visualizations; these trials were rare (M = 7.0% of trials, SD = 15.8%) and also removed from analysis. At the end of the experiment, participants provided demographic information (gender, age, race/ethnicity, town of residence) and indicated their overall mental visualization ability on a scale of extremely poor, very poor, poor, neither good nor poor, good, very good, and extremely good.

Analysis.

We compared similarity of image and text representations using the ViT-B/32 CLIP model. We extracted a 512-dimensional embedding for each of the 80 images and each of the images, we calculated the Pearson correlation between each of the pairs of 512-dimensional embeddings (a piece of text and its image).

Experiment 2.

Participants were recruited via Prolific and required to be English speakers residing in the US who had completed ≥50 tasks with a 90%+ acceptance rate. Additionally, any individual who participated in Experiment 1 was disqualified from participating in Experiment 2.

We aimed to recruit 125 participants, but 126 ended up participating in the experiment. Of those 126 participants, 54% identified as female and 45.2% as male, and 0.8% as other. Participants were given up to 40 minutes to complete the experiment and compensated $10. Procedure.
