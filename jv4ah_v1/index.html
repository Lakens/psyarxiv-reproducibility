How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Across biology, artificial intelligence and social systems, interacting components develop specialised functions and collective capabilities that were not individually specified in advance. Examples range from reorganised living cells to functionally differentiated neural networks and collective decisions in animal groups. 2. The SOIPS engine: how interaction can generate organisation

The SOIPS engine describes the conditions and causal processes through which organisation can develop. It requires components whose states or relationships can change; information-mediated causal interactions among or within them; a temporal and relational structure for those interactions; constraints that shape what can occur; and the physical resources needed to realise the processes. These are complementary aspects of a generative process, not five independent mechanisms.

A state change alters a component's current condition or relationships. Plasticity is more specific: it changes connections, response rules or other features governing subsequent activity. Plasticity can support learning and reorganisation, but consequential information processing need not always involve it. [10] Information-mediated interaction occurs when a difference in one state can causally influence another state, process or collective activity. The key requirement is causal accessibility: a state difference must be capable of making a difference through an identifiable causal pathway. Mere statistical correlation is insufficient. Such interactions can occur within cells, along neural pathways, between artificial processing units or through exchanges among organisms.

Emerging systems can also change their own connections, thresholds or rules, altering the conditions for further development:

Information-mediated interaction → changed organisation or constraints → changed possibilities for subsequent interaction.

All SOIPS processes depend on physical mechanisms and resources. Biological systems use molecular, electrical and mechanical interactions, while artificial systems rely on physical computing hardware. These mechanisms constrain which forms of information processing and organisation are possible. The effectiveness of information exchange also depends on how information is organised and directed: more signalling does not necessarily improve coordination, particularly when information is unreliable, excessive or poorly routed. [15] At its simplest, the engine describes a continuing causal process:

State difference → information-mediated causal interaction → changed state or relationship → further interaction.

Across multiple components, these interactions can become organised, and the organisation may change the pathways and constraints that generated it:

Interaction → organisation → changed interactions and constraints → further organisation.

This is the self-organising aspect of SOIPS: interactions among components generate organisation without its detailed form being specified in advance. As that organisation develops, it can itself change the interactions and constraints that shape further development. This then provides a basis from which more differentiated functions can develop.

Dimension B identifies three elementary functional capability families: retention, inference and regulation.

Retention (B1) is the preservation of information about previous states or events in a form that remains potentially available for later processing or behaviour. A retained chemical condition, altered neural connection or stored record allows information from the past to influence future activity. [20] The information need not actually be used; what matters is that it remains available for possible subsequent use. Simply maintaining a physical state does not constitute functional retention unless that state can influence later processing.

Inference (B2) is the derivation of a usable conclusion, estimate or implication from available information and its relationships. This differs from arbitrary transformation. A neural or computational system can infer an unobserved condition without symbolic deliberation or a sophisticated internal world model. [21] Regulation (B3) is information-guided influence organised around maintaining, restoring or steering an outcome or condition. This differs from a causal change that has no relevant outcome-directed organisation.

Feedback and feedforward control can both implement regulation, and simple control does not inherently require learning or prediction. [11, 12] Consider a company deciding where to advertise. Its stored sales records make past performance available (retention); analysis indicates which sales channel is likely to produce the best return (inference); and managers adjust expenditure towards a desired outcome (regulation). These functions can also occur separately: a record may be retained without being analysed, an estimate can be derived from current inputs, and a simple controller can regulate without learning from past experience.

The relationship between Dimensions A and B is reciprocal. For example, Functional signalling can coordinate responses and contribute to regulation; representations and models can support inference or regulation; and context-sensitive organisation can select and combine these processes. Retention preserves information for later use; inference generates information for subsequent processing; and regulation can alter operating conditions, information access or connections among components. Thus, Dimension A describes informational organisation, while Dimension B describes elementary functional capabilities. Their interactions can generate composite capabilities and reshape subsequent organisation.

4. From self-organisation to composite capabilities

Self-organisation is the generation or maintenance of coordinated patterns, structures or functions through component interactions without specifying every resulting arrangement component by component. It can occur within externally designed architectures or inherited biological constraints. Emergence describes properties and functions that arise at the collective level because of those interactions. Self-organisation concerns how organisation develops; emergence concerns what develops from it. [22] Components may become functionally differentiated through their positions, inputs, histories and constraints.

Some roles are imposed, while others develop through interaction. In trained neural networks, specialisation can arise without assigning every unit its eventual role. In Anthrobots, human airway-derived cells cultured outside their ordinary tissue environment self-assemble into motile structures, exhibiting collective behaviours not expressed in their original tissue [2]. A company may likewise reorganise its existing staff and knowledge to pursue a new opportunity. In each case, changing the context changes which collective functions can develop, whether the components are biological, computational or social.

5. Consciousness as a composite capability

The preceding analysis suggests an approach to consciousness that begins not by assuming a unique physical substrate but by asking which informational and functional relationships compose the capability under investigation.

Elementary cognition is the functional use of organised information, representations or inferred relationships to interpret conditions, derive conclusions or guide responses within relatively stable processing

The Self-Organising Information-Processing Systems Hypothesis arrangements. Flexible cognition is the capability to select, coordinate or adapt cognitive processing across changing contexts, conditions or tasks. Elementary cognition can contribute to flexible cognition without being identical to it. Context-sensitive informational organisation (A4) is an architecture that can support flexible cognition, whereas flexible cognition is the resulting function or capability.

The distinction between elementary and flexible cognition is illustrated by three systems. A conventional thermostat represents the current temperature and a target temperature, compares them and uses the result to regulate heating. Under the broad functional definition adopted here, this illustrates elementary cognition: organised information is interpreted and used to guide a response within a relatively fixed processing arrangement. A bee can use learned information to navigate towards food (elementary cognition). Honeybees can also adjust their routes following displacement or reverse previously learned odour-reward associations (examples of flexible cognition) [19, 23]. An LLM can interpret a familiar request using learned relationships (elementary cognition), or coordinate different information and reasoning approaches for a novel task (flexible cognition) [24]. Flexibility concerns how processing is selected and combined, not whether model weights or neural connections change on each occasion. The defining relation is not the simultaneous presence of two independent capacities. It is the participation of awareness-related information in flexible cognition: information about relevant conditions becomes available to adaptable, context-sensitive processing. Attention, memory, monitoring, reasoning and self-representation may contribute, but the proposal does not make any one of these a universal prerequisite. In particular, sophisticated self-modelling or extensive declarative memory need not be present in every consciousnessrelated configuration.

This account allows different functional profiles. One system might demonstrate extensive flexible reasoning with limited information about its own operating state; another might flexibly navigate and learn from environmental information while using fewer kinds of internal representations. Such comparisons require identifying the information, the processes to which it is available and the functional consequences of that access, rather than ranking systems along a single scale of complexity and/or capability.

One neuroscientific point of contact is Global Neuronal Workspace Theory, which proposes that conscious access involves the amplification and widespread availability of information across specialised neural processing systems [25, 26]. This provides a possible neural implementation of a relationship central to SOIPS: information becoming available to participate in multiple cognitive functions. Research involving attention,

SOIPS extends these questions beyond particular neural mechanisms, asking how awareness-related information can participate in flexible cognition across different forms of informational organisation.

Phenomenal consciousness refers to the first-person character of experience. SOIPS offers a possible route towards explaining its emergence: if particular configurations of organised neural activity constitute phenomenal experience, then understanding how interactions among components generate those configurations is part of explaining how experience arises. Electrical stimulation of face-selective cortex in a patient altered reported face perception, demonstrating how changes in neural activity can influence what is experienced [30]. This raises a further question: can the organisational conditions that give rise to phenomenal experience be identified, and could comparable organisations emerge in other substrates?

The pain-axis findings offer a different kind of intervention in artificial networks. Tagliabue et al. [8] identified patterns of internal activity associated with pain in 25 language models, which responded preferentially to descriptions of harm directed at the model itself. Artificially increasing this activity produced graded firstperson expressions of distress and, in three fine-tuned models, altered choices in hypothetical harmful scenarios. These observations reveal pain-like functional responses, but neither establish nor rule out phenomenal pain. For SOIPS, they motivate investigating whether such representations participate in the organised relationship between awareness and flexible cognition, and whether that organisation could contribute to subjective experience in a non-biological substrate.

The SOIPS account of consciousness also raises questions about organisational scale. An individual neuron participates in the brain's information processing but does not contain the coordinated activity of the entire brain. Similarly, an individual bee need not access the collective information assembled during a colony's decision, and a person in an institution need not perceive every aspect of its distributed processing. [31] The limited perspective of a constituent is not a measure of the capabilities of the whole. Under SOIPS, a collective could be investigated for flexible cognition and awareness at its own scale, with those functions identified through collective information access and coordinated behaviour.

6. Cumulative development: capabilities generating further capabilities

A central SOIPS claim is that organisation does not merely produce an isolated function. Once established, capabilities can alter the conditions of subsequent processing and themselves become components of additional organisation. Two related processes make this possible.

Functional elaboration occurs when existing informational structures or functions change, combine or become coordinated to support further capabilities within an organisational scale. Retention may extend the temporal reach of inference; inference can contribute to prediction; and inference together with regulation can support planning or more flexible action. Learned changes can modify the representations available to later processing. Thus:

Information-mediated interaction → informational organisation → functional capabilities → changed informational organisation and interactions → further capabilities.

Organisational recursion occurs when a functional system becomes a component of another system, bringing already developed capabilities into a new collective. A tissue uses the signalling, regulatory and contractile capacities of its cells; an artificial agent can use the learned representations of a model together with memory and tools; teams coordinate the knowledge and actions of individuals. The important development is not simply an increase in size, but the ability of a new system to organise functions its components already possess.

7. Returning to the evidence: implications and tests

In Xenobots and Anthrobots, existing cellular machinery supports unfamiliar movement and collective forms when tissue organisation and culture conditions change. Some Xenobots were deliberately assembled from embryonic tissues, while ciliated Xenobots developed through self-organisation after embryonic tissue was excised and cultured. [1, 34] Anthrobots similarly self-assemble from human airway-derived cells under altered culture conditions. [2] These different routes to organisation illustrate how component capabilities can be recombined into new collective functions. The Anthrobot neural-culture assay additionally raises questions about how cellular assemblies detect, respond to or alter their surroundings. What informational representations or cognitive functions, if any, contribute to these behaviours remains a question for targeted interventions.

In artificial neural networks, training can produce specialised functions without assigning roles to individual units, with some contributions distributed across many units [35]. Neuroscience-style localisation and interventions, including causal tracing, weight editing and activation-direction manipulation, can reveal how these internal representations influence behaviour [36, 37]. More broadly, different architectures can independently recover aspects of the same underlying structure: models trained on chemical strings, molecular graphs and atomic coordinates develop similar representations of physical relationships, while graph neural networks and Transformers independently recover the spatial structure of geometric problems [5, 6, 7]. Intriguingly, Transformers equipped with recurrent positional encodings can also develop spatial representations resembling the place and grid cells of the hippocampal formation [38]. Together, these findings motivate experiments investigating how internal representations influence cognition and behaviour, become available to awareness-related processing, and might contribute to phenomenal experience.

In bee colonies and human institutions, independently developed capabilities become available to larger information-processing organisations. The relevant inquiry is which knowledge, evaluations and responses are accessible to the collective, how these are coordinated, and whether the resulting organisation supports flexible cognition and awareness at its own scale.

Across these examples, SOIPS identifies which organisational relationships enable which capabilities. That analysis generates testable implications. Altering the accessibility, reliability or routing of information should change a capability that depends on that relationship. Changing relevant constraints should redirect the organisation that can emerge. Disrupting retained history, inferential relationships or regulation should produce distinguishable functional consequences where those contributors are independently identifiable. To test how capabilities emerge, we must identify and measure their proposed organisational contributors, rather than infer their presence from the resulting behaviour alone. A stronger cross-domain test would specify comparable organisational relationships in different substrates and ask whether functionally corresponding interventions produce comparable consequences. Existing studies demonstrate important parts of the proposed framework; the stronger claim of a shared, predictive organisational principle requires direct comparison across domains. SOIPS proposes that information-mediated interactions can generate capabilities that alter the conditions of further organisation and become building blocks of new capabilities and systems. This provides a common way to investigate functional specialisation in cells and neural networks, collective decisions in colonies and institutions, and especially how flexible cognition and awareness can combine into consciousness.

8. Perspectives and conclusion

The potential value of SOIPS lies not simply in identifying capabilities such as memory, agency or consciousness, but in changing how their origins are investigated. Rather than asking only whether a system possesses a capability, the framework asks which informational relationships, constraints and functions make that capability possible. Distinguishing the generative engine, informational organisation (Dimension A), elementary functional families (Dimension B) and composite capabilities that arise, offers a way to work backwards from observed behaviour to candidate mechanisms, or forwards from organisational changes to predicted functions.

This perspective places consciousness within a broader family of potentially naturally occurring organisational capabilities, rather than treating it as an exclusively human property or the inevitable endpoint of increasing complexity. SOIPS proposes that consciousness involves awareness-related information participating in flexible cognition. It therefore directs attention towards that relationship, rather than biological ancestry, language, intelligence or resemblance to humans alone. The possibility of consciousness in other animals, artificial systems or collectives becomes a question about their actual organisation.

For artificial intelligence, SOIPS suggests an engineering perspective complementary to increasing model size and training resources. Specialised internal functions can develop under constraints without being explicitly assigned to individual units. Understanding how architecture, objectives, information access and feedback

The Self-Organising Information-Processing Systems Hypothesis shape this differentiation could help explain why capabilities emerge and how they can be strengthened or disrupted. For AI agents and multi-agent systems, practical questions include which information should be shared, retained or routed; how agents should specialise; and how inference, evaluation and regulation should be coordinated. The aim is not merely to assemble capable components, but to create conditions under which useful collective capabilities can develop.

In human institutions, collective capabilities depend not simply on the expertise of individual members, but on how that expertise is organised and used. Research on teams links collective performance to transactive memory (knowledge of who knows what) and demonstrates how information can become distorted during communication [39, 40]. These findings raise a broader SOIPS question: how do records, communication pathways, incentives and institutional rules determine which collective capabilities can develop and persist? A hospital or research team may possess considerable expertise yet struggle when relevant information is inaccessible or responsibilities conflict. SOIPS suggests investigating whether changing these organisational relationships improves collective performance, and how institutions preserve capabilities despite changes in membership.

In biology, SOIPS draws attention to how cells, tissues and neural circuits acquire collective functions through interactions shaped by constraints. Development, morphogenesis and neural specialisation provide opportunities to examine these relationships. Dysfunction may also involve failures of informational coordination or conflicting regulation across scales, potentially relevant to cancer and disorders of neural processing [41, 42]. Such applications would require experiments identifying which organisational changes produce particular functional consequences.
