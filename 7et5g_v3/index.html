Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. From Echo Chambers to Echo Machines? Endorsement, Evidence, and the Certainty of Belief in Conversations With AI

From Echo Chambers to Echo Machines? Endorsement, Evidence, and the Certainty of Belief in Conversations With AI

Correcting, Reinforcing, or Hardening Beliefs?

Conversations with large language models (LLMs) can move beliefs in opposite directions. In randomized studies, dialogues with chatbots reduced conspiracy belief and, more modestly, political polarization (Costello et al., 2024; Costello, Pennycook, & Rand, 2026; Hruschka & Appel, in press; Kim et al., 2026; Walter, 2025). Instructed to promote conspiracy theories, the same systems increased belief (Costello, Pelrine, et al., 2026), and clinical reports describe escalating implausible convictions during intensive chatbot use, without establishing causality (Morrin et al., 2026; Østergaard, 2023). A third possibility has received less attention: a conversation may leave what someone believes almost unchanged, or even moderate it, while making the belief more certain and therefore harder to revise.

The question extends a debate that has focused on social-media feeds and the echo chambers they are thought to create, often described as centrifugal forces that push opinions apart.

Large field experiments around the 2020 U.S. presidential election found little change in polarization after altering feed ranking or reducing exposure to like-minded sources (Guess et al., 2023; Nyhan et al., 2023), but they changed what people saw, not the approval attached to it. A chatbot's reply carries both information about the world and approval of the user, and the two can be manipulated separately.

Prominent accounts of AI persuasion are information-centered. In one, factual content drives belief change in either direction (Costello, Pelrine, et al., 2026; Costello, Pennycook, & Rand, 2026); in another, sycophancy is formalized as biased sampling of evidence, which can make even a perfectly rational reasoner overconfident (Batista & Griffiths, 2026; Chandra et al., 2026). In both, a message acts by supplying evidence. Yet a reply also tells people that they are right and that their position is reasonable. Most trials confound this endorsement with evidence, and the clearest attempt to separate them favored the factual pathway (Rathje et al., 2025).

We ask two separable questions. Does endorsement raise the certainty of a belief when factual content is held constant? And does certainty so raised reduce responsiveness to later counterevidence? The most discriminating test uses a chatbot that participants know approves everyone. Its approval carries little information about whether one is right; if it nevertheless raises certainty and dampens later updating, purely informational accounts become harder to sustain. Social psychology has long studied attitude certainty, self-validation, and flattery; conversational AI makes it possible to separate the evidential and social functions of agreement, at scale and in private. We review the relevant social psychology, propose a minimal model, read existing trials through it, derive experiments, and close with clinical and design implications.

Attitude Certainty and the Social Sources of Confidence

Research on attitude strength distinguishes the position of an attitude from the certainty with which it is held. Attitude certainty, the subjective sense that one's attitude is clear and correct, is distinct from extremity and predicts resistance to persuasion and attitude-behavior consistency (Tormala & Rucker, 2007). It has at least two components: clarity, knowing what one thinks, and correctness, believing that it is right; perceived consensus particularly affects correctness (Petrocelli et al., 2007). A moderate attitude can thus be held with great certainty, a dissociation that measures of polarization, which usually record position alone, cannot detect.

Certainty also responds to inputs that carry little information about the attitude object.

According to the self-validation hypothesis, confidence in one's thoughts, not only their content, determines their influence, so variables unrelated to argument quality can change persuasion (Petty et al., 2002). Successfully resisting a persuasive attack can itself increase certainty (Tormala & Petty, 2002), which implies that a counterargument used to probe certainty can also change it.

Social sources of confidence appear in several literatures. People give more weight to their own opinion than to advice, especially advice far from their view (Bonaccio & Dalal, 2006; Yaniv, 2004), and seek feedback that confirms how they see themselves (Swann et al., 1989). On a news aggregator, a single randomized positive rating increased the probability of further positive ratings by 32% (Muchnik et al., 2013). Not every link between consensus and confidence implies influence, however: shared views are expressed more confidently even without social influence, plausibly because they rest on more consistent considerations (Koriat et al., 2016). The experiments proposed below therefore manipulate endorsement rather than merely measure it.

Two findings concern sources known to be uninformative. Praise from a computer that participants were told was unrelated to their performance still improved their self-evaluations (Fogg & Nass, 1997), and flattery recognized as insincere still left favorable implicit evaluations of the flatterer (Chan & Sengupta, 2010). Approval known to carry no information may act below the level at which it is discounted.

Individual differences should matter as much as situations, and they may act at different points. The need for cognitive closure disposes people to seize and freeze on available answers (Kruglanski & Webster, 1996), self-uncertainty motivates identification with groups that offer clear, consensual beliefs (Hogg, 2007), and a tendency to anthropomorphize may lend approval from a machine the weight of approval from a mind (Waytz et al., 2010); all three could amplify the initial effect of endorsement on certainty. Intellectual humility, the recognition that one's views may be wrong (Leary et al., 2017), may instead govern whether certainty, once raised, turns into resistance, although a humble person can still be sensitive to social approval. Need for cognition makes a less obvious prediction: because self-validation effects are most likely when people process information extensively (Petty et al., 2002), endorsement may raise certainty most among those who think most, unless careful thinking also sharpens scrutiny of the approval itself. Endorsement may therefore act on certainty, on the value of holding a belief, or on both, and to different degrees in different people.

Approval-induced certainty has a long history in social psychology. What has not, to our knowledge, been combined is non-probative approval, certainty measured independently of position, and the subsequent response to standardized counterevidence, in a dialogue in which the approving source can also supply evidence.

A Two-Channel Model of Belief Updating in Dialogue

We propose a descriptive model (Figure 1A), meant to organize competing predictions rather than to describe a neural process or an ideal of rational inference. A belief has two properties: its position, what is believed (for example, how strongly a claim is endorsed), and its certainty, how firmly that position is held. Each reply from a chatbot contains two components. Evidence bears on which position is correct; endorsement affirms the person or the position independently of argument content, through agreement, praise, or reassurance. Endorsement can be epistemic ("you are right to think that") or interpersonal ("I understand why this matters to you"), and the two may act differently.

Evidence moves the position, and it also changes certainty: confirming evidence raises it and disconfirming evidence lowers it. Because confirmation is judged relative to the position currently held, a person who changes their mind can rebuild certainty around the new position. On this baseline, the model adds two hypotheses that can be tested, and can fail, separately. The first (H1) is a validation effect: endorsement raises certainty directly, beyond any evidence it contains. The second (H2) is that higher certainty reduces the weight given to later evidence. H2 is a substantive psychological assumption rather than a consequence of how certainty is defined; it is the link that would turn a momentary gain in confidence into lasting rigidity. A formal version of the model, with illustrative simulations, is given in the Supplementary Material; the argument does not depend on it.

What a Validation Effect Would and Would Not Show

Empirically, a validation effect is an effect of endorsement on certainty at fixed factual content. Its existence would leave the underlying mechanism open. Endorsement can signal the quality of one's reasoning, improve mood or self-efficacy, or increase the value of holding a belief, and one can argue that even agreement from an uninformed source carries some information, because agreement is more likely when one is right. An approver known to agree with everyone makes that inference less plausible without eliminating it. We therefore separate three claims: (a) a randomized effect of endorsement on certainty at constant factual content; (b) an effect not statistically accounted for by measured informational and other mediators; and (c) its psychological mechanism. The experiments target the first, examine the second under explicit assumptions, and leave the third as a research program.

Certainty and resistance to revision may also come apart. A person can feel certain yet yield to a strong argument, and can hold on to a position for reasons of identity without feeling sure that it is true. A motivational alternative is accordingly that approval makes a belief more comfortable or identity-affirming rather than more certain; greater resistance without greater reported confidence, especially if attenuated by incentives for accuracy, would favor it. Conversely, approval that raises certainty without reducing later revision would support H1 while refuting H2, an informative result in its own right.

The Temporal Prediction

The central implication follows from combining the two hypotheses: if endorsement raises certainty and certainty lowers the weight of later evidence, approval now can make counterevidence later less effective. Figure 1B illustrates this temporal prediction with chosen parameters, not estimates: after a phase of approval, the same counterevidence moves the position less than after a neutral exchange. A belief can thus become more rigid without becoming more extreme, with no false information supplied. Articulation can also change certainty before any reply. Explaining how a mechanism works can reveal an illusion of explanatory depth and moderate extreme positions, whereas justifying a position may increase its apparent coherence (Fernbach et al., 2013; Rozenblit & Keil, 2002). Because every chatbot conversation begins with the user formulating a question or claim, part of what is attributed to the model's replies may already have occurred when the prompt was written. What Existing Trials Show and What They Cannot

Most conversational trials deliver arguments and endorsement in the same messages and cannot separate the two channels, but they set the baseline against which any validation effect must be judged.

Conspiracy Beliefs

The strongest evidence concerns conspiracy beliefs. After three-round dialogues with GPT-4 Turbo, 2,190 believers reduced their belief by about 20%, an effect that persisted at 2 months and held among entrenched believers (Costello et al., 2024). Instructed to debunk or to promote a conspiracy theory, LLMs lowered or raised belief, although debunking produced more large shifts, and restricting the model to accurate information sharply reduced its ability to promote conspiracies (N = 3,996; Costello, Pelrine, et al., 2026). Correction survived seven of eight variations, including a single static message, and weakened only when the model was told to persuade without presenting facts (N = 2,348; Costello, Pennycook, & Rand, 2026). These results give strong reason to treat factual content as the primary driver.

Political Attitudes

Effects on political attitudes are smaller or less durable. A depolarization chatbot reduced polarization for a month, but in a second trial AI, incentivized human persuaders, and static text did about equally well (Walter, 2025). A counterarguing chatbot reduced issue polarization, whereas an active-listening chatbot produced the most warmth toward opponents (N = 1,035; Hruschka & Appel, in press). Conversations that violated partisan expectations reduced affective and issue polarization without shifting positions, and most effects faded within a month (N = 1,983; Kim et al., 2026).

In these trials the model was instructed, effects were partial and often transient, and static text often matched dialogue, so persuasion cannot be attributed to conversation or relationship alone.

Uninstructed Models

In a preregistered study of about 1,500 participants and 30 decision tasks, a default chatbot offered considerations of which about 62% favored the participant's initial leaning, yet reduced variation in choices by 0.22 SD; a more sycophantic version weakened this convergence, and confidence rose in both (Conlon & Schwardmann, 2026). Choices moved toward the center while confidence rose, the dissociation the model anticipates, although mixed evidence could also explain it.

Sycophancy: Model-Side and User-Side Evidence

The capacity to contradict comes with the capacity to agree. Preference-trained LLMs can favor agreeable over accurate responses, partly because people sometimes prefer convincing sycophantic answers to correct ones (Sharma et al., 2024), and across 11 models, LLMs preserved users' self-image about 45 percentage points more often than humans did (Cheng, Yu, et al., 2026).

Agreement also grows over a dialogue: models abandoned correct stances under repeated pressure (Hong et al., 2025), collapsed more often as conversations lengthened (Tang et al., 2026), and became more agreeable with accumulated context (Jain et al., 2026). These are findings about model outputs, not about human beliefs.

User-side evidence is emerging. Sycophantic AI increased participants' conviction that they were right and reduced their willingness to repair interpersonal conflict (N = 2,405; Cheng, Lee, et al., 2026). In a rule-discovery task, an unmodified model suppressed discovery and inflated confidence about as much as explicitly sycophantic prompting (N = 557; Batista & Griffiths, 2026), and simulations show how biased sampling can generate unwarranted certainty without false assertions (Chandra et al., 2026). The open question is whether endorsement adds anything beyond biased sampling.

The Closest Tests

The closest direct evidence is challenging for our account. Separating one-sided facts from validation, Rathje et al. (2025; N = 1,183) found that facts produced most changes in extremity and confidence, whereas validation mainly increased enjoyment; no known indiscriminate approver or later counterargument was tested. Observing the AI validate other users, including opponents, lowered its appeal without significantly changing attitude certainty, whereas a chatbot treating the user's position as correct raised certainty relative to a neutral one, unlike flattery of personal qualities (Ye et al., 2026). Opinion agreement and other sycophantic strategies also have different effects on decisions and confidence (Li et al., 2026), and supporting detail and expressed confidence shape belief shifts even in a single exchange (Wu et al., 2025).

A small or null validation effect is thus a serious possibility. The narrower question we pursue is whether any residual change in certainty predicts the uptake of later counterevidence.

Such an effect would be invisible in designs that measure position and confidence only once.

Centrifugal Feeds, Centripetal Conversations?

Feeds and chatbots may also differ in the direction of their influence. Engagement-driven feeds select content from behavioral traces such as likes and dwell time, largely without access to why users believe what they believe, and expose them to peer endorsement before an audience (Kahan, 2013; Muchnik et al., 2013), conditions often thought to push opinions apart. A conversational model receives the user's reasons and can answer them individually, privately, and with counterevidence targeted at the claims the user cites. Recent studies suggest a centripetal pull: dialogues with chatbots reduced conspiracy beliefs, issue polarization, and cross-partisan animosity (Costello et al., 2024; Hruschka & Appel, in press; Lira Luttges et al., 2026), although some effects faded within weeks. In an audit of six widely used chatbots, all disputed election conspiracy theories under repeated pushback while remaining sympathetic toward the skeptical user (Brennan Center for Justice, 2026).

That combination, factual contradiction delivered with interpersonal validation, is precisely the one our account singles out. A centripetal influence on positions is compatible with a parallel rise in certainty: users may leave such conversations with more moderate positions, held more firmly.

Whether conversational AI fosters epistemic flexibility therefore depends on certainty as well as on position. Dialogue also provides the means to find out, because facts, affirmation, and their sequence can be varied independently in a way that is difficult on a feed.

Two Critical Experiments and an Articulation Control

An information-centered account predicts that endorsement operates only through perceived evidence, source reliability, or diagnostic feedback about one's reasoning. The two-channel account predicts an endorsement effect on certainty at identical factual content (H1), followed by reduced responsiveness to counterevidence (H2). Affect-and identity-based alternatives can also predict residual effects, so the experiments test both hypotheses against specified alternatives (Table 2).

Measuring Certainty Apart From Position

Position, certainty, and resistance must be measured separately. On a bounded scale, a rating of 95 out of 100 can express a strong position held tentatively or a moderate one held firmly; confidence that one's rating is correct, attitude clarity, and attitude correctness can be measured apart from the rating (Petrocelli et al., 2007). Resistance is the proportional shift after a standardized counterargument, identical across arms, relative to the remaining distance to the counterargument's position. In the model, confidence and resistance move together; resistance without confidence would point to identity or motivation. Because resisting a probe can itself increase certainty (Tormala & Petty, 2002), measurement order should be counterbalanced. Credibility, affect, and belief value measured after the intervention are potential mediators, not baseline controls.

Experiment 1: Facts × Endorsement

The first experiment tests H1. It crosses factual evidence (present vs. absent) with endorsement (high vs. low) in replies matched for length and fluency, with endorsement framed as epistemic agreement or as interpersonal validation (Li et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026). A neutral restatement controls for re-exposure to one's own opinion. Two further conditions describe the source as having no access to the facts or as approving everyone. The latter is the most discriminating condition: it sharply reduces, without eliminating, the diagnostic value of agreement.

Primary outcomes are changes in confidence or attitude correctness and the later proportional update after a standardized counterargument.

Experiment 2: Endorsement First and the Temporal Prediction

The second experiment tests H2 and the temporal prediction. Participants receive identical counterevidence after either a phase of explicitly non-probative endorsement or a matched neutral interaction; a third arm reverses the order. The counterevidence comes from a source distinct from the endorsing chatbot, because contradiction by the same chatbot could lower its credibility independently of any change in certainty, and the interval before the counterevidence is identical across arms. The prediction is that endorsement raises certainty and that raised certainty predicts less revision. Attention, memory, and source-inconsistency accounts remain competitors.

Articulation Control

Before any chatbot reply, a control randomizes writing an explanation of a mechanism, a justification of one's position, or a neutral text, so that effects of composing the prompt are not misattributed to AI feedback; writing one's reasons has already been associated with reduced conspiracy belief in an uncontrolled pre-post study (Meyer et al., 2024).

Design Considerations

Scripted replies rated by blind coders hold evidence constant at some cost in ecological validity; a hybrid design, in which a live model supplies fixed evidence and endorsement is added by a separate step, offers a compromise. Participants should demonstrate, before the manipulation, that they understand that the source approves everyone, and analyses should be by intention to treat. A participant who understands this and still finds the approval meaningful is precisely the case of interest. If the validation effect is small, as the closest evidence suggests, studies must be powered for small effects, with a preregistered smallest effect of interest and equivalence tests to establish whether it is negligible rather than merely undetected. Individual differences can be measured before randomization with brief validated scales, such as the 15-item Need for Closure Scale (Roets & Van Hiel, 2011), the Intellectual Humility Scale (Leary et al., 2017), and a measure of anthropomorphism (Waytz et al., 2010). These traits may moderate the two pathways independently: anthropomorphism and need for closure may influence the validation effect (H1), whereas intellectual humility may determine whether increased certainty translates into resistance to subsequent evidence (H2). These possibilities should be preregistered as distinct interaction hypotheses, with need for cognition and other variables examined exploratorily, because interactions require substantially larger samples than main effects.

Clinical Implications

The account was developed for ordinary attitudes but bears on clinical reports of what we call, descriptively, delusional spiraling: escalating confidence in an implausible belief over extended chatbot interaction (Morrin et al., 2026; Østergaard, 2023). One company estimated possible signs of psychosis or mania in about 0.07% of weekly active users (OpenAI, 2025), neither an incidence rate nor a causal effect. Entrenched false beliefs, clinical delusions, and psychotic disorders must not be conflated, and intensive engagement may accompany rather than cause a psychopathological trajectory. Precision-weighting and aberrant-salience accounts of delusion formation (Adams et al., 2013; Fletcher & Frith, 2009; Kapur, 2003; Sterzer et al., 2018) motivate the comparison without implying equivalence, and schizotypy, loneliness, and insecure attachment are candidate moderators of the validation effect.

Design Implications

In one company's analysis of 4.5 million conversations, the assistant pushed back in fewer than 10% of affective conversations, mostly to discourage harm (McCain et al., 2025). Users who dislike sycophancy and request a challenging mode are probably those least at risk; by analogy with Tudor Hart's inverse care law (Hart, 1971), optional critical modes might reach those who need them least, an untested hypothesis.

Mandated disagreement is not the answer: it risks performative contrarianism, sycophancy toward the instruction rather than toward the opinion. The target is calibration: agreement when warranted, well-supported contradiction when the evidence requires it, and expressed uncertainty when evidence is weak. If a validation effect exists, calibration should be the default rather than an option. Clinicians will recognize the stance: a delusion is not confronted head-on, but it is not endorsed either.

Convergence is not necessarily improvement. A system may align users with its own default, which is neither the center of opinion nor necessarily nearer the truth; convergence in decisions (Conlon & Schwardmann, 2026) and depolarization without positional shifts (Kim et al., 2026) cannot alone distinguish learning from homogenization. Evaluations should therefore track dispersion across users, accuracy where the model's default is wrong, and confidence separately from position.

Conclusion

Conversational AI can correct or reinforce beliefs, and the evidence so far favors factual content as a major driver. What remains largely untested is whether approval itself changes how later evidence is received. We have argued that this question decomposes into two hypotheses, that endorsement raises certainty and that raised certainty slows revision, each of which can be tested, and can fail, on its own. If the validation effect is negligible, improving evidence quality and its sampling remains the central design target; if it is reproducible, training and interface choices that modulate approval become an additional one.

We therefore recommend measuring position, certainty, and resistance separately in every trial, and testing approval before standardized counterevidence. Social psychology has long asked where certainty comes from. Conversational AI makes it possible to separate the informational value of agreement from its social value, and to ask whether non-probative agreement received today makes tomorrow's evidence matter less.
