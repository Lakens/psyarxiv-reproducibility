What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

Open science is a national priority in Canada, yet the transition from policy to practice depends on understanding the researchers whose behaviour these policies seek to change. We report a nationwide survey that comprehensively assesses the state of open science across Canadian higher education.

Researchers and trainees recruited through 18 institutions (N = 705) rated 13 open science practices across five dimensions: awareness, knowledge, attitudes, perceptions, and implementation. Roughly one in three respondents had never heard of open science before the survey. Among those who had, attitudes were consistently positive and there was a widespread desire to learn more, but self-assessed knowledge was low and concentrated in two long-established practices -open access and open-source software -and implementation exceeded 20% for only four of the 13 practices. Researchers pointed to a lack of training and know-how, rather than a lack of interest, as the primary barriers. Two further patterns compounded these gaps: respondents underestimated how favourably their peers viewed open science while overestimating how much their peers practised it, and their stated intentions for the coming year ran well ahead of historically observed rates of adoption. Together, these findings describe a research community that is willing but underequipped and largely unaware of its own consensus, indicating that the main barriers to open science in Canada lie in capacity and perception rather than attitudes.

As the Canadian Roadmap for Open Science recognizes, the transition to open science is complex and requires a sustainable, long-term strategy (Office of the Chief Science Advisor of Canada, 2020).

Developing such a strategy depends on a deep understanding of researchers' knowledge, attitudes, perceptions, and practices, and how they perceive their colleagues' views and behaviors. Policies, mandates, and coordinated efforts succeed only when the researchers whose behavior they aim to change see them as reasonable, feasible, and necessary (Caillaud et al., 2021; Kelly, 1999; Vicente-Saez et al., 2021). Yet the literature offers limited insight. Few studies have undertaken an assessment of these factors systematically and at scale. Others give valuable, closely observed accounts of particular disciplines, institutions, or practices, but these accounts remain scattered and have yet to consolidate into a comprehensive picture.

Research capable of identifying the factors that drive or impede the adoption of open science practices, and of informing policy-making, remains scarce (see Baždarić et al., 2021; Levin et al., 2016; Nawroth et al., 2026; Trinh et al., 2025). Without it, ongoing and future initiatives risk resting on untested assumptions about how researchers think and behave, and progress likely suffers as a result. The present study seeks to address this gap by focusing on researchers and trainees in Canadian higher-education institutions, the sector where these tensions are most acute. We present the results of what is, to the best of our knowledge, the first nationwide survey designed to comprehensively assess researchers' knowledge, attitudes, perceptions, and practices regarding open science in this sector.

Method Instrument

To assess the current state of open science in higher education institutions in Canada, we developed and implemented a self-report instrument spanning 13 practices and five dimensions. The 13 practices were: open access, preprints, preregistration, open data, open code, open peer review, open grants, open notebooks, using OERs, authoring OERs, using open-source software, developing open-source software, and citizen science. For each practice, the five dimensions measured awareness, knowledge, attitudes, perceptions, and implementation-reflecting respectively whether participants recognised the practice, understood it, their feelings toward it, how they perceived others' attitudes and uptake of it, and whether they had applied it in their research workflows. Awareness, both for open science in general and for each open science practice, was measured dichotomously, with possible values of 0 (not aware) and 1 (aware). Current and desired knowledge were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (no knowledge / novice) to 5 (expert), and personal and perceived attitudes on a 5-point scale from 1 (strongly oppose) to 5 (strongly favour). Perceived implementation (the percentage of researchers in participants' discipline who had implemented each practice) and predicted implementation (participants' own likelihood of implementing each practice within the next 12 months) were measured using a percentage scale from 0-100% with 1% accuracy. Actual implementation was derived from the responses to a multiple-choice question allowing for multiple answers where participants indicated the practices they had implemented.

In addition to these dimensions, the instrument captured demographic information (age, gender, discipline, and career stage), participants' perceptions of institutional support for open science, and the extent to which their institution's hiring, tenure, and promotion policies valued open science engagement. Participants who had implemented a practice reported approximately when they first did so, and those who had implemented none were asked what had prevented them. Other questions collected quantitative and qualitative data for further context.

Participants

The study used a non-probabilistic, purposive sampling approach to maximize data points while representing diverse regions. Survey invitations were distributed to publicly available email addresses of researchers and trainees across career stages and disciplines at 18 Canadian higher-education institutions spanning all five main regions (Atlantic Provinces, Central Canada, Prairie Provinces, West Coast, and Northern Territories). Reach likely extended beyond direct contacts, as recipients were encouraged to forward the invitation to interested colleagues and students. Response rates could not be estimated, as responses were anonymous and location data were not requested. Participants were considered eligible if they consented to the study, reported residing in Canada, completed at least 50% of the survey, and did not fail an item controlling for sustained attention (n = 705).

Among eligible participants, nearly half were faculty members (n = 330, 47%). The remainder were undergraduate students (n = 55, 8%), master's students (n = 80, 11%), PhD students (n = 56, 8%), postdoctoral researchers (n = 22, 3%), other research staff (n = 83, 12%), and participants in other roles (n = 79, 11%). Participants were also categorized according to their broad research area. Natural Sciences and Engineering had the most respondents (n = 251, 36%), followed by Social Sciences (n = 166, 24%), Health Sciences (n = 143, 20%), The Humanities and the Arts (n = 56, 8%), Media and Information Studies (n = 47, 7%), and other research areas (n = 42, 6%). In terms of gender identity, most respondents self-identified as women (n = 409, 58%), followed by men (n = 271, 38%), and non-binary individuals (n = 17, 2%), with the remainder of participants indicating transgender, other, or not disclosed (n = 8; 1%). Age distribution was broadly even across the 25-54 range, with younger age groups skewing female and older groups slightly male, consistent with broader trends in Canadian academia (Napierala, 2022; Uppal & Hango, 2022). Procedure

The study was assessed and approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee at Concordia University (Certification No. #300166679) prior to data collection. Consistent with open science practices, the study was prospectively preregistered on the Open Science Framework (OSF). The study protocol and materials, including full details of the survey structure and item-level response values, were also made publicly available through the preregistration record (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/4TCA8). The survey was implemented and distributed through QualtricsXM, with data collection carried out between July 2024 and June 2025. All participants were provided with information outlining the nature and purpose of the study, and each indicated their informed consent via an online form.

Data Analysis

Analyses were conducted in R 4.5.1. Knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions were analysed among respondents aware of each practice, so sample sizes vary by item (Table 2). Actual implementation rates were calculated against the full eligible sample (N = 705), with participants unaware of open science counted as non-implementers. Institutional support and policy percentages were calculated among respondents to those items (n = 435), and barrier percentages were calculated over total barrier selections (129 selections from 85 non-implementers). P values within each family of tests were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.

Calibration Analyses

To test whether overestimation depended on participants' own implementation status, we compared each participant's estimate with a leave-one-out measure of actual prevalence. For every practice, implementers' estimates were less accurate than non-implementers'. Differences in mean absolute error ranged from 9.55 (open access) to 20.51 percentage points (open notebooks), and all were statistically significant after correction. Effect sizes were small for authoring OERs and open peer review, and moderate for all remaining practices (rank-biserial correlations = 0.21-0.43).

To establish the direction of these errors, we fitted an ordered beta mixed-effects model. It predicted perceived prevalence as a function of actual prevalence, implementation status, and their interaction, with random intercepts for participants and practices. Perceived prevalence rose with actual prevalence (β = 3.60, SE = 0.50, p < .001), so participants' estimates tracked which practices were more and less common. At a given level of actual prevalence, implementers gave higher estimates than non-implementers (β = 0.67, SE = 0.07, p < .001).

The interaction was not significant (β = 0.50, SE = 0.30, p = .097). The data therefore support a uniform upward shift among implementers (see Figure 10). To account for the possibility that implementation rates reflect differential cumulative histories, we asked participants to recall when they first adopted each open science practice. Discussion

The current study provides a comprehensive picture of open science knowledge, attitudes, perceptions, and implementation in Canada, drawing on 705 participants from 18 institutions and 13 practices. The findings converge on several tensions: researchers who are familiar with open science tend to support it, express a desire to learn more about it, and report positive attitudes toward most of the practices assessed. Yet implementation rates remain low across the majority of practices and are concentrated in a small cluster of historically established activities, while a third of eligible participants reported no prior familiarity with open science.

In the following sections, we discuss three themes, each with implications for policy and institutional practice: the limited reach of awareness efforts and the gaps in knowledge and capacity among researchers who are aware; misperception of peer attitudes and behaviour; and the disconnect between stated intentions and past adoption.

The Perception Paradox

Researchers' own attitudes toward open science differed sharply from their estimates of their colleagues' attitudes. Across practices, half of researchers described their colleagues as neutral or opposed to open science, whereas fewer than one in five described themselves this way. Researchers therefore underestimate their colleagues' support for open science. This pattern resembles pluralistic ignorance, in which many people privately hold a view but believe they are in the minority.

Researchers who think their colleagues are indifferent or opposed may be reluctant to adopt these practices, even though most of those colleagues share their views.

Researchers misjudged their colleagues' behavior in the opposite direction. They overestimated how their colleagues practice open science, by about 20 percentage points on average, while underestimating how much those peers valued it. Believing that colleagues already practise open science therefore does not seem enough to prompt researchers' own adoption.

One explanation lies in how researchers interpret their colleagues' motives. Thomas et al. (2025) Institutional dashboards that monitor open science practices, an approach already being explored (see Cobey et al., 2023), would give institutions an accurate baseline for setting targets and tracking progress, rather than relying on researchers' estimates, which our data show are inflated. Dashboards that also reported researchers' attitudes would show both how far practice lags behind support and how widespread that support already is. Clearer guidance on how open science contributions factor into hiring, tenure, and promotion could further reduce uncertainty about the career value of adopting these practices.

Stated Intentions and Adoption Trajectories

Researchers were optimistic about their own future adoption of open science practices. Their predicted likelihood of implementing each practice within the next 12 months exceeded current implementation by 20 to 40 percentage points. Respondents' recalled adoption histories suggest these predictions are unrealistic. Even open access, the fastest-growing practice, would take about eight years to reach its predicted level at its pace over the past decade, and most other practices would take longer. Two factors may explain why intentions outpace historical adoption. First, respondents' projections were much more closely aligned with their estimates of current peer implementation rates than to actual ones, which suggest that they expected to catch up with the adoption levels they believed already existed. Because those beliefs were inflated, especially among researchers who already used a practice, the predictions built on them were inflated too. Second, social desirability may play a role. Recent guidelines and mandates from funding agencies and international bodies may make adopting open science feel professionally expected, encouraging respondents to report high intentions, regardless of their capacity to act on them. This need not mean their enthusiasm is insincere. Supporting open science in principle, however, is not the same as being ready to implement specific practices, especially when most respondents reported low knowledge and limited institutional support. This enthusiasm is promising, but realistic expectations are essential to drive concrete and sustainable action. It is important to normalize the idea that open science adoption is inherently gradual and incremental, recognizing that researchers typically build their practice one step at a time rather than transforming their workflow all at once. The notion of an open science buffet (Bergmann, 2023) captures this idea: researchers can selectively engage with practices that suit their current capacity, resources, and discipline, and add others over time. Institutions could set implementation timelines and institutional benchmarks based on historic growth rates rather than stated intentions, and back them with clear policies and resources such as dedicated staff and training opportunities.

Limitations and Future Directions

A few limitations temper these findings. The sample was not randomly drawn, and those who chose to respond may have been more engaged with open science than researchers in general. If so, awareness, knowledge, and implementation are likely even lower among Canadian researchers as a whole. The same self-selection, together with social-desirability, may have inflated the positive attitudes and intentions reported here. Adoption trajectories rest on participants' retrospective recall and should be read as approximate. Finally, some subgroups were small, so comparisons involving them warrant caution.

These limitations point to productive next steps. Longitudinal studies that follow researchers over time would establish whether the strong intentions reported here translate into actual adoption. The instrument developed here lends itself to this purpose: it could be administered repeatedly within a single institution to track change, or adapted to sectors beyond academia such as government, industry, and the college sector, to clarify how far these patterns generalize. Experimental studies could also test whether targeted training, point-of-need support, or information about colleagues' actual attitudes changes what researchers do, not just what they intend.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate that the main barriers to open science in Canada lie in capacity and perception rather than in attitudes. Researchers broadly support open science, want to know more about the practices we assessed, and hold more positive attitudes than they believe their colleagues do.

These results describe a research community that is willing but underequipped, and largely unaware of its own consensus. A third of respondents had never heard of open science. Among those who had, knowledge was concentrated in a few long-established practices, and respondents who had not implemented practices pointed to a lack of know-how rather than lack of interest. Because researchers underestimate how favourably their colleagues view open science, much of that support stays hidden.

Their intentions for the coming year, meanwhile, run well ahead of historical adoption rates.

These gaps point to concrete next steps: extend basic awareness efforts to researchers who have not yet been reached, build training where knowledge is thinnest, make colleagues' support for open science visible, and set institutional expectations to realistic rates of change. Because the research community is already favourably disposed to open science, the remaining work is to build the infrastructure that turns that support into practice.

Despite years of sustained advocacy, institutional mandates compelling researchers to adopt practices such as open access, and a proliferation of roadmaps, training initiatives, and events-both across Canada and internationally-a substantial share of researchers at all career stages remain entirely unaware of open science. The pattern deepens when examining knowledge of specific open science practices. Given the decades of sustained effort invested in the field, along with the wealth of open science resources now available, one might reasonably expect at least intermediate familiarity across the board. Yet the data tell a different story. Knowledge is heavily concentrated in two historically dominant practices-open access and using open-source software, the same two practices that led implementation a decade ago. Notably, open access is the only practice to surpass the 50% threshold for proficient and expert users. Viewed from the other end of the spectrum, 6 out of 13 practices had knowledge clustered at the novice and advanced beginner levels, with developing open-source software, open notebooks, and open code recording the highest proportions of responses in the lowest knowledge categories. Implementation rates were correspondingly limited, with only 4 of the 13 practices exceeding 20%: open access, using open-source software, open data, and publishing preprints. When asked what prevented implementation, responses converged on capacity: lack of training and specific know-how together accounted for over half of all barrier selections. The strong desire to learn more across all practices contextualizes these barriers, pointing to a community that is motivated but underequipped. Subgroup differences showed a similar pattern. Postdoctoral researchers and faculty held the least positive attitudes but implemented open science most often, and Humanities & Arts had among the most positive attitudes yet the lowest implementation, again suggesting that capacity rather than willingness limits adoption. These findings reveal that the path from knowledge to implementation is far from linear, requiring more deliberate and targeted intervention than mandates alone can achieve. While much of the current discussion in the open-science space has shifted from defining open science to discussing strategies for implementation, our results suggest that awareness-building initiatives, such as entry-level materials, workshops, webinars, and courses, remain important for advancing open science uptake. Notably, when such materials are produced by specific institutions or communities, they are likely to resonate more compellingly with their members, stressing the importance of institutional and grass-roots commitment that goes beyond merely endorsing open science as a valuable ideal. Beyond awareness, practical implementation scaffolds, such as templates, step-by-step guides, and checklists, can lower the barrier to entry for unfamiliar practices. Leveraging cluster effects within departments or disciplines, where early adopters model and normalize open science practices among peers, may further accelerate uptake. Perhaps most importantly, training should be embedded at the point of need, delivered when and where researchers are most likely to act. Linking to guidance on data management plans within grant submission portals, or surfacing open peer review resources during the review process itself, meets researchers at critical decision points rather than requiring them to seek out knowledge and support on their own.

If researchers in our sample interpret their colleagues' open science as compliance rather than conviction, the two misperceptions fit together: colleagues appear to practise open science without valuing it. In a survey of education researchers, Makel et al. (2021) reported the opposite pattern: participants believed their peers used less open practices than themselves, which led the authors to suggest that researchers may hold an unduly bleak view of open science in their field. The reversal may reflect a genuine shift in the research landscape between the collection of their data and ours, though differences in samples and measures could also contribute. As open science practices have become more common, more often mandated, and more visible, researchers' sense of how much their peers engage may have risen faster than actual adoption. When researchers overestimate how much colleagues practise open science but underestimate how much they value it, inaction can persist even where support is broad. Addressing this perception paradox requires making support for open science more visible. Because much of this support appears to remain private, communities of practice and peer networks could help bring discussions and resources into shared spaces where researchers can see that their colleagues agree with them. Sharing survey results such as those reported here within departments or institutions could correct the misperception more directly. Institutions could reinforce these efforts by signaling clear support and by reforming research assessment to recognize open science contributions. So far, this potential appears largely unrealized: most researchers rated their institution's support as no better than average, and more than half did not know whether open science contributions counted toward hiring, tenure, or promotion.
