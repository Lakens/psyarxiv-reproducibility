Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. 1. Introduction

Does narcissistic personality disorder involve expressive language disorder? The question sounds clinical, yet a direct search finds nothing to review. In November 2026, a PubMed search of titles and abstracts that paired narcissism with "expressive language," "language disorder," or "language impairment" returned no records. The empty result points to a mismatch of levels between the two concepts. Expressive language disorder is a developmental category of structural language that concerns vocabulary, grammar, and sentence production (World Health Organization [WHO], 1992, F80.1). Current systems place it under language disorder (American Psychiatric Association [APA], 2022), and an international consensus uses the term developmental language disorder (Bishop et al., 2017). The current manual also lists social (pragmatic) communication disorder, introduced with DSM-5 for difficulty in the social use of communication (APA, 2022; Norbury, 2014). That category does not fit either, as Section 3.1 shows. Nothing in the diagnostic description of narcissistic personality disorder involves missing vocabulary or grammar (APA, 2022). In a population sample of 9-year-olds, narcissistic traits even went with higher verbal ability (Fontaine et al., 2008). The difficulties lie elsewhere: in putting feelings into words, and in negotiating meaning with another person.

The evidence that does exist looks contradictory. A recent meta-analysis of 14 studies (N = 8,006) found that narcissism as a whole is unrelated to alexithymia (r = .03; Song et al., 2026).

Beneath that null lie opposite signs. In the same meta-analysis, grandiose narcissism correlated negatively with alexithymia (r = -.08), whereas samples that combined vulnerable and grandiose measures correlated positively (r = .09). Vulnerable narcissism was measured in just one of the included studies. Communication research shows a second split. Speakers high in narcissism expect to persuade in speech and in writing, but raters credit their speeches and discount their essays (Foster et al., 2025). Even the most intuitive linguistic marker fails, because narcissism is unrelated to the use of first-person singular pronouns (Carey et al., 2015). These splits carry concrete costs. First, assessment relies on self-report, and the grandiose pole reports more skill than it shows. Narcissism correlates positively with self-reported emotional intelligence and negatively with ability measures of it (Michels & Schulze, 2021). People high in grandiose narcissism also rate their empathy more highly than people high in vulnerable narcissism, while objective measures are rarely given alongside (Eddy, 2021). A self-report alexithymia score can therefore classify a fluent but affectively thin speaker as emotionally skilled. Second, the vulnerable pole, where an expressive deficit is most plausible, is almost unmeasured. Third, the two poles call for different help. A person who cannot name a feeling needs support in naming it. A person who speaks well but cannot let speech change in dialogue needs something else. Pooling the poles under one construct hides both needs from the clinicians who assess them. This review therefore changes the question. It asks whether narcissism involves a domainspecific failure at the interface of affect and pragmatic form. It also asks whether that failure takes different shapes at the grandiose and vulnerable poles. To answer it, the review uses Weber's ideal-type method as Etzrodt (2022) extends it. Concepts are defined clearly, their consequences are derived consistently, and each derived claim is exposed to refutation. The review also borrows a conceptual pair from Etzrodt's (2024) reading of Weber, which treats Durkheimian anomie and Marxist alienation as opposites. Anomie is "formless life," and alienation is "lifeless form" (Thom, 1984 , as cited in Etzrodt, 2024, p. 660). Transposed to expression, the pair names two opposite failures of one relation. Affect finds no form, or a form keeps out the affect that would change it.

The review makes six contributions. First, it relocates the expressive difficulty from structural language to the relation between affect and form, which explains why direct searches find nothing. Second, it reads the near-zero meta-analytic correlation as the sum of two opposite failures rather than as the absence of one. Third, it explains why grandiose self-reports diverge from performance: the divergence is a predicted property of one ideal type. Fourth, it identifies shame as the affect on which both poles fail, in opposite ways. Unlike a general alexithymia account, it therefore predicts that naming failures concentrate on shame and related selfconscious emotions. Fifth, it explains why developmental language disorder raises the risk of personality disorder without a dose-response relation to language severity. Sixth, it states seven propositions, each with the observation that would refute it, and shows where they separate the types from three rival accounts.

2. Method: ideal types with refutation conditions

Weber constructed ideal types by accentuating selected features of a phenomenon into a consistent conceptual picture that no concrete case matches exactly (Weber, 1949). Because concept formation follows the researcher's interests, objectivity (better called intersubjectivity) rests on clear definitions applied systematically (Etzrodt, 2024, p. 667). Etzrodt (2022) argues that this consistency is necessary but not sufficient, since a consistently derived interpretation can still be false. Popper's falsificationism supplies the missing step: general statements cannot be verified, but they can fail against observation (Popper, 1959). Etzrodt (2022) adds a condition from Schutz. The researcher's interpretation of actors' motives must itself be tested in concrete situations.

The method suits narcissism for a specific reason. Grandiose and vulnerable narcissism are not separate kinds of people. Clinical reviewers treat grandiosity and vulnerability as two phenotypic themes (Pincus & Lukowitsky, 2010). They warn that labels such as overt and covert narcissism wrongly turn these two modes of expression into separate types. Structural work finds dimensions, and its most parsimonious solution has three factors: agentic extraversion, narcissistic neuroticism, and self-centered antagonism (Crowe et al., 2019). Momentary data show movement between the themes, because people who are dispositionally grandiose report vulnerable as well as grandiose states over time (Edershile & Wright, 2021). If the two forms are treated as subtypes, these facts are anomalies. If they are treated as ideal-typical poles, the facts are expected, because a person can stand at different distances from each pole and move between them.

The review proceeds in four steps. It defines the relation between affect and form and its two failures. It derives each ideal type and sets it against the evidence. It places developmental language disorder relative to the types. Finally, it states each derived claim as a proposition with a refutation condition. The evidence was located through searches of PubMed, OpenAlex, and the Semantic Scholar corpus in November 2026. Meta-analyses and systematic reviews were preferred wherever they exist, and primary studies were used where no synthesis is available.

Sources published online first are cited by their issue year. The evidence comes from four literatures that seldom cite one another, and the next section sorts them by what each leaves unresolved.

3. Four literatures and what each leaves unresolved

>Four literatures and what each leaves unresolved

3.1 Developmental language research

Developmental language research defines language difficulty structurally, and its outcome studies treat childhood language disorder as a risk marker. In an Ottawa cohort followed from age 5, children with early language impairment had more anxiety disorders at age 19, mostly social phobia (Beitchman et al., 2001). The same study found higher rates of antisocial personality disorder among boys with language impairment. In a Danish clinical cohort, 469 people diagnosed with developmental language disorder in childhood were compared with 2,345 matched controls (Mouridsen & Hauschild, 2009). Over a mean follow-up of 34.7 years, a personality disorder diagnosis was registered for 4.9% of the cases and 2.2% of the controls (Mouridsen & Hauschild, 2009). This literature leaves two questions open. It cannot isolate narcissism, because registry diagnoses follow the ICD, which has no separate category for narcissistic personality disorder. ICD-10 lists it under other specific personality disorders (WHO, 1992, F60.8). It also shows risk without explaining it, because the severity of childhood expressive and receptive impairment did not predict a later diagnosis (Mouridsen & Hauschild, 2009).

The nearest diagnostic neighbor is social (pragmatic) communication disorder, which describes persistent difficulty with the social use of verbal and nonverbal communication (Norbury, 2014).

It too misses the narcissistic case. It is a developmental profile that appears across neurodevelopmental disorders, and its pragmatic difficulties depend in part on structural language (Norbury, 2014). The difficulties linked to narcissism instead vary with threats to the self and change under instruction (Hepper et al., 2014; Janusz et al., 2021). Developmental language research therefore offers no category, structural or pragmatic, for a failure that depends on affect.

3.2 Personality research on narcissism and emotion

Personality research shows that the emotional correlates of narcissism split by pole. The alexithymia split described above is one example (Song et al., 2026). A meta-analysis of 26 studies found that emotion dysregulation correlates with vulnerable narcissism (r = .37) but not with grandiose narcissism (r = .04; Tziliaskopoulos et al., 2027). Vulnerable narcissism is also linked to habitual expressive suppression (ρ = .37; k = 2; Walker et al., 2022). In the same metaanalysis, total and grandiose narcissism showed no significant association with suppression or reappraisal. In empathy research, patients with narcissistic personality disorder show impaired emotional empathy but intact cognitive empathy (Ritter et al., 2011). A systematic review of clinical and nonclinical samples concludes that cognitive empathy appears intact while affective empathy may be reduced (Eddy, 2021). This literature leaves the expressive question open. Most of it rests on self-report, which the grandiose pole inflates (Michels & Schulze, 2021). It also describes emotional dispositions without saying how affect becomes speech that another person can take up.

3.3 Language-use research

Language-use research asks what people high in narcissism actually say. The folk marker failed.

Across five laboratories and 4,811 participants, narcissism was unrelated to the use of firstperson singular pronouns (Carey et al., 2015). Across 15 samples, grandiose narcissism correlated positively with second-person pronouns, swear words, and sports words (Holtzman et al., 2019). It correlated negatively with anxiety words, tentative words, and perceptual words, and every effect was smaller than |r| = .10. Persuasion studies reveal a gap between claimed and credited skill. People high in narcissism rate their creativity more highly than blind coders do, yet they persuade targets when pitching ideas, partly through enthusiasm (Goncalo et al., 2010).

They expect to persuade both in speech and in writing, and raters credit only the speeches (Foster et al., 2025). This literature leaves the mechanism open. It records markers and outcomes but offers no account of why fluency should help in one modality and fail in another. Its modality contrast also comes from separate studies whose samples and methods differ, so its authors caution against comparing the modalities directly (Foster et al., 2025).

3.4 Interaction research

Interaction research follows narcissism across meetings and turns of talk. At zero acquaintance, people high in narcissism are more popular, and the entitled and exploitative aspects of narcissism are the most attractive at first sight (Back et al., 2010). Over repeated meetings the picture reverses. In discussion groups that met seven times, self-enhancers made positive first impressions and were rated negatively by the seventh week (Paulhus, 1998). In a three-week group study, narcissistic admiration had an initially positive but declining effect on popularity (Leckelt et al., 2015). Narcissistic rivalry had a growing negative effect, carried by arrogant and aggressive behavior. Repair, in the relational sense used here, means acknowledging, apologizing for, or revising one's own contribution. It is the clearest casualty. Narcissism predicts unwillingness to apologize through low empathy and low guilt, and the effect belongs to rivalry rather than admiration (Leunissen et al., 2017). Conversation analysis of couple therapy shows clients with narcissistic problems resisting questions, controlling the topic, and displaying independence (Janusz et al., 2021). These moves appeared when the first consultation threatened their self-image. This literature describes conduct but not the expressive process behind it. It shows that collaboration fails over time without saying what fails in the speaker's expression.

3.5 The joint gap and the position of this review

The four literatures meet at one relation that none of them measures directly. The question is whether a speaker's affect reaches a linguistic form that a listener can take up, and whether that form stays open to revision in dialogue. Developmental research measures form without affect, and personality research measures affect without form. Language-use research counts forms without their affective source, and interaction research records outcomes without their expressive mechanism. This review takes the relation itself as its object and argues that narcissism disturbs it in two opposite ways.

4. Affect and form

Expression, in the sense used here, is the act of giving affect a linguistic form that another person can take up. The form is pragmatic because it is addressed to a listener. It is judged by what it offers for uptake and by whether it can change in the exchange. Expression succeeds under two conditions. The form must carry the affect, which means that the feeling is identified and named.

The form must also stay open, which means that the speaker can revise or withdraw it in response to the listener. Conversation analysis finds this openness in ordinary talk: speakers routinely repair trouble in their own utterances, and self-correction is preferred to correction by others (Schegloff et al., 1977).

Weber's typology of action clarifies the first condition. In Etzrodt's (2024, p. 661) summary, Weber derived four ideal types of action: traditional action based on habituation, affectual action based on emotion, value-rational action, and purposive-rational action. Weber placed purely affectual behavior at the borderline of meaningful action and often beyond it, as in an uncontrolled reaction to an exceptional stimulus. Affect enters the field of meaning when it is released consciously, which Weber called sublimation (Weber, 1978, p. 25). Psychology describes the same passage in its own terms. Multiple code theory describes a referential process that links subsymbolic bodily experience with symbolic and verbal codes (Bucci, 1997).

Alexithymia research treats weakness in this link as a disorder of affect regulation (Taylor et al., 1997). Constructionist work argues that language helps to make up both the experience and the perception of emotion (Lindquist et al., 2015).

Etzrodt's pair of opposites supplies the two failures. He defines Durkheimian anomie as a person's inability to find and realize meaning in life. Its cause is a lack of integration and regulation, or too much freedom (Etzrodt, 2024, p. 660). He defines Marxist alienation, in general terms, as a state of being trapped by one's own creations, which prevents a person from developing freely (Etzrodt, 2024, pp. 659-660). Anomie results from too much freedom and alienation from too little, so that anomie is "formless life" and alienation is "lifeless form" (Thom, 1984 , as cited in Etzrodt, 2024, p. 660). In anomie the problem is a lack of meaning, while in alienation it is a lack of life (Etzrodt, 2024, p. 660).

This review transposes the pair from the relation between person and society to the relation between affect and linguistic form. The transposition is an ideal-typical construction. It does not claim that narcissistic speakers are anomic or alienated in the sociological sense. Freedom keeps a precise meaning in the transposition. It refers to how tightly affect is bound to linguistic form, not to how inhibited a person's conduct is. In anomic expression affect is bound too loosely and escapes form. In alienated expression it is bound too tightly to one self-made form. A speaker who is bold in conduct therefore still cannot express what that form excludes. Anomic expression is affect that lacks form. It is not regulated by naming, and a listener cannot take it up.

Alienated expression is form that admits only the affects that fit it. It is a self-made instrument of speech that has come to govern the speaker, and the speaker cannot revise it. Collaborative expression lies between the two, where affect has form and the form stays open. The types classify expressions, not people. A person can stand closer to one pole on average, and the same speaker can fail on either side at different moments. Trait measures therefore predict where a speaker's expression usually falls, and momentary measures track the shifts. Table 1 sets out the two types.

5. Ideal type A: anomic expression

>Ideal type A: anomic expression

5.1 Derivation

The type concerns spontaneous expression, that is, what happens to affect in ordinary speech without instruction. If affect does not reach a speakable form there, three consequences follow.

First, the speaker loses the regulation that naming provides. Labeling affect diminished the response of the amygdala to negative emotional images (Lieberman et al., 2007). Affect labeling regulates emotion implicitly, even when it does not feel like regulation (Torre & Lieberman, 2018). Second, emotional experience stays undifferentiated. People who draw finer distinctions among their emotions are less likely to turn to binge drinking, aggression, or self-injury when distressed (Kashdan et al., 2015). In the developmental model of emotional awareness, the lowest levels register emotion as bodily sensation or action tendency rather than as a differentiated feeling (Lane & Schwartz, 1987). Third, the affect still seeks release, and without a form it is released as action. Weber's description of affectual action as an uncontrolled reaction at the edge of meaning fits this case (Weber, 1978, p. 25).

5.2 Evidence at the vulnerable pole

The evidence near the vulnerable pole fits this derivation. Narcissism correlates with difficulty identifying feelings (r = .14; k = 5), and samples that include vulnerable narcissism carry the positive correlation with alexithymia (Song et al., 2026). The link between narcissism and emotion dysregulation is concentrated at the vulnerable pole (Tziliaskopoulos et al., 2027). In the same meta-analysis, the specific difficulties concerned accepting emotions, controlling impulses, and finding regulation strategies. Vulnerable narcissism is also linked to habitual suppression (Walker et al., 2022). In an experimental study, people high in vulnerable narcissism reported suppressing emotion more often (Loeffler et al., 2020). Yet in the same study, narcissism was unrelated to the ability to regulate emotion on instruction. This is the pattern the type specifies in advance: the failure lies in spontaneous expression, so instructed regulation is not the critical test. The firmest support for the type comes from the dysregulation and suppression findings.

The alexithymia evidence shows the predicted signs, and Proposition 2 tests spontaneous naming directly.

5.3 From formlessness to anger and blame

Unnamed affect does not stay private. Shame-proneness correlates with anger arousal, resentment, and a readiness to blame others when things go wrong (Tangney et al., 1992). Kohut (1972) described narcissistic rage as a reaction to injury of the self. Psychoanalytic, developmental, and interpersonal models have long placed shame, rather than guilt, at the center of narcissistic self-regulation (Kesman et al., 2026). In a study of 405 adults, vulnerable narcissism predicted a tendency toward anger and hostility (Czarna et al., 2021). Neuroticism accounted for much of this link, and poor strategic management of emotion contributed to hostility (Czarna et al., 2021). Anomic expression supplies a mechanism that connects these findings.

An injury that cannot be named as shame cannot be offered to a listener as a feeling. It is voiced instead as an accusation or held back as withdrawal. The listener then meets blame or silence and has no affect to respond to.

5.4 A rival explanation: neuroticism

A simpler rival explains the vulnerable pole by neuroticism alone. In dominance analyses across four samples, neuroticism accounted for 65% of the variance in vulnerable narcissism that the five-factor domains predicted (Miller et al., 2018). The variance not shared with neuroticism was specific to disagreeable traits such as distrustfulness and grandiosity (Miller et al., 2018). The ideal-type method turns this rival into a test. Autism research offers a precedent. The alexithymia hypothesis holds that emotional difficulties seen in autism stem from co-occurring alexithymia rather than from autism itself (Bird & Cook, 2013). Anomic expression is defined at the interface of affect and form. It therefore predicts that failures of affect naming carry part of the link between vulnerable narcissism and anger, even when neuroticism is held constant (Proposition 3). The disagreeable remainder is also where the type meets its opposite (Section 7).

6. Ideal type B: alienated expression

>Ideal type B: alienated expression

6.1 Derivation

In general terms, alienation is a process in which people create things to make life easier and then find their lives dominated by those creations (Etzrodt, 2024, p. 659). In expression, the created thing is the speaker's self-presentation. Grandiose narcissism involves a moment-tomoment pursuit of social status (Grapsas et al., 2020). In the admiration and rivalry model, a grandiose self is pursued through two pathways (Back et al., 2013). The admiration pathway runs through dominant and expressive behavior that others read as assertive (Leckelt et al., 2015). A practiced self-presentation serves that pursuit. Once established, it becomes the form through which each utterance must pass. Three consequences follow. Because the form is rehearsed, it is fluent. Because the form has no place for feelings that do not fit it, such as doubt or shame, it is thin in those affects. And because revising it would mean revising the self it presents, it is closed. Etzrodt (2024, p. 668) argues that Weber's iron cage is better explained by an extended concept of alienation than by disenchantment. Alienated expression is a small-scale version of that cage: a purposive instrument of speech that hardens into habit and then governs the speaker. Schutz's analysis of habitual knowledge describes how such routines guide conduct without a fresh choice in each situation (Schutz & Luckmann, 1973).

6.2 Fluent form

The fluency shows where a rehearsed form pays most: in brief, monologic, first encounters.

People high in narcissism are popular at zero acquaintance, and observable verbal and nonverbal behavior helps explain their appeal (Back et al., 2010). Their pitches are judged creative partly because of their enthusiasm, although blind coders find their products no more creative than those of others (Goncalo et al., 2010). Their speeches are rated as relatively persuasive (Foster et al., 2025, Study 1). Grandiose narcissism is associated with assertive rather than defensive selfpresentation tactics (Hart et al., 2017). The advantage fades where a rehearsed form helps least.

Essays that must build an argument on the page are rated as relatively unpersuasive (Foster et al., 2025, Studies 2A to 2C).

6.3 Lifeless and closed form

The form is thin in the affects that would threaten it. Across 15 samples, grandiose narcissism correlated negatively with anxiety words and tentative words (Holtzman et al., 2019). Patients with narcissistic personality disorder show impaired emotional empathy and intact cognitive empathy (Ritter et al., 2011). A meta-analysis with a combined 32,200 participants shows the same split for grandiose narcissism. It was related to low affective empathy more strongly on behavioral measures (r = -.251) than on self-report (r = -.145; Urbonaviciute & Hepper, 2020).

In the same meta-analysis, it was unrelated to behaviorally measured cognitive empathy. People high in grandiose narcissism can thus infer what a listener thinks without sharing what the listener feels. Self-reports describe the opposite. Narcissism correlates positively with selfreported trait emotional intelligence and negatively with ability-based emotional intelligence (Michels & Schulze, 2021). Grandiose narcissism also correlates negatively with self-reported alexithymia (Song et al., 2026). Under this type, these self-reports are expected, because the selfpresentation includes a claim of emotional skill that performance measures do not bear out.

The form is also closed. Self-enhancers' positive first impressions turned negative over seven weekly meetings (Paulhus, 1998). In a three-week study, the popularity of people high in narcissism declined as the rivalry-driven pathway of arrogant and aggressive behavior gained weight (Leckelt et al., 2015). The style that won the first meeting persisted after it stopped paying. Unwillingness to apologize, the plainest refusal to revise one's own speech, is tied to rivalry (Leunissen et al., 2017). In couple therapy, clients with narcissistic problems resist questions and control the topic when their self-image is under threat (Janusz et al., 2021).

6.4 Capacity and habit

The type draws a distinction that a deficit account cannot draw. Alienation, as defined above, concerns freedom rather than ability. Perspective-taking instructions weakened the links between maladaptive narcissism and low empathy, in self-reported empathy and in heart rate (Hepper et al., 2014). The ability is present, and the form keeps it out of ordinary expression until an outside instruction opens the form. A systematic review reaches a compatible conclusion: the socialcognitive profile of narcissism may reflect motivation, or the self-serving use of intact skills, rather than deficit (Eddy, 2021). The type also separates itself from a strategic-choice reading.

7. Where the types meet

>Where the types meet

7.1 A shared source: antagonism

The two types fail collaboration from opposite sides. Anomic expression gives the listener no affect to take up, and alienated expression gives the listener no opening to change anything. Both failures draw on antagonism. The narcissism spectrum model places entitled self-importance at the core that grandiose and vulnerable narcissism share (Krizan & Herlache, 2018). Self-centered antagonism is one of the three factors of narcissism (Crowe et al., 2019). The variance in vulnerable narcissism that neuroticism does not explain is disagreeable (Miller et al., 2018).

Rivalry, the antagonistic side of grandiose narcissism, drives both the refusal to apologize and the decline in popularity (Leckelt et al., 2015; Leunissen et al., 2017). Antagonism therefore explains why both poles fail at repair, and the types explain why they fail differently.

7.2 A shared target: shame

The types also fail on the same kind of affect. At the grandiose pole the form still carries feeling.

Speakers high in narcissism pitch their ideas with enthusiasm (Goncalo et al., 2010), and their texts contain more swear words alongside fewer anxiety and tentative words (Holtzman et al., 2019). The form is lifeless in a narrower sense. It leaves out affect that would weaken the self it presents, such as doubt or the fear of falling short. Neuroscience suggests that this affect is present but unreported. Grandiose narcissism goes with heightened vigilance and stress responses after ego threat, and self-reports seldom show these responses (Jauk & Kanske, 2021).

The type names shame as the prototype of the excluded affect.

A systematic review of 27 studies finds the two poles split on shame in the directions the types imply (Kesman et al., 2026). In its nonclinical samples, vulnerable narcissism related positively to shame, with moderate to strong effects. Grandiose narcissism measured with the Narcissistic Personality Inventory related slightly negatively. Links were more variable for pathological grandiosity, which the review reads as a more reactive form. That reactivity fits the movement described in Section 7.3. In its clinical studies, narcissistic personality disorder went with low shame-proneness but high shame aversion and elevated implicit shame. The review concludes that it is shame, not guilt, that differentiates the two poles (Kesman et al., 2026). Shame is therefore the affect that the two poles share and handle in opposite ways. In anomic expression it floods the speaker without reaching words. In alienated expression it is kept out of the form, which stays fluent because it never has to carry shame. Propositions 2 and 4 test the naming side of this claim. Both predict that naming failures concentrate on shame and related self-conscious emotions. A general deficit in identifying feelings, as in alexithymia, predicts no such concentration.

7.3 Movement between the poles

The types also account for movement between the poles. Dispositionally grandiose people report vulnerable as well as grandiose states, and entitlement is central to these momentary processes (Edershile & Wright, 2021). Under the framework, such a shift is a movement of the same speaker from alienated to anomic expression. It should be most likely when shame breaks through the self-presentation that holds the form together. An experiment supports this sequence.

Young adolescents high in narcissism became more aggressive than their peers only once they had been shamed (Thomaes et al., 2008). The effect was strongest when narcissism came with high self-esteem, a combination close to the grandiose pole. The aggression is the anomic outcome that Section 5.3 describes. Jauk and Kanske's (2021) review of 34 neuroscience studies also links interpersonal functioning in narcissism to deficits in social-affective processing, with altered activity in the salience network. These findings converge on an interface between affect and social processing, as the types expect.

8. Developmental language disorder as a distal marker

If the expressive difficulty in narcissism were a structural language deficit, childhood language disorder should predict narcissistic pathology in proportion to its severity. The evidence shows risk without that proportion. In the Danish cohort, personality disorder diagnoses were more than twice as common among people diagnosed with developmental language disorder in childhood (Mouridsen & Hauschild, 2009). Yet sex, IQ, the presence of receptive impairment, and the degree of expressive and receptive impairment did not predict a diagnosis (Mouridsen & Hauschild, 2009). The same cohort shows that a dose-response relation can appear: the degree of expressive language disorder was associated with schizophrenia-spectrum diagnoses (Mouridsen & Hauschild, 2008). The absence of such a relation for personality disorder is therefore informative. It suggests that the path runs through the experiences that language disorder brings rather than through language structure itself.

Two such experiences fit the framework: weaker emotion regulation and social anxiety. Children with language impairment receive lower teacher ratings of emotion regulation than their peers (Fujiki et al., 2002). The finding suggests a route toward the anomic type, which Proposition 7 tests. Early language impairment also predicts anxiety disorders in young adulthood, most of them social phobia (Beitchman et al., 2001).

Childhood ability shows no single direction either. In a population sample of 4,713 nine-yearolds, narcissistic traits went with higher verbal and nonverbal ability (Fontaine et al., 2008). The association held after conduct problems and hyperactivity were taken into account. Among 361 adolescents from samples with different levels of risk for antisocial behavior, narcissism was unrelated to verbal ability and related to lower nonverbal ability (Allen et al., 2013). Low language ability is therefore not a uniform risk direction for narcissism. Developmental language disorder is best treated as a distal marker of vulnerability whose effects pass through emotion regulation and social experience. Under the framework, it raises the likelihood of anomic expression without causing it.

9. Propositions and refutation conditions

Each proposition below follows from the types and is paired with the observation that would refute it. Propositions 1 to 4 test the defining features of the types. Propositions 5 to 7 test consequences of those features for modality, choice, and development. Table 2 lists measures suited to each test.

Proposition 1 (localization).

Narcissism at either pole relates more strongly to indicators of the affect-form relation than to structural language indicators. The first include spontaneous affect naming, emotional granularity, and repair and apology in conversation; the second include vocabulary, grammar, and sentence complexity. Because pragmatic measures depend partly on structural language (Norbury, 2014), the test holds structural language constant. Refutation: structural indicators relate to narcissism as strongly as affect-form indicators do, or the affectform link disappears once structural language is controlled.

Proposition 2 (anomic expression in performance). Vulnerable narcissism predicts weaker spontaneous affect naming on performance and observer measures, beyond higher self-reported difficulty identifying feelings. The deficit is largest for shame and other self-conscious emotions.

The test therefore needs naming tasks that include shame-eliciting scenes, scored apart from scenes that elicit basic emotions. Refutation: performance-based emotional awareness and observer-rated alexithymia show no deficit at the vulnerable pole. The proposition also fails if the deficit is equal across emotion categories, in which case general alexithymia suffices.

Proposition 3 (the anomic path beyond neuroticism). Weak affect naming carries part of the association between vulnerable narcissism and anger or blame in conflict talk. This indirect path remains when neuroticism is held constant. Refutation: affect naming carries none of the association, or the path disappears once neuroticism is controlled. In that case the vulnerable pole reduces to neuroticism.

Proposition 4 (alienated expression in measurement).

The grandiose advantage on selfreported alexithymia and trait emotional intelligence shrinks or reverses on performance and observer measures. The gap is also larger for affective than for cognitive skills. This second prediction separates the type from general self-enhancement, which predicts overstatement of both. Within affect, the gap is largest for shame and other self-threatening emotions. Refutation: the advantage holds equally on performance-based emotional awareness, ability emotional intelligence, and observer ratings.

The proposition also fails if the gap is no larger for affective than for cognitive skills, or no larger for self-threatening than for other emotions.

Proposition 5 (modality, dialogue, and time). Within the same persons, grandiose narcissism predicts higher rated persuasiveness in spoken monologue at first acquaintance. The advantage shrinks or reverses in writing, in dialogic tasks, and over repeated contact. The minimal design has the same speakers give a speech and write an essay in counterbalanced order, rated by blind raters. Refutation: the advantage appears as strongly in written or dialogic tasks, or the spoken advantage fails to appear within persons.

Proposition 6 (habit rather than choice).

Incentives that reward collaboration do not by themselves change the self-promoting form of grandiose speakers, whereas perspective-taking instruction does. The comparison is fixed in advance: the instruction effect should exceed the incentive effect. Manipulation checks confirm that participants registered the incentive and adopted the perspective. Refutation: the incentive effect equals or exceeds the instruction effect, which would favor a strategic-choice reading over the alienation type.

Proposition 7 (developmental path).

A history of developmental language disorder relates to later personality pathology through emotion regulation and peer experience rather than through childhood language severity. It also relates more closely to anomic than to alienated features.

The test controls for executive function and family socioeconomic status, which could drive both language and later outcomes. Refutation: childhood severity predicts later narcissistic features after these paths are taken into account, or a history of language disorder relates equally to both types. 10. What the types predict that rival accounts do not

Three rival accounts explain parts of the evidence. A structural-deficit account treats the difficulty as low language ability. A trait-only account derives it from the three factors of narcissism (Crowe et al., 2019; Miller et al., 2018). On this account, neuroticism makes people poor at naming feelings and prone to anger, agentic extraversion makes them fluent and selfassured, and antagonism makes them uncooperative. A strategic-choice account treats grandiose speech as a rational choice of tactics (Hart et al., 2017). Table 3 sets out where the four accounts diverge.

The trait-only account is the strongest rival, and five predictions separate it from the types. First, the types predict a self-report bias that runs in opposite directions at the two poles. At the grandiose pole, they also predict a larger bias for affective than for cognitive skill, an asymmetry a trait account has no reason to expect. The empathy meta-analysis shows it (Urbonaviciute & Hepper, 2020). At the grandiose pole, self-reports understate the affective deficit that behavioral measures reveal, while behavioral measures show cognitive empathy intact. Second, the types predict that affect naming carries part of the vulnerable link to anger after neuroticism is controlled (Proposition 3). The trait account expects neuroticism to absorb it. Third, the types predict that instruction changes grandiose speech more than incentive does (Proposition 6), a contrast on which the trait account is silent. Fourth, the types predict a shift from alienated to anomic expression when the self-presentation is threatened. A stable trait profile does not entail that within-person shift, which momentary, experimental, and neuroscientific evidence makes plausible (Edershile & Wright, 2021; Jauk & Kanske, 2021; Thomaes et al., 2008). Fifth, the types predict that naming failures concentrate on shame and related self-conscious emotions (Propositions 2 and 4). A trait account, like a general alexithymia account, predicts difficulty across emotions.

Existing data already count against the other two rivals. The structural-deficit account predicts a dose-response relation between childhood language severity and later personality pathology, which the Danish cohort did not show (Mouridsen & Hauschild, 2009). The strategic-choice account predicts that grandiose speakers drop a style once it stops paying. Instead, the arrogant and aggressive pathway gained weight while popularity declined (Leckelt et al., 2015).

A strategist could persist only by failing to register the decline, and self-enhancers did keep rating themselves above their peers' ratings of them (Paulhus, 1998). Such a blind spot is what a closed form predicts. Proposition 6 separates the two readings, because its manipulation checks confirm that participants registered the incentive.

11. Discussion

>Discussion

11.1 What the framework solves

The framework solves six problems that the separate literatures leave open. The first is a classification problem. Asking whether narcissism involves expressive language disorder pairs a structural diagnosis with a problem of affect and form. Relocating the difficulty explains why direct searches find nothing, and why a speaker high in narcissism can be fluent and inarticulate at once. The second is an aggregation problem. A near-zero correlation between narcissism and alexithymia (Song et al., 2026) is what two opposite failures produce when they are pooled. The types also predict the signs that the moderator analyses found. The third is a measurement problem. The gap between grandiose self-reports and performance (Michels & Schulze, 2021) becomes a predicted property of alienated expression, which tells researchers where self-report will mislead. The fourth is a specificity problem. Speakers at the grandiose pole pitch with enthusiasm and swear more, yet use fewer anxiety and tentative words (Goncalo et al., 2010; Holtzman et al., 2019). The types expect this selectivity, because the alienated form keeps out the affects that threaten the self. Shame is the prototype of those affects, and it is also the affect that the anomic speaker cannot name. Naming failures should therefore concentrate on shame and related self-conscious emotions rather than spread across all emotions. The fifth is a developmental anomaly. Developmental language disorder raises the risk of personality disorder without a dose-response relation to severity (Mouridsen & Hauschild, 2009). The framework expects this, because the relevant failure lies in the relation between affect and form rather than in structural language. The sixth is a testing problem. Seven propositions, each with a refutation condition, give the field a way to decide between the types and their rivals.

11.2 Assessment and intervention

For assessment, the framework recommends pairing every self-report of emotional skill with a performance or observer measure, and coding conversation for repair, apology, and expressed uncertainty. Affect naming should be scored separately for self-conscious emotions, such as shame, and for basic emotions. A total score would hide a failure concentrated on shame. For intervention, the framework separates two needs. At the anomic pole, the task is to give affect, and shame above all, a form. Work that builds affect labeling and emotion differentiation targets that failure directly (Kashdan et al., 2015; Torre & Lieberman, 2018). At the alienated pole, vocabulary is not the problem. The task is to open the form to the affects it excludes.

Perspective-taking instructions raise empathy in people high in narcissism, which shows that this is possible (Hepper et al., 2014). A single protocol for both poles would leave one of the two failures untreated.

11.3 Theoretical contribution

The review also extends Etzrodt's reading of anomie and alienation. Etzrodt (2024) uses these concepts to interpret Weber's rationalization processes in economic, political, and religious life.

Defined at the level of a single speaker, the same pair organizes a two-faced construct in personality psychology as two opposite failures of one relation. The result is a template for other constructs with a grandiose and a vulnerable face. Instead of averaging the faces, researchers can define the relation that both disturb and derive each face as an ideal type with its own refutation conditions.

11.4 Scope

The framework makes no diagnostic claim, and its types classify expressions rather than persons.

The difficulties it describes sit at the interface of affect and pragmatic form. They are not equivalent to language disorder or social (pragmatic) communication disorder in DSM-5-TR, or to their ICD counterparts. This scope keeps the framework from pathologizing pragmatic difference.

12. Conclusion

In the evidence reviewed here, narcissism leaves language ability intact but disturbs the relation between affect and form, in two opposite ways. At one pole, affect finds no form and returns as anger or silence. At the other, form keeps out the affects that would threaten it and stays fixed after it stops working. Shame is the affect at stake in both. Weber's ideal types, extended by falsification as Etzrodt proposes, make the two failures precise enough to test. Etzrodt's pair of anomie and alienation names them: formless affect and lifeless fluency. The research question therefore becomes whether the affect of people high in narcissism reaches a form that another person can take up and change.
