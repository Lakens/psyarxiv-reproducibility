Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It failed to detect 27 statements. Thus, among all statements flagged by the module, 63% were genuine cases (positive predictive value). However, the module missed 42% of all true statements (false negative rate).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Background: While current frameworks present essential principles for effective youth involvement, they provide limited practical advice on how participation can be organized and maintained throughout research projects. Meaningful involvement requires not just chances for youth to engage, but also the structural, relational, and procedural conditions that allow them to have real impact.

Objective: This paper intends to present a practice-based framework for developing research infrastructure that enables meaningful youth involvement and to demonstrate its use across the research process.

Methods: Building upon fifteen years of hands-on experience in youth engagement at Jimmy's, we adapt a practice-based ecosystem-based model into a framework for participatory research, guided by equity principles and Lundy's child participation model. The Youth Resilience Groningen study acts as a practical example to demonstrate how this framework can guide the organization of youth engagement, encompassing the formation of partnerships, defined roles, resources, facilitation, co-design, and decision-making processes.

Results: The framework places equity as its main guiding principle and outlines four interrelated pillars: the research organization, researchers and research personnel, emerging research collaborators, and the broader network of community and research allies. These pillars are formed through three parallel processes: Doing, Learning, and Organising. In the Youth Resilience Groningen study, six teens were hired as junior researchers, aided by Jimmy's, and participated in determining research priorities, creating study materials, developing questionnaires, planning recruitment strategies, and developing educational guest lectures for secondary school classes, delivered as a token of appreciation for their participation in the study. The study demonstrates how organizational structures and continuous support can enable youth participation while addressing ethical, methodological, and institutional limitations.

Conclusion: Effective youth engagement goes beyond just including young individuals in research efforts. It relies on an ecosystem that allows their insights and viewpoints to guide research choices. The practice-based framework provides flexible direction for structuring, overseeing, and enhancing meaningful youth participation in research projects.

1. Introduction

Youth participation is increasingly recognised as an important component of research (e.g., Rouncefield-Swales et al., 2021). Young people possess experiential knowledge about their lives, social environments, support needs, and interactions with services that cannot be fully substituted by professional or scientific expertise. Their involvement may increase the relevance of research questions, improve the comprehensibility of study materials and instruments, reveal assumptions held by adult researchers and strengthen the practical usefulness of research findings. Furthermore, involving young people in decisions that concern them is increasingly regarded as an ethical principle rather than merely a methodological preference.

Multiple models and frameworks have been developed to conceptualise youth participation. For example, the ten principles of the European Citizen Science Association's (ECSA) that emphasise youths' active involvement in scientific work, the production of genuine scientific outcomes, mutual benefit, feedback, acknowledgement, evaluation and transparent access to results; Hart's ladder that describes eight levels of youth participation, ranging from nonparticipation to youth-initiated decision-making shared with adults (Hart 1992); and Shier's pathways to participation, (Shier 2001). In all these models, meaningful participation is described in terms of what young people do. For example, they advise, co-create, are involved in data-collecting, help in interpreting findings or communicating the results. Less attention is paid to the conditions that enable them to perform these roles, or how such participation can be organised across research projects. Models that focus on the conditions are for example the typology of youth-adult partnerships each describe conditions under which young people can become influential rather than merely be consulted (Wong, Zimmerman & Parker 2010) and the Lundy's Model of Child Participation (Lundy 2007). In particular Lundy's model of space (creating safe and inclusive spaces for young people to participate); voice (information and support to enable them express their views); audience (making sure their perspectives are heard by relevant parties such as decision-makers); and influence (helping their voices to have influence and inform decision-making processes) seems to resonate in Europe as a useful framework for creating conditions by which youth can contribute on a more equal level to adult experts, and for moving their involvement from sec consultation to meaningful involvement (see Figure 1).

Youth participation often takes place within organisations that were not originally designed for that. Knowing and acknowledging that youth should have a 'voice' does not automatically guide a research team on how to recruit such youth, distribute decision-making authority, budget for their participation, manage possible disagreements, or maintain a healthy collaboration over time (Turuba et al., 2022). Moreover, university contracts, ethical procedures, funding arrangements and publication conventions may also limit the extent to which youth can make decisions, how quickly decisions can be made and which forms of knowledge are formally recognised. Meaningful youth participation needs more than the mere presence of young people. It asks for arrangements through which their knowledge can shape the design, questions, decisions, and products of research (Tyl et al., 2026). However, despite widespread agreement about the value of youth participation, it remains unclear how participation can be embedded structurally in the everyday organisation of research as little empirical work describes this organisational process in sufficient detail.

In the present paper, we therefore present a practice-informed and theory-grounded framework for building research infrastructure for meaningful youth participation together with a detailed description of the application of the framework throughout the research process of the Youth Resilience Groningen study. This study aimed to investigate young people's psychosocial needs, help-seeking behaviour, experiences with existing facilities and preferences regarding future services. The findings were intended to support scientific knowledge, regional policy, service development, and the allocation of public resources (for more information, see: https://doi.org/10.34894/WB7YJ5). 2. The Framework: An Ecosystem for Meaningful Youth Participation in Research 2.1. Equity as the organising principle

Equity constitutes the central organising principle of the framework. In participatory research, equity does not imply equal authority or that the participating youth, the researchers, the practitioners and the policymakers should have identical responsibilities. Researchers are often the ones responsible for scientific integrity, research ethics and FAIR data governance, while policymakers and practitioners or practical organisations operate within their own professional and public mandates. In turn, youth mostly contribute with their 'lived experience': forms of experiential and contextual knowledge that are not available through scientific or professional expertise alone. Equitable collaboration therefore concerns whether different forms of knowledge are recognised as legitimate and whether all partners have a genuine opportunity to influence the decisions that are open to influence.

Important to note is that true equity may require uneven forms of support. A young person who has little experience with research meetings may need a bit more preparation, accessible information or individual guidance, compared to experienced researchers. Treating both participants identically would not necessarily create equal possibilities for participation. However, equitable participation also requires researchers to possess the skills and attitudes needed to communicate with young people on an equal footing, recognising and valuing their perspectives and contributions.

2.2. Four pillars of participatory research infrastructure

Research infrastructure commonly refers to the resources and systems required to conduct research. In participatory research, this infrastructure also includes arrangements such as relationships, procedures for decision-making, contracts, compensation, facilitation, communication routines, psychological safety, feedback and opportunities for reflection. Although these elements are less visible than other forms of research output (e.g., the survey), they still help determine whether young people can exercise sustained influence throughout the project. In other words, the participatory research infrastructure depends on an ecosystem that extends beyond participating young people and its researchers: a network of young people, practitioners, policymakers and other partners who share responsibility for participation.

Based on the progressive insight that the ecosystem is key, and grounded in the theory of Lundy et al (ref), a practice-informed framework for meaningful youth participation was formulated by Jimmy'sfoot_0 . The approach focuses on building an ecosystem for meaningful youth participation, consisting of four interrelated pillars of factors for success. First, organisational conditions and processes should be arranged to facilitate structural collaboration with young people, including supportive governance, a participatory culture and sustainable working procedures. Second, professionals need the knowledge, skills and attitudes required to design and facilitate participatory processes and collaborate equitably with young people. Third, connections with a diverse community of young people should be established, with flexible opportunities and appropriate conditions enabling them to contribute in different ways and at different moments. Fourth, a network of relevant partners should be developed, supported by clear agreements and appropriate conditions for collaboration. Translated to the research context, these pillars concern the research organisation, researchers and research staff, young research partners, and the wider network of community and research partners.

2.3. Developing infrastructure through Doing, Learning and Organising

The conditions of the ecosystem are developed through three parallel lines of working: Doing, Learning and Organising. Doing involves working collaboratively with young people, professionals and partners on concrete questions or projects, producing immediate results while gaining practical experience in participatory working. Learning makes this development explicit through reflection, training, peer learning and coaching. Organising concerns establishing the organisational conditions, structures and working procedures needed to embed participation sustainably. Applied to research, these three lines imply that conducting participatory research, developing the competencies of researchers and young people, and establishing supportive institutional arrangements should take place in parallel.

Across these activities, Jimmy's emphasises involving young people from the beginning, taking their knowledge, experiences and ideas seriously, and creating opportunities for different young people to contribute to questions that matter to them. In the research context, these practicebased commitments can be further specified through principles concerning clarity about influence, accessible communication and feedback, appropriate recognition of contributions, openness to unexpected perspectives and joint reflection. These principles inform how meaningful collaboration is organised within and across the four ecosystem pillars.

3. Applying the Framework Throughout the Research Process

>Applying the Framework Throughout the Research Process

3.1. Establishing partnerships and defining research priorities

Young people, youth participation organisations, municipalities, schools and providers of youth walk-in facilities were involved to help ensure that the study addressed questions considered relevant by its intended users and that the research procedures were acceptable and accessible to adolescents. Rather than establishing one advisory meeting at the end of protocol development, the programme created two partly connected routes for participation: a Youth Core Team and regional network of youth walk-in facilities.

In parallel, representatives of regional youth walk-in facilities participated in consultation meetings coordinated by Jimmy's. These organisations contributed knowledge about local service provision, young people's encounters with support and the practical conditions under which walk-in facilities operated.The contribution of the regional organisations further ensured that the research questions addressed decisions that were relevant to the implementation of youth walk-in facilities.

The Youth Core Team first contributed to the framing of the research problem and the questions the study should address. Discussions focused on how young people understand mental wellbeing, when support is needed and which circumstances make a youth walk-in facility approachable or unapproachable.These discussions helped shift the study beyond a general assessment of mental health and resilience, and in the end more attention was given to young people's preferences, support needs, help-seeking intentions and the barriers and facilitators surrounding conversations about mental health.

3.2. Establishing roles, responsibilities and ethical arrangements

A Youth Core Team consisting of six adolescents was appointed as a group of junior researchers at the University of Groningen. Each junior researcher received an appointment of 4 hours per week. This formal appointment was intended to recognise that participation required time, responsibility and work, and that young people's contributions should therefore not depend solely on voluntary availability. The young people were consequently involved not as representatives of a target population, but as members of the research team. They did not participate in the surveys themselves.

Jimmy's occupied a boundary-spanning position between these two routes. The organisation supported the Youth Core Team in preparing for and participating in research meetings, while simultaneously coordinating input from community organisations and walk-in facilities. This role was important because the groups used different forms of language and knowledge.

Researchers were familiar with study protocols, measurement instruments and ethical requirements, whereas young people and practitioners more often approached the study through recognisable situations, experiences and practical consequences. Facilitation therefore involved more than organising meetings. It included translating between these perspectives while attempting to preserve the substance of each contribution.

Participant information letters and consent forms were reviewed and revised with the Youth Core Team. Particular attention was paid to length, language, and the distinction between formal completeness and practical comprehensibility. Information that is legally accurate may still be difficult for an adolescent to understand, particularly when it uses institutional terminology or provides several types of information simultaneously. Importantly, the aim was not simply to make the materials shorter or more attractive. The materials also needed to communicate voluntariness, confidentiality, potentially sensitive content and the possibility of discontinuing participation.

3.3. Co-designing and conducting research

Under the guidance of Jimmy's, the Youth Core Team met on a weekly basis and contributed to the research questions, study design (e.g., creating a secondary-education-level guest lecture about research into resilience where the survey was part of), participant information, consent procedures, questionnaire, recruitment and dissemination.

Co-creation was organised as an iterative process in which the Youth Core Team was asked to brainstorm on topics that should be included in the research, materials were developed by the research team, reviewed with young people and community partners, revised and, where necessary, reviewed again.

Their feedback informed project-specific questionnaire items concerning preferences for youth walk-in facilities and barriers and facilitators to help-seeking. The team also advised on the broader study design, including how participation could be made safe and accessible and how teachers should introduce the research in classroom settings.

Co-creation did not mean that every element of the study was designed from the beginning by all partners. Some components, including validated psychological measures and formal ethical requirements, imposed boundaries on what could be changed. The central question was therefore which decisions were open to influence (i.e., being explicit about the influence they could exercise), and how youth and practice knowledge could be incorporated without compromising scientific validity or legal requirements.

4. Monitoring and Reflecting on Meaningful Participation 4.1. Monitoring the participation ecosystem

In the original Jimmy's approach for organisations such as municipalities, monitoring concerns both the result of the concrete project and the development of the ecosystem for youth participation. At the start of a collaboration, the intended result is determined together. During the collaboration, progress is reviewed regularly and adjustments are made where needed. In parallel, the organisation builds its own ecosystem for youth participation step by step.

At the beginning of the collaboration, Jimmy's and the organisation complete the Jimmy's Participation Scan together. This shows where the organisation currently stands on the four pillars: people, organisation, young people and partners. They then determine together which pillars and success factors have the highest priority for the collaboration. During and at the end of the project, development on the selected pillars is evaluated. Jimmy's recognises that attention to each pillar is important, but that time and resources are limited.

In participatory research, a similar approach can be used to monitor the development of the research infrastructure. Researchers, participating youth and partners can examine the starting situation across the four pillars, identify which conditions need attention most and evaluate progress during as well as after the study. In addition to the development of the conditions, both parties can reflect on whether the participatory youth were able to influence research decisions, how their contributions were used and what could be improved in future collaborations.

Importantly, the four pillars and their related success factors should be understood as a compass for reflection and development, rather than as a rigid set of criteria for evaluating participation. The model highlights key conditions necessary for meaningful participation but does not specify the exact methods for achieving them or the criteria for determining when they have been adequately fulfilled. Researchers, the participating youth, and their partners should work together to evaluate how well the success factors have been addressed in their particular situation, and identify any additional steps that might be necessary for improvement.

4.2. Reflecting on influence throughout the research process

Throughout implementation, researchers and participating youth should try to regularly reflect on whether the intended collaboration is being realised, which things have influenced decisions and how, and where limitations for participation are experienced. For example, it is advised for researchers to keep track of which suggestions young people made during the development of research questions, questionnaires or recruitment materials, so that there is a paper trail of which suggestions were used, and which were not. Jacquez and Vaughn (2025) developed the Report of Engagement in Community Research (REACH) tool to document who was involved in which stage of the research, what exactly they contributed and how. Such documentation can also help research teams reflect on how participation was organised and report it more transparently. When suggestions could not be adopted, to be transparent ideally the reasons should be discussed with the youth. This is particularly relevant when ethical requirements or methodological considerations limit what can be changed or other decisions play a role where youth might not be aware of. Young people can also be asked by a neutral party whether they felt able to express different opinions, and whether they feel they had sufficient information to contribute and whether they understand/accept which decisions were open to their influence and which were not. This way, reflection will concern not only whether young people participated, but also what happened with their contributions. This is also in line with best practice, as others have also called for creating opportunities for feedback and joint interpretation throughout the research process, including discussion of how participants' contributions have informed research decisions and findings (ECSA, 2015; Staniszewska et al., 2017).

4.3. Learning and improving research infrastructure

The last step is giving feedback, and evaluating the participatory research collaboration.

Experiences from these activities can help make changes to the research process and contribute to building skills, establishing procedures, and organizing future participatory research efforts.

Researchers, participatory youth, and their partners can come together to discuss what aspects of the collaboration were effective and what challenges were encountered during participation. For instance, this could relate to the time given to prepare for meetings, the availability of research resources, the assistance offered to young individuals, or the manner in which decisions were conveyed. Based on these shared reflections, researchers, participating youth and partners can jointly identify which arrangements have worked well, where further development is needed and what could be adapted in future projects. This may involve allocating time and resources to allow young people to take part in research funding, creating more defined steps for working together and receiving input, or enhancing the abilities of researchers to support inclusive and collaborative approaches.

This difference shows the three parallel lines of Doing, Learning and Organising in the original Jimmy's approach: carrying out meaningful activities, developing the knowledge and skills required for collaboration, and establishing organisational conditions that support ongoing participation. In the context of research, the goal is not only to enhance involvement in a single study but also to build the ability of research teams and their collaborators to effectively support meaningful youth participation in future projects.

5. Discussion: Methodological Contributions, Limitations and Transferability

>Discussion: Methodological Contributions, Limitations and Transferability

5.1. Methodological contribution

The framework does not require the same level of participation in every research activity. Instead, it encourages researchers to think about how the practical knowledge and/or lived experience of young people can help make the research more relevant, easier to access, and better understood, and what kind of support is necessary to achieve this. Making these boundaries clear can stop participation from being just a formality when people are formally involved but not given chances to have real influence (Lundy, 2007). Most important are the establishment of a common objective, the allocation of functions and impact, the provision of support and necessary resources, the organization of communication and feedback, and the creation of opportunities to learn and revise the partnership. In doing so, it becomes clear not only what meaningful youth participation entails, but also the infrastructure through which it can be organised and sustained.

5.2. Tensions and practical limitations

Weakness in one domain may undermine progress in another. Inviting a diverse youth team has limited value when members do not feel safe to disagree. Shared decision-making is difficult when young people receive documents too late to prepare. Appropriate remuneration may recognise participation, but cannot compensate for the absence of influence.

Co-creation did not mean that every element of the study was designed from the beginning by all partners. Some components, including validated psychological measures and formal ethical requirements, imposed boundaries on what could be changed. The central question was therefore which decisions were open to influence (i.e., being explicit about the influence they could exercise), and how youth and practice knowledge could be incorporated without compromising scientific validity or legal requirements.

5.3. Limitations of the framework

These principles originated from professional practice rather than from a formal scientific evaluation. In the present project, they were consequently treated as practice-informed propositions that required translation, testing and refinement within a specific research setting. Furthermore, the translation from professional to research setting was not one-to-one: some principles could be operationalised directly, whereas others were constrained by ethical, methodological or institutional requirements.

Although in this study we focused on a single study as a case study, the framework is not drawing on a single research setting, as Jimmy's has a lot of other research projects where they collaborate with. So far, the framework itself has undergone systematic evaluation, albeit predominantly by public and professional organisations (e.g., municipality) and not universities or research setting. Nevertheless, the experience within the Resilient Groningen Study was very positive.

5.4. Transferability to other research settings

The framework is not designed to be universal. Local relationships, institutional requirements, resources, and the involvement of young people and organizations determine the nature of participatory arrangements. Instead, we recognize a group of flexible design guidelines and thoughtful questions for reflection. Early involvement does not always mean that all research questions are created together at the beginning, especially when funding agreements or scientific goals have already been set in place.

Researchers should clearly state which parts of the problem definition and study design are still open to change, and provide chances for young people and other partners to give their input within these limits (Lundy, 2007; Shier, 2001).

6. Conclusion

This framework is based on Jimmy's practice-oriented approach to involving young people and transforms it into a research structure that is organized around four connected pillars. Doing, Learning and Organising brings together equity, the influence of young people, and institutional conditions, while highlighting the development of participatory practice, skills, and organizational capacity.

Using the concrete example from Youth Resilience Groningen, the framework offers researchers practical advice on how to organise, monitor, and enhance meaningful youth involvement in research. It can help research teams and participating partners to create a research infrastructure and ecology that enables assessment of the areas where young people can exert influence, the conditions needed to make contributions, and where possibly ethical, methodological or institutional constraints remain.

By applying the framework, research teams can develop the infrastructure needed to support meaningful youth participation. In line with Doing, Learning, and Organising, this means connecting participation in individual research activities to the building of necessary skills, new relationships, and organisational structures that support continued participation over time.
