Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Physical exercise (PE) is widely recognized for its benefits on physical and mental health. Yet its mechanisms of action on psychological outcomes remain insufficiently understood, especially regarding the role of tonic (under resting conditions) and challenge-induced response of the autonomic nervous system. This study examined the impact of a six-month personalized, endurance exercise program on autonomic cardiovascular and psychological indices in sedentary middle-aged adults. Forty-two participants (35-65 years old) were recruited to undergo laboratory assessments of heart rate variability (HRV), baroreflex sensitivity (BRS), heart rate (HR), and mean blood pressure (MBP) at rest and during a handgrip test (i.e., a response to a physical challenge), alongside validated questionnaires assessing depression, anxiety, resilience, somatization, and emotion regulation at baseline, and after three and six months of physical training. Results revealed significant improvements in autonomic cardiovascular function with a specific temporal pattern: HR decreased and BRS increased after three months and remained stable at six months, while HRV and MBP improved only after six months. These effects were all observed at the tonic level, with the training not affecting the cardiovascular response to the handgrip test. Unexpectedly, no stable effect was found on psychological variables after three or six months of personalized PE. In addition, changes in cardiovascular markers did not predict improvements in psychological outcomes.

This study reinforces long-term PE programs as a scalable means to enhance cardiovascular and autonomic health, but yields limited evidence that such autonomic gains are associated with meaningful psychological improvements.

1. Introduction

A substantial body of research indicates that physical exercise (PE), defined as "a subset of physical activity that is planned, structured and repetitive, and has as a final or an intermediate objective the improvement or maintenance of physical fitness" (Caspersen et al., 1985), has significant health benefits across the lifespan (Penedo & Dahn, 2005). Regular engagement in PE not only improves physical health but would also promote well-being by improving mood and affect and reducing stress-related symptoms such as depression and anxiety, even in non-clinical populations (Conn, 2010; Lin, 2023; Mochcovitch et al., 2016; for a review, see Penedo & Dahn, 2005). Beyond these well-documented benefits, PE represents a scalable and cost-effective intervention with minimal adverse effects. However, the mechanisms of action underlying these benefits remain insufficiently understood. A clearer understanding of these mechanisms is necessary to optimize outcomes and develop tailored interventions that can be integrated into public health strategies to promote mental health and well-being.

One proposed mechanism involves the autonomic nervous system (ANS). The ANS comprises the sympathetic branch, which increases heart rate and blood pressure, and the parasympathetic branch, which exerts opposing effects (for a review, see Wehrwein & Joyner, 2013). Regular PE shifts autonomic regulation towards reduced sympathetic and enhanced parasympathetic activity and improves cardiorespiratory efficiency (Fu & Levine, 2013). Most research has focused on autonomic cardiovascular regulation, showing that PE enhances autonomic cardiovascular flexibility by strengthening vagal tone, as reflected in faster autonomic nervous system recovery following exercise (Heffernan et al., 2007; Otsuki et al., 2007). A comparable pattern characterizes resilience, defined as "the process and outcome of successfully adapting to difficult or challenging life experiences" (APA, 2018). Adaptive responding to challenge involves transient sympathetic activation followed by rapid parasympathetic restoration once the threat subsides (Ottaviani, 2018; Ziegler, 2012).

Following the Neurovisceral Integration Model, a dynamic autonomic cardiovascular control with strong parasympathetic activity has been linked with numerous beneficial stress-related psychological outcomes, including lower depressive and anxious symptoms and better emotional regulation (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?KcdcYa Paniccia et al., 2017; Thayer et al., 2012). Still, whether and how PE promotes mental health and well-being via enhancing autonomic cardiovascular control is understudied.

The Neurovisceral Integration Model posits that both resting and stressor-evoked autonomic cardiovascular control are related to mental health and well-being (Thayer & Lane, 2009; Smith et al., 2017). However, recent studies suggest that the association between autonomic cardiovascular control and stress-related psychological outcomes, such as depressive symptoms and emotion regulation, is more consistently observed for autonomic reactivity to emotional challenges than for tonic (i.e., resting) ANS activity (Li et al., 2024 (Li et al., , 2026;; Schiweck et al., 2019). In line with this, the cross-stressor adaptation hypothesis posits that PE and psychological stress responses share overlapping physiological pathways, such that repeated exercise exposure trains the organism to respond more adaptively to psychological stressors (Sothmann et al., 1996). Thus, improvements in mental well-being following regular PE may depend on enhanced autonomic responsiveness to challenge rather than baseline activity alone. However, the current evidence suggests a modest link between regular physical activity and cardiorespiratory fitness with autonomic cardiovascular regulation in response to psychosocial stressors (e.g., Martín-Vazquez, 2010; Reyes del Paso et al., 2017; Ardvison et al., 2020; see Forcier et al. 2006 and Jackson & Dischman, 2006 for a meta-analyses, and Mücke et al., 2018 for a systematic review). One potential explanation is that psychosocial stress paradigms introduce substantial contextual and cognitive variability, which may obscure PE-related physiological adaptations, and strong habituation effects are observed in repeated administration (e.g., autonomic responses are attenuated from the second exposure onward), thereby limiting their suitability for longitudinal designs. Moreover, most studies investigating the association between exercise and autonomic cardiovascular control at rest and in response to psychosocial challenges are cross-sectional (Mücke et al., 2018), underscoring the need for intervention-based evidence to clarify causal effects. Therefore, more research is needed to better understand how PE translates into better ANS regulation and how this links to mental health and well-being.

Within this context, physical challenges may provide important insights into the effects of PE on ANS regulation and its implications for mental health. Physical challenges activate the autonomic cardiovascular system in a manner similar to psychosocial or emotional stressors, characterized by parasympathetic withdrawal and increased cardiac sympathetic activity (e.g., Michael et al., 2017). In contrast to psychosocial challenges, non-aerobic physical challenges can be standardized relative to the participant's maximum capacity and are particularly suitable for longitudinal designs because they are less susceptible to habituation effects. Importantly, stress-related psychological disorders such as depression and anxiety have been associated with dysregulated autonomic response to physical challenges (e.g., Guinjoan et al., 1997; Udupa et al., 2007; Bylsma et al., 2015; Asbrand et al., 2017). However, an important gap in the literature is whether PE-induced changes in autonomic reactivity constitute a mechanism linking exercise to mental well-being. Investigating autonomic cardiovascular responses to standardized physical challenges may provide a more direct assessment of PE-induced adaptations within the cardiovascular control system. Overall, systematic investigations connecting psychological outcomes with exercise-related autonomic adaptations remain scarce, despite their potential to advance mechanistic understanding and inform targeted clinical interventions. It is also important to note that most studies have focused on the effect of physical activity on vagally-mediated heart rate variability (HRV; reflecting parasympathetic control and https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?7XKCbe Pilowsky et al., 2013), heart rate (HR), and blood pressure (BP) (Etayo-Urtasun et al., 2026). The effect of long-term PE programs on baroreflex function (reflecting ANS capability to regulate blood pressure via the baroreceptor reflex; Pilowsky et al., 2013) has only been investigated in three small studies, showing mixed and small effects (for a meta-analysis see Etayo-Urtasun et al., 2026), and the potential effect of PE on mental well-being via changes in baroreflex function has never been investigated.

In this study, we investigated the effect of a personalized, supervised, PE program on cardiovascular markers of ANS regulation and stress-related psychological indices of mental health of sedentary middle-aged participants, after three and six months of training. Cardiovascular activity was measured at rest and during the handgrip test (a validated and standardized physical challenge (Fukumoto et al., 2022; Tapiainen et al., 2022). We hypothesized that from baseline to three months and to three months to six months, participants would show autonomic cardiovascular function improvement observable by (1) higher vagallymediated heart rate variability, (2) higher baroreflex function, (3) lower Heart rate (reflecting sympathetic and parasympathetic influence on heart activity), and (4) mean blood pressure (MBP) at rest and a (5) lower reactivity (response) on to the handgrip test. At the psychological level, we expected a psychological improvement observable by (1) lower depressive symptoms, (2) lower anxious symptoms, (3) higher resilience and (4) more use of emotion regulation strategies (research has shown that this leads to higher well-being; Garnefski et al., 2001).

Moreover, we expected less somatic symptoms. We also hypothesized that psychological improvement from baseline to three months and from three months to six months would correlate with the changes in cardiovascular ANS markers over the same intervals, namely that improvement in symptoms, resilience and emotion regulation strategies would be predicted by higher HRV, lower HR, lower MBP and higher BRS at rest, and lower responses to the handgrip test.

2. Methods

This study is part of a larger research project investigating the impact of a 6-month endurance exercise program on the epigenetic clock (Van Damme et al., 2026). In the current study, we focus on psychological well-being and cardiovascular markers of ANS activity (HRV, HR, MBP, and baroreflex function) at rest and during a handgrip protocol. We refer the readers to Van Damme et al. (2026) for a detailed description of intervention adherence and the effects of the training program on the epigenetic clock, and anthropometric (body mass index, body fat, lean mass, and bone density), health-related (diet, and alcohol), sleep, fitness (VO2max, peak power output, and grip strength), and other cardiovascular (nightly HR, nightly HRV, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, pulse wave velocity) parameters.

2.1 Participants

Forty-two participants were selected to participate in this study. In prior research, the effect of physical activity on ANS and psychological well-being yielded heterogeneous effectsizes ranging from small-to-medium to large (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?PhM5ON Amekran & El Hangouche, 2024; Conn, 2010; Lin, 2023). However, because a small effect size would not be clinically meaningful, we aimed for a medium effect size. Following G*Power, to obtain this effect size (d = 0.5, with power = 0.80, alpha = 0.05), 28 participants are needed in a within-subject design. We tested 50% more participants (i.e., 42), which is justified by the potentially high probability that people would drop out due to the length and level of demand of the training and our practical impossibility to recruit additional participants after the start of the study.

Inclusion criteria were: being between 35 and 65 years old, having no established medical conditions (e.g., diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, neural conditions were excluded while people with blood sugar imbalance, abnormal cholesterol levels, slightly elevated blood pressure or obesity were accepted) and not using medication that could impact the epigenetic clock and/or inhibit training response (e.g., metformin and antihistamines). The recruitment targeted individuals with physically inactive lifestyles assessed by daily activity levels, sedentary behavior, and lack of participation in sports. From 151 candidates, 42 were selected, ensuring equal representation of males and females and an approximately even distribution across the age groups: 35-39, 40-44, 45-49, 50-54, 55-59, and 60-65 years.

The final sample included in this study was of 36 participants (Mage = 48,40, SDage = 8,58, 52,78% females). Among the 42 participants who attended the first test day, four participants dropped out of the training program (one due to an injury, one due to lack of motivation, and two due to personal reasons). One participant continued in the study but did not complete the training due to personal reasons, and cardiovascular data from one participant is missing due to problems with the signal. This study followed the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Ghent University and Ghent University Hospital (ONZ-2022-0632). Participants provided informed consent prior to the study.

2.2 Measures and Material

2.2.1 Cardiovascular measures

Cardiovascular variables were recorded and analyzed through the Biopac MP160 system and Biopac Acqknowledge software 4.3 (Biopac Systems Inc., USA). The participants were in semi-Fowler's position with straight knees. Electrocardiography (ECG) was recorded through three electrodes attached under the right and left clavicles and on the left lower rib, at 1000 Hz. Continuous blood pressure was acquired through a CNAP 500® system (CNSystems Medizintechnik AG) at 100 Hz. The cuff and the finger sensor were put on the left arm of the participant. ECG and blood pressure signals were preprossed using custom flexible algorithms to detect R-R intervals and beat-to-beat systolic and diastolic peaks. Possible artefacts were checked by visual inspection and corrected by linear interpolation when present (Reyes del Paso et al., 2010).

As the main outcome of HRV, we used the root mean square of successive R-R interval differences (RMSSD), a time-domain index that is relatively free of respiratory influence (Laborde et al., 2017). As exploratory analyses, we also report the effect of the training on the high-frequency HRV (HF), and the low-frequency HRV (LF), two frequency-domain indices.

The HF and LF indices were computed by means of Fast-Fourier transformations in the frequency ranges of 0.15-0.40Hz and 0.04-0.15Hz by using the Kubios software (Niskanen et al., 2004).

As indices of baroreflex function, we calculated the baroreflex sensitivity (BRS), which measures the magnitude of change in inter-beat interval (IBI) per unit of change in systolic blood pressure (SBP), and the Baroreflex Effectiveness Index (BEI), which indicates the proportion of progressive changes in SBP (SBP ramps) followed by the expected IBI reflex modulation (Di Rienzo et al., 2001; Reyes del Paso et al., 2017). Both BRS and BEI were quantified through the spontaneous sequence method in the time domain. The minimal required cardiac cycles was set at 3, the IBI change threshold at 1 millisecond and the SBP at 1mmHg.

The spontaneous sequence method locates sequences of 3 to 6 consecutive cardiac cycles in which SBP increases ("up" sequences) are accompanied by increases in IBI and those in which SBP decreases ("down" sequences) are accompanied by IBI decreases. Correlation between SBP and IBI values within the sequence has to be greater than .85 to consider it as a reflex sequence. When one of these reflex sequences was detected, a regression line was computed across its SBP-IBI values. BRS is indexed by the slope of the regression line, as the change in IBI per unit of change in SBP (ms/mmHg). The BEI is calculated as the ratio between the number of SBP ramps followed by reflex changes in IBI and the total number of SBP ramps (reflex and non-reflex) (Reyes del Paso et al., 2017).

2.2.2 Psychological and somatic measures

Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item (GAD-7) scale.

The GAD-7 (Donker et al., 2011; Spitzer et al., 2006) consists of 7 items about anxiety symptoms experienced in the last two weeks. Participants answered on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from0 = not at all to3 = almost every day. The total score (between 0 and 21) was calculated by summing all individual items, with higher scores indicating higher levels of anxiety. In the current study, the GAD-7 showed Cronbach's alpha of 0.9, 0.88 and 0.69 for GAD-7 related to T1, T2 and T3, respectively.

Patient

Cognitive Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (CERQ).

The CERQ (Garnefski et al., 2001) consists of 36 items assessing nine cognitive emotion regulation strategies typically used after negative events: self-blame, acceptance, rumination, positive refocusing, refocus on planning, positive reappraisal, putting into perspective, catastrophizing, and blaming others.

Participants responded on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 ((almost) never) to 5 ((almost) always). Scores for each of the nine subscales (between 4 and 20) were computed by averaging the four corresponding items, with higher scores indicating greater use of the respective emotion regulation strategy. In the current study, all subscales showed a Cronbach's alpha of 0.67 or higher for the three evaluation periods.

2.2.3 Handgrip protocol

The grip test (Chirinos et al., 2020) was performed using an E-LINK H500 Hand Kit hand grip dynamometer (Biometrics Ltd., Gwent, UK). First, to determine their maximal strength, the participants gripped twice the hand grip as hard as possible. The highest value was used as a reference value. In a second phase, the participants played an interactive computer game where they had to keep a ball between two lines by gripping the hand grip dynamometer at approximately 25% of their maximal strength. Participants were instructed to not perform Valsalva maneuvers. They were asked to let the experimenter know when they thought they would have difficulty holding out for one more minute. When they did, the experimenter performed a pulse wave velocity measurement. Once it was completed, participants released the hand grip dynamometer. A single experimenter (E.R.R., an experienced cardiologist) performed the handgrip assessment for all the participants.

2.2.4 Training program

To personalize the program intensity, participants performed a VO₂max test during each lab session. The VO₂max test consisted of an incremental step exercise on a cycle ergometer Cyclus 2, RBM Elektronik-automation GmbH, Leipzig, Germany), starting at 60W with an increase of 40 Watt per 5 min for the men and 30 Watt per 5 min for the women. Participants were asked to keep a pace constant and between 70 and 90 rotations per minute and were encouraged during the test to keep going. The test stopped when the participant could no longer bicycle. During the test, pulmonary gas exchange (Cosmed quark cpet system, Omnia Software version 1.6.10, Benelux, Nieuwegein, The Netherlands) and blood lactate concentration (through a capillary blood sample from the earlobe; Lactate Pro 2, Arkray, Kyoto, JAPAN) were recorded. We then computed the VO2max (i.e., highest 30-seconds average VO2), the aerobic threshold (i.e., when the lactate had increased by 0.5 mmol/L from baseline) and the anaerobic threshold (using the Dmax method). Those values were used to determine the training zone of each participant and hence personalize the training program, which was aimed at increasing the VO2max. The training was supervised by our exercise scientists and monitored by personal coaches (from Golazo Energy, https://energy.golazo.com/) during biweekly online meetings with the participants. The training schedule was discussed between the participant and the coach, aiming to have three training sessions, for a total of approximately 3h30 to 7h of sport per week. Participants also received a wearable clock (Garmin Forerunner 255, Garmin Ltd., Olathe, KS, USA) so that their training sessions and some basic health variables such as sleep duration and HRV during the night could be monitored. They also attended three workshops on bike usage, nutrition and sleep.

Following the World Health Organization recommendations (≥150 min/week, ~65 hours over 26 weeks), successful completion was determined at the end of the intervention, before further data analysis, as having more than 66% adherence to the training program based on Garmin-measured training time. Thirty-three participants completed at least 66% of the training. The other three participants completed between 43 and 54% of the training.

Given that the conclusions of the study are the same if those participants completing less than 66% of the training are excluded from the analyses, we report the results with the entire sample.

2.3 Procedure

Participants came to the lab before starting the training program, and at 3 and 6 months after the beginning of the program for an assessment session. Between sessions, they performed the training program independently by cycling at locations of their choice.

Before each assessment session, participants were also asked to complete at-home demographic questionnaires, the GAD-7, the PHQ-9, the PHQ-15, the CERQ and the CD-RISC. If they had not done so, they filled them in upon arrival at the lab. For the three assessment sessions, we asked participants to not change their habits the day before (e.g., eat and sleep during their regular hours, have their usual breakfast/lunch) with three exceptions: they were asked to (1) refrain from physical exercise at least 24h before the appointment, (2) not eat immediately before the session (3) reduce their consumption of alcohol the day before the study and, if possible, not drink alcohol the night before. Physiological data analyzed in this study were collected during the recording of ECG and blood pressure at rest and during a handgrip test. When entering the room, the participants underwent a measurement of carotid artery-sternum-femoral artery and the maximal grip strength test. Then, after placing equipment on the participants and calibrating, we ask them to be still for 5 minutes for the baseline recording (resting period) and then, to perform the handgrip test while the ECG and continuous blood pressure were recorded. The pulse wave velocity measurement was carried out before and after the handgrip test. The same day of the assessment session, participants underwent a body composition determination by DEXA-scan, a medical sport examination, a blood sampling, a V02max test, and a consultation session with a discussion of the results. The data reported in this study correspond to the ECG and continuous blood pressure recorded at rest and during the handgrip test.

2.4 Data analysis

All data were analyzed in R 4.4.2 (R Core Team, 2024) in conjunction with Rstudio 2024.09.1+394. Linear mixed effects regressions (LMER) were used to investigate the effect of training on cardiovascular and psychological variables. For cardiovascular variables (RMSSD, HF, LF, HR, MBP, BRS, and BEI), Time (Baseline, 3 months, and 6 months) and Condition (Resting, Grip Test) were used as fixed factors. For questionnaires (GAD, CD-RISC, PHQ-15, PHQ-9, and CERQ subscales), Time (Baseline, 3 months, and 6 months) was used as a fixed factor. In all these models, we included random intercepts for subjects, and we included the percentage of completed training as covariate. P-values are provided using the Kenward-Roger degrees of freedom approximation. To decompose interaction effects, Tukey adjusted pairwise comparisons were carried out using the 'emmeans' package (Lenth & Lenth, 2018).

LM and LMER were computed using the 'lmerTest' package (Kuznetsova et al., 2017). A Bonferroni correction was used for the analyses with the CERQ subscales, and therefore, only results p<0.006 (0.05/9) were considered statistically significant.

Results reported in Van Damme et al. (2026) demonstrate that the training protocol reduced handgrip strength in this sample. Thus, we repeated the analyses with all the cardiovascular markers including maximal handgrip strength as a control variable. The statistical conclusions of the study remind the same, except for HF. For this HRV index, the factor Condition becomes not significant (p=0.067 instead of p=0.046), although the direction of the effect remains the same.

3.4. Associations between changes in cardiovascular markers and scores in psychological questionnaires.

We used LMER models to investigate whether changes in cardiovascular markers predict changes in psychological factors. As the dependent variable, we included the score on GAD, PHQ9, PHQ15, CDRISC, or the Others-blame subscale from the CERQ. As independent variables, we included Time (3 months, 6 months), change in RMSSD, HR, MBP, or BRS (calculated as the change from baseline to 3 and 6 months), the percentage of training achieved by the participant, and baseline scores on the questionnaires. Given that the results reported above indicate that the effect of training is specific for baseline cardiovascular activity, and not for reactivity (i.e., the factor Time was statistically significant, but the interaction between Time and Condition was not significant in all models), we performed these models including only the resting period.

In all these models, the change in cardiovascular markers did not predict the score on GAD, PHQ9, PHQ15, CDRISC and Self-Blame (all p>0.068). The interaction between Time and change in cardiovascular markers was also not significant (p>0.067). The percentage of training achieved by the participant did not show a significant effect in all these models (all p>0.223).

4. Discussion

In this study we investigated the effect of a personalized supervised six-month endurance PE program on cardiovascular markers of ANS regulation and psychological indices of mental health in a group of sedentary middle-aged individuals. Regarding cardiovascular ANS indices, we observed a decrease in HR and an increase in BRS after three months of training, and the change remained stable after six months of training. The effects of training on HRV (as measured by RMSSD), and MBP were observed only after six months of training, but not yet after three months. Importantly, these effects were specific for tonic autonomic cardiovascular activity, and no change in the cardiovascular reactivity to the handgrip test was observed. Frequency-domain HRV indices (HF and LF) increased after six months of training, but the change was not statistically significant, possibly reflecting their greater variability relative to time-domain measures such as RMSSD. The main effects on cardiovascular markers remain the same even after controlling for changes in handgrip strength. Regarding psychological indices, we observed a decrease in the use of other-blaming ("putting the blame for what you have experienced on the environment or another person"; Garnefski & Kraaij, 2007, pp 141-142) as an emotion regulation strategy after three months of training. However, this change did not stabilize and returned to baseline levels after six months of training. None of the other psychological constructs (self-reported depression symptoms, anxiety, resilience, somatization, and other emotion regulation strategies) changed after three or six months of training. Importantly, our results also showed that the changes in cardiovascular markers of ANS regulation do not translate into psychological improvements.

Our findings demonstrate that a supervised, personalized six-month exercise intervention improved tonic cardiovascular regulation in sedentary middle-aged adults.

Specifically, participants exhibited higher HRV (RMSSD), higher BRS, and lower HR and MBP both at rest and during handgrip performance. However, training did not modify cardiovascular reactivity to the handgrip test. Improvements in BRS were observed for both "up" and "down" sequences, indicating enhanced vagal activation and withdrawal (Martín-Vázquez & Reyes del Paso, 2010; Reyes del Paso et al., 2017). Beyond autonomic regulation, additional analyses in this cohort reported by Van Damme et al. (2026) showed that the exercise program slowed epigenetic aging and improved cardiorespiratory fitness (VO₂max), diet quality, alcohol consumption, and other cardiovascular health markers (nightly HR, nightly RMSSD measured with ambulatory wearables, diastolic blood pressure, and pulse wave velocity). Together, our findings reinforce the role of structured PE programs as an effective strategy for enhancing tonic autonomic regulation (e.g., Debi et al., 2019; Fu & Levine, 2013; Martín-Vázquez & Reyes del Paso, 2010; Navarro-Lomas et al., 2022; Reyes del Paso et al., 2017; Soares-Miranda et al., 2012; Ueno & Moritani, 2003) and physical health, and hence promoting physiological resilience to cardiovascular disorders in adults. It is important to note that improvements in BRS and HR emerged after three months, whereas RMSSD and MBP changed only after six months. This novel result on the temporal pattern of PE-induced changes in ANS indicates that exercise first influences baroreflex functioning, followed by longer-term adaptations in vagal modulation and vascular regulation. Thus, sustained interventions appear necessary to obtain clinically meaningful cardiovascular benefits in sedentary individuals.

Although the personalized physical exercise (PE) training enhanced vagal activity and baroreflex function, two markers of autonomic regulation associated with stress-related mental disorders (Broadley et al., 2005; Chalmers et al., 2014; Perna et al., 2020), it did not affect selfreported depression, anxiety, resilience, or somatization. Changes in autonomic cardiovascular activity were also unrelated to psychological outcomes. The only psychological effect was a reduced tendency to blame others, which was present after three months but not after six months of training. These findings are consistent with a recent systematic review showing small and heterogeneous effects of physical activity interventions on repetitive thinking, a major risk factor for depression and anxiety, in healthy adults (Wang et al., 2025). Although PE improved tonic cardiovascular autonomic activity both at rest and during the handgrip test, it did not alter the magnitude of the phasic response. This suggests that improving tonic autonomic regulation alone may be insufficient to influence mental well-being. Supporting this interpretation, recent large-scale studies found that resting HRV was unrelated to depression and rumination despite being associated with physical activity (Li et al., 2024 (Li et al., , 2026)). Using data from a large middle-aged population study (n=2252) spanning 10 years, Li et al. (2026) demonstrated in cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses that HRV measured at rest is associated with physical activity, with moderate levels showing the strongest effects, but not with depressive symptoms, social support, and work stress. Similarly, Li et al. (2024) observed no cross-sectional association between resting HRV and rumination in a sample of 1189 healthy young adults. Together, our findings indicate that PE-related increases in resting autonomic cardiovascular control do not necessarily lead to improvements on mental health and well-being in sedentary healthy adults, and contradict the link between resting HRV and mental health proposed by the Neurovisceral Integration Model (Thayer and Lane, 2009).

Theoretical and empirical evidence suggests that exaggerated physiological reactions to stressors are associated with reduced mental health and well-being (e.g., Vanderhasselt et al., 2025). In this context, it is possible that PE does not translate into psychological benefits unless autonomic reactivity to stressors is also modified. According to the cross-stressor adaptation hypothesis (Sothmann et al., 1996), PE may improve well-being by enhancing autonomic regulation responses to challenges. However, it is important to note that evidence supporting this hypothesis is generally modest and largely based on cross-sectional research (Forcier et al., 2006; Jackson & Dishman, 2006; Mücke et al., 2018). For example, a recent study in untrained individuals showed that a 6-month aerobic exercise intervention did not alter cardiovascular (heart rate and blood pressure) or endocrine (cortisol and ACTH) responses to acute psychosocial stress compared to a non-training control group (Ardvison et al., 2020).

Notably, both groups exhibited attenuated stress responses over time, suggesting habituation to the stress paradigm. To minimize habituation effects and reduce interindividual variability in emotional responses to psychosocial stress tasks, the present study employed a standardized physical challenge, which activate the autonomic cardiovascular system in a manner similar to psychosocial or emotional stressors (i.e., decrease in parasympathetic control and increased cardiac sympathetic activity) and is less affected by habituation in longitudinal designs. Also, important in the context of this study, previous studies have shown a link between the autonomic response to physical challenges and stress-related disorders such as depression and anxiety (Guinjoan et al., 1997; Udupa et al., 2007; Bylsma et sl., 2015; Asbrand et al., 2017).

Nevertheless, no PE-related effects on autonomic cardiovascular responses were observed.

Although it remains important to determine whether the present intervention influences autonomic responses to psychosocial stress specifically, our findings add to the growing body of evidence suggesting small or null effects of aerobic PE interventions on autonomic cardiovascular reactivity to challenges. Taken together, although PE has been consistently associated with improvements in mental well-being, further research is needed to determine under which conditions PE may strengthen adaptive autonomic responses to challenges and whether such changes mediate its beneficial effects on mental health. For instance, the training program in this study was largely technical and performance-oriented, conducted individually, and emphasized physiological improvement with limited social interaction. Recent theoretical work suggests that social connection and support, along with lifestyle factors (including physical activity) constitute central components of mental well-being and that may influence stress regulation (Vanderhasselt et al., 2025). Accordingly, future research should investigate whether exercise programs that incorporate socially enjoyable and engaging elements may lead to stronger psychological benefits via enhancing stress regulation.

Although this study has multiple strengths, including the length of the training and the closely monitored, objectively personalized PE program, some methodological aspects should be considered when interpreting our results. First, although the selected individuals were physically inactive, which has been associated with lower HRV and poorer mental health, the participation in this study necessitated motivation, physical effort, engagement and regular talk with the coach for a long period. As a result, we may have recruited individuals who, despite being physically inactive, may have greater psychological resources. Future studies should focus on participants who are not only inactive but also show high symptoms of anxiety and depression. Second, we used a pre-post design without a control group. Thus, we cannot exclude the possibility that the effects observed are attributable to other uncontrolled factors.

Nevertheless, the prospective nature of the study provides important evidence that could have not been obtained in cross-sectional designs. Still, future studies comparing PE with a control group are needed to replicate these findings.

In conclusion, our findings show that a 6-months sport training can improve tonic BRS, HR, HRV, and MBP, adding further evidence of its potential as a scalable way to improve tonic cardiovascular ANS activity. However, it also showed no effect on depression, anxiety, or somatic symptoms, resilience and emotion regulation nor did changes in cardiovascular ANS indices predict changes in psychological variables. These null findings therefore do not support theoretical models suggesting that gains in tonic autonomic regulation are associated with improved regulation of stress-related psychological symptoms and mental well-being, asking for more research causally modulating ANS to assess its impact on mental well-being.
