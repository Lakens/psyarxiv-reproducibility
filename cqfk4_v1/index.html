Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

We try to exclude figure and table notes like “* p < .05”, but may not succeed at excluding all false positives.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

This study examines reciprocal relationships between affect and engagement and disengagement with sustainable development in a cohort of 580 Portuguese adolescents assessed serendipitously before COVID-19 (ages 12-13), during lockdown (ages 13-14), and approximately one year after lockdown (ages 13-14). Random-intercept cross-lagged panel modelling (RI-CLPM) distinguished stable between-person differences from within-person fluctuations. Adolescents with higher negative affect generally reported lower engagement and higher disengagement. Within individuals, higher-than-usual negative affect before the pandemic predicted greater subsequent disengagement during lockdown. In turn, higher-thanusual negative affect during lockdown predicted greater subsequent engagement. No crosslagged effects involving positive affect or from engagement or disengagement to affect were statistically significant. The findings suggest that negative affect may precede different responses to sustainable development across contextual conditions. Efforts to promote engagement should therefore consider adolescents' emotional experiences alongside their circumstances, rather than assume that negative affect necessarily undermines engagement or that evoking it will encourage sustainable action.

A Three-Wave RI-CLPM Linking Adolescent Affect and their Engagement with Sustainable Development

Over the past three decades, the constructs of engagement and disengagement -which both broadly capture qualities of an individual's subjective experiences of, and interactions with, aspects of their external environments -have been widely applied to the fields of educational (Fredricks et al., 2004; Reschly & Christenson, 2022; Wang, Degol, et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2024) and organizational psychology (Kahn, 1990; Rich et al., 2010; Shuck et al., 2013) due to their potential to address issues such as work absenteeism (Neuber et al., 2022), school dropout (Fall & Roberts, 2012) and problem behaviors (Wang & Fredricks, 2014). As such, recent years have seen scholars focus their attention on understanding the factors that facilitate engagement and/or protect against disengagement in diverse contexts (What shapes engagement and disengagement?) and their consequences (What are the benefits of being engaged? What are the costs of disengagement?).

This study examines the link between affect, our underlying experience of feeling, emotion and mood (Russell, 2003), and engagement with sustainable development (henceforth ESD). ESD is a relatively recent construct that offers a holistic lens for understanding how people interact with the pressing societal challenge of sustainable development (Moreira, 2021; Moreira et al., 2021 Moreira et al., , 2022)). Understanding the factors that influence ESD -such as potentially affect -is relevant and a topic of importance for achieving international goals (United Nations General Assembly, 2015; World Commission on Environment and Development, 1987) because it has the potential to inform approaches for promoting and maintaining behavioral patterns that are pro sustainable development.

While the specific definition of engagement has been widely debated (Reschly & Christenson, 2012 , 2022; Shuck et al., 2013), it is generally viewed as a broad phenomenon with emotional, cognitive, and behavioral components. These components together capture an individual's experiences of, and interactions with a specific aspect of their external environment (ranging from engagement in specific tasks/goals to engagement in broader contexts and more abstract notions), as expressed in their internal affective and cognitive states, and observable goal -directed actions (Rich et al., 2010; Wang, Degol, et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2024). Findings from various prior studies have shown the three components are highly correlated (Skinner et al., 2008) and load on a general engagement factor (Houle et al., 2022; Moreira et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2016), implying a composite score can be used to examine an individuals' global engagement.

Recently, Moreira et al. incorporated these key features into a theoretical framework for understanding the developmental and relational processes at the foundation of human functioning in response to sustainable development issues (Inman et al., 2024; Moreira et al., 2021 Moreira et al., , 2022)). According to these authors, ESD depends on the occurrence of psychological processes that support adaptive human adaptation and functioning, which can express as adaptive internal affective/emotional and cognitive states, as well as observable behaviors. For example, people scoring high in ESD are more likely to endorse statements such as "I'm concerned for the future of our planet" (emotional), "I believe small actions can lead to big changes in sustainable development" (cognitive), and "I strive to incorporate sustainable practices into my daily life " (behavioral).

Within this framework, ESD was distinguished from the concept of disengagement with sustainable development (henceforth DSD), which also has emotional, cognitive, and behavioral components. Capturing patterns of what might be socially construed as maladaptive human adaptation and functioning towards sustainable development, DSD results from distinct organizations of psychobiological processes that are not present in the mere absence of ESD (Afrahi et al., 2021; Skinner et al., 2009; Wang, Fredricks, et al., 2019). Thus, DSD should not be understood as the polar opposite state to ESD. As distinct dimensions, ESD and DSD can co-occur, with people able to experience mixed states comprised of some amount of both ESD and DSD. The scale used to measure ESD in this study, the Engagement/Disengagement in Sustainable Development Inventory (Moreira et al., 2021), can also be used to measure DSD. People high in DSD are more likely to endorse statements like "I get annoyed by sustainable development issues" (emotional), "I believe that individual actions have little impact on sustainable development" (cognitive), and "I don't actively seek out sustainable options" (behavioral).

Like engagement and disengagement more broadly, a key feature of ESD and DSD is that they are conceptualized as experiential states that emerge from the dynamic processing of information from our external environments and internal states (Inman et al., 2024). Consequently, ESD and DSD are responsive to external factors (that is, they are shaped by a person's interactions with people, objects, and symbols within their experienced contexts), but also dependent on internal subjective experiences comprising coordinated psychological processes with affective, cognitive, motivational and physiological components. While many studies in the extant literature have examined the contextual facilitators of engagement (Moreira & Lee, 2020), there has been far less focus on how internal factors such as psychological traits or statesincluding affective states -influence engagement or disengagement (Wang & Degol, 2014).

Affect refers to feeling states that can differ in their valence (positive/pleasant vs. negative/unpleasant) and level of activation or arousal (Yik et al., 2011 (Yik et al., , 2023)). When these states are experienced consistently over time, they form the foundation of an individual's subjective experience of mood (see Fox et al. 2018). Critically, both negative and positive affect have distinct state-like and trait-like components, with the latter representing relatively stable dispositions for experiencing specific emotional states over time. The potential relevance of affect in understanding the emergence of ESD can be understood through the lens of the broaden-and-build theory of positive emotions (Fredrickson, 2001). Now supported by robust evidence (Fredrickson et al., 2008; Rowe et al., 2007; Vanlessen et al., 2016), this theory suggests that affective states have an impact on a person's attentional and cognitive processing, with negative states narrowing attention and prompting specific behaviors (e.g., 'fight or flight' resulting from fear), and positive states broadening attention and fostering flexibility in responding and creativity (e.g., exploration or play). Over time, the broadening effects of positive affect are believed to contribute to the accumulation of enduring resources, such as personal resilience and social closeness, facilitating adaptive functioning in response to environmental challenges (Fredrickson, 2004; Fredrickson & Branigan, 2005; Fredrickson & Joiner, 2018). Moreover, accumulated resources promoted by positive affect can empower people "to seek out and actively engage in various life events" (Kuiper et al., 2000). Thus, it is possible to formulate the hypothesis that ESD is positively associated with positive affect, both at the within-and between-person levels. While there has not yet been a direct test of this hypothesis, some indirect supporting evidence can be found in two distinct areas of research.

The first is research on engagement in other contexts, such as school (for a review see Wang, Degol, et al., 2019) and work (for a review see Shuck et al., 2017). A two-wave study on school engagement showed that positive affect in high-school students predicted higher future engagement and lower future disengagement, with negative affect showing the opposite pattern (King et al., 2015). Building on this evidence, a cross-lagged panel study of primary school children by Kwok and Fang (2021) found that positive emotions predicted subsequent study engagement on year later. In a study comparing two independent samples of 8 th graders, one responding to a survey before COVID19 and the other responding to the same measures during COVID19, positive affect was found to be more strongly associated with school engagement for the COVID19 sample (de Faria et al., 2023). Using a weekly diary study design in university students, Rodríguez-Muñoz et al. (2021) showed that weekly within-person fluctuations of positive affect were associated with within -person fluctuations of student engagement. A two-wave study on work engagement in a teacher sample also demonstrated similar associations, with positive emotions predicting increased future work engagement, and negative emotions predicting lower work engagement (Burić & Macuka, 2018). Finally, also with a teacher sample, a diary study showed that daily positive affect was positively associated with daily work engagement, at both within-and between-person levels (Muehlbacher et al., 2024).

A second line of evidence indirectly supporting a positive association between ESD and positive affect is from research into psychological concepts that share overlap with ESD (Schneider et al., 2021). For example, a meta-analysis of 30 samples found a weighted positive correlation of r = .22 between positive affect and connection to nature, a construct involving cognitive, affective, and experiential components that is conceptually related to ESD (Capaldi et al., 2014). In a study by Jacobs and McConnell (2022) , specific positive self-transcendent emotions such as awe, love, and compassion predicted pro-environmental beliefs, nature connectedness and self-reported sustainable behaviors (although other positive emotions such as joy and pride did not show this pattern). Finally, research testing structural equation models (SEM) has demonstrated significant positive relationships between positive affect and environmental concern, which is theoretically related to cognitive ESD (Coelho et al., 2017). However, other studies have failed to confirm an association between positive affect and indicators of ESD (Lange & Dewitte, 2019) suggesting that the relationship between affect and ESD is complex (Schneider et al., 2021; Schneider & van der Linden, 2023).

Running counter to the predictions of the broaden-and-build theory, there is mounting evidence that negative affective experiences may also be a motivator of ESD (Brosch, 2021; van Valkengoed & Steg, 2019). For example, an analysis of data from the European Social Survey showed that worry about climate change was an indirect predictor of specific energy-saving behaviors (Bouman et al., 2020). Moreover, experimental research has shown that negative emotions such as guilt, shame and anxiety can support adaptive responses to sustainable development issues (Rees et al., 2015), although in some cases may instead have a paralyzing effect and lead to withdrawal or support denial (Davidson & Kecinski, 2022; Innocenti et al., 2023). Such contrasting findings and predictions highlight the need for further research to fully understand how affect relates to ESD. Further demonstrating the complex relation between affect and ESD, research findings suggest that ESD may itself promote positive affect (Kasser, 2017). One potential mechanism underlying this relationship is that high ESD is intrinsically rewarding. Supporting this hypothesis, experimental research by Taufik et al., (2015) showed that learning one has behaved pro-environmentally elicits a positive emotional response, a response commonly referred to as a "warm glow", and literally increases physical warmth. More recently, studies using consequential behavioral tasks showed pro-environmental behavior predicted stronger subsequent warm-glow experiences even when the acting pro-environmentally was tedious (Zhou et al., 2026) . Complementary evidence from a 10-day experience-sampling study and randomized controlled experiment with adult samples demonstrated that engaging in proenvironmental behavior increased subsequent subjective wellbeing (Prinzing, 2024). Importantly, according to Prinzing, these findings were more consistent with the hypothesis that pro-environmental behavior promotes well-being through the satisfaction of basic psychological needs for competence, autonomy and relatedness than through "warm glow" alone. This possibility is consistent with self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci, 2017) and earlier proposals that sustainable behavior may support autonomy, competence, and relatedness and thereby enhance well -being (Kasser, 2009).

Despite evidence that ESD can promote positive affect, there are also plausible circumstances where ESD may decrease positive affect or increase negative affect. As noted by Venhoeven et al. (2013), certain pro-sustainable behaviors associated with ESD may be less inherently pleasurable and have a hedonic cost compared to nonsustainable alternatives. For instance, individuals may find vegan meals less pleasant than non-vegan options, and turning down the thermostat during winter for energy conservation may be perceived as less pleasant than keeping the house warm. This has contributed to the common perceptions that sustainable living requires personal sacrifice that can pose a threat to overall life quality (Brown & Kasser, 2005). Importantly, however, the affective consequences of ESD may not be uniformly positive or negative, and may simultaneously have rewarding and aversive components. Consistent with this possibility, in a cross-sectional study using the Youth Engagement in Global Sustainability Scale, youth engagement was significantly positively associated with positive affect but essentially unrelated to negative affect (Moreira, 2021). This suggests that higher ESD may be accompanied by greater positive affect without a corresponding reduction in negative affect, which is consistent with the view that positive and negative affect are related but separable dimensions (Larsen et al., 2017; Tellegen et al., 1999).

To summarize, mixed research findings within the available literature suggest the relationship between affect and ESD/DSD is complex (Brosch, 2021; Brosch & Steg, 2021; Schneider et al., 2021). On the one hand, people who experience more positive affect may be better equipped psychologically to engage with the global challenge of sustainable development. On the other hand, negative affect, and particularly negative emotional reactions when confronted with the negative consequences of unsustainable practices, can also drive ESD. In turn, while ESD may lead to positive affective experiences because it is intrinsically rewarding, it may also lead to negative experience if it implies self-sacrifice. However, it remains unclear whether fluctuations in positive and negative affect within adolescents precede subsequent fluctuations in ESD or DSD, or whether these associations operate in the reverse direction.

Understanding these dynamics requires an integrative perspective that considers reciprocal interactions between individual, cultural/social and environmental processes. Social-cognitive theories of personality, for example, conceptualize behavior as emerging dynamically from interactions between psychologically meaningful features of situations and internal cognitive-affective processes, including affect, goals, expectations, and situational appraisals (Cervone, 2023; Mischel, 2009; Mischel & Shoda, 1995). Related perspectives in grounded and situated cognition similarly emphasize that cognition does not occur independently of bodily states and environmental context, but is embedded within ongoing interactions between the person and their surroundings (Barsalou, 2008; Clark, 2013). Importantly, affect is not merely an outcome of these processes: substantial evidence indicates that affective states can themselves influence judgment, interpretation, and even perceptual construal of external stimuli (Clore & Palmer, 2009; Siegel et al., 2018). Predictiveprocessing and interoceptive-inference accounts provide a complementary neurocognitive perspective, proposing that perception and action arise through continual interactions between top-down predictions, sensory information, and representations of internal bodily states (Seth & Friston, 2016). From this perspective, ESD and DSD can be conceptualized as dynamically expressed states that may fluctuate not only with affective experience but also with changes in the broader social and environmental context. The COVID-19 pandemic provides a particularly informative contextual discontinuity in this regard: pre-pandemic, lockdown, and subsequent periods involved markedly different social and environmental conditions that may have altered how adolescents interpreted and responded to sustainability-related challenges.

In a longitudinal study of Portuguese adolescents, Pedras et al. (2024) found that the positive association between pre-pandemic subjective well-being and ESD during lockdown was weaker among adolescents who more strongly understood COVID-19 as a consequence of human-nature interactions. This finding suggests that adolescents' interpretations of a shared crisis may be relevant to the relationship between their internal experiences and ESD. However, this study focused on subjective well-being as a composite of cognitive and emotional indicators, leaving unclear the distinct contributions of positive and negative affect. Moreover, the study ended during lockdown and did not distinguish stable between-person differences from withinperson fluctuations. The present study extends this work using data from the same longitudinal project, including a third assessment approximately one year after the onset of the pandemic. We focus specifically on positive and negative affect as separate dimensions, examining whether each precedes, or follows, changes in ESD and DSD. Using RI-CLPM, we distinguish between-person differences from within-person fluctuations and examine these prospective associations across two successive intervals, without assuming that the pattern observed from before the pandemic to lockdown also characterizes the subsequent interval.

In terms of hypothesized relations between affect and ESD/DSD, at the between-person level prior research led us to anticipate that (1) positive affect would be positively associated with ESD and negatively associated with DSD; and (2) negative affect would be positively associated with DSD and negatively associated with ESD . Given the lack of prior research specifically testing longitudinal associations between affect and ESD or DSD, and given the apparent complexity of their causal associations, we did not make specific predictions regarding within-person lagged effects.

Method Participants

Data are from a longitudinal study on student engagement with sustainable development in Portuguese schools that ran from 2019 to 2021. For this broader study, we recruited participants from approximately 150 middle schools and mixed-grade schools across the Northern, Central, and Lisbon regions. All seventh-grade students from these schools were invited to join the study, ensuring diverse student demographics. In Portugal, the age of entry to 7 th grade is 12 years old. Data from the first two waves of this project were previously analysed by Pedras et al. (2024) ; the present study uses an overlapping sample with observations across all three waves.

The main analytic sample for this specific study consisted of adolescents who completed surveys in the final semester of 7 th (May-June 2019), 8 th (May-June 2020), and 9th grades (May-June 2021) with at least a 75% response rate for all surveys. A total of 580 adolescents (317 girls; 247 boys; 16 missing data) from 29 schools were included based on these criteria. The average age of participants completing the first survey in 2019 was 13.1 years old, reflecting the fact that the survey was administered toward the end of the school year. A few individuals reported a nationality other than Portuguese (Brazilian = 2; Chinese = 1; Dual-nationality = 4). Adolescents were only included in the study if they gave their informed assent and if they provided a consent form signed by a parent or legal guardian.

Measures

Engagement and Disengagement with Sustainable Development

We evaluated ESD and DSD using their respective scales from the Engagement/ Disengagement in Sustainable Development Inventory (EDiSDI) (Moreira et al., 2021). For each item of the EDiSDI, participants rate how true a statement is for them using a Likert scale ranging from 1 (Totally False) to 5 (Totally True). ESD was calculated as the total score across 12 items that measure cognitive (3 items), emotional (4 items), and behavioral indicators (5 items). DSD was calculated as the total score across 15 items that also capture cognitive (8 items), emotional (3 items) and behavioral indicators (4 items).

Psychometric studies support the psychometric adequacy of the EDiSDI and the decision to focus on total ESD and DSD as opposed to specific subscales. For example, the ESD and DSD scales have shown to have excellent internal consistency reliability in multiple samples of Portuguese adults (ωs > .89), and in Portuguese adolescents (ωs > .91) (Moreira et al., 2022). The same study also found evidence of construct validity in Portuguese adolescents, with positive correlations between mean average ESD scores and environmental identity (r = .50) and nature relatedness (r = .45), as well as evidence of scalar measurement invariance between male and female adolescents. Furthermore, confirmatory factor analyses conducted by Moreira et al. (2021) supported modelling ESD and DSD as bifactor structures with dominant general factors.

Positive and Negative Affect

Positive affect (PA) was assessed using the 10 items of the Portuguese Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (Galinha & Pais-Ribeiro, 2005a , 2005b) that capture positive affective states (e.g., "Enthusiastic"). Negative affect (NA) was evaluated using the remaining 10 items which capture negative affective states (e.g., "Nervous"). For all PANAS items, participants are asked to rate the extent to which they have experienced each of these states in the last few weeks from 1 (Very slightly or Not at all) to 5 (Extremely). By specifying this timeframe, the PANAS was evaluating participants' general tendencies to experience positive and negative affect (i.e. more trait-like affect) as opposed to transient states. For each scale, we calculated a total sum score.

Procedures

Surveys were administered to students during school hours by teachers. The surveys included various questionnaires, with the measures relevant to this study included at each time point. The surveys were administered in paper format in 7 th grade (henceforth T1) and online format in 8 th and 9 th grades (henceforth T2 and T3). The change in format was due to the onset of the COVID19 pandemic.

Data Analysis

Analyses were performed using R (R Core Team, 2023) via RStudio (Posit Team, 2024). The number of missing cases was small because the sample was determined on the basis of a >75% response rate. Using the mice package (Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011), we imputed missing data for the study variables using the predictive mean matching method.

Preliminary Analyses

We conducted confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using the lavaan package (Rosseel, 2012) to confirm the theoretical internal structure of each scale at each wave of data collection. To assess model fit we used the Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA). Values of CFI ≥ .95 are considered good and values ≥ .90 are considered acceptable. For RMSEA, values < .08 are considered good and values < .10 are acceptable. Models were tested using the robust maximum likelihood (MLR) estimator. For PA and NA, we tested unidimensional models, allowing for correlated residuals between items presented sequentially in the PANAS. For ESD and DSD we tested second-order models. The results from the separate CFAs showed evidence that the measurement models were generally valid at all the time points (see Table S1).

We used the semTools package (Jorgensen et al., 2021) to test the reliability of factors within these models. For ESD and DSD, we tested second-order factor reliability using the following internal consistency estimates: ω L1, ω partialL1 and ω L2 . For the positive affect and negative affect models, we tested internal consistency reliability of the firstorder factors using α and ω. For all indices we considered values ≥ .70 as being acceptable. All variables at each moment presented adequate estimates of reliability on the basis of this threshold (see Table S2). To test how much observed variability is stable between individuals for each variable, we estimated intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC). High ICC values indicate there is little within -person variation and that most variability is stable between individuals. ICC's for this study were moderateto-low: ESD = .404, DSD = .439, PA = .135 and NA = .145 Finally, the assumption of longitudinal measurement invariance, which is required for longitudinal modelling (Xu et al., 2020), was then assessed for tested models by comparing a series of nested models with increasing parameter restrictions. These models tested configural invariance, weak invariance (constrained loadings), and strong invariance (constrained loadings and intercepts). Models were compared using the ΔCFI ≤ .010 criteria (Cheung & Rensvold, 2002). For the second-order ESD and DSD models there was evidence of strong invariance. For the unidimensional PA and NA models there was evidence of weak invariance, but not strong invariance (see Table S3). However, as argued by Mulder and Hamaker (2021), weak invariance is sufficient to provide a basis to examine longitudinal associations between latent variables.

Main Analyses

We used the lavaan package (Rosseel, 2012) to investigate the reciprocal causal relations between ESD, DSD, PA and NA over time. To determine the most appropriate model for our longitudinal data we first compared several SEM models including a baseline stability model, a cross-lagged panel model (CLPM), and a random-intercept CLPM (RI-CLPM). CLPMs analyze causal processes in longitudinal data but may overlook stable trait-level associations. RI-CLPMs address this concern by incorporating a random intercept to account for time-invariant stability (Hamaker et al., 2015). We specified the RI-CLPM following the online supplement and lavaan code accompanying Mulder and Hamaker (Mulder & Hamaker, 2021). First, for each variable a randomintercept was specified as a latent factor, with each having as its indicators the three repeated observed scores for ESD, DSD, PA and NA affect, respectively. All factorloadings on the random-intercepts were fixed to 1. Second, observed scores within waves were each regressed on a within-person latent variable (within-person residual), also with the factor-loading fixed to 1. Autoregressive and cross-lagged regressive effects between these within-person residuals were freely estimated. In a second ancillary RI-CLPM, we fixed the covariances between residuals of within -person centered variables to equality.

The CLPM is nested within the RI-CLPM, allowing for direct comparisons (Lucas, 2023). Importantly, autoregressive paths have different interpretations in CLPMs and RI-CLPMs. In CLPMs, cross-lagged effects reflect associations between variables over time, whereas in RI-CLPMs, these effects reflect associations among wave-specific deviations from a person's stable-trait level. We interpreted the size of cross-lagged effects according to the thresholds recommended by Orth et al. (Orth et al., 2022):.03 = small effect;.07 = medium effect; and.12 = large effect. Further guided by Orth et al., we used the following thresholds to interpret random intercept correlations (.10 = small;.23 = medium; and.51 = large) and T1 correlations (.07 = small;.16 = medium; and.38 = larger). Because Mardia's test for multivariate normality indicated a violation of the assumption, model fit was evaluated using scaled versions of standard indices and criteria: CFI ≥ .95, RMSEA < .06, and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) < .08. All models were estimated using the MLR estimator. To account for multiplicity arising from the simultaneous testing of parameters within the RI-CLPM and control the probability of making a Type I error, the adjusted Bonferroni 2 procedure proposed by Smith and Cribbie (2013) was applied to establish adjusted α per test (Smith & Cribbie, 2013). For this, all freely estimated parameters were treated as one family of tests.

Between-Person Effects

At the between-person level, a strong negative association was found between trait-level ESD and DSD (r = -.81, 95% CI [-1.00, -0.62], p < .001), indicating adolescents who, on average, reported higher ESD also tended to report lower DSD across the study period. Furthermore, trait-like DSD had a significant positive correlation with trait-like NA (r = .46, 95% CI [0.25, 0.68], p < .001) and a significant negative correlation with trait-like PA (r = -.39, 95% CI [-0.70, -0.08], p = .015), indicating adolescents who, on average, reported lower affective wellbeing also tended to report increased DSD across the study. Trait-like ESD was found to have a significant negative association with trait-like NA, (r = -.47, 95% CI [-0.70, -0.23], p < .001). Trait-like ESD had a positive association with trait-like PA (r = .32, 95% CI [0.13, 0.51]), but this was not judged statistically significant using the adjusted α per test.

Within-Person Effects

At the within-person level, all but one of the autoregressive effects were nonsignificant, suggesting that momentary state-like fluctuations in the variables did not tend to persist from one year to the next. The exception was the autoregressive path for DSD from T2 to T3 was positive and significant (β = 0.32, 95% CI [0.17, 0.47], p < .001). This indicates a "carry-over" effect, where a momentary increase in DSD above one's personal average during the first months of COVID was followed by a significant increase above average approximately 1-year post-pandemic. Regarding cross-lagged effects, a significant positive path was found from NA at T1 to DSD at T2 (β = 0.21, 95% CI [0.08, 0.35], p = .002), indicating that momentary increases in NA above one's average level pre-pandemic predicted a subsequent increase in DSD in the first month of the pandemic. In contrast, a significant positive cross-lagged path was found from NA at T2 to ESD at T3 (β = 0.39, 95% CI [0.21, 0.57], p < .001), indicating that momentary increases in NA above one's average at the start of the pandemic predicted a subsequent increase in ESD.

Discussion

The objective of this longitudinal study was to examine the reciprocal relationships between human affect and ESD/DSD over time. To achieve this objective, we analyzed data from the same sample of adolescents over three years. Serendipitously, data collection at T1 occurred before the COVID -19 pandemic, data collection at T2 occurred during an early lockdown period of the COVID -19 pandemic, and data collection at T3 occurred approximately one year after the onset of the pandemic. As such, each data collection moment coincided with markedly different social contexts. Using a RI-CLPM approach, we were able to separate between-person differences from within-person processes; that is, whether changes in adolescents' affect or ESD/DSD relative to their own typical level predicted subsequent changes in other constructs. Importantly, because these within-person fluctuations occurred across data collection moments characterized by substantially different social and environmental conditions, their interpretation should also be considered in relation to the broader context in which they emerged. This considered, we focus our discussion on results concerning the central research question of this article (How do affect and ESD influence one another over time?) before discussing study strengths and limitations.

A first finding concerned the direction of the longitudinal relationship between affect and ESD/DSD. Overall, the within-person results suggested that affect more consistently preceded subsequent changes in ESD/DSD than ESD/DSD preceded changes in affect. This pattern is consistent with emerging longitudinal evidence showing that within-person increases in positive and negative affect can prospectively predict subsequent environmentally relevant behavior, whereas reciprocal effects from green behavior to later affect are often weaker or absent over longer measurement intervals (Stein et al., 2025 (Stein et al., , 2026)). Such findings are compatible with theoretical perspectives that position affect as an antecedent of cognition and action: positive affect may broaden cognitive and behavioral repertoires and support adaptive, futureoriented behavior, whereas negative affect can narrow attention toward more immediate concerns (Fredrickson, 2001).

A second major finding emerged when specifically examining prospective relationships from pre-pandemic (T1) to pandemic lockdown (T2). The analysis showed that within-person deviations in negative affect at T1 were positively and significantly related to subsequent within-person deviations in DSD at T2. Put more simply, adolescents who experienced higher negative affect than was typical for them at T1 subsequently reported higher-than-expected levels of DSD during the pandemic. The model also presented tentative evidence that adolescents experiencing higher-thanexpected positive affect at T1 subsequently reported higher-than-expected ESD during the pandemic, although this effect did not remain statistically significant under the adjusted α level used to control familywise Type I error.

One plausible interpretation of these findings, as we hypothesized in the introduction, is that adolescents' affective functioning prior to the pandemic shaped their capacity to respond adaptively when confronted with the substantial disruption and uncertainty that followed. This interpretation is consistent with broaden -and-build theory, according to which positive emotional experiences broaden cognitive and behavioral repertoires and, over time, contribute to the development of enduring psychological and coping resources that can subsequently be drawn upon during adversity (Fredrickson, 2001) . Prospective studies support this resource-building function of positive affect: positive emotions have been shown to predict subsequent increases in broad-minded coping and psychological resilience (Cohn et al., 2009; Fredrickson & Joiner, 2002) , while research conducted around major unforeseen crises suggests that such pre-existing affective resources can contribute to more resilient responses when adversity occurs (Fredrickson et al., 2003).

Evidence from environmental psychology provides more specific support for this interpretation. Recent longitudinal research has found that positive affect predicts greater subsequent pro-environmental behavior, whereas negative affect can predict reduced subsequent engagement. For example, higher-than-usual positive affect has been shown to predict subsequent increases in proactive and citizenship green behavior in multi-wave RI-CLPM studies (Stein et al., 2026), while anticipated positive affect has similarly been found to predict greater subsequent engagement in proenvironmental behavior (Strömbäck et al., 2025) . Particularly relevant to the present study, Stein et al. (2026) also found that within-person increases in negative affect predicted subsequent increases in counterproductive green behavior. Thus, although the present study assessed general rather than environmentally specific affect, its within-person findings converge with emerging evidence that elevated negative affect can, under some circumstances, precede environmental disengagement.

A third major finding emerged when examining prospective relationships from pandemic lockdown period (T2) to approximately 1-year later (T3). Within-person deviations in negative affect at T2 were positively and significantly related to subsequent within-person deviations in ESD at T3. Thus, adolescents who experienced higher negative affect than was typical for them in the first months of the pandemic subsequently reported higher-than-expected levels of ESD. The model also provided tentative evidence that higher-than-usual positive affect at T2 was associated with greater subsequent DSD, although this effect did not remain statistically significant under the adjusted α level used to control familywise Type I error. Taken together, these findings point to a prospective role for elevated negative affect and raise the possibility that comparatively poorer affective well-being during this particular period of global crisis, may, under some circumstances, have contributed to later ESD.

Although this finding may be interpreted as contradicting our initial expectation that positive affect would facilitate ESD through processes such as 'broaden-and-build' (Fredrickson, 2001), growing evidence suggests that negative affective experiences may also be a driver of ESD. Recent research among adolescents, for example, has found positive associations between climate anxiety and pro-environmental behavior (Becht et al., 2024), while meta-analytic evidence indicates that climate anxiety, worry, guilt, anger and other forms of negative climate-related affect are frequently associated with stronger pro-environmental intentions and behavior (Kühner et al., 2025). Importantly, these relationships appear to depend partly on how such emotions are appraised and regulated: negative affect is more likely to translate into constructive engagement when individuals perceive opportunities for effective action or use problem-or meaning-focused coping strategies (Kovács et al., 2024; Veijonaho et al., 2025).

Experimental research has demonstrated that confrontation with human-caused environmental damage can evoke negative emotional reactions, including anger and guilt (Harth et al., 2013; Rees et al., 2015). This may be particularly relevant to the context in which the present within-person effect emerged. Evidence indicates the first months of the COVID-19 pandemic were characterized by substantial disruption and negative emotional experiences among young people. For example, Lawrance et al. (2022) found that young people associated the pandemic particularly with anxiety, frustration, isolation, disconnection, loss, and concern about their future. Importantly, there is also empirical evidence that experiences of the pandemic became linked to environmental concerns and sustainability-related responses. Across samples from the United Kingdom and United States, Shulman et al. (2022) found that greater personal experience with COVID-19 was associated with stronger environmental concern and pro-environmental behavioral intentions, with environmental concern statistically mediating the latter relationship. Similarly, Daryanto et al. (Daryanto et al., 2022) found that individuals who more strongly attributed the pandemic to excessive human intrusion into nature reported greater environmental awareness, which was in turn associated with more pro-environmental behavior. The earlier analysis of this project similarly found that pre-pandemic subjective well-being was less strongly associated with ESD during lockdown among adolescents who more strongly attributed COVID -19 to human-nature interactions (Pedras et al., 2024). Although based on overlapping data, this finding identifies adolescents' interpretations of the pandemic as a possible consideration when explaining the present results. Negative affect during lockdown may have accompanied concerns that encouraged subsequent ESD, although the present analysis did not test these appraisals or their role in the observed association.

Strengths and Limitations

The longitudinal design of this study, and the relatively large sample sourced from multiple schools across Portugal increases confidence of the internal validity of the results. Confidence in the findings, and particularly within-person inferences, is also enhanced by our finding of strong scale reliability at each moment, moderate-to-low ICCs, the conservative adjusted alpha to control for Type I error, and the observation of at least weak longitudinal measurement invariance across variables. Nonetheless, we acknowledge the study has several limitations, which we shall consider in turn.

First, we acknowledge that in the pursuit of testing specific associations and with the aim to having parsimonious and easy-to-interpret models we did not consider demographic variables (e.g. sex) and therefore their potential confounding effects in the analyses. Future studies could consider incorporating such variables into the CLPM and RI-CLPM analyses to test whether they have impact on the cross-lagged effects reported in this study. Another limitation related specifically to the CLPM and RI-CLPM analyses was that we did not incorporate measurement models. Estimating measurement models for each wave-specific assessment of affect and ESD/DSD may provide more robust estimates of the cross-lagged effects, and therefore should be considered in future studies (although we caution that the large number of model parameters would necessitate a larger sample).

Second, it is important to consider aspects of the study design that can impact on the generalizability of the study results to the target population, which for this study was Portuguese adolescents. The analytic sample included only adolescents who participated at all three assessments and met the response-completeness criterion.

Selective retention may therefore have influenced the observed associations, limiting their generalizability to the original cohort. We also note that the sample comprised adolescents enrolled in just one academic year. As such, it is unclear whether the same findings would emerge if adolescents from a different academic year had also been followed.

Thirdly, the availability of a longitudinal dataset tracking adolescents from preto post-COVID19 serendipitously provided an opportunity to understand how affective experiences during the first months of the pandemic, when a global crisis linked to unsustainable human actions was undeniably very salient, shaped people's experiences and interactions with the topic of sustainable development. However, this specific historical context may mean that our findings, and thus our conclusions about the longitudinal associations between affect and ESD/DSD may not generalize to more normative circumstances. We also note that the pandemic required different data collection methods to be used at T1 (paper surveys in classrooms) and T2/T3 (online surveys during online classes), which could contribute to variations in the observed relationships and should be considered when interpreting the results.

Conclusion

Overall, this longitudinal RI-CLPM study shows that affective experiences may play a role in shaping people's interactions with, and experiences of, sustainable development, and therefore are an important factor to consider in efforts to promote ESD and/or reduce DSD. More specifically, the study showed that when adolescents experienced higher negative affect than was typical for them during the COVID lockdown, this was followed by increases in subsequent ESD. Thus, negative affect during an environmental or social crises, although unpleasant, may in some circumstances motivate adaptive responses. At the same time, the study also showed that when adolescents experienced higher negative affect than expected before COVID, this was followed by increases in DSD during lockdown. Thus, the study seems to suggest that whether elevated negative affect precedes ESD or DSD depends on the context in which it is experienced. Thus, aligning with Schneider et al. (Schneider et al., 2021), we caution against rushing to apply interventions that seek to promote ESD by evoking emotions, as the outcomes may vary as a function on when the intervention is applied.
