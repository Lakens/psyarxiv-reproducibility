Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Abstract

Callous-unemotional (CU) traits reflect low empathy, guilt, and concern for others and are associated with persistent antisocial behavior, aggression, and delinquency. Although CU traits vary across adolescence, less is known about factors predicting increases versus decreases in severity. Using four annual assessments spanning early to middle adolescence from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Social Development (ABCD-SD) Study, we characterized CU trajectories using latent class growth analysis and examined changes in CU severity between annual assessments. Logistic regression tested whether parenting and emotion dysregulation prospectively distinguished shifts in CU severity among adolescents who moved between categories. Three developmental trajectories emerged: Low (46.9%), Moderate (40.1%), and Elevated (13.0%). Among adolescents beginning at moderate severity, greater positive parenting was associated with movement to low rather than elevated CU severity (OR = 0.69, p < .001), whereas greater negative parenting (OR = 1.21, p = .024) and emotion dysregulation (OR = 1.18, p = .036) were associated with movement to elevated rather than low severity. Among adolescents beginning at low severity, greater positive parenting was associated with movement to moderate rather than directly to elevated severity (OR = 0.82, p = .045), whereas greater emotion dysregulation was associated with movement directly to elevated rather than moderate severity (OR = 1.37, p = .003). These findings demonstrate that CU trajectories can remain relatively stable at the group level even as adolescents show year-to-year shifts in CU severity, and identify parenting and emotion dysregulation as prospective predictors and potentially modifiable targets for reducing CU traits.

Introduction

Callous-unemotional (CU) traits are characterized by limited empathy and guilt, reduced concern for others, and shallow or diminished emotional expression (Frick et al., 2014). Elevated CU traits have also been longitudinally associated with aggression, delinquency, violence, substance use, and adverse social and functional outcomes (Baskin-Sommers et al., 2015; Fontaine et al., 2011; McMahon et al., 2010). Despite their association with persistent behavioral difficulties, CU traits are not uniformly stable across adolescence; youth differ in both their overall levels of CU traits and how these traits change over time (Fanti et al., 2017; Fontaine et al., 2011; Simmons et al., 2024). Person-centered longitudinal studies have captured these differences by classifying youth according to their patterns of CU traits across multiple assessments. Research spanning adolescence and early adulthood has identified relatively stable low, moderate, and high CU trajectories (Baskin-Sommers et al., 2015), whereas studies beginning earlier in development have identified more heterogeneous developmental patterns, including increasing and decreasing patterns of CU traits alone or jointly with conduct problems (Fanti et al., 2017; Fontaine et al., 2011). However, because trajectory approaches summarize each youth's overall pattern across assessments (Baskin-Sommers et al., 2015; Fanti et al., 2017; Simmons et al., 2024), they can obscure shifts between severity levels that some youth experience along the way. Consequently, less is known about the frequency and predictors of shifts in CU severity.

Parenting is one factor that may contribute to adolescents' shifts in CU severity. A substantial literature has linked dimensions of parenting, including warmth, involvement, harshness, monitoring, and discipline, to CU traits across childhood and adolescence (Waller et al., 2013; Waller & Hyde, 2017). Longitudinal studies have linked harsh parenting with higher subsequent CU traits, whereas greater parental warmth and positive parenting have been associated with lower subsequent CU traits (Waller et al., 2012 (Waller et al., , 2014)). Lower parental involvement, monitoring, and knowledge have also been associated with more elevated developmental trajectories (Waller, Baskin-Sommers, & Hyde, 2018). Genetically informed research has further examined the extent to which associations between parenting and CU traits reflect environmental versus genetic influences. Research with monozygotic twins has linked greater parental warmth with lower CU traits and greater harshness with higher CU traits beyond genetically mediated effects (Waller, Hyde, et al., 2018), although more recent genetically informed longitudinal work suggests that effects of negative parenting on CU traits may be small and partially attributable to genetic and shared environmental influences (Pezzoli et al., 2024).

Parenting has also been associated with developmental change in CU traits: changes in maternal involvement have accompanied increases and decreases in CU traits during childhood (Fanti et al., 2017), and negative parenting has been associated with within-person increases in CU traits during adolescence (Simmons et al., 2024). Together, this literature suggests that parenting is associated not only with adolescents' levels of CU traits, but also with their developmental course. Although prior research has linked parenting to CU severity and developmental change, less is known about whether parenting distinguishes adolescents whose CU severity increases from those whose severity decreases.

Emotion dysregulation may also contribute to changes in CU traits across adolescence.

Emotion dysregulation is characterized by difficulties in emotional awareness and clarity, impulse control, goal-directed behavior when distressed, acceptance of emotional experiences, and access to effective regulation strategies (Gratz & Roemer, 2004). These difficulties have been linked to multiple forms of child and adolescent psychopathology, including aggression, impulsivity, and conduct problems (Paulus et al., 2021). Although CU traits have historically been associated with reduced emotional responsiveness, youth with elevated CU traits vary considerably in their emotional functioning, and emotion dysregulation is evident among at least some youth with elevated CU traits (Craig & Moretti, 2019; Craig et al., 2021). Recent prospective evidence also suggests that emotion regulation difficulties are associated with subsequent CU traits during childhood (Wilke et al., 2026). However, longitudinal research examining whether emotion dysregulation predicts developmental change in CU traits remains limited, particularly during adolescence. Therefore, as with parenting, it remains unclear whether emotion dysregulation distinguishes how adolescents' CU severity changes over time.

The present study addresses these gaps using longitudinal data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Social Development (ABCD-SD) Study. We examine three questions: (1) What developmental trajectories of CU traits emerge across adolescence?

( 2) To what extent do adolescents remain within the same CU severity level over time? and (3) Among adolescents who shift between CU severity levels, do parenting and emotion dysregulation predict the direction or magnitude of these shifts?

Based on prior trajectory research, we first hypothesized that low, moderate, and elevated CU trajectories would emerge and would differ primarily in overall CU severity rather than rates of change (Baskin-Sommers et al., 2015). Our second question, concerning the extent to which adolescents remained at stable CU severity over time, was exploratory. Third, based on longitudinal evidence linking dimensions of parenting to subsequent CU traits (Fanti et al., 2017; Simmons et al., 2024; Waller et al., 2013; Waller, Baskin-Sommers, & Hyde, 2018) and prospective evidence linking emotion regulation difficulties to subsequent CU traits (Wilke et al., 2026), we hypothesized that greater positive parenting would predict movement toward relatively lower severity, whereas greater negative parenting and emotion dysregulation would predict movement toward relatively higher severity among adolescents whose CU severity changed. (Garavan et al., 2018). The ABCD-SD Study includes approximately annual assessments of delinquency, victimization, personality features, and related social-development constructs (Brislin et al., 2025). The present study focused on four annual assessments of callous-unemotional (CU) traits collected during ABCD-SD waves S02-S05. These waves were selected because they provided repeated assessments of CU traits spanning early to middle adolescence and allowed for both longitudinal trajectory analyses and examination of wave-to-wave transitions in CU severity. The ABCD Study received centralized institutional review board approval from the University of California, San Diego, and participating study sites received local institutional review board approval. Informed consent and assent were obtained in accordance with study procedures.

Methods

Participants

Across S02-S05, CU data were available for 2,297 youth, who contributed data at between one and four waves. Age data from linked core ABCD assessments were available for 2,288 participants (99.6%). At the earliest available corresponding core ABCD assessment, participants' mean age was 12.02 years (SD = 0.70). Among participants with available sex data, 47.9% were female. Additional demographic characteristics, including race/ethnicity, household income, highest caregiver education, and caregiver partnership/employment status, are presented in Online Resource 1, Table S1. Comparisons between adolescents included in and excluded from the LCGA, using the earliest available nonmissing demographic observations from the core ABCD dataset, revealed statistically significant differences in recorded age, household income, highest caregiver education, and caregiver partnership/employment status. Excluded adolescents were slightly older and were more likely to come from households with lower incomes and lower caregiver educational attainment. Effect sizes were generally small to modest. No statistically significant differences were observed in sex or race/ethnicity (see Online Resource 1, Table S1).

For the transition analyses, transitions were defined using adjacent assessments (S02→S03, S03→S04, and S04→S05). This approach ensured that observed changes in CU severity were localized to the period between consecutive assessments and that predictors were measured before the subsequent CU severity category was assessed. Comparing nonadjacent assessments could obscure intermediate shifts in CU severity and make the timing of change less clear. Across the 2,297 participants included in the trajectory analyses, 4,643 adjacent assessment intervals were available from 2,003 youth (87.2% of the trajectory sample); the remaining youth did not have CU data at two consecutive assessment waves and therefore could not contribute an adjacent assessment interval. Of these 4,643 adjacent assessment intervals, 2,719 (58.6%) showed stability within the same CU severity category, whereas 1,924 (41.4%) showed movement between categories. Because our aim was to examine shifts in CU severity, primary analyses included only assessment intervals during which adolescents moved between CU severity categories. Thirty-six transition intervals (1.9%) were excluded because age or sex information from the core ABCD dataset was unavailable, resulting in a final analytic sample of 1,888 transition intervals contributed by 1,254 unique youth. Because participants could contribute transitions across multiple pairs of adjacent assessments, the number of transition intervals exceeded the number of unique participants.

Callous-Unemotional Traits

Callous-unemotional (CU) traits were assessed using the 15-item subset of the youthreport Inventory of Callous-Unemotional Traits (ICU) administered as part of the ABCD-SD Personality Disposition assessment. Items were rated on a 4-point scale ranging from 0 (Does not apply at all) to 3 (Applies very well). Seven positively worded items were reverse coded so that higher scores consistently reflected greater CU traits. Item responses were summed to create a total score with a possible range of 0 to 45 for complete assessments. Total scores were computed when at least 8 of the 15 items were completed. Item-level missingness was minimal: 7,263 of 7,264 ICU assessments (99.99%) contained complete item responses, and the remaining assessment contained 14 of 15 responses and therefore met the prespecified scoring criterion. No item-level imputation was performed. Higher total scores indicated greater CU traits. The full ICU has demonstrated acceptable reliability and construct validity across adolescent samples (Essau et al., 2006; Roose et al., 2010), with meta-analytic evidence supporting the reliability and external validity of the total score (Cardinale & Marsh, 2020).

Parenting

Parenting practices were assessed using the youth-report Alabama Parenting Questionnaire (APQ). In the ABCD-SD data, items were coded from 0 (Never) to 4 (Always).

Two composite scores were derived for the present analyses. Positive parenting was calculated as the mean of 11 items assessing parental warmth, involvement, and positive parenting behaviors.

Negative parenting was calculated as the mean of 16 items assessing negative parenting practices, including poor monitoring and supervision, inconsistent discipline, and punitive parenting behaviors. Higher mean scores reflected greater endorsement of the corresponding parenting dimension. Composite scores were calculated when participants completed at least five items contributing to the relevant scale. Among the 1,888 transition intervals included in the primary analyses, all positive-parenting assessments contained complete item responses.

Negative-parenting assessments were complete for 1,887 intervals, and the remaining interval contained 14 of 16 item responses and therefore met the prespecified scoring criterion. No itemlevel imputation was performed. The APQ has demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity in community samples of children and adolescents, with evidence supporting its factor structure and measurement across youth-and parent-report forms (Elgar et al., 2007; Florean et al., 2023; Russell et al., 2016).

Emotion Dysregulation

Emotion dysregulation was assessed using the 36-item youth-report Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS). In the ABCD-SD data, responses were coded from 0 to 4, with higher values indicating greater frequency. Eleven positively worded items were reverse coded so that higher scores consistently reflected greater emotion dysregulation. Item responses were averaged to create a total mean score. Scores were computed when participants completed at least 10 of the 36 items. All DERS assessments contained complete item responses, including those corresponding to the 1,888 transition intervals included in the primary analyses; no itemlevel imputation was performed. Although the DERS assesses multiple facets of emotion regulation-including emotional identification, emotional clarity, impulse control, goal-directed behavior when distressed, and acceptance of emotional experiences-the present study examined only the overall emotion dysregulation score. Higher scores reflected greater overall difficulties with emotion regulation. The DERS has demonstrated good reliability and construct validity in adolescent community samples (Neumann et al., 2010), and its factor structure has been examined specifically among early adolescents in ABCD-SD (Smith et al., 2024).

Delinquency

Delinquent behavior was included in sensitivity analyses to evaluate whether associations observed in the primary transition models remained after adjustment for prior or concurrent delinquency (see Sensitivity Analyses). Delinquent behavior was assessed using the 48-item youth-report Self-Reported Delinquency Scale (SRD). Items assessed engagement in a range of delinquent behaviors and were rated on a 3-point scale, with higher values indicating greater involvement in delinquent behavior. Item responses were averaged to create a mean delinquency score. Scores were computed when participants completed at least 20 of the 48 items. Higher scores reflected greater overall involvement in delinquent behavior. Fewer than 0.5% of SRD assessments failed to meet the prespecified scoring criterion. Among the 1,888 transition intervals included in the primary analyses, valid baseline and wave-matched delinquency scores were available for all intervals; no item-level imputation was performed. Self-report delinquency measures developed within the National Youth Survey have been extensively evaluated with respect to reliability and validity, including comparisons with official records of offending (Elliott et al., 1985; Huizinga & Elliott, 1986). For the sensitivity analyses, delinquent behavior was operationalized in two ways. Baseline delinquency was derived from the lifetime delinquency assessment administered at ABCD-SD wave S01, whereas wave-matched delinquency was derived from the delinquency assessment collected at the beginning of each transition interval.

Demographic Covariates

Age and sex were obtained from the core ABCD dataset and linked to ABCD-SD observations by participant identifier and corresponding assessment wave. Because core ABCD and ABCD-SD assessments were not necessarily conducted concurrently, age from the corresponding core assessment served as an approximation of age at the ABCD-SD assessment. Both variables were included as covariates in all primary transition and sensitivity regression models. Age was treated as a continuous, mean-centered variable, and sex was coded as female or male. Race/ethnicity, household income, highest caregiver education, and caregiver partnership/employment status were examined descriptively to characterize the analytic sample.

Characterization of CU Development

Latent class growth analysis (LCGA) was used to identify patterns of longitudinal CU development across adolescence. Models were estimated in R using the flexmix package (Grün & Leisch, 2008), with model specification informed by the LCGA implementation described by Wardenaar (2022). Linear trajectories of CU traits were modeled as a function of assessment wave, with class-specific intercepts and linear slopes estimated while residual variance was constrained to equality across classes. One-, two-, three-, and four-class solutions were estimated using all available CU assessments, allowing participants to contribute differing numbers of assessment waves and resulting in an unbalanced longitudinal design. Models used 50 random starts and a maximum of 500 iterations to reduce local maxima. The Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and integrated completed likelihood (ICL) were used to compare competing models, with lower values indicating better fit. Solutions containing classes representing fewer than 5% of the sample were considered less favorable for retention because of concerns regarding class size and interpretability. The final model was selected based on the combined consideration of statistical fit, class size, parsimony, and substantive interpretability. Participants were assigned to the trajectory corresponding to their highest posterior probability of class membership. Differences in parenting and emotion dysregulation across the retained CU trajectory groups were examined using linear mixed-effects models with trajectory group and assessment wave as fixed effects and participant as a random intercept. Tukey-adjusted pairwise comparisons of estimated marginal means, averaged across assessment waves, were used to compare trajectory groups.

Transition Analyses

Transition analyses examined within-person movement between relative levels of CU severity from one assessment to the next. Observed Inventory of Callous-Unemotional Traits (ICU) scores at each assessment were categorized into low, moderate, and elevated CU severity using empirically derived cut points based on the pooled distribution of ICU scores across waves S02-S05. Because validated clinical cutoffs are not available for the abbreviated ICU administered in the ABCD-SD Study, percentile-based cut points were used to define relative levels of CU severity while maintaining adequate observations within each category for stable estimation. Scores ≤6 were classified as low severity, scores from 7 to 11 as moderate severity, and scores ≥12 as elevated severity. These observed severity categories were conceptually distinct from the latent trajectory classes identified by the LCGA and were used solely to characterize movement between adjacent assessments. Accordingly, transition categories were not derived from or conditioned on LCGA trajectory membership. Transition observations were created by pairing adjacent assessments (S02→S03, S03→S04, and S04→S05).

Separate logistic regression models were estimated using assessment intervals in which adolescents changed CU severity categories, stratified by CU severity at the beginning of each interval. Specifically, models compared transitions from low to elevated versus moderate severity, from moderate to elevated versus low severity, and from elevated to low versus moderate severity. Positive parenting, negative parenting, and emotion dysregulation measured at the beginning of each interval were entered simultaneously as predictors of subsequent CU severity. All models were adjusted for age and sex, as described above.

Because some transition types became infrequent when analyses were further stratified by assessment interval, particularly among adolescents beginning at elevated CU severity, transition intervals were pooled across adjacent assessment intervals for the primary analyses.

Pooling increased statistical stability while allowing estimation of overall associations between beginning-of-interval predictors and subsequent CU severity across adolescence. Cluster-robust standard errors were used to account for multiple transition intervals contributed by the same participant. Positive parenting, negative parenting, and emotion dysregulation were standardized using the pooled transition sample before estimating the primary and interval-specific models.

Delinquency variables were standardized for sensitivity analyses, and age was mean-centered.

Accordingly, odds ratios for standardized continuous predictors represent the multiplicative change in the odds of the modeled transition associated with a one-standard-deviation increase in the predictor, holding other variables constant. Odds ratios for age represent the corresponding change associated with a one-year increase, whereas odds ratios for sex represent the comparison between the coded categories. Interval-specific models were subsequently estimated as sensitivity analyses to evaluate whether associations were consistent across assessment intervals (see Online Resource 1, Tables S4-S5).

Sensitivity Analyses

Interval-specific sensitivity analyses evaluated whether associations from the primary transition models were consistent across assessment periods by re-estimating the logistic regression models separately within each interval. Because statistical evidence for an association between emotion dysregulation and transition outcomes was weaker during the final assessment interval, supplementary resampling analyses evaluated the extent to which interval-specific differences in sample size may have contributed to this pattern (see Online Resource 1, Table S6). Additional sensitivity analyses evaluated whether the primary associations remained after adjustment for delinquent behavior. Separate models adjusted for baseline delinquency measured at ABCD-SD wave S01 and wave-matched delinquency measured at the beginning of each transition interval (see Online Resource 1, Tables S7-S8).

Software

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.5.2 (R Core Team, 2025). Latent class growth analyses were estimated using the flexmix package. Data management and descriptive analyses were conducted primarily using packages within the tidyverse; cluster-robust covariance matrices and associated coefficient tests were obtained using the sandwich and lmtest packages, respectively.

Discussion

The present study examined patterns of callous-unemotional (CU) trait development across adolescence by integrating person-centered trajectory analyses with wave-to-wave transitions between observed CU severity categories. Latent class growth analyses identified three distinct developmental trajectories characterized by low, moderate, and elevated CU traits that remained differentiated across adolescence, with differences between groups primarily reflecting overall CU levels rather than rates of change. However, substantial movement between CU severity categories occurred across successive assessments. Among adolescents who transitioned, greater positive parenting was associated with movement toward lower severity among moderate-start adolescents and with smaller increases among low-start adolescents.

Greater negative parenting was associated with movement toward higher severity among moderate-start adolescents. Greater emotion dysregulation was associated with movement toward higher severity among moderate-start adolescents and with larger increases among lowstart adolescents. Together, these findings show that relatively stable developmental trajectories can occur alongside substantial changes in CU severity across adolescence, and that the factors associated with changes in CU severity depend on adolescents' starting level of CU severity.

Characterizing CU Development Across Adolescence

All three trajectory groups demonstrated modest increases in CU traits across adolescence, with differences between groups primarily reflecting their overall levels of CU traits rather than pronounced differences in rates of change. This pattern resembles findings from justice-involved males, among whom relatively stable low, moderate, and high CU trajectories were identified across adolescence and early adulthood (Baskin-Sommers et al., 2015). In contrast, studies beginning earlier in development have identified stable-high, increasing, decreasing, and low trajectories (Fanti et al., 2017). Other work has identified increasing joint profiles of CU traits and conduct disorder symptoms, suggesting that observed developmental patterns may also depend on whether CU traits are examined independently or alongside other externalizing behaviors (Eisenbarth et al., 2016). Collectively, these findings suggest that the number and shape of CU trajectories may vary according to developmental period, sample characteristics, measurement approach, and analytic strategy. Thus, the absence of pronounced increasing or decreasing trajectories in the present study should be interpreted as characterizing patterns of CU development in this community sample, rather than evidence that such trajectories do not occur.

Parenting and Movement Across CU Severity Levels

The parenting findings support a distinction between positive and negative dimensions of the caregiving environment in CU development. Greater positive parenting predicted movement toward lower severity among moderate-start adolescents and movement to moderate rather than elevated severity among low-start adolescents, whereas greater negative parenting predicted movement toward higher severity among moderate-start adolescents. Although these associations emerged across different starting levels, their direction was consistent with longitudinal evidence linking warm, positive parenting to lower subsequent CU traits and harsh or unsupportive parenting to higher CU traits (Waller et al., 2013 (Waller et al., , 2014;; Waller, Baskin-Sommers, & Hyde, 2018). However, the association with negative parenting was attenuated after adjustment for delinquency, whereas associations with positive parenting were more robust to this adjustment.

This attenuation may reflect overlapping associations of negative parenting with both CU traits and broader externalizing behaviors, consistent with prior evidence linking negative parenting to elevated externalizing symptoms (Brady et al., 2024). Together, these findings suggest that parenting is relevant to CU development during adolescence, with particularly consistent evidence for positive parenting.

Parenting may be particularly relevant to CU development because the capacities that CU traits disrupt, including empathy, guilt, and concern for others, develop in part through repeated interactions with caregivers. Developmental models propose that warm, responsive parenting supports the emergence of empathy and prosocial behavior, whereas low warmth and harsh parenting heighten risk for CU behavior, particularly among youth with existing vulnerabilities (Waller & Hyde, 2018). Consistent with this account, parental warmth and positive expressiveness have been linked longitudinally with empathy-related responding and social functioning (Zhou et al., 2002). The present findings extend this work in two ways. First, they suggest that these caregiving processes remain relevant in adolescence, a period when peer influences grow and parental influence is often assumed to wane (Brown & Bakken, 2011; Meyer et al., 2026). Second, they show that parenting is associated not only with stable differences in CU traits between adolescents, but also with the direction of change among adolescents who shift between CU severity levels.

The distinction between positive and negative parenting also appears in intervention research, which further suggests that parenting is a potentially modifiable target. In the Fast Track trial, intervention-related increases in parental warmth were indirectly associated with lower subsequent CU traits, whereas reductions in harsh discipline were associated with lower conduct disorder symptoms (Pasalich et al., 2016). Parent training has also produced reductions in CU traits among children with conduct problems (Fleming et al., 2022). Together with the present findings, this work suggests that positive and negative parenting may relate to CU traits through distinct processes, and that interventions targeting CU traits may benefit from building warmth in addition to reducing harshness. The present findings suggest that parenting remains relevant to changes in CU severity during adolescence and highlight the importance of testing whether strengthening positive parenting and reducing negative parenting can alter CU development during this period.

Emotion Dysregulation and Movement Across CU Severity Levels

Greater emotion dysregulation predicted movement toward higher CU severity among moderate-start adolescents and movement directly from low to elevated severity, rather than from low to moderate severity, among low-start adolescents. These findings suggest that emotion dysregulation may be relevant to changes in CU severity even among adolescents who do not initially show elevated CU traits. More broadly, difficulties in regulating emotion have been implicated in adolescent externalizing behavior and may contribute to persistent patterns of maladaptive responding (Baskin-Sommers et al., 2022; Modecki et al., 2017). One possibility is that adolescents who have difficulty regulating negative emotions, such as anger or frustration, may be more likely to respond impulsively or aggressively during interpersonal conflict.

Difficulties tolerating distress may also reinforce these responses when they provide temporary relief from negative emotions (Baskin-Sommers et al., 2022). Over time, repeated difficulties managing distress and inhibiting maladaptive responses could contribute to increasing CU severity, although specific processes underlying this association require further investigation.

Thus, emotion dysregulation may reflect a vulnerability that increases risk for rising CU severity in some adolescents, rather than a defining feature of CU traits themselves.

The present findings suggest that emotion regulation may be an important consideration address processes specific to CU traits (Perlstein et al., 2023; Waaler et al., 2024).

Interpreting Transitions Across Starting CU Severity Levels

The meaning of each transition depended on adolescents' starting CU severity. The moderate-start analysis provided the clearest test of direction because both possible destinations were adjacent to the starting category and represented movement toward lower or higher CU severity in absolute terms. Among these adolescents, greater positive parenting predicted movement toward low severity, whereas greater negative parenting and emotion dysregulation predicted movement toward elevated severity. These findings indicate that parenting and emotion dysregulation distinguish the direction of change among adolescents starting from the same level of CU severity.

The low-and elevated-start analyses address a different question. Adolescents starting at low severity could only move upward, so the contrast distinguished a smaller shift to moderate severity from a larger shift directly to elevated severity. Likewise, adolescents starting at elevated severity could only move downward, so the contrast distinguished a shift to moderate severity from a larger shift directly to low severity. These models therefore inform the magnitude of change within a single direction rather than the direction itself. Among low-start adolescents, greater positive parenting was associated with moving to moderate rather than elevated severity, suggesting that positive parenting may be associated with smaller increases when CU severity rises. In contrast, greater emotion dysregulation was associated with moving to elevated rather than moderate severity, suggesting that dysregulation may be associated with larger increases.

Because relatively few adolescents moved directly from low to elevated severity, findings from the low-start model should be interpreted with caution and replicated in larger samples.

Distinguishing these contrasts has implications for both research and prevention.

Methodologically, trajectory analyses alone would have obscured these findings, as they summarize each adolescent's overall pattern rather than the shifts that occur between assessments. Examining transitions conditional on starting severity revealed that the same factors may relate to different aspects of change depending on where adolescents begin. For adolescents with moderate levels of CU traits, parenting and emotion dysregulation were associated with whether severity subsequently decreased or increased, suggesting that this group may be at a developmental crossroads where caregiving and regulatory factors are linked to the direction of change. For adolescents with low CU traits who showed increases, positive parenting and emotion dysregulation were associated with how large those increases were, suggesting that these factors may help identify youth at risk for larger increases in CU severity. If replicated in designs capable of testing causal effects, these patterns could inform prevention efforts tailored to adolescents' current CU severity, such as strengthening caregiving among youth with moderate traits or targeting emotion regulation among low-severity youth who show signs of rising CU traits.

Limitations

Before concluding, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the longitudinal design established temporal ordering between predictors measured at the beginning of an assessment interval and subsequent CU severity, the observational nature of the study precludes causal inference. Parenting, emotion dysregulation, and CU traits may influence one another reciprocally over development. For example, in younger children, greater parental warmth predicts lower subsequent CU behavior, while higher CU behavior also predicts lower subsequent parental warmth (Waller et al., 2014). Such reciprocal processes may be especially relevant during adolescence, as youth increasingly shape their interactions with caregivers. Thus, although parenting was measured before each transition, the present analyses cannot determine whether parenting contributes to changes in CU severity, reflects caregivers' responses to existing characteristics, or both. Unmeasured individual, family, or contextual factors may contribute to the observed associations. Accordingly, the present findings identify prospective correlates of changes in CU severity rather than causal mechanisms. Future research using designs that explicitly model reciprocal processes, as well as intervention studies that directly manipulate parenting or emotion regulation processes, will be important for determining whether these prospective associations reflect modifiable mechanisms of CU development.

Second, the study relied primarily on youth self-report measures of CU traits, parenting, emotion dysregulation, and delinquent behavior. Although youth reports provide important information about adolescents' internal experiences and perceptions of their family environments, reliance on a common informant may increase shared-method variance and may not fully capture these constructs from other perspectives. Future research incorporating parent and teacher reports, observational measures of parent-child interactions, behavioral assessments, and clinical evaluations could determine whether the present findings generalize across informants and methods.

Third, attrition across assessment waves resulted in fewer observations at later assessments and reduced precision for analyses conducted at later intervals. This limitation was particularly relevant when models were stratified by both starting CU severity and assessment interval, resulting in relatively small numbers for some transition types. Participants also contributed differing numbers of adjacent assessment intervals, resulting in unequal opportunities to exhibit a transition across the study period. The supplementary resampling analyses suggested that reduced sample size may have contributed to weaker statistical evidence for emotion dysregulation at the final interval, underscoring the need for caution when interpreting differences across assessment periods. Longer-term studies with greater retention could clarify whether these associations persist across adolescence.

Finally, the present study was conducted in a community sample, which may limit generalizability to populations characterized by higher levels of CU traits, behavioral problems, or contextual risk. Replication in clinical and justice-involved samples will therefore be important for determining whether the observed associations between parenting, emotion dysregulation, and changes in CU severity extend to youth with more persistent externalizing problems.

Conclusions

The present study extends research on CU development by examining both developmental trajectories and shifts between CU severity levels across adolescence. Although low, moderate, and elevated trajectories remained differentiated over time, many adolescents moved between severity levels from one assessment to the next. Among those who changed, positive parenting was associated with lower subsequent CU severity, whereas negative parenting and emotion dysregulation were associated with higher subsequent severity.

Examining transitions alongside trajectories therefore provides a more complete picture of CU development, distinguishing not only whether adolescents move toward lower or higher severity, but also, for those starting at low severity, how large those shifts are. Broadly, these findings suggest that CU traits in adolescence are less fixed than stable group trajectories imply, and that modifiable features of youths' family and emotional lives are associated with how CU severity changes across adolescence and the direction their development takes.

Parenting and Emotion Dysregulation Across Trajectory Groups.

To characterize differences in parenting and emotion dysregulation across the latent CU trajectory groups, repeated assessments of positive parenting, negative parenting, and emotion dysregulation were compared across the Low, Moderate, and Elevated trajectories identified in the retained threeclass LCGA solution. Separate linear mixed-effects models were estimated for positive parenting, negative parenting, and emotion dysregulation, with trajectory group and assessment wave included as fixed effects and participant included as a random intercept to account for repeated observations. Tukey-adjusted pairwise comparisons of estimated marginal means, averaged across assessment waves, were conducted to evaluate differences between trajectory groups.

Interval-Specific Analyses.

To evaluate whether associations between parenting, emotion dysregulation, and changes in CU severity were consistent across assessment intervals, the primary transition models were re-estimated separately for each adjacent assessment interval (S02→S03, S03→S04, and S04→S05). Analyses included only intervals in which adolescents transitioned between CU severity categories. Models were stratified by starting CU severity and included positive parenting, negative parenting, and emotion dysregulation simultaneously, with adjustment for age and sex.

These findings indicate that heterogeneity in CU development was characterized primarily by differences in overall CU severity rather than by markedly different rates of longitudinal change. Parenting and emotion dysregulation also differed significantly across CU trajectory groups. Descriptive means and standard deviations were calculated across all available assessment observations from S02-S05 within each trajectory group.
