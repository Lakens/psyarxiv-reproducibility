Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The present study tested whether the self-reference effect (SRE)-individuals' tendency to engage with information related to themselves-can increase engagement with science content on social media. In a 12-week field experiment, we published 34 Instagram posts and 10 YouTube videos featuring a science lab's activities. Captions and video titles were randomly assigned to either a self-referencing (SR) condition (using second-person pronouns), or an other-referencing (OR) condition (using thirdperson pronouns). Engagement was assessed using platform metrics such as likes and shares. Contrary to expectations, SR content did not yield higher engagement, whereas OR content yielded significantly higher shares. Exploratory analyses further indicated that OR content yielded higher composite engagement scores. These findings suggest that self-referencing language may have limited effectiveness for science communication in social media settings. Importantly, this study opens avenues for further research on how content framing strategies influences science content engagement on social media.

1. Introduction

Social media is a powerful tool in modern society, shaping how individuals share information and connect with others. Its role in science communication has also grown (Brossard & Scheufelehttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?L9Uxg1 2022; Mede et al., 2025) , with more researchers using platforms like Instagram, X, and YouTube to engage the public. For scientific laboratories, particularly new ones, establishing an online presence is crucial to building visibility and outreach (Collins et al., 2016; Metag, 2020; Taddicken & Krämer, 2021). However, gaining audience attention on social media is challenging when competing with entertainment and algorithm-driven posts (Brossard & Scheufele, 2022). Therefore, it is important for science content creators to understand what factors influence audience engagement.

One potential strategy for increasing scientific content engagement may be the selfreference effect (SRE), a well-established concept in cognitive psychology (Rogers et al., 1977). The SRE is the tendency for people to better remember and engage with information that relates to themselves. For example, individuals demonstrate better memory and comprehension for sentences containing first-or second-person pronouns (Leonhardt & Pezzutti, 2017; Liu et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2011). Self-referencing information also elicits positive brand attitudes (Perkins & Forehand, 2012). Though the role of SRE language in social media posts has not been explored extensively, these previous findings suggest that self-referencing may be an effective strategy for increasing audience engagement.

This study uses a newly established science lab at REDACTED University in Ontario, Canada, as a field experiment to investigate how SRE influences audience engagement with its social media accounts. We established the lab's social media on both YouTube and Instagram, manipulated pronoun use in post captions and titles, and collected audience engagement metrics. We hypothesized that self-referencing captions and titles would lead to higher engagement metrics (e.g., likes, comments, subscriptions) compared to other-referencing captions.

1.1 Why Do New Labs Need Social Media Presence?

The global usage of social media is continuously rising (Statista, 2025). For research laboratories, social media could be a useful tool to increase online visibility (Metag, 2020).

Social media offers several advantages for science communication. By allowing researchers to explain scientific concepts in accessible ways, social media can make scientific information available to audiences who do not regularly engage with academic journals or conferences (Collins et al., 2016; Taddicken & Krämer, 2021; Zhang et al., 2025). Social media also helps research labs increase the impact of their work. Publications promoted on social media are associated with higher downloads and citation counts (Agarwal et al., 2016; Bik & Goldstein, 2013; Fenner, 2014). In addition, social media enhances outreach by connecting labs with potential participants, collaborators, and the broader community (Collins et al., 2016). In marketing research, social media has also shown positive effects in promoting brand image (Klassen et al., 2018). These features make social media a promising tool for research laboratories seeking to communicate their work, increase visibility, attract talented personnel and research participants, and engage diverse audiences. These benefits, however, depend on whether labs can establish the necessary reach on social media. A key barrier is that scientific content must compete with entertaining content, making it difficult to capture and sustain audience attention (Brossard & Scheufele, 2022). Therefore, exploring useful strategies that increase audience engagement is critical for laboratories seeking to build an effective and sustainable social media presence.

1.2 Different Social Media Platforms Used in Science Communication

A wide range of social media platforms have been used for science communication, including blogs, Facebook, Instagram, YouTube, X (formerly Twitter), and LinkedIn. These platforms support activities such as explaining scientific concepts, sharing research updates, engaging with public audiences, and facilitating professional networking (Collins et al., 2016). However, each platform differs in its format, audience, and affordances, resulting in distinct strengths and limitations for communicating content (Bik & Goldstein, 2013; Lawson & Morales-Tirado, 2023). As a result, the selection of social media platforms plays a critical role in shaping how scientific information is communicated and how audiences engage with it.

In the present study, platform selection was guided by our target audience and communication goals. As a university laboratory, our main audience was students and young adults. The lab aimed for both rapid engagement through short-form content and in-depth communication with more elaborated content. Based on these considerations, Instagram and YouTube were selected as our primary platforms.

Instagram is particularly effective for reaching younger audiences and promoting rapid engagement through visual and interactive features. Demographically, Instagram's largest user group is young adults aged 18-34, well-aligned with our target audience (Statista, 2025). Instagram supports multiple content formats, including images, short videos, and stories. Interactive features, such as polls and direct messaging, enable users to engage with content in real time (Caspari, 2022). However, Instagram content typically has short visibility periods due to algorithm-driven feeds, resulting in more surface-level and short-lived audience interest (Cotter, 2019).

YouTube complements Instagram by supporting longer and more elaborated communication. Unlike Instagram's focus on short, attention-driven content, YouTube allows creators to explain scientific concepts in depth through longer videos,making it suitable for promoting deeper understanding and sustained engagement with complex topics (Dubovi & Tabak, 2020). Youtube also includes accessibility features, such as closed captions, to help make content available to a more diverse audience. As such, YouTube has been widely used in science communication and education (Herrington & Sweeder, 2025). However, YouTube has limitations. Reaching a large audience often depends on having an established subscriber base within the platform's algorithm. Furthermore, producing longer videos requires significant time, effort, and technical resources (Bärtl, 2018).

1.3 Measures of Social Media Engagement

To evaluate the impact of social media content, it is essential to quantify measures of social media success. Common social media metrics that reflect digital consumer engagement include likes, comments, shares, subscriptions, reach, impressions, clickthrough rate, view count, and viewing duration. These metrics can be organized into three levels of public engagement: consuming, participating, and generating (Taddicken & Krämer, 2021).

At the level of consuming, metrics capture passive exposure and initial attention. Reach and impressions reflect how widely and frequently content is displayed to users (Mui & Ming, 2020). Similarly, view count indicates whether users initiated playback. Viewing duration reflects how long they remained engaged, providing an indicator of audiences' sustained attention (Chatzopoulou et al., 2010). On YouTube, click-through rate (CTR) similarly reflects consuming behaviour, indicating the proportion of users who choose to watch a video after being exposed to its thumbnail and title (Shah & Nasnodkar, 2021).

At the level of participating, metrics reflect active but relatively low-effort interactions. Likes indicate users' immediate positive reactions and perceived appeal (Sabate et al., 2014; Kay et al., 2020), although they are often reflexive and require minimal cognitive effort. Shares also fall within this category, as they involve redistributing existing content and are often driven by identity expression and value alignment (Labrecque et al., 2020). Subscription numbers reflect an ongoing relationship between audiences and content creators, indicating sustained interest and the potential for future engagement (Yang et al., 2022).

At the level of generating, metrics capture user contributions that involve creating and expressing original content. Comments require users to produce responses that reflect their thoughts and interpretations of the content, often involving greater cognitive and emotional engagement (Gavilanes et al., 2018). This form of engagement represents a more active contribution.

Engagement metrics also vary in their transparency. Metrics such as likes are directly observable user actions, whereas metrics such as reach and view count depend on platform-specific definitions and algorithmic thresholds, which may not be fully disclosed. In addition, platform algorithms influence the distribution of content; for example, a "rich-get-richer" effect has been observed on YouTube, where already popular content is more likely to be further promoted (Borghol et al., 2012). Therefore, engagement metrics are reflective of both audience behaviour and algorithmic amplification.

Importantly, in the present study, "success" is operationalized in terms of engagement metrics. While these metrics capture observable engagement behaviours, they do not directly reflect how the audience cognitively processes, understands, or retains the content. Nor do they capture important offline or longer-term outcomes from online engagement. For example, a student may discover a research opportunity through a social media post and subsequently join a laboratory-an impact that would not be reflected in conventional engagement metrics. Therefore, engagement metrics should be interpreted as indicators of online interaction rather than comprehensive measures of content impact. We return to the limitation of using engagement metrics to quantify social media success in the Discussion.

1.4 Factors that Influence Audience Engagement

To optimize the effectiveness of social media strategies in science communication, it is crucial to analyze factors influencing engagement. Audience engagement with science communication on social media is influenced by multimodal features. Elements like video titles, thumbnails, and leading captions act as the first point of contact and strongly affect whether users choose to view content (Foster, 2020).

Language and content features further shape how audiences interact with science posts. Communicating science with clear, accessible language increases audiences' engagement, while heavy jargon use can discourage interaction (Shulman et al., 2020). Use of humor improves users' engagement intention (Yeo et al., 2020); emotional or practical content is more likely to be shared (Milkman & Berger, 2014). Importantly, people tend to share content that reflects positively on themselves-such as informative or "smart" content-to support positive self-presentation (Milkman & Berger, 2014). Thus, audiences evaluate scientific content in relation to their self-and public image.

1.4.1 Self-Reference Effect

The self-reference effect (SRE) refers to the cognitive psychology finding that individuals can better recall information encoded in relation to themselves, compared to less personal information. Rogers et al. (1977) proposed that self-referential information engages individuals' self-schemas, enabling deeper interpretation and more efficient information integration into one's memory. As a result, phrases involving information that cues oneself become more distinctive and more easily recalled. Self-reference is typically experimentally induced by manipulating pronouns. For example, Shi et al. (2011) had participants view everyday object nouns preceded by either a first-person ("my") or third-person ("his") possessive pronoun. During a surprise recall test, nouns paired with first-person possessive pronouns were recalled more accurately than those paired with third-person pronouns.

Beyond memory, self-referential content can also shape people's attitudes toward brands. In a study by Perkins and Forehand (2012), participants learned two artificial brand families paired with either first-person or third-person pronouns. Self-associated brands were later categorized more quickly and evaluated more positively than otherassociated brands. On social media, self-referential language has been used as a marketing strategy to engage audiences. Self-relevant language use helps capture consumers' attention and increase their psychological closeness to the message source (Tausczik & Pennebaker, 2010). Cruz and Pezzuti (2017) examined more than 15,000 Facebook marketing posts and found that messages containing second-person pronouns (e.g., you, your) generated significantly higher levels of engagement metrics, including likes, comments, and shares, compared to messages without pronouns (e.g., "Think of all information that is saved on your computer" versus "Think of all information that is saved on the computer"). Second-person pronouns prompted consumers to think about the message in relation to themselves, which encouraged self-relevant processing and enhanced engagement with the content.

Self-referential language can have varied effects depending on the message content and the engagement metrics being examined. Labrecque et al. (2020) analyzed Facebook posts from 90 consumer brands, and found that second-person pronouns were most strongly associated with increases in comments, but showed less consistent effects on likes and shares. These effects further varied by product type. For utilitarian products, chosen for functional or practical reasons, second-person pronouns increased likes and comments but were associated with fewer shares. For hedonic products, linked to enjoyment or leisure, second-person pronouns reduced sharing compared to third-person pronouns.

Despite previous studies demonstrating that self-referential language can enhance memory, attitudes, and engagement, its use in science communication on social media remains unclear. With research laboratories increasingly relying on social media to engage with public audiences, yet typically lacking the resources to promote content through paid advertising, language-based strategies such as self-referential framing represent a potentially low-cost approach to increasing visibility and engagement. Because science communication differs from marketing in its goals and audience expectations, it is unclear whether findings from commercial contexts will generalize to scientific domains. The present study therefore investigates whether self-referential language increases audience engagement with science content on social media.

We use a new cognitive neuroscience lab at REDACTED University as a field experiment to explore whether self-referential video titles and photo captions enhance audience engagement with science content on YouTube and Instagram. We hypothesize that videos and posts with SRE titles and captions will score higher on engagement metrics (e.g., likes, shares, subscription, reach), compared to those with other-referencing titles and captions.

2. Method

The first author created new YouTube and Instagram accounts for the lab and an introductory post that is excluded from the study. To build an initial audience, the accounts followed relevant accounts within the lab's professional network and were promoted at an academic conference. At the start of data collection, the Instagram account had 43 followers and the YouTube account had 2 followers.

Content creation and posting occurred over a 12-week period from November 2024 to February 2025. Content covered four thematic categories: (1) lab research highlights, (2) behind-the-scenes events, (3) daily life in the lab, and (4) expert interviews.

2.1 Posting Procedure

Posting time and frequency were held constant on both platforms. On Instagram, a total of 34 posts were published, comprising 24 photo posts and 10 videos (reels). Photo posts were published weekly on Mondays and Wednesdays around 1:00 pm, while videos were posted weekly on Sundays around 3:00 pm.

On YouTube, the same 10 videos were published weekly on Sundays around 3:00 pm, matching the Instagram reels in content and posting time. All videos were produced with comparable lengths (ranging from 6 min 13 s to 7 min 5 s) and used a consistent title font, thumbnail style, and ending logo.

2.2 Self-Reference Manipulation

For each post, two caption versions were prepared prior to publication: one using selfreferential (SR) language and one using other-referential (OR) language. SR captions used pronouns that directly addressed the audience (e.g., you, your, we). OR captions replaced audience-directed pronouns with third-person references (e.g., the lab, students, listeners). Across conditions, captions were matched with content, grammatical structure, and pronoun frequency. For example: SR: "Here's your inside look at the Synaptic Rodeo show! Your gaze changes dynamically with music." OR: "Here's the lab's inside look at the Synaptic Rodeo show! The audience's gaze changes dynamically with music."

The 34 Instagram photo posts were then randomly assigned to either the SR or OR condition to determine which caption version was used for posting. Each condition has a total of 17 posts, including 12 photo posts and 5 video posts. The thematic category of these posts was relatively balanced across conditions and was not directly manipulated.

For Youtube videos, two title versions (SR and OR) were also created in addition to descriptions for each video, again controlling for sentence structure and only differing in pronoun use. For example: SR: "Your 33 Questions with Dr. REDACTED FOR PEER REVIEW" OR: "REDACTED FOR PEER REVIEW Lab's 33 Questions with Dr. REDACTED" Each YouTube video was assigned to the same condition and has identical descriptions as its corresponding Instagram video post.

3.1 Instagram engagement metrics

To test the hypothesis that posts with SR captions receive higher audience engagement metrics than those with OR captions, we conducted 2*2 ANOVAs (SR/OR and post/video) on Instagram metrics. A sensitivity power analysis indicated that, given the current sample size (α = .05, power = .80), the study was powered to detect effects of at least η² = .21, corresponding to a large effect size; we therefore had limited sensitivity to detect small or medium effects.

The caption condition had a large effect on the number of shares (F(1) = 6.229, p = .018, η² = .17; see Fig. 1). However, the effect went in the opposite direction of the hypothesis: OR posts were shared on average 2.29 times (SD = 2.49), while SR posts were only shared 0.59 times (SD = 1.18). There was no significant main or interaction effect of post format (photo or video). The other metrics (likes, reach, and SG) were not significantly affected by condition or format (all p > .28; see full ANOVA tables S1a-S1d).

3.2 YouTube engagement metrics

We descriptively compared the YouTube SR and OR conditions' performance on average watching time, CTR, impressions, likes, unique viewers, and views after one day (Fig. 3) and across all time points (Fig. 4). SR captions afforded a higher average CTR; OR captions received higher mean impressions across all three time points, while having higher mean unique viewers and views after one week and globally. Unique viewers and views increased with time.

3.3 General Engagement Score

Many engagement metrics were highly correlated with each other (see Supplemental figures S1, S2) and, therefore, perhaps indicators of the same construct (audience engagement). We conducted a Principal Component Analysis and ranked posts based on this general engagement score (Figure 5, Figure 6; full PCA details in Supplement). Posts 32 and 34 were excluded from this calculation because some engagement metrics were unavailable in Instagram Insights. On YouTube, the top video #7 featured an introduction to the lab's eye-tracking technology. The next in the rankings, videos #6 and #2, both featured the lab's behindthe-scenes of live music research experiments. SR and OR posts were approximately equally represented in the top and bottom halves of YouTube videos.

3.4 Descriptive Analyses of Additional Instagram Post Characteristics

A post-hoc descriptive analysis examined potential relationships between other Instagram post characteristics and audience engagement (see supplemental figures S3-S12).

3.4.1 Post Theme/Topic

"Behind-the-scenes" posts generally demonstrated higher reach, shares, and subscriber growth and general engagement score. "Daily life in the lab" posts demonstrate moderate and relatively consistent engagement across posts. Lab "research highlights" and "expert interviews" generally demonstrated lower overall engagement, despite a few high-performing outlier posts.

3.4.2 Number of Faces Present in Post Thumbnails

Posts containing two or more visible faces generally demonstrated below-average general engagement scores. All five of the highest-ranking posts have only one visible face within the thumbnail.

3.4.3 Eye-tracking Glasses or Technological Equipment in Thumbnails

Posts featuring eye-tracking glasses, the lab's signature technology, demonstrated higher reach and shares. However, the two posts with the highest overall engagement score did not contain eye-tracking glasses. Posts with visible technological equipment demonstrated lower shares and engagement rankings compared to posts without equipment.

3.4.4 Naturalistic Photography

Posts using thumbnails photographed in natural settings (see Figure 9) without digitally designed graphic elements generally demonstrated higher numbers of likes.

4. Discussion

We investigated whether social media captions using self-referential (SR) language lead to better audience engagement with scientific content. Although SR language has been associated with enhanced engagement in cognitive psychology, neuroscience, and marketing contexts (Cruz & Pezzuti, 2017; Leonhardt & Pezzutti, 2017; Liu et al., 2023; Perkins & Forehand, 2012; Shi et al., 2011; Tausczik & Pennebaker, 2010), the present findings suggest that SR effects may not generalize to science communication on social media.

On Instagram, SR captions did not show a significant advantage over OR captions in any of the four engagement metrics. Instead, we found that posts with OR captions received significantly more shares. OR-captioned posts also ranked higher on the general engagement PCA metric. One possible explanation is our audiences' motivation to engage with infotainment content. For engagement driven by audience enjoyment, as opposed to practical utility, an overuse of self-referencing pronouns could backfire and lead to less optimal engagement (Labrecque et al., 2020), potentially reducing perceptions of content credibility and emotional appeal. Another possible reason is the limited sample size of our posts due to time and resource constraints. The study was only adequately powered to detect large effects, and may therefore have been unable to identify smaller but potentially meaningful effects.

On YouTube, videos with OR titles and captions had higher mean values on the engagement metrics watch time, impressions, views, and unique viewers. SR captions showed higher mean click-through rates (CTR) than OR captions; however, this pattern is likely influenced by how CTR is calculated (clicks divided by impressions). Because OR captions generated higher impressions, the denominator in the CTR calculation increased, which explains the lower CTR.

4.1 Potential Advantages of OR Language

Our exploratory evidence suggests that OR captions may enhance engagement with scientific communication. One potential advantage of OR language is its ability to foster a shared perspective between the communicator and the audience (Clark & Brennan, 1991). Third-person pronouns may present information as broadly relevant, allowing content to appeal to a wider audience rather than targeting viewers with direct or personal experience related to the message. Conversely, SR language may unintentionally alienate some readers who do not directly identify with the communicator's experiences. In this study, many posts highlighted lab events that occurred in specific physical settings. In these contexts, SR language may have only been relatable to individuals who directly attended these events.

In addition, third-person pronouns may signal objectivity, authority, and credibilityqualities that are central to effective science communication (Fiske & Dupree, 2014).

Given that the primary audience of our posts consisted of scientists, students, and science enthusiasts, their preference for OR language may reflect alignment with academic communication norms, which typically emphasize objectivity over personal narrative. Furthermore, the inclusion of the "REDACTED FOR PEER REVIEW Lab" name as part of the OR language may have introduced a brand-related effect that could have influenced engagement outcomes.

4.2 The Effect of Platform, Media Format, and Time on Engagement

Comparing media formats, photo posts gained an overall higher engagement than video among Instagram users. Our results align with prior research demonstrating that photos often outperform videos in scientific education contexts on Instagram (Kauffman et al., 2020). Photos are more accessible to users who may be unable to play videos aloud, require less sustained attention, and support self-paced learning (Kauffman et al., 2020). Additionally, our videos (around 7 minutes) were significantly longer than typical Instagram reels (under 30 seconds), potentially making them less aligned with platformspecific audience expectations and contributing to lower performance.

Comparing platforms, Instagram generated higher engagement than YouTube. This difference may be attributed to Instagram's large student user base (Pavelle & Wilkinson, 2020). In our experience, it was easier to connect and collaborate with other students, scientists, and organizations on Instagram. Moreover, Instagram's interactive functionalities, such as sharing posts through private messages, posting collaborative content with other accounts, and receiving story mentions may have further amplified engagement. In contrast, YouTube visibility tends to be more dependent on an established subscriber base and accumulated views, making it difficult for newer or smaller channels to gain traction (Burgess & Green, 2009).

Subscriber growth on Instagram, and views and unique viewers on YouTube, increased over the time of the data collection. In contrast, most other engagement metrics remained relatively stable over time, with most of the audience interaction occurring within the first day of posting. This pattern suggests that social media engagement for science content tends to be immediate, likely driven by algorithmic promotion of fresh content and audiences' habitual consumption of new posts. There was no evidence that posts created later in the data collection period performed better, suggesting that a growing number of subscribers (at least in the lower three-digit range) did not substantially amplify engagement.

4.3 The Effect of Other Factors on Engagement

This study further explored how content-related characteristics were associated with audience engagement on Instagram. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

The top-performing posts highlight the importance of interactivity. The highest-ranking post was related to a conference at which the account had been shared, and this prior interaction with the target audience may have contributed to higher engagement. The second-highest-ranking post was a quiz-style post inviting audiences to guess a Christmas song, and thus its interactive format may have contributed to its popularity.

Overall, posts featuring behind-the-scenes content tended to demonstrate higher engagement. One possible explanation is that BTS posts often feature event-based or experiential content, which may enhance perceived novelty. Such content may provide audiences with a sense of "insider access" to scientific activities that are not routinely visible, thereby increasing its shareability and engagement potential.

Posts with a single visible face in the thumbnail tended to receive more engagement compared to posts with multiple or no visible faces. Since human faces are strong visual cues that quickly attract attention on social media (Bakhshi, Shamma, & Gilbert, 2014), posts with faces may be more likely to draw viewer attention than those without faces. On the other hand, multiple faces may create competing focal points within the same image, making it harder for viewers to quickly identify a key element in a fast-scrolling social media environment.

Findings related to technological equipment were mixed. Technological equipment may increase perceived novelty, but may also make the content less immediately accessible to broader audiences, as images of unfamiliar research tools can require more cognitive effort.

Finally, posts using natural photographic thumbnails generally demonstrated higher engagement compared to posts with digital or graphic-based designs. This may reflect perceived authenticity and visual processing fluency, as natural imagery is often processed more effortlessly and may be perceived as more genuine. Prior research suggests that perceived authenticity can play an important role in audience engagement and trust formation, which could translate to better engagement measures (Morhart et al., 2015; Zhang, 2026).

These findings suggest that beyond SR/OR language in post captions, factors such as format, content, and platform also play important roles in shaping engagement with science communication on social media, providing directions for future research.

5. Limitations and Future Directions

To ensure the validity of the findings of this field experiment, potentially confounding variables such as posting times, caption structures, and video format were carefully controlled, allowing observed differences in engagement to be more confidently attributed to the self-referencing manipulation. Despite these precautions, some potential limitations should be considered.

One major limitation is the influence of platform recommendation algorithms on post visibility. Engagement metrics do not purely reflect audience interest, as they are also heavily shaped by algorithmic decisions that promote or suppress content. Certain posts might receive disproportionate visibility for reasons unrelated to content quality or language conditions. In addition, social media platforms do not fully disclose formulas used to generate engagement metrics, further complicating the interpretation of these metrics.

Another limitation is that the current metrics do not capture all dimensions of audience engagement. While these metrics provide indirect indicators of audience motivation, attitude, and interest, they do not directly assess emotional responses or deeper processing of content. In this study, the comments were excluded from analyses due to a floor effect. Because comments often reflect deeper cognitive or emotional engagement, the low comment frequency may indicate that observed engagement primarily represents surface-level interaction. Future studies could incorporate alternative measures, such as surveys or physiological assessments, to provide a deeper understanding of audience engagement. It would also be valuable to collect metrics involving real-world uptake of social media content (e.g., tracking the number of new participants signing up to participate in the lab's research, or the number of new students applying to work in the lab). Such metrics would require more involvement from the researchers but may ultimately be more meaningful in informing whether science communication via social media translates to real-world impact.

Future research should further investigate the effectiveness of OR captions in enhancing audience engagement. Exploring the potential brand effect of including the lab's name in captions versus neutral other-referencing pronouns is another promising direction. To address existing limitations, future field experiments should incorporate larger sample sizes while keeping a tight control on extraneous factors

6. Costs and Benefits of Social Media for Researchers

For researchers, the costs of managing a lab's social media presence must be weighed against the potential benefits. Science communication on social media requires substantial effort, from account creation and promotion to content planning, filming, editing, and caption writing. In addition, social media management involves ongoing engagement with other organizations, promoting events , and interacting with audiences. Given that many research laboratories lack dedicated social media personnel, these responsibilities often compete with other research priorities (Banse et al., 2026; Calice et al., 2022). Labs with limited resources may therefore struggle to maintain an active presence. Labs with extensive resources may question if social media content creation is the best use of grant funding and aligned with tax-payer priorities.

Despite these challenges, social media remains a valuable tool for science communication. In this field experiment, the lab's social media presence supported outreach and engagement with scientific content. Anecdotally, conversations with students suggest that social media has helped spark interest in the lab, with some students asking questions or reaching out to learn more. The lab has also used its social media accounts to share lab-related events and connect with scientists beyond its immediate geographic area, suggesting potential benefits of social media beyond the engagement measured in this study.

7. Conclusion

With social media playing an expanding role in public engagement with science, understanding strategies influencing audience engagement is crucial. This study found no overall engagement advantage of self-referencing language compared to otherreferencing language. In fact, captions using OR language resulted in significantly higher sharing behaviour on Instagram and showed potential for increasing engagement on YouTube. This study contributes to a growing understanding of how language strategies can be leveraged to enhance the reach and impact of science communication on social media platforms, and provides practical guidance for scientists seeking to maximize the visibility and accessibility of their work online.

2. Exploratory Factor Analysis

We conducted an exploratory factor analysis to explore the aggregated effect of the metrics we collected for each platform. We examined the same metrics as before and included only day data for each post. To understand the interrelationship across metrics and their fit for factor analysis, we created a correlation matrix across metrics and calculated the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) values for each platform. The KMO statistic evaluates whether the observed correlations among variables are sufficiently strong to support factor analysis, with higher values indicating greater shared variance among variables and better suitability for factor extraction. For Instagram metrics (Figure S1), likes were strongly correlated (r=0.59) with reach and moderately correlated (r=0.44) with shares. There was also a strong positive correlation (r=0.62) between reach and shares. SG showed weak correlations with the other three metrics (all r < 0.3). While all four metrics had KMO values above 0.6, indicating adequate sampling adequacy for factor analysis, we excluded SG from the factor analysis to improve the fit of the general factor model and enhance interpretability. For YouTube metrics (Figure S2), there was a strong negative correlation between impressions and CTR (r = -0.81). A strong positive correlation was observed between likes and views (r = 0.90), likes and unique viewers (r = 0.74), and views and unique viewers (r = 0.82). A medium positive correlation was found between CTR and average watch time (r = 0.66). We retained the metrics with KMO values greater than 0.5 in the factor analysis, which left likes, views, impressions, and unique viewers.

We then conducted principal component analysis and extracted a general engagement factor that accounts for most of the variance for each of the metrics included in the two platforms, respectively. This general factor explained 67% of the variance in Instagram metrics, with reach having the highest loading (r=0.88). For YouTube metrics, the general factor explained 74% of the variance, with views and unique viewers having the highest loading (r=0.97).
