Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Background. Alcohol use may reflect both intentional processes and failures of self-regulation, yet their relative relevance to everyday drinking remains unclear. Therefore, we examined whether perceived control, planned use, craving, and inhibitory control were associated with alcohol use among regular drinkers at momentary and daily timescales. Methods. A total of 142 regular alcohol users (40 males, 102 females; M = 33.30 years, SD = 14.76) completed a fourday experience sampling method (ESM) study with three daily prompts assessing alcohol use, craving, perceived control, and planned use, directly followed by a mobile Go/No-Go task assessing inhibitory control. Bayesian multilevel models examined whether inhibition errors, planned use, craving, and perceived control were associated with drinking at momentary and daily timescales.

At the momentary level, evidence suggested credible between-person associations between planned use and perceived control and momentary alcohol consumption, and credible negative within-person associations for perceived control and craving. At the daily level, planned use and perceived control showed credible between-person associations with daily alcohol consumption, and females reported lower daily consumption than males. Within-person planned use and inhibition errors were not credible at either level, and no credible within-person associations were found for daily alcohol use.

Perceived control emerged as the most consistent concurrent correlate of alcohol use among regular drinkers at momentary and daily timescales, with planned use emerging as a credible between-person correlate. In contrast, craving and inhibitory control were not robustly associated with drinking. Together, these findings highlight the relevance of perceived control and planned drinking for understanding everyday alcohol use, suggesting that drinking may not simply reflect momentary urges or failures of inhibitory control in regular drinkers. Future research should clarify the temporal and causal relationships among these processes and test whether moments of reduced perceived control represent useful targets for just-in-time interventions.

Introduction

In order to fully understand the motivational (i.e., craving and planned use) and selfregulatory (i.e., perceived and inhibitory control) states that are associated with drinking behavior, not only do we need to understand who is at greater risk of drinking (between-person differences) but also when an individual may be at risk (within-person fluctuations). While between-person variation helps us understand who is at elevated risk of hazardous drinking, it does not explain why an individual is more likely to drink at a specific moment compared to another. Only variables that fluctuate within a person can identify momentary risk of drinking needed to develop more targeted prevention and intervention efforts. Mounting evidence suggests that self-regulatory processes fluctuate dynamically across time and context, and that these momentary changes are linked to greater alcohol consumption, poorer treatment outcomes, and higher relapse risk (see, e.g., [1] for a meta-analysis).

Most standardized intervention approaches (i.e., one-size-fits-all approaches to prevention and treatment) tend to assume linear pathways based on between-person differences in efforts to reduce alcohol use; however, they fail to account for the within-person fluctuations that emerge. The antecedents and consequences associated with alcohol use vary across moments, contexts, and within persons [2], which calls for more individualized approaches, such as just-in-time adaptive interventions (JITAIs), that can better target and prevent hazardous drinking among regular drinkers [3]. A JITAI can therefore only be triggered by a momentary state if that state is known to co-occur with moments of elevated drinking risk, which requires evidence on the concurrent within-person associations between momentary states and alcohol consumption.

To better understand these dynamic processes that tend to fluctuate on a momentary or daily basis, researchers have increasingly examined within-person associations with alcohol use using the experience sampling method (ESM) [4] [5] [6] [7] [8] [9] [10]. This intensive longitudinal approach allows researchers to better capture key proximal variables surrounding a behavior in the very moment that behavior is likely to occur within one's natural environment, therefore maximizing the data's ecological validity and minimizing respondent recall bias [2, 11]. While self-report ESM surveys are able to capture an individual's drinking intentions and perceptions at any given moment, they simply cannot capture one's level of cognitive control in that moment. One related but distinct mechanism believed to contribute to individual differences in addictive behaviors is inhibitory control, defined as the ability to suppress automatic or prepotent responses in favor of goal-directed behavior [12] [13] [14] [15] [16].

Relatively few ESM studies have involved multimodal assessment methods, combining behavioral task assessments with self-report surveys, even though such tasks are already used in laboratory settings given their potential for measuring behavioral mechanisms more objectively [15, 17, 18]. This limited body of research is likely due to methodological constraints, as assessing a behavioral mechanism such as inhibitory control requires millisecond level accuracy that has only recently become feasible on smartphones [19]. Nevertheless, recent studies have demonstrated the feasibility, reliability and validity of measuring within-person fluctuations in response inhibition with Go/No-Go tasks in ESM studies [18, 20]. A widely used task targeting response withholding is the Go/No-Go task [21], which requires a respondent to press in response to a "go" stimulus and withhold a response to a "no-go" stimulus.

Meta-analytic evidence supports a dose-response relationship, with greater alcohol exposure linked to more pronounced inhibitory impairments [1, 22]. However, the few studies that have examined within-person associations between momentary fluctuations in inhibitory control and drinking, all involving heavier drinkers, have yielded mixed results [17, 23, 24]. In Jones et al. [23], a sample of 100 individuals motivated to reduce their alcohol use engaged in a two-week combined ESM and stop-signal task study. Multilevel modeling found that daily alcohol consumption was predicted by planned consumption (and craving), whereas inhibitory control did not emerge as a significant predictor. However, they did find that the deterioration in inhibitory control across daily moments was a significant predictor of higher use later that day, even after controlling for planned use and craving. Moreover, in a similar study involving heavy drinkers, Burton and Jones [17] found that poorer neutral-cue inhibitory control (measured using stop-signal reaction times combined with ESM) and frequency of craving were associated with subsequent drinking moments but concluded that inhibitory control alone did not predict alcohol consumption. In a more recent study by Zech et al. [24], a large sample diagnosed with an alcohol use disorder completed a one-year ESM study involving a cognitive control task to measure response inhibition. The findings showed that decision making tasks, not cognitive control tasks, predicted subsequent alcohol consumption after one month. Zech et al. [24] indicated that the null finding related to inhibitory control may be attributed to the measure showing greater fluctuations at shorter time intervals demonstrated by one of their previous studies [20]. Overall, these studies highlight a growing body of research demonstrating the utility of examining momentary fluctuations of inhibitory control in alcohol users. However, how inhibitory control fluctuates on a daily or momentary basis, and what role this mechanism plays in the short term in early stages of alcohol use, remains unclear.

According to temporal self-regulation theory (TST) [25], a conflict between one's intentions (based on long-term goals) and temporal contingencies (immediate reward, short-term outcomes) can lead to maladaptive health behaviors. The TST framework can be used to inform our understanding of hazardous drinking such that one's self-regulatory capacity (i.e., perceived and inhibitory control) will ultimately determine whether one's planned use or cue-driven craving will prevail at a given moment. Furthermore, the TST framework builds on the Theory of Planned Behavior [26], which has been widely applied to health-related behaviors, including alcohol use, where intentions and perceived behavioral control have been strongly linked to consumption [27]. More recently, self-report ESM studies have extended these frameworks to the momentary level, examining the associations between perceived control and planned use with alcohol consumption. Most notably, Remmerswaal et al. [28] found in a seven-day ESM study of 172 regular drinkers that lower perceived control (b = -3.40, p = .012) and higher craving (b = 18.22, p < .001) at random assessments prospectively preceded drinking episodes, and that both were associated with the number of drinks consumed during drinking episodes.

However, that study did not examine the role of inhibitory control alongside these other selfregulatory predictors in momentary and daily changes in alcohol use. In order to fully capture all aspects of self-regulatory capacity when measuring fluctuations in daily drinking, both one's perceived control and one's level of response inhibition should be assessed [18].

Regular drinkers generally retain greater capacity for intentional control, making this population particularly informative for examining early vulnerabilities in self-regulation prior to the onset of dependence. Neurocognitive changes in reward, stress, and control systems progressively diminish voluntary regulation as drinking levels increase [16]. Inhibitory control impairments are more consistently observed among dependent or heavy-drinking samples than among regular drinkers, suggesting a key role for drinking severity [13, 29]. Yet, we still do not know whether during the moments that regular drinkers choose to drink they exhibit the retained capacity of inhibitory control.

Taken together, the existing literature highlights two key gaps. First, it remains unclear whether momentary fluctuations in inhibitory control are concurrently associated with drinking in regular drinkers, above and beyond well-established self-regulation indicators of drinking (e.g., perceived control, craving, and planned use), particularly when drinking is planned.

Second, perceived control, inhibitory control, planned use, and craving have rarely been examined together or across multiple time scales at both the between-and within-person level.

Therefore, the present study's multimodal approach provides additional insight into whether combining both measures is indeed feasible given the added participant burden, and into questions that a self-report measure alone cannot answer. We aimed to assess these gaps by using a four-day mobile ESM study administering random self-report assessments followed by an inhibitory control behavioral task. Using Bayesian multilevel models, we evaluated both withinand between-person associations across random prompts (momentary model) and daily aggregates (daily model) to examine the role of perceived control, craving, planned use, and inhibitory control errors (measured by the Go/No-Go task) in relation to alcohol consumption in a large sample of regular (weekly minimum) drinkers.

At the within-person level, we hypothesized that moments characterized by poorer inhibitory control (i.e., more inhibition errors on the Go/No-Go task at a given prompt) [17], lower perceived control, higher craving, and higher daily planned drinking would be associated with greater alcohol consumption reported during concurrent random assessments (RAs) [28, 30].

At the between-person level, we expected similar patterns, with individuals showing on average poorer inhibitory control, higher craving, and higher planned use reporting higher consumption, though we anticipated these effects might be weaker given prior ESM evidence that betweenperson effects are often less robust than within-person ones [6, 10, 31]. In addition, we examined whether within-and between-person associations differed across momentary and daily time scales; these analyses were exploratory.

Materials and Methods

Participants.

The present study included a convenience sample of 142 participants (40 males, 102 females; M = 33.30 years, SD = 14.76, range = 18-77). Eligibility criteria included: legal drinking age (18+), self-reported regular alcohol use (defined as more than one alcoholic drink per week), Dutch language proficiency, and possession of a smartphone. Participants were recruited through the researchers' social networks and invited via email. Recruitment started after receiving ethics approval on May 3, 2018, and continued through the end of data collection, which was completed on October 28, 2018. Those interested were provided with a detailed information letter including instructions on how to download the study applications to their personal smartphone devices. Participants were informed of the voluntary nature of the study, including the right to withdraw at any time, and confidentiality regarding the researchers' use of their personal data. Once they joined the study, participants could enter a raffle to win part of a 400-euro sum divided into six prizes (two of 100 euros, four of 50 euros). This study was approved by the Erasmus University Rotterdam Faculty Ethics Committee of the Erasmus School of Social and Behavioral Sciences.

Procedure. Participants downloaded two applications to their smartphone devices: the RealLife Exp application (LifeData app; www.lifedatacorp.com) for surveys and the Inquisit Player application (Milliseconds app) for the Go/No-Go task. Informed consent was obtained digitally via the LifeData app, followed by a baseline survey on demographics, alcohol use, and impulsivity, which participants also filled out through the app. Participants were instructed to start the study on a Thursday and continue for a total of four consecutive days through Sunday (participants who started on a later day had their four-day window shifted accordingly). Three daily prompts were randomly administered between 10 in the morning and 10 in the evening (one around 11:00, one around 16:00 and one around 20:00), in which they were given a maximum of 90 minutes to complete a given survey. After the 90-minute expiration, the survey would disappear and be recorded as a missed response. The random prompt was followed by a link to complete a Go/No-Go behavioral task in the Milliseconds app. Participants were also instructed to complete a self-initiated alcohol assessment whenever they consumed alcohol during the study period but were not immediately prompted with a random notification. However, self-initiated responses were not included in the present analyses as they did not coincide with the randomly administered Go/No-Go tasks that followed the random assessments. Participants were instructed to continue with their usual alcohol consumption throughout the study period.

Baseline Measure: Alcohol Use Problems.

The Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) [32, 33]; Dutch translation [34] was used to evaluate the severity of alcohol use. In the current sample, the AUDIT showed good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = .81), which is comparable to a previous report that also used the AUDIT to assess hazardous drinking (α = .75) [17]. Detailed item content and scoring are reported in S1 Appendix (section 1.1).

ESM Measure: Craving.

Participants rated their craving for alcohol at the current moment on a 0 to 100-point Visual Analog Scale (VAS), ranging from 0 (no urge) to 100 (strongest urge).

ESM Measure: Perceived Control.

Participants rated their perceived ability to control their drinking with a single-item measure: "How much control do you feel you have over your drinking?" Responses were recorded on a VAS of 0 (no control) to 100 (a lot of control).

ESM Measure: Alcohol Consumption.

During morning random assessments (RAs), participants were asked to report the number of glasses of alcohol they consumed during the previous night (one standard glass equivalent to about 10 g of pure alcohol) [32]. The afternoon and evening RAs asked participants to report the number of glasses they consumed in the previous two hours up until filling out the prompt.

ESM Measure: Planned Use.

During the morning RA, participants were asked to estimate the total number of drinks they were planning to consume for the upcoming day.

The Go/No-Go Task. Participants were instructed to complete a five-minute (100 trial) adapted mobile Go/No-Go task [35] (Fig 1) using the Inquisit Player application (Milliseconds app) on their smartphone, in which they needed to press the screen for green (go) targets and refrain from pressing for blue (no-go) targets. Vertical cues were followed by green (go) targets in 80% of cases, while horizontal cues were followed by blue (no-go) targets in 80% of cases. In line with common practice in Go/No-Go and ESM quality control, we excluded blocks with accuracy below 60% (slightly above chance, 50%), indicating unreliable engagement with the task [23, 36].

Additional task exclusion criteria are reported in S1 Appendix (section 1.2).

Fig 1. Cued Go/No-Go task.

A fixation cross was presented on the screen for 800 ms, followed by a blank screen for 500 ms. Next, a white rectangle cue was positioned vertically or horizontally in the center of the screen for 100-500 ms, followed by either a green (go) target or a blue (no-go) target. In 80% of trials, vertical cues predicted green (go) targets and horizontal cues predicted blue (no-go) targets.

Statistical Analysis.

Before fitting our daily and momentary multilevel models examining fluctuations in alcohol use, we first examined how each variable of interest changed on average across the four-day ESM study. We ran a series of Bayesian mixed-effects models using Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling in R version 4.5.3 [37] using the brms package (version 2.23.0) [38], in which each dependent variable (momentary alcohol use, craving, perceived control, and planned alcohol use) was predicted from elapsed time since the first RA of the study, with random intercepts for participant. Inhibitory control was not included in these linear time trend models because Go/No-Go task performance across prompts was already examined separately, and no meaningful linear trend was observed.

Next, we ran two Bayesian multilevel models at the momentary (prompt) level and the daily level. Given that we found no credible evidence of any trend with alcohol consumption over elapsed time of the study, we did not include a cumulative time variable in the multilevel models.

However, we did include a covariate to account for the interval since the previous prompt in the momentary model. No measure of time was included in the daily model. Before fitting these models, we examined patterns of missing data, which indicated the data were not missing completely at random (see section 1.3 in S1 Appendix). We therefore accounted for the missingness using multiple imputation by chained equations (mice package, version 3.19.0) [39], which assumes data are missing at random. The original sample comprised 143 individuals who completed a baseline assessment and enrolled in the ESM phase of the study by downloading the necessary digital applications. One participant was dropped due to poor Go/No-Go task performance (more than 50% invalid blocks), resulting in a final analytic sample of 142 participants. Of the final 142 participants, 11 individuals did not complete any random assessment within the four-day time window (of the 11, 6 completed only self-initiated assessments whereas 5 did not provide any valid ESM responses after joining the ESM phase), resulting in a total of 131 included in the daily assessment model analyses (430 person-days, 1 to 4 days each). The momentary model, however, required at least two random assessments to account for the autoregressive (AR₁) residual structure, resulting in a final sample of 124 participants (1,046 observations). Any assessments that were completed outside of a participant's four-consecutive-day window (based on the day of their first valid random assessment until the end of the fourth consecutive day) were excluded from the final analyses. In both models, time-varying predictors were decomposed into within-person (state) and between-person (trait) components using personmean centering. The person-mean centering was computed in R for the time-varying predictors (craving, perceived control, inhibition errors, and time since the previous prompt) before the models were fitted. These were computed within each of the 30 imputed datasets and then standardized. Planned use, which was only reported at morning prompts, was treated as a day-level predictor: the morning report was used at all three of that day's prompts in the momentary analyses.

Days with a missing morning report were imputed once per person-day rather than at each prompt, to avoid introducing within-day variation in planned use that did not come from the observed data (section 1.3 in S1 Appendix). The momentary model added an autoregressive (AR₁) structure to account for residual temporal dependence between consecutive ESM prompts [40], while the daily model used aggregated momentary variables to examine average day-level consumption. Full model equations, prior specifications, sampler settings, missingness rates and imputation procedure, and rationale for the random-effects structure are reported in S1 Appendix (sections 1.3 to 1.6).

Convergence was assessed using trace plots, Gelman-Rubin statistics (R ( ), and inspection of divergent transitions on representative single-imputation fits; parameter estimates were pooled across all 30 imputations using Rubin's rules [38, 41]. For both models, trace plots indicated good mixing and stationarity of the chains, per-imputation Gelman-Rubin parameters were within acceptable limits (R ( ≤ 1.05), and no divergent transitions or sampler warnings were observed.

Posterior predictive checks indicated reasonable daily model fit; however, the Gaussian likelihood did not fully capture the right-skewed distribution of momentary alcohol use. Using a Gaussian likelihood estimation keeps the model estimates in values of drink units, which makes them directly comparable across momentary and daily timescales, as well as keeping in line with previous approaches to modeling alcohol consumption linearly [23]. Another advantage to the Gaussian likelihood estimation is the use of the actual imputed values as opposed to the nonnegative integer outcome requirement needed for a negative binomial distribution (using rounded integers instead of exact values). However, given the wide dispersion of the current study's data, one can argue for a negative binomial distribution instead. Therefore, we ran sensitivity analyses refitting the models with a negative binomial distribution to assess whether the credible effects held. The negative binomial models yielded similar results, with two exceptions: the small withinperson craving association in the momentary model was no longer credible under the negative binomial distribution, whereas age, which was not previously credible under the Gaussian likelihood, became credible with the negative binomial distribution (β = 0.21, 95% CrI [0.07, 0.34]; section 2.0 and Tables B to D in S1 Appendix). Three additional sensitivity analyses are discussed in further detail in S1 Appendix. These included the impact of omitting the predictors inhibitory control and planned use (both of which had high levels of missing data) from the momentary and daily models (section 3.0, Tables E and F), examining the daily model with an added weekend covariate (section 4.0, Table G), and a correlation matrix to check for multicollinearity between the predictor variables (section 5.0, Tables H to J). In addition to assessing the Bayesian R² for model variance, we computed the variance explained at level 1 (within-person) and level 2 (between-person), calculated as the proportional reduction in variance relative to an intercept-only model fitted on the same imputed data using the same sampler settings and priors [42]. The momentary intercept-only model retained the AR₁ residual structure so that the residual variance is comparable between the intercept-only and full model. Model parameters were interpreted based on posterior means and 95% credible intervals; intervals excluding zero were regarded as evidence of a credible association, whereas intervals including zero lacked credible evidence [43, 44].

Discussion

The current study had two primary aims: the first was to examine whether momentary fluctuations in inhibitory control are concurrently associated with alcohol use in regular drinkers, above and beyond perceived control, craving and planned use, particularly when drinking is planned. The second aim of the study was to examine within-person fluctuations and betweenperson differences in perceived control, inhibitory control, planned use and craving together across momentary and daily time scales of drinking. Perceived control emerged as the most robust credible correlate of drinking in both the daily and momentary models. In other words, at the between-person level, individuals who generally reported lower perceived control over their drinking were more likely to report higher levels of alcohol consumption in both the daily and momentary models. Additionally, at the within-person level, moments characterized by lower perceived control than usual were also associated with greater alcohol use, although this association held true only in the momentary model. Planned use also emerged as a consistent self-regulatory correlate but only at the between-person level in both the daily and momentary models. Within-person differences for planned use were not associated with daily or momentary consumption. There were three unexpected findings based on our initial hypotheses. First, there was no evidence of a credible association between inhibitory control (measured by errors on the Go/No-Go task) with alcohol consumption at either the momentary or daily level. Second, the momentary model demonstrated a small yet credible negative within-person association between craving and alcohol use, whereas no evidence of an association was found at the daily level.

Third, we did not expect to find associations mainly at the between-person level, with no withinperson associations for any predictor in the daily model. Of the covariates, gender was credible at the daily level only, with females reporting lower consumption than males (β = -0.57, 95% CrI [-1.14, -0.005]), and time since the previous prompt was credible in the momentary model (longer intervals since the previous prompt were associated with more drinking; β = 0.31, 95% CrI [0.20, 0.42]), whereas age was not credible in either model.

Perceived control emerged as the most consistent correlate of alcohol consumption across the current analyses. This pattern is consistent with the Theory of Planned Behavior framework, where perceived behavioral control is considered a key determinant of both intentions and behavior, reflecting individuals' perceived ability to regulate their behavior [26, 27]. The present findings are also consistent with previous ESM research on alcohol use by Remmerswaal et al. [28], who found that lower perceived control was prospectively associated with a greater likelihood of drinking. Specifically, regular drinkers reported greater impairments with selfcontrol in the assessments directly followed by drinking compared to those that did not lead to subsequent drinking. The current study findings extend this prospective association by showing that lower perceived control is associated with concurrent drinking at random assessments in a multilevel model alongside craving, planned use, and inhibitory control, separating within-and between-person levels at momentary and daily time scales. These findings conceptually support the notion that self-regulation shapes drinking behavior at both trait (i.e., stable individual differences in perceived control over regulating one's drinking behavior) and state levels (i.e., momentary changes in perceived control associated with drinking within the context of one's daily life). Nevertheless, the present findings do not speak to a directional association between perceived control and later alcohol use but indicate an increased risk of alcohol consumption in moments in which individuals report reduced levels of perceived control. Thus, the present study highlights the need to identify moments of lower perceived control as an indicator of concurrent drinking, which may be useful in future studies targeting momentary efforts to support individuals aiming to reduce alcohol consumption.

Importantly, perceived control reflects a broader subjective appraisal of one's ability to regulate drinking, whereas Go/No-Go performance assesses the narrower cognitive ability to withhold a dominant response. The finding that perceived control, but not performance-based inhibitory control, was associated with alcohol consumption may therefore reflect differences in what these measures assess rather than the relative importance of these overall constructs for drinking behavior. It is also important to note that self-report and behavioral measures of inhibition and self-regulation are often only weakly associated, as they appear to capture different aspects of self-regulatory functioning [47, 48]. This interpretation is consistent with Hachenberger et al. [18], who found that within-person fluctuations in Go/No-Go performance were only weakly related to momentary self-reports of self-control. Thus, in everyday drinking contexts, individuals' subjective sense of control may be more closely associated with alcohol use than momentary variation in response withholding.

The analysis examining inhibitory control, measured by inhibitory (commission) errors on the Go/No-Go task, did not provide credible evidence of an association with alcohol consumption at the daily level. For the momentary model, the within-person association was in the hypothesized positive direction (more errors were associated with more drinking) but fell just short of credibility (Gaussian: β = 0.14, 95% CrI [-0.01, 0.29]; negative binomial: β = 0.10, 95% CrI [-0.001, 0.21]), with roughly 97% of the posterior above zero. The absence of credible evidence may be due to the following reasons. First, accuracy scores indicate that the Go/No-Go task was relatively easy to perform (average accuracy ~98%), which may have resulted in a ceiling effect and hence in restricted variance to inform predictions of alcohol use and detect individual differences in inhibitory control [17, 49]. Another possibility is that problems with momentary inhibitory control may be less salient among regular drinkers than in heavier or dependent drinkers because their inhibitory control capacity is not chronically impaired (as is often observed in heavier or dependent use) and they are therefore less likely to perform poorly on momentary Go/No-Go tasks [1, 16, 50, 51]. However, future studies should explore whether this is also true for more general trait levels of inhibitory control among regular drinkers.

Furthermore, compliance on the inhibitory control task was relatively low (40.4%; 635 of 1,572 scheduled prompts, or 60.3% of completed random assessments) compared to that of the ESM random assessments (67.0%; 1,053 of 1,572). In particular, participants in the current study were more likely to skip the task at evening prompts (56% missing vs. 40% missed in the afternoon and 32% in the morning) and at prompts where they reported higher levels of drinking, higher craving, and lower perceived control (Table A in S1 Appendix). While this compliance was low, the completed task blocks showed a 98.1% go accuracy rate and an overall 0.8% rate of careless responding. The 40.4% completion rate stands in stark contrast to the 92% completion rate in Jones et al. [23], which involved a heavier drinking sample but used one application that combined the ESM survey and the behavioral task. The added burden of needing to go into a separate application for the Go/No-Go task likely contributed to the noticeably lower task compliance compared to the higher ESM compliance in the current study.

Although individuals who generally planned to drink more also tended to consume more alcohol, planned use was not associated with same-day variation in drinking within individuals at either the momentary or daily level. This does not align with previous research consistently showing that intentions are associated with same-day drinking [8, 10, 23]. The daily within-person estimates of planned use were imprecise but in the expected direction, likely a result of the study's limited power to detect within-person differences, as each participant contributed at most four days and 31.6% of person-days were missing a morning planned use report. Whether someone's drinking plans are carried out on a given day is susceptible to changes related to one's social context, opportunity and access to alcohol, mood, and perceived control, none of which were captured by the single morning report of planned use. The current findings can be interpreted within the TST framework, which emphasizes the role that temporal contingencies (i.e., immediate rewards and short-term outcomes) have in widening the gap between one's intentions and the behavioral outcome (alcohol use). Self-regulatory capacity (perceived control and inhibitory control measured in the current study) is proposed to matter most when there is a conflict between one's intentions (i.e., planned use) and behavioral tendencies (i.e., craving).

Therefore, when drinking is intended and there is no conflict between one's intentions and behavioral tendencies, there is no need for self-regulatory capacity to come into play, and the drinking that follows does not necessarily indicate any failure of inhibition. Even so, perceived control remained associated with drinking, over and above craving, planned use and inhibitory control, in the current sample of regular drinkers. Unlike the Go/No-Go task, which measures response performance, perceived control is linked to both intentions and expected drinking. This may explain why perceived control, unlike inhibitory control, remained associated with drinking after accounting for both planned use and craving.

The unexpected negative association between craving and alcohol consumption in the momentary model may reflect the timing of random prompts rather than a true suppression effect. While most ESM literature supports a positive association between craving and alcohol use, this discrepancy likely reflects a fundamental difference in assessment design. More specifically, craving was captured at the moment of assessment, while alcohol use was measured over a broader reference window (the previous night at morning prompts; the past two hours at afternoon and evening prompts). A participant's craving level at the moment of assessment may not reflect their craving state throughout the full window over which drinking was reported. A participant who drank earlier in the two-hour window may therefore report elevated alcohol use alongside reduced current craving (a post-consumption state), while a participant whose craving had not yet translated into drinking may report elevated current craving alongside lower recent use (an anticipatory state).

Consistent with this, Burton and Jones [17] found that greater craving intensity was associated with lower concurrent alcohol consumption (β = -0.04, 95% CI [-0.06, -0.01]), while still predicting subsequent use, suggesting a timing-related negative association.

More broadly, existing literature supports a dose-response relationship between craving and drinking severity, suggesting that craving may act as a proximal driver of alcohol use in heavier use or alcohol-dependent populations [1, 2, 52]. The small credible negative within-person craving association observed in the primary Gaussian model was no longer credible under the negative binomial specification (see Table D in S1 Appendix), further supporting the interpretation that this association is likely attributable to temporal misalignment.

Strengths.

Using an ESM design, we were able to capture more ecologically valid data on alcohol consumption as it occurs in the moment in one's daily life within one's natural environment. Participants were instructed to complete the four-day study starting on a Thursday in order to maximize the number of drinking opportunities by including the entire weekend in the study period. The incorporation of a Go/No-Go task coupled with an ESM design adds to a small group of studies that have used this multimodal approach in examining daily and momentary alcohol use [17, 23, 24]. By incorporating this kind of standardized behavioral task with an ESM design, we were able to get a more complete picture of how multiple self-regulatory and impulsive processes are associated with fluctuations in momentary alcohol use.

Limitations and Future Directions.

Nevertheless, certain limitations should be taken into consideration with the current findings. First, the slight mismatch between the time reference used to measure craving and alcohol may have influenced the negative association that emerged at the momentary level. Second, although participants ranged in age from 18 to 77, the sample mean of 33.30 years suggests between-person findings may be most generalizable to younger drinkers, and caution should be exercised when extrapolating to older populations. Third, a possible limitation was the four-day study duration, a relatively short time to fully capture the fluctuations in momentary and daily predictors of alcohol use that might have emerged over a longer study period, especially among infrequent users. Fewer days per person lead to wider within-person intervals, and 61 of the 131 participants contributed fewer than four days of data.

Therefore, it is possible that the lack of any credible within-person associations in the daily model is a power-related issue. Fourth, evidence for the use of behavioral tasks such as the Go/No-Go in predicting real-world outcomes is still limited compared to that from self-report measures [20, 48]; therefore, additional research examining their utility and real-world applicability is still needed. If future studies opt for a multimodal assessment protocol (i.e., combining ESM and a behavioral task), we recommend using one digital mobile application to administer all assessments, minimizing potential missed responses that may arise when participants are asked to leave one application to finish with another. Fifth, a notable limitation was the level of missing data for planned use (missing on 31.6% of person-days, 136 of 430) given that it was only assessed during morning prompts.

Future studies examining planned substance use should consider expanding the study period or incorporating the question about planned use into all momentary prompts.

Lastly, we did not examine conventional lagged associations because the timing of the ESM assessments did not provide consistently aligned prospective time windows. Given the irregular spacing between prompts, linking predictors at one assessment to alcohol use at the next could result in overlapping exposure windows or leave substantial periods of drinking unmeasured. The current analyses therefore address which states co-occur with greater alcohol use within individuals rather than which states prospectively predict subsequent drinking. As such, our findings may help identify candidate time-varying correlates for future JITAIs [3, 53], although prospective evidence is needed before these variables can be considered reliable intervention triggers.

Conclusion

Taken together, perceived control was the most consistent correlate of alcohol consumption across levels and timescales. Planned use was associated with drinking between individuals but not with same-day variation within individuals, while craving and performancebased inhibitory control were not robustly associated with alcohol use. These findings suggest that subjective perceptions of regulation may be more closely linked to everyday drinking than momentary inhibitory capacity in regular drinkers, and that alcohol consumption does not necessarily reflect inhibitory failure when drinking is intentional. Future research is needed to establish temporal direction and evaluate whether perceived control can function as a useful tailoring variable for adaptive interventions.
