Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Challenges associated with supporting a child with a neurodevelopmental condition (NDC) may mean that wellbeing among these families is subject to different dynamics than among other families.

In this study, we modelled a shared basis for family wellbeing in the Norwegian Mother, Father, and Child Cohort Study (MoBa) using measures of life satisfaction, depression, and anxiety.

Next, we tested for structural differences in the shared basis of family wellbeing between different groups of families of children diagnosed with an NDC and comparison families without diagnoses using multi-group structural equation modelling. Finally, we examined how measures of children's, mothers', and fathers' genetic liabilities to neurodevelopmental conditions and related traits were associated with wellbeing and whether these associations differed by group.

We identified two well-fitting models that parameterised a shared basis for family wellbeing. Structurally, family wellbeing was similar between almost all NDC-diagnosis groups and the comparison group. The exception was the autism-diagnosis group, where the pattern of links between different family members' wellbeing factors differed significantly to that of the comparison group. Any other group differences were related to the overall level of wellbeing or lower-level patterns of reporting on life satisfaction, depression, and anxiety. Mothers', fathers', and children's indices of genetic liability to various traits were associated with wellbeing, typically via their own wellbeing or a shared family wellbeing factor. However, we did not observe any differences in genetic associations between NDC-diagnosis and comparison families.

A similar, shared familial basis for wellbeing between families with and without children with NDC diagnoses indicates that the specific dynamics of wellbeing in families remain consistent, even as levels of overall wellbeing may differ. This finding may have implications for the kind of support that can be offered to improve wellbeing among families of individuals diagnosed with NDCs.

1. Background

Families of children with a neurodevelopmental condition (NDC) often face unique challenges involving understanding and supporting their neurodivergent child's particular strengths and difficulties, navigating and advocating for their child in complex systems of health, care, and educational support, and managing time for work and social activities (Tint & Weiss, 2016; Whitehead et al., 2015). Parents and siblings of children with an NDC often report lower wellbeing than parents of typically developing children (Hamlyn-Wright et al., 2007; Lamsal & Ungar, 2021; Raina et al., 2005; Tint & Weiss, 2016). Moreover, the specific dynamics of family wellbeing may differ in the context of NDCs (Leone et al., 2016; Peer & Hillman, 2014; Susmarini et al., 2024).

NDCs are a group of conditions involving differences in how the brain develops and functions, typically emerging in childhood (Thapar et al., 2017). Many children with NDCs, especially in environments that are not well matched to their needs, may experience sensory overload, difficulties with emotion regulation, distress-related aggressive actions, or selfinjurious behaviours (Thapar et al., 2017). These variations in behaviour and functioning can shape how families navigate everyday life and may call for additional resilience and adaptive strategies, with potential implications for overall family wellbeing (Garrido et al., 2025). Many previous studies indicate that parents of children with an NDC experience on-average lower quality of life, and increased stress and mental health difficulties such as depression and anxiety (Masefield et al., 2020; Scherer et al., 2019; Schnabel et al., 2020). However, the wellbeing of individual family members cannot be understood in isolation, but should instead be viewed as part of a broader system of interaction between family members (Whitchurch & Constantine, 1993). Given that families often provide crucial support to individuals across the lifespan (Thomas et al., 2017), understanding the specific dynamics of family wellbeing in the context of NDCs is an important empirical challenge.

Given the high heritability of NDCs (Faraone & Larsson, 2019; Lichtenstein et al., 2010; Tick et al., 2016), some researchers have used genetic information to try to understand family wellbeing in the context of NDCs. For example, a recent study found that parental polygenic indices (PGIs) representing a measure of their genetic liability to higher wellbeing were associated with fewer reported emotional, behavioural, and temperament difficulties in offspring during early childhood (Wootton et al., 2025). These outcomes are elevated among those with NDCs (Thapar et al., 2017) as well as being linked to mental health diagnoses later in childhood and adolescence (Bakken et al., 2025; Lancefield et al., 2016; Loth et al., 2014).

Similarly, there is some evidence of indirect genetic effects by parental PGIs for NDCs for children's emotional and behavioural difficulties (Allegrini et al., 2025). However, there is a clear lack of research using genetic liabilities to NDCs and related traits in both parents and children to examine the dynamics of family wellbeing as a whole.

In this study, we aim to investigate the shared basis of family wellbeing in the context of diagnosed NDCs and related genetic liabilities. To this end, we use questionnaire, genomic, and health-registry data of Norwegian families to A) model the shared basis of family wellbeing; B) understand how genetic variants associated with NDCs and related traits are linked to family wellbeing; C) test for structural differences in the shared basis of wellbeing between families of children with an NDC diagnosis and comparison families without these diagnoses; and D) assess whether the genetic contributions are different between these groups.

2. Methods

The hypotheses and methods were preregistered (Glaser et al., 2025). Deviations from the preregistration are listed in Table S1.

2.1 Study sample

The Norwegian Mother, Father and Child Cohort Study (MoBa) is a population-based pregnancy cohort study conducted by the Norwegian Institute of Public Health (Brandlistuen et al., 2025).

Participants were recruited from all over Norway from 1999-2008. The women consented to participation in 41% of the pregnancies. The cohort includes approximately 114.500 children, 95.200 mothers and 75.200 fathers. MoBa is regulated by the Norwegian Health Registry Act.

The current study was approved by The Regional Committees for Medical and Health Research Ethics (2016/1702). The MoBa dataset contains genetic data from mother, father, and child trios (Paltiel et al., 2014). We used genotyped data that has undergone phasing, imputation, and quality control procedures as outlined in Corfield et al. (2026).

We grouped the full sample into families by linking together all children of the same mother.

After assignment of an index child in each family (see below under 2.2.2 NDC diagnoses), we randomly selected one sibling per family (where available) to be retained for analysis in a sibling sample. Where available, we included data of the index child's father.

2.2 Measures

2.2.1 Outcomes

To measure family wellbeing, we used scales for life satisfaction, depression, and anxiety for each family member. All children's and mothers' items were reported by mothers in the year the child turned 8, whereas fathers' items were reported by fathers themselves in 2015 (regardless of their child's age). See supplementary materials for details on outcome measure scales and internal consistency. For all outcomes, scale scores were calculated by calculating a mean score of all non-missing items and multiplying this score by the number of items in the scale, if at least half the items were non-missing. All items showed good to excellent internal consistency (Cronbach's α = .75-.94; see Supplementary Table S2).

2.2.2 NDC diagnoses

We identified all children who have received a diagnosis of an NDC at least at two occasions according to NPR and assigned them (or, if multiple children qualified, randomly assigned one) to be the index child. We did this for any NDC, ADHD, and autism. For ADHD and autism, we also created subgroups by removing families of index children with both ADHD and autism diagnosis (i.e., creating ADHD-only and autism-only groups). We also split the any-NDCdiagnosis group into families of earlier (i.e., ≤ 10 years) and later (i.e., > 10 years) diagnosed index children. Finally, we grouped all families together who did not have a child diagnosed with an NDC during follow-up in NPR (henceforth the comparison group) and assigned one child at random from each of these families to act as the "index" child.

2.2.3 Polygenic indices

For each family member, we calculated polygenic indices (PGIs) for a range of neurodevelopmental conditions and related traits. PGI reflect a person's genetic liability for a trait, based on a weighted sum of the effects of common genetic variants on that trait or condition in prior a genome-wide association study (GWAS; Pingault et al., 2022). We calculated PGI based on summary statistics from the most recent well-powered GWAS based on samples European-like ancestry for ADHD (Demontis et al., 2023), autism (Grove et al., 2019), anxiety (Strom et al., 2026), cognitive ability (Savage et al., 2018), depression (Adams et al., 2025), neuroticism (Nagel et al., 2018), schizophrenia (Trubetskoy et al., 2022), and wellbeing (Baselmans et al., 2019) using weights estimated by LDpred-auto model in the LDpred2 software (Privé et al., 2021). These traits were selected as they represent neurodevelopmental conditions or related traits, as well as a range of traits and conditions associated with mental wellbeing. See supplementary materials for more details on polygenic index estimation.

2.3 Analyses

See Figure 1 for an overview of analyses.

2.3.1 Modelling the shared basis of family wellbeing

To parameterise a shared basis of family wellbeing, we used structural equation modelling (SEM) to test whether underlying (latent) family variables could explain the variance-covariance structure of life satisfaction, depression, and anxiety in children, mothers, fathers, and siblings. We specified 5 hypothesised factor models with varying numbers of latent variables and factor structures. These were 1) Single factor; in which all observed variables from all family members loaded on a single common factor; 2) Correlated factors; in which observed variables for each family member loaded on correlated individual factors; 3) Two-level; in which correlations in (2) were replaced with an overarching factor; 4) Domain-specific correlated factors; and 5) Domainspecific two-level; which were structured as per (2) and ( 3), but with factors linked to traits rather than family members. For a graphical overview of these models, see supplementary figures S1-5. All models included the child's sex and birth year, and the sibling's birth year as covariates. We assessed the fit of each model using a chi-square test and a selection of fit indices, including the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), standardised root mean square residual (SRMR), Akaike information criteria (AIC), and Bayesian information criterion (BIC). We considered CFI and TLI values over .95 and RMSEA and SRMR values below .06 to be indicative of good fit. AIC and BIC were used to compare all 5 models, where models with the lowest AIC and BIC values were preferred. All models with acceptable fit were carried forward in our downstream analyses.

2.3.2 Assessing genetic associations with wellbeing

Next, we tested whether family members' genetic liability to a range of neurodevelopmental conditions and related traits predicted latent family wellbeing. To do this, we extended each of our selected models from the previous step, by regressing the highest-order latent wellbeing factor(s) onto fathers', mothers', and children's polygenic indices for ADHD, anxiety, autism, cognitive functioning, depression, neuroticism, schizophrenia, and wellbeing, simultaneously.

For each family member we compared this model to a model in which we constrained all genetic effects of that family member to be 0 and assessed the resultant loss of fit using likelihood ratio tests (LRT). Because LRTs are highly sensitive to small changes in fit when sample sizes are large, we also assessed differences in CFI and RMSEA, where changes exceeding ΔCFI = -.01 or ΔRMSEA = .015 were considered indicative of a substantial loss of fit based on standard benchmarks (Chen, 2007). Significant (LRT) and substantial (ΔCFI/ ΔRMSEA) loss of fit from constraining the genetic effects would indicate that genetic liabilities associated with neurodevelopmental conditions and related traits affect family wellbeing via direct and/or indirect effects.

2.3.3 Comparing family wellbeing among NDC-diagnosis subgroups and the comparison group

To assess whether the structure of family wellbeing was similar for families of children with or without different NDC diagnoses, we ran the models of family wellbeing taken forward from 2.3.1 using a measurement invariance framework (Putnick & Bornstein, 2016) . For each group comparison, we first ran a model in which all parameters were freely estimated. We then progressively constrained the model by forcing first only factor loadings, then additionally latent covariances, observed variable intercepts, and finally all parameters including latent means to be the same between groups. After each additional constraint, we assessed the change in fit using LRT, ΔCFI, and ΔRMSEA according to the thresholds reported in 2.3.2. For each of the NDCdiagnosis groups (see 2.2.2), we performed multigroup comparison against the comparison group. Additionally, we compared the earlier-diagnosed group to the later-diagnosed group.

Comparisons between groups with severely unbalanced sample sizes (i.e., exceeding a 2:1 ratio) risk not being able to detect group differences (Yoon & Lai, 2018). As such, we ran 100 iterations of each model on a random subsample from the larger group matching the sample size (and number of siblings) from the smaller group. We then took the average of the change in fit indices across all models for inference. This was the case for all group comparisons, except for the more balanced earlier-vs. later-diagnosed comparison.

2.3.4 Comparing genetic associations with family wellbeing between NDC-diagnosis subgroups and the comparison group

Finally, we compared models including genetic factors between all groups specified in section 2.3.3. to assess whether genetic effects on the shared basis of family wellbeing are different between different groups of families. For each group comparison, we ran one instance of each model where all parameters were freely estimated, and one where the genetic effects were constrained to be equal between groups. We conducted an LRT and calculated change in fit as described above to assess whether genetic effects on the shared basis of family wellbeing were different between groups.

2.4 Software

All analyses were run in R version 4.4.3. We used phenotools version 0.4.2 (Hannigan et al., 2021) to extract and preprocess variables from MoBa and NPR. Analytic code is available on https://github.com/psychgen/famwb_ndc. We used lavaan version 0.6.20 (Rosseel, 2012; Rosseel et al., 2026) to perform structural equation modelling.

4. Discussion

Here we examined the shared basis of family wellbeing and the extent to which its structure differs between different NDC-diagnosis subgroups and a comparison group in the MoBa cohort. We identified two theoretical models of shared family wellbeing that explained the correlations between observational measures of wellbeing in children, mothers, fathers, and siblings in our data adequately. We found associations of family members' genetic liabilities to neurodevelopmental conditions and related traits on both their own and, in the case of mothers, other family members' wellbeing. Finally, we found that family wellbeing is broadly structurally and genetically similar between families of children with diagnosed NDCs and those with children without diagnoses of these conditions. The primary exception was that the covariance structure of family wellbeing was different between families of autistic children and families of typically developing children, although this seemed to be driven by relatively small differences between the two groups across all correlations rather than one or a few significant differences. Otherwise, group differences were found only in terms of the levels of observed anxiety, depression, and life satisfaction, rather than the way family members' wellbeing was interrelated.

4.1 Family wellbeing builds on individual family members' wellbeing

From our five pre-specified models, only the two models with wellbeing factors for each family member fitted the data well. In contrast, the models in which the observed variables loaded either directly onto a latent factor for overall family wellbeing, or onto domain-specific (i.e., family anxiety, family depression, and family life satisfaction) latent factors, did not fit the data well. This suggests that family wellbeing associates between family members at a higher level, rather than at a domain-specific level. In other words, higher anxiety for one family member's anxiety might not be specifically associated higher anxiety of other family members, but instead might be related to their broader wellbeing via the individual's own general wellbeing. This is in line with findings from a previous study suggesting that the associations between family members' mental health symptoms are not domain-specific (Jiang et al., 2025).

4.2 Genetic contributions to family wellbeing

Our genetic models showed several different genetic contributions to family wellbeing. In our correlated factors model, we found associations between several parental and child PGIs and their own wellbeing factors. Children's wellbeing was negatively associated with their own PGI for cognitive functioning and negatively with their own PGI for neuroticism, whereas parents' wellbeing was negatively associated with their own PGIs for depression and neuroticism, and positively with their PGI for wellbeing. We found cross-family member associations for mothers only, with maternal PGIs for autism and depression being negatively associated with father's wellbeing, the maternal PGI for cognitive functioning being negatively associated with index children's wellbeing, and the maternal PGI for wellbeing being positively associated with index children's and siblings' wellbeing. We also observed similar associations between these maternal PGIs and the higher-level family wellbeing factor in the Two-level model.

It is notable that different measures of genetic liability were associated with parents and children's own wellbeing, which could reflect changes in genetic contributions to wellbeing across the lifespan. This is in line with findings from previous research where the associations between specific PGIs and emotional and behavioural problems change over time (Bakken et al., 2025; Shakeshaft et al., 2024). However, in our study, these differences can also be explained by parents self-reporting on their wellbeing whereas children's wellbeing was reported by mothers using a different set of scales. The finding that maternal PGIs were predominant in explaining variation in shared familial wellbeing is also somewhat consistent with previous results from Jiang et al. (2025), who found a central role of maternal depressive feelings within a network analysis of family mental health. An important caveat, again, is that mothers reported on their own, the index child's, and sibling's wellbeing, meaning that maternal PGI effects on family wellbeing might also capture variance that is due to reporting tendencies, it is notable that maternal PGI effects on fathers' (self-reported) wellbeing were also found in the model where these were tested for.

4.3 Differences in wellbeing of NDC-diagnosis subgroups and the comparison group

We found that the structure of family wellbeing, as modelled using our data, varied relatively little in the context of neurodevelopmental conditions. The exception to this general rule was that wellbeing factors of individual members of families from the autism-diagnosis group were differentially correlated than those of comparison families. The common pattern of differences that appeared to drive this result was a reduction in the magnitude of the associations between mothers', fathers', and index children's wellbeing and that of the nonindex siblings (.30/-.05/.22), relative to the same parameters from the model of comparison families (.37/.14/.60), although the index child-father correlation was also changed markedly (.31 vs .12). To interpret this finding, it is first important to emphasise that none of these parameters were directly statistically compared; hence, their collective effect on the relative fit of the constrained vs. unconstrained models is the only robust basis for inference. Moreover, the apparent importance of the sibling correlations indicate the effect should be interpreted with caution, since this was among the smallest groups in the models (N=725 for the autism group). With these caveats aside, taken at face value this finding indicates a somewhat different basis for wellbeing among families of autistic children that could warrant further investigation.

While structurally similar in terms of wellbeing, we found that families from the other NDC-diagnosed groups differed to families in the comparison group by reporting, on average, lower wellbeing than families from the comparison group. This is in line with previous research showing that caregivers of children diagnosed with an NDC report lower quality of life and increased mental health difficulties (Garrido et al., 2025; Masefield et al., 2020; Scherer et al., 2019; Schnabel et al., 2020). For the NDC-diagnosis subgroups, we observed that the baseline scores for observed life satisfaction, depression, and anxiety were different between each subgroup and comparison families. That is, for similar levels of latent wellbeing, questionnaire item responses were different for subgroups of families of NDC-diagnosed children than comparison families.

We also observed that wellbeing for families of later-diagnosed (i.e., later than 10 years) children is higher than that for families of earlier-diagnosed children, which may partially be explained by early-diagnosed children experiencing more difficulties in early childhood (Hrdlicka et al., 2024), although this relationship is likely confounded in complex ways (Glaser et al., 2026) -including the relationship between earlier diagnosis and earlier provision of support. However, these results could also be due to the timing of the questionnaires, as index children's and mother's questionnaires were taken when the index child was 8 years old. Within the same cohort, but focusing only on children with an autism diagnosis, the observational relationship between age at autism diagnosis and wellbeing show a similar pattern at age 8, but an inverse pattern when outcomes are measured at 14 years (Glaser et al., 2026).

Finally, we found no differences in genetic associations with wellbeing for any of the group comparisons. While an absence of evidence for differences is not equivalent to demonstrating genetic similarity, a lack of genetic differences is consistent with the broad structural similarity described above.

4.4 Limitations

This study is subject to some limitations. First, the cohort includes an overrepresentation of families with higher socioeconomic backgrounds and education levels, and these participants have higher response rates over time (Vejrup et al., 2022). As such, it is important to consider potential selection and attrition biases (Biele et al., 2019). Second, mothers reported on their own, but also the index children's and siblings' outcomes, which could introduce common method bias and inflate correlations between variables because they were reported on by the same person (Podsakoff et al., 2024). Third, for each group of family members, all outcomes came from a single point in time, although the difference between those time points may have differed substantially between families. That is, index children's and mothers' outcomes were measured when the index child was 8 years old, fathers' outcomes were measured in 2015 regardless of their child's age, and siblings' outcomes were measured when the sibling was 8 years old. Although we tried to adjust for these differences by including index children's and siblings' birth year as covariates, this adjustment is unlikely to have perfectly accounted for these measurement discrepancies. Fourth, complete ascertainment of sibling data was not possible, meaning siblings were not completely representative of the true sibling population, limiting our ability to confidently interpret sibling effects. Finally, because this was a crosssectional design, it is possible that associations may not persist over time. As such, future research should aim to employ longitudinal designs, potentially looking at factors associated with stability and change in family wellbeing over time.

5. Conclusion

In this study, we found that family wellbeing in the MoBa cohort is best described by models with interrelated latent wellbeing factors for each individual family member, suggesting a familial basis for wellbeing. We found that family wellbeing modelled in this way was broadly genetically and structurally similar regardless of whether or not families included a child with a diagnosed neurodevelopmental condition. Where differences in these families were observed, they primarily involved lower overall wellbeing or differential reporting on constituent measures of wellbeing (life satisfaction, depression, and anxiety). The finding that family members' wellbeing is intrinsically interlinked -and in a broadly consistent way irrespective of diagnosed neurodevelopmental conditions among children -suggests that support is likely to be more effective when the family is considered as a whole, rather than focussed on a single family member, so that index children, parents, and siblings are all considered. Families of children with an NDC reported lower wellbeing on average, which may reflect differences in the specific stressors faced by these families, rather than differences in how family wellbeing is structured.

Longitudinal studies are needed to establish the direction of influence between family members and to identify intra-familial (e.g., caregiving demands) and extra-familial stressors (e.g., navigating complex systems of care, stigma, advocating for a child's needs) that might impact family wellbeing.

Key points and relevance

What is known • Family members of children with a neurodevelopmental condition often report lower wellbeing compared to families of typically developing children, though there has been little investigation into differences in the specific dynamics of family wellbeing.

What is new?

• The structure of family wellbeing is broadly similar between families of children with and without a neurodevelopmental condition.

• Families of children with a neurodevelopmental condition typically experienced lower overall wellbeing or reported more depression and anxiety symptoms or lower life satisfaction.

What is relevant?

• The interrelatedness of individual family members' wellbeing -irrespective of neurodevelopmental condition diagnoses among children -supports familylevel approaches targeting wellbeing and motivates longitudinal research on the direction of influence between family members.
