Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Delusions are beliefs with extraordinary content, yet computational accounts of delusions have not explained how this content arises. We propose that extraordinary content emerges when an ordinary mechanism, commoncause inference, is applied to semantically disparate observations. We tested this with large language models (LLMs) by systematically manipulating semantic disparity between observations. Single causes inferred by these models from ordinary observations generally resembled true causes, yet became increasingly delusion-like as observations became more disparate. Observation disparity predicted delusionality, and this relationship could be explained geometrically via semantic analogues of common-cause properties. We further tested this idea in a large corpus of real-world delusions. In cases documenting pre-delusional observations, subsequent delusional beliefs were consistent with what LLMs would infer and exhibited corresponding common-cause properties. Together, our findings identify a computational route through which extraordinary delusion-like content can emerge from ordinary causal inference, and demonstrate its relevance to clinical delusions. Introduction

Delusions-unusual beliefs held rigidly despite counterevidence-are a hallmark of psychosis and severe mental illness. A striking aspect of delusions is their extraordinary content, ranging from persecution and infidelity to bizarre beliefs involving supernatural control. Such content is consequential because it can drive behavior. In the extreme, such behaviors include self-mutilation of organs based on the individual's beliefs (e.g., that the organ was possessed by evil forces) 6 . Yet, how such unusual beliefs arise in the first place remains poorly understood. Existing computational theories have focused on abnormalities in belief updating 1 , leaving open a fundamental question: how does the extraordinary content of delusional beliefs arise?

Delusions typically emerge after a "pre-delusional" period, during which multiple salient and uncanny experiences co-occur without an obvious explanation 7 . Theoretical accounts broadly agree that delusions form to make sense of these experiences 8 , but do not explain why the resulting explanations are often so extraordinary 9 . The classic case of the soldier K.B. illustrates this gap 10 . Within a short period, K.B. noticed a seemingly out-ofplace pile of roadside stones, thought people acted strangely around him, experienced heart palpitations, and felt his own movements were unnatural. None of these observations were extraordinary on their own. Yet, in seeking an explanation, K.B. came to believe that he had been selected for a special mission and was being remotely controlled by a "wave apparatus". How can ordinary but disparate experiences give rise to such extraordinary explanations?

We propose that extraordinary delusional content can arise as a computational consequence of seeking a latent common cause for ordinary but disparate observations. Individuals routinely infer common causes for cooccurring events 11, 12 , such as inferring rain to explain both umbrellas and puddles (Fig. 1A). Our account predicts that as observations become more semantically disparate, explanations that attribute them to a single common cause become increasingly extraordinary, potentially invoking powerful hidden agents or preternatural mechanisms (Fig. 1B)-such as K.B.'s wave apparatus.

We tested this account using pretrained large language models (LLMs) and real-world cases of delusions. LLMs can generate candidate causal explanations from observations 13 and capture human-like behavior 14 , with semantic representations that resemble human neural responses 15, 16 . We prompted LLMs to infer single causes for sets of natural-language observations that varied in semantic disparity (Fig. 1C and Fig. 1D). As observations became more semantically disparate, the inferred causes became more delusion-like. Their content included persecutory-like and passivity-like themes (e.g., mind control) and depended on the content of their observations. The inferred causes nonetheless remained relevant to the observations they were generated to explain and showed semantic analogues of the probabilistic properties expected of common causes. We then tested whether these inferred causes resembled real-world delusions, using a new, large corpus of detailed clinical case reports. In cases with documented pre-delusional observations, real-world delusions showed common-cause-like relationships to those observations, and LLM-inferred causes generated from each person's observations resembled their subsequent delusions. Moreover, more delusion-like LLM-inferred causes more readily accommodated observations designed to challenge them. Together, these results suggest that commoncause inference over disparate observations provides a simple computational route to extraordinary delusion-like content and may help explain why such beliefs become rigid.

LLMs recover latent true causes from their observations

To confirm that LLMs can infer a latent true cause when one exists, we tested whether LLMs could recover known causes from observations generated to follow from those causes. To construct a dataset, we wrote sentences describing events or states that could produce multiple effects; we call these true causes. They spanned individual (e.g., "you felt lonely"), interpersonal (e.g., "a coworker invited you to grab lunch"), societal (e.g., "a nationwide teacher strike disrupted school schedules"), and environmental (e.g., "the city was flooded") domains (full list in Supplementary Data 1). For each true cause, we used GPT-4o to generate 20 possible observations. The true causes and their observations described ordinary situations: ones that are common, naturally occurring or consistent with historical precedent (Methods). We then formed sets of 2, 5, 10, or 20 observations from each true cause and prompted GPT-4o and Sonnet 4.6 separately to explain the observations with a single common cause, 10 times per set (henceforth, inferred causes; Fig. 1D; see Supplementary Data 2 for the prompt and Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Fig. S1 for prompt validation).

Because an inferred cause can capture the same meaning as the true cause without sharing its exact wording, we compared the sentences in a semantic space, where sentences with similar meanings have embeddings that point in similar directions (our primary model was OpenAI's text-embedding-3-small; Methods; Fig. 1C). We quantified true-cause recovery as the cosine similarity (hereafter, similarity) between inferred causes and true causes.

Inferred causes were on average more similar to the true causes of their observation sets than expected when true causes were randomly shuffled across observation sets (permutation tests over observation-set means, all P < 0.001; Fig. 2A). Semantic similarity also reliably discriminated pairings of inferred causes with true causes of their observation sets from pairings of inferred causes with true causes of other observation sets (all AUCs ≥ 0.96; Fig. 2B; Supplementary Table S1). Both effects tended to increase with the number of observations, consistent with more observations providing more evidence about a shared cause. Both effects were present in Sonnet 4.6, which did not generate the observations, and replicated in an independent dataset of hand-written true causes and their observations (Supplementary Fig. S2). Even inferred causes that somewhat diverged from their true causes often offered reasonable explanations of the observations (Fig. 2C), suggesting that recovery did not depend on reproducing the wording of the true cause.

To visualize how true-cause recovery was organized in semantic space, we reduced the dimensionality of the semantic embeddings and then clustered them. The observations formed thematically coherent clusters (Fig. 2D). We then projected the true causes (Fig. 2E) and LLM-inferred causes (Fig. 2F) into the same space. The causes largely occupied observation-dense regions, where many observations lie close together in the projection. Given the nature of the observations, observation density in this projection qualitatively depicts the distribution of ordinary observations (quantified later as the global observation distribution; Emergence of extraordinary content from common-cause inference).

Delusion-like inferred causes emerge from disparate observations

Having established that LLMs can infer true causes from observations, we next asked what they would infer when the observations were disparate. We constructed random observation sets by sampling 2, 5, 10, or 20 observations at random from the full observation pool, keeping only sets with observations generated by at least two different true causes (Methods). Using the same prompt as before, each LLM inferred a single cause 10 times for each set. The following analyses use all observation sets and their GPT-4o inferred causes (Sonnet 4.6 replications are in Supplementary Notes 1-6).

We visualized these inferred causes in the same space as in Fig. 2. Unlike causes inferred from single-cause observation sets, causes inferred from random observation sets often occupied observation-sparse regions of this space, away from most observations (Fig. 3A). Many of the inferred causes invoked extraordinary delusion-like content, such as supernatural agents, aliens, magical forces and powerful hidden influences, even though such content appeared in neither the prompt nor the observations.

To quantify delusion-like content, we used GPT-4o to rate each inferred cause on features commonly found in real-world delusions, including the presence of an external agent or force, extraordinary or hidden processes, and external influence over behavior, emotions or thoughts (Methods). We averaged these ratings to obtain a delusionality score (range = 0-9). 15.6% of all inferred causes exceeded a high-delusionality threshold (based on qualitative assessment: delusionality > 2.5, Methods; Supplementary Fig. S3; this was exceeded by 2.1% from single-cause observation sets and 25.4% of inferred causes from random observation sets). Inferred causes in observation-sparse regions had the highest delusionality scores (Fig. 3B; other measures of delusion-like content showed the same pattern, Supplementary Fig. S4), whereas the observation sets themselves scored near zero (Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Fig. S3). Randomly sampled sets of hand-written observations also yielded high-delusionality inferred causes (Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Table S2).

We then asked whether these LLM-inferred causes resembled real-world delusions. We compiled a real-world delusion corpus of 383 cases and extracted each case's delusional content, which we call a real-world delusion (Methods). For each LLM-inferred cause, we defined real-world delusion similarity as its maximum cosine similarity to any of the real-world delusions. LLM-inferred causes with higher delusionality scores had higher real-world delusion similarity (r = 0.65 across individual inferred causes, 95% CI = [0.63, 0.67], P < 0.001; Fig. 3C and Supplementary Table S3). High-delusionality inferred causes spanned clinically recognized delusional themes, which we classified using GPT-4o ratings (validated against clinician coding; Methods). We grouped these into two main subtypes for later analyses: persecutory (agents with harmful intent) and passivity (external control of a person's thoughts, feelings or actions) (Fig. 3D for distribution and Fig. 3F for examples; further examples by theme and subtype in Supplementary Table S4).

Despite their extraordinary content, the LLM's explanations of how each inferred cause accounted for its observations were often seemingly reasonable (Fig. 3E). Observation geometry predicts delusion-like inferred causes

Observation distance is positively associated with delusion-like content

If delusion-like content in an inferred cause depends on the observations being explained, then properties of these observations should predict the delusionality of the inferred causes. We therefore tested whether the delusionality of inferred causes increased with the semantic disparity among their observations. We quantified this disparity as observation distance, defined as the mean pairwise cosine distance between a set's observations. Observation distance is small when observations have similar meaning (e.g., "John lost his appetite" and "John began losing weight rapidly") and large when they do not (e.g., "John lost his appetite" and "Julia organized her closet neatly"). As predicted, greater observation distance was associated with higher mean delusionality (mean over 10 inferred causes per observation set; r = 0.52, 95% CI = [0.49, 0.54], P < 0.001; Fig. 4A; Supplementary Table S5). This association remained positive after controlling for the number of observations, and even when restricting to single-cause observation sets (Supplementary Note 3 and Supplementary Fig. S5).

Observation distance captures whether the observations are similar to each other, but not what they are about. Some observation sets may naturally invite delusion-like explanations: for example, losing one's wallet and receiving bank alerts may lead to a persecutory-like inference of identity theft. To capture the shared semantic content of an observation set, we defined observation content as the normalized mean of its observation embeddings (centered on the average of the global observation distribution; Methods). Observation content also predicted mean delusionality (because observation content is a high-dimensional vector, we used cross-validated ridge regression: cross-validated prediction r = 0.30, 95% CI = [0.25, 0.34], P < 0.001; Fig. 4B; Supplementary Table S5). Importantly, observation distance improved the prediction of mean delusionality beyond observation content (cross-validated ΔR²; Fig. 4C; Supplementary Table S6). Because ΔR² does not indicate the direction of the association, we also estimated the observation-distance partial correlation with delusionality, accounting for observation content (Methods). The association remained positive (cross-validated partial r = 0.56, 95% CI = [0.53, 0.58], P < 0.001).

We next tested whether the effect of observation distance was specific to delusion-related content. We repeated the same ΔR 2 and observation-distance partial correlation analysis, replacing delusionality with the mean similarity of inferred causes to delusion-related measures (including real-world delusions and delusion-related scale items) and non-delusion-related measures (general psychopathology-related scale items; see Fig. 4D caption for the measures; item standardization described in Supplementary Data 3). Observation content improved prediction of every measure beyond observation distance (cross-validated ΔR 2 range = 0.31-0.49, all P < 0.001; Supplementary Table S6). Observation-distance partial correlations were positive for every delusion-related measure (cross-validated partial r = 0.20-0.34) and more positive than for the non-delusionrelated measures (all eight paired differences in cross-validated partial r = 0.48-0.66, all P < 0.001; Fig. 4D; Supplementary Tables S6 and S7). Thus, whereas observation content broadly predicted the content of inferred causes, observation distance selectively associated with their similarity to delusion-related measures.

Observation content predicts delusional subtype

Because observation content predicted inferred-cause content generally, we tested whether it also predicted the subtype of high-delusionality inferred causes. We focused on passivity-like and persecutory-like inferred causes because they were well represented in the dataset and closely resembled real-world delusions with the same subtype (Methods). Observation content, but not observation distance, distinguished persecutory-like from passivity-like inferred causes (n = 803 inferred causes from 358 observation sets; cross-validated ridge logistic regression: observation-content-only AUC = 0.73, 95% CI = [0.68, 0.78], P < 0.001; observation-distance-only AUC = 0.42, P = 0.964). Adding observation content to the observation-distance-only model improved the discrimination of these subtypes (ΔAUC = 0.32, 95% CI = [0.24, 0.39], P < 0.001), but adding observation distance to the observation-content-only model did not (ΔAUC = 0.0004, P = 0.35; Fig. 4E; Supplementary Table S8). Observation content therefore helped determine what inferred causes were about, including their delusional subtype, whereas observation distance predicted the degree to which those explanations were delusion-like.

To identify which features of the observations were associated with this subtype distinction, we had GPT-4o code each observation for four non-exclusive features: internal (a subjective experience), external (an event that is observable by a bystander), social (involving people or social interactions) and negative valence (aversive content; Methods). We then regressed each observation set's subtype score (held-out prediction of the observation-content-only classifier above) on the proportion of its observations receiving each code. Observation sets with a larger proportion of negatively valenced observations had more persecutory-like (lower) subtype scores (OLS regression, controlling for the number of observations; β = -0.36, 95% CI = [-0.44, -0.27], P < 0.001), whereas sets with a larger proportion of internal observations had more passivity-like (higher) subtype scores (in the same model: β = 0.35, 95% CI = [0.24, 0.46], P < 0.001; Fig. 4F; Supplementary Table S9).

Delusion-like inferred causes are common-cause-like

Inferred causes depended on their observations, but this alone does not establish that they exhibited properties expected of common causes. The common-cause principle 5 implies two key properties of a common cause (Fig. 5A). First, the cause should screen off the observations from one another: conditioning on the cause should make them independent. For example, observing umbrellas suggests there are puddles too, but adds little information about puddles once you know it rained. Second, the cause should have positive relevance: it should make each observation more likely. Rain makes both umbrellas and puddles more likely. We tested whether inferred causes were common-cause-like, using semantic analogues of both properties (Methods). For screening-off, we used semantic embeddings and computed the residual observation similarity after accounting for the inferred cause, which should be small if the inferred cause explains the observation similarities. For positive relevance, we had GPT-4o judge whether the inferred cause made each observation more likely and computed a positive-relevance rate: the proportion of observations judged to be made more likely.

Inferred causes were common-cause-like on both measures. Across all inferred causes (n = 13,840), inferred causes left lower residual observation similarity in their own observation sets (0.087 cosine-similarity units lower; 95% CI = [0.081, 0.093]) and had a higher positive-relevance rate (41.8 percentage points higher; 95% CI = [40.2, 43.4]; both P < 0.001; Fig. 5B shows the corresponding standardized differences) than other random inferred causes. The same pattern held when restricted to high-delusionality inferred causes (n = 2,158): residual observation similarity was lower (0.019 cosine-similarity units lower; 95% CI = [0.015, 0.024]) and the positive-relevance rate was higher (17.7 percentage points higher; 95% CI = [15.8, 19.6]; both P < 0.001; Fig. 5B; Supplementary Tables S10 and S11). Thus, even high-delusionality inferred causes exhibited semantic signatures consistent with common-cause explanations.

Emergence of extraordinary content from common-cause inference

How can an inferred common cause of ordinary observations acquire extraordinary content? Our semantic analogue of screening-off requires a common cause to account for the similarity between its observations (Methods). Under an idealized symmetric screening-off model, less similar observations constrain their inferred common cause to share less semantic content with each observation. This places more of the cause outside the subspace spanned by its observations, predicting that cause-to-own-observation-subspace distance should increase with observation distance (Methods; Fig. 5C). As predicted, larger observation distance was associated with larger cause-to-own-observation-subspace distance (averaged within observation set; OLS regression, controlling for number of observations: β = 0.78, 95% CI = [0.73, 0.82], P < 0.001; Fig. 5D; Supplementary Table S12).

A cause far from its own observation subspace need not be unusual relative to ordinary observations in general. Larger, more disparate observation sets capture more of the variation in the global observation distribution (Supplementary Note 5 and Supplementary Fig. S6), leaving less of that variation outside their subspace. If these sets capture the main directions of ordinary semantic variation, causes departing from their subspace may also depart from the global observation distribution. We measured this as the cause-to-global-observationdistribution distance (using Mahalanobis distance 17, 18 ; Methods). As predicted, it increased with observation distance (OLS regression: standardized slope at the mean number of observations, β = 0.64, 95% CI = [0.60, 0.67], P < 0.001; Supplementary Table S12), and more steeply for larger observation sets (observation distance by number of observations interaction, β = 0.17, 95% CI = [0.13, 0.21], P < 0.001).

Finally, we asked whether these geometric distances were associated with delusion-like content. We regressed mean inferred-cause delusionality onto both of these cause-distance measures (averaged across an observation set's inferred causes; OLS regression, controlling for number of observations; Methods). In a single model, both the cause-to-own-observation-subspace distance (β = 0.19, 95% CI = [0.13, 0.25], P < 0.001) and the cause-toglobal-observation-distribution distance (β = 0.51, 95% CI = [0.44, 0.58], P < 0.001) were positively associated with delusionality. More delusion-like inferred causes lay further from both their own observations and the global distribution of ordinary observations, consistent with their concentration in observation-sparse regions of semantic space (Fig. 3B).

Real-world cases are common-cause-like

If real-world delusions arise from common-cause inference over disparate observations, they should relate to their motivating observations in the same way that LLM-inferred causes relate to theirs. Testing this required case reports describing both salient observations preceding delusion formation (pre-delusional observations) and the delusional beliefs that followed. Five cases in our real-world delusion corpus contained sufficiently detailed information on both (Methods). From these, we extracted pre-delusional observations (7-33 per case, 83 total), excluding references to delusion-like interpretations and overt hallucinations, and delusional beliefs (1-7 per case, 18 total) for each case. These pre-delusional observations included internal bodily changes (e.g., "his heart rate increased suddenly"), personally significant but mundane external events (e.g., "he remembered an episode of Columbo"), and social interactions (e.g., "he tried to talk with his companions, but they only gave one-word answers"), among others. They mostly described events that individually were ordinary (full list in Supplementary Data 4). As with the LLM dataset, each case's real-world delusion exceeded the highdelusionality threshold (range = 5.6-9), while their pre-delusional observation sets had minimal mean delusionality (range = 0-0.6).

Using the same screening-off and positive-relevance measures as in the LLM analyses, we treated each case's delusional beliefs as candidate common causes of its own pre-delusional observations. On average, they had lower residual observation similarity (0.015 cosine-similarity units lower, 95% CI = [0.009, 0.021], P = 0.008) and higher positive-relevance rate (16.4 percentage points higher, 95% CI = [8.0, 28.1], P = 0.033; Fig. 6A) to their own pre-delusional observations than did the delusional beliefs from other cases. These results suggest that delusional beliefs are common-cause-like with respect to their pre-delusional observations.

Beyond being common-cause-like, the case's delusional beliefs shared the geometry of high-delusionality LLMinferred causes. Like the LLM observation sets that produced high-delusionality causes, the pre-delusional observation sets were disparate: their observation distance exceeded the 90 th percentile of single-cause observation sets. We applied the OLS models fitted to the LLM dataset to each case's observation distance and number of observations and compared the predicted distances with the mean distances of the case's delusional beliefs (Fig. 6B). Prediction error for the cases was comparable to that for held-out LLM observation sets for the cause-to-own-observation-subspace distance (root mean squared error, RMSE = 0.04 vs. 0.07) and for the causeto-global-observation-distribution distance (RMSE = 5.18 vs. 5.32). These results suggest that the geometry of the case's delusional beliefs was broadly consistent with the patterns observed in LLM-inferred causes.

We then tested whether an LLM-inferred cause generated from a case's pre-delusional observations resembled the delusional content subsequently reported in that case. Using the same common-cause prompt, we had the LLM infer a single cause (100 times) from each case's pre-delusional observation set and scored each delusional belief by its maximum similarity to these inferred causes (Fig. 6C). Delusional beliefs were on average more similar to causes inferred from their own case's observations than to causes inferred from other cases' pre-delusional observations (0.08 cosine-similarity units higher, 95% CI = [0.05, 0.12], P = 0.008; Fig. 6D). To test whether this similarity reflected only the content shared with the observations, we kept only the parts of the delusional beliefs and inferred causes that lie outside the case's observation subspace (the span of its predelusional observations; Fig. 5C) and re-ran the same analysis. Similarity between delusional beliefs and LLMinferred causes remained higher for same-case than other-case inferred causes (mean difference in residual dot product = 0.06, 95% CI = [0.02, 0.11], P = 0.008). This suggests that same-case inferred causes shared semantic features with the delusional beliefs that were not directly captured by the observation embeddings.

The case of K.B. illustrates this result (Fig. 6C). From his pre-delusional observations alone, including a pile of roadside stones, the strange behavior of those around him and abrupt heart palpitations, the LLM inferred an external hidden mechanism controlling him. None of K.B.'s observations mentioned an external controller, yet the inferred cause resembled the wave apparatus he later came to believe in.

Rigidity of delusion-like inferred causes

Delusions are clinically rigid, persisting despite attempts to disprove them. Delusion-like inferred causes often invoke powerful agents, undetectable processes, and remote influence. Such extraordinary explanations may accommodate a broad range of possible observations, making it harder to find observations that challenge them (Fig. 7A-B). We operationalized this property as the negative-relevance rate: the proportion of observations that the LLM judged to be less likely if the inferred cause were true than if it were false.

Delusion-like inferred causes arose disproportionately from disparate observation sets, so comparing inferred causes across observation sets would confound the content of the inferred causes with the observations that elicited them. We therefore compared inferred causes from the same observation set, using a linear mixedeffects regression that isolated within-set effects while controlling for observation distance and number of observations (Methods). We restricted the analysis to all 5,300 inferred causes from the 530 sets that yielded both high-and low-delusionality inferred causes (to ensure within-set variability in delusionality).

Within observation sets, greater delusionality was associated with lower negative-relevance rates both for the original observations for which the inferred causes had been generated and for ten new observations sampled at random from the global observation distribution, excluding the original observation set. The associations therefore extended beyond the observations the causes were generated to explain (Supplementary Note 6; Supplementary Fig. S7).

In psychiatry, a belief is judged to be delusional based partly on its persistence despite contradictory evidence, or evidence that challenges the belief 19 . We thus asked whether it would be harder to construct observations that challenged more delusion-like inferred causes. For each inferred cause, we had the LLM construct ten ordinary observations intended to make the inferred cause less likely (challenge observations). Greater delusionality was associated with a lower negative-relevance rate for these challenge observations (within-set slope = -14.44 percentage points per one overall SD of delusionality, 95% CI = [-15.40, -13.48], P < 0.001; Fig. 7C shows percentage points against the raw delusionality score). Thus, even observations designed specifically to challenge an inferred cause were less often judged to count against more delusion-like causes (Supplementary Note 6; Supplementary Table S13). Rigidity may thus be a natural byproduct of constructing inferred causes that accommodate disparate observations.

Discussion

A longstanding puzzle about delusions is how someone attempting to explain salient experiences 4 comes to hold extraordinary or bizarre beliefs 20 . We propose that common-cause inference over semantically disparate observations offers a computational route to such extraordinary content. When prompted to infer a single cause for ordinary observations, LLMs generated coherent but increasingly delusion-like explanations as the observations became more disparate. Semantic geometry accounted for how these explanations departed from ordinary content, whereas the content of observations helped determine their delusion-like themes, including persecutory and passivity.

Real-world clinical cases showed patterns consistent with this account. As expected clinically, each individual's pre-delusional observations were ordinary on their own but semantically distant from one another (e.g., occurring across different domains or contexts; see Supplementary Data 4). The extent to which delusions departed from ordinary semantic content was predictable based on the geometry of pre-delusional observations, using models fitted to the LLM dataset. Despite their extraordinary content, the delusional beliefs were common-cause-like with respect to their pre-delusional observations, showing semantic analogues of commoncause properties. Moreover, LLM-inferred causes based on an individual's pre-delusional observations captured specific contents of the delusional beliefs that individual later developed. These analyses are limited by the paucity of case reports with in-depth descriptions of pre-delusional observations, but together they suggest that the structure identified in the LLM experiments is also present in real-world delusion formation.

Why might disparate observations evoke common-cause inference? During the pre-delusional period, individuals often experience a sudden influx of salient and diverse events 10 . One possibility is that these events do not seem to cause each other, so searching for a common cause is natural. During psychosis, such a common cause does in fact exist: the disease process. Thus, common-cause inference may be tracking a real upstream cause for pre-delusional experiences, such as altered prediction-error signaling 1,2 . On this account, inferring that a common cause exists would be appropriate, even if the specific cause inferred was mistaken. Anotherperhaps less parsimonious-possibility is that delusion formation involves a bias toward simpler causal structures, such as favoring a single cause over multiple causes, even when observations may be better explained separately. A third possibility is that the generative model itself is altered 21 , such that observations that appear semantically disparate to outside observers are represented as closely related by the individual. Two pieces of clinical knowledge argue against this view. First, the persistent uncertainty and search for an explanation during the pre-delusional period 10 suggest a demanding inference process, which is more easily explained by distant, hard-to-reconcile observations. Second, delusional explanations tend to entail more elaborate belief systems 22, 23 than subjectively similar observations would require.

The same common-cause framework may also help explain delusional rigidity, the mechanism of which remains debated 21 . More delusion-like inferred causes often invoked hidden, powerful agents and preternatural, undetectable processes. Causes of this kind may accommodate a wide range of observations, leaving fewer observations that clearly count against them. Consistent with this possibility, among LLM-inferred causes from the same observation set, fewer new observations provided evidence against the more delusion-like inferred causes, and this was true even for challenge observations generated specifically to contradict them. This suggests that delusional rigidity could reflect the unfalsifiability of the beliefs 24 , rather than resistance to counterevidence per se. This distinction could have implications for the definition, assessment, and treatment of delusions.

Common-cause inference also provides a natural role for priors, a well-studied concept in psychosis. LLMinferred causes from the same disparate observation set varied widely in content (Supplementary Note 7 and Supplementary Fig. S8), consistent with high uncertainty. It is under such conditions that prior beliefs can exert strong influence. Previous work has implicated the strength of prior beliefs in delusions 3 , but which priors matter remains unclear 21 . Our account points to semantic priors: knowledge and expectations shaped by individual history, traits and culture 25, 26 that could steer common causes toward specific regions of semantic space. This could help explain why superordinate themes such as unseen agency recur across cultures 27 while their specific contents, such as spirits or technologies, vary 28 . Common-cause inference may thus combine a shared structure with person-specific content.

Pretrained LLMs allowed us to study how natural-language explanations are formed to account for observations. While LLMs can fail at complex reasoning 29 , they often perform well on simple causal reasoning tasks, such as inferring causes for events using world knowledge 13 . These results are consistent with sufficiency: applying common-cause inference to ordinary observations produced delusion-like content, without delusion-like observations or an altered generative model. The delusion-like causes that emerged were constrained by their observations, both in how they related to them and in their thematic coherence (i.e., delusional subtype). Highdelusionality inferred causes sometimes emerged even when the observations had been generated from a single true cause (Supplementary Note 3), so they did not simply result from forcing a common cause where none exists. These inferred causes differ from LLM "hallucinations", defined as confabulated outputs that are nonsensical or unfaithful to the inputs 30 . In contrast, these delusion-like inferred causes are common-cause-like explanations of their observations. Future work should test whether a similar common-cause inference process contributes to chatbot-associated delusional beliefs 31.

In conclusion, our findings identify common-cause inference over semantically disparate observations as a computational route to extraordinary delusion-like contents, with potential implications for understanding the persistence of such beliefs.

Methods

We first describe construction and validation of the LLM dataset and real-world delusion corpus, followed by the semantic representations and measures, and then analyses.

LLM dataset

True causes and observations

To construct a natural-language observation dataset, we created 146 short sentences describing events or states from which effects could follow (true causes). They spanned individual, interpersonal, societal and environmental situations, and each described something ordinary (i.e., common, naturally occurring, or consistent with historical precedent). They varied in subject and scale (referring to no one, "you," a named individual, a group or a broader population) and described present or past events, including historical ones. The complete list of true causes appears in Supplementary Data 1.

For each true cause, we used a separate API call with OpenAI's GPT-4o 32 to generate 20 effect sentences (observations). The prompt was designed to produce short, standardized sentences with little irrelevant variation in wording. Its core instruction was (full prompt in Supplementary Data 2):

The following is a causal event: "{cause sentence}" Generate 20 plausible effects that a single person could experience or observe as a direct result of the above event.

We used a temperature of 0.5 to encourage varied outputs and hand-edited some observations for consistency with the prompt instructions. This yielded 2,920 unique observation sentences (146 causes × 20 observations).

Single-cause and random observation sets

We created sets of 2, 5, 10, or 20 observations (𝑛). For each 𝑛, we created 146 single-cause and 200 random observation sets (584 and 800, respectively, for 1,384 total). For each 𝑛 and each true cause, we formed singlecause sets by sampling 𝑛 observations uniformly without replacement from that cause's 20 observations. We formed random sets by sampling 𝑛 observations uniformly without replacement from the pooled observations of all true causes, retaining only non-duplicate sets with observations from at least two different true causes.

Generation of inferred causes

We prompted GPT-4o 32 and Anthropic's Claude Sonnet 4.6 33 (both at temperature = 0.5) to infer a common cause for each observation set. For each LLM, we generated 10 outputs per set. Each used a separate API call with the observations in a random order. We used the following core instruction (which purposefully omitted any leading language related to delusions or relevant theory):

Explain the following observations with a single cause that can account for all of them. The cause does not need to be likely. This prompt produced 𝑛 + 1 sentences: the first stated the inferred cause, and each subsequent sentence explained how the inferred cause accounted for one observation (see full prompt in Supplementary Data 2). We refer to the first sentence as the inferred cause and to all sentences together as the full explanation. The phrase "the cause does not need to be likely" was intended to broaden the range of inferred causes beyond conventional causes considered most likely a priori (see Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Fig. S1 for prompt validation). This phrase also helped counter the tendency of LLMs trained with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback to give agreeable, conventional responses rather than accurate ones 34 (note that this prompt produced accurate inferred causes when there was a true cause of the observation set: Fig. 2A).

Hand-written dataset

To test whether the main results depended on LLM-generated observations, a human assistant wrote 61 causes with varying numbers of observations describing possible effects, blinded to the LLM dataset and following the same prompt instructions. We repeated the true-cause recovery and delusionality analyses (described below) on this hand-written dataset with GPT-4o (Supplementary Notes 1 and 2).

Real-world delusion corpus

Case eligibility

We constructed a corpus of real-world delusions by extracting the delusional content from published clinical case reports. We searched for reports using databases (e.g., Google Scholar, PubMed), psychosis-specific journals (e.g., Schizophrenia Bulletin), case report journals (e.g., Case Reports in Psychiatry) and classic texts (e.g., from early psychopathologists such as Emil Kraepelin, Klaus Conrad, and others), and checked the references from eligible cases. Cases were eligible for inclusion if: a) the full text was available and published in an academic journal or book; b) the belief was held by a specific patient (not "patients in general"); c) the authors indicated that the patient had been clinically evaluated and found to have a delusion; d) at least one sentence described the delusional content; e) the patient was reported to have a primary diagnosis of a non-affective psychotic disorder (i.e., psychotic symptoms not primarily attributable to a mood disorder). We excluded cases with a reported primary diagnosis of psychosis associated with a medical or neurological disorder, substance-induced or shared delusion. We included 383 cases from 141 documents. Diagnoses were schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder (47.5%), first-episode or early psychosis (20.6%), delusional disorder (11.2%), postpartum psychosis (4.4%), brief psychotic disorder (2.3%), psychotic disorder NOS (2.1%) and obsolete labels from older texts that were considered non-affective (11.7%; including dementia praecox, paraphrenia and cycloid psychosis). A complete description of the dataset will be published elsewhere.

Real-world delusions

We extracted each case's delusional content in four steps. First, we manually separated the case's text from other cases and background material. Second, we prompted Google's Gemini 1.5 Flash 35 (temperature = 0) to remove text unrelated to the individual's beliefs (e.g., information about psychological tests, family history, or other psychosocial information). Third, we prompted the same model to rewrite the delusional content as standardized sentences, each expressing a single belief in the form "The person believed that __," otherwise retaining the original wording. We refined both prompts against manually processed cases (Supplementary Data 2). Fourth, we manually checked the output against the prompt instructions and, in fewer than 5% of cases, removed non-delusional content or restored missed delusional content from the source text. We also replaced overt clinical references (e.g., references to "delusion," diagnosis, medical staff, suicidal ideation, hospital stays, medication) with neutral wording unless they were essential to the belief itself. This yielded 4,634 standardized sentences (per case: median = 6, IQR = 3-11, range = 1-265; mean = 12.1, SD = 24.2), which need not correspond one-to-one with sentences in the source text. Each case's sentences formed one real-world delusion, which could include more than one delusional belief.

Semantic representations

Semantic embeddings and similarity

We represented text as semantic embeddings using text-embedding models, which map text to fixed-length vectors intended to capture semantic content. From the LLM dataset, we embedded each observation, true cause, and inferred cause separately. From the real-world delusion corpus, we embedded each real-world delusion and for the five cases with pre-delusional observations (see Real-world cases with pre-delusional observations), each pre-delusional observation and delusional belief separately. The main analyses used OpenAI's textembedding-3-small model 36 (1,536 dimensions). Selected analyses were repeated with Qwen3-Embedding-4B model 37 (2,560 dimensions with last-token pooling; Supplementary Tables S1, S3, S5-S10 and S12). We unitnormalized all embeddings (bold denotes vectors) and measured semantic similarity with cosine similarity 38 : , which equals the inner product for unit vectors.

UMAP projection and clustering

We reduced the observation embeddings to 10 dimensions with Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection 39 (UMAP; number of neighbors = 30, minimum distance = 0.25, cosine metric with spectral initialization). We clustered the resulting observation coordinates using Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise 40 (HDBSCAN; Euclidean distance, minimum cluster size = 5, minimum samples = 5, leaf-based cluster selection). Observations not assigned to a sufficiently dense cluster were treated as outliers. Using BERTopic 41 , we merged observation clusters by average-linkage agglomerative clustering using cosine distances between their mean embeddings in the original space, producing 14 topic clusters and an outlier group. We labeled each topic by inspecting up to 12 observations with the highest cosine similarity to its mean embedding, with labels drafted iteratively by an LLM. We then projected the true-and inferred-cause embeddings into the fitted 10-dimensional UMAP space and assigned them to clusters; true and inferred causes that could not be assigned were considered outliers.

For visualization, we fitted a separate two-dimensional UMAP (number of neighbors = 30, minimum distance = 0.10, cosine metric, spectral initialization) to all observations, true causes and unique inferred causes. We reused the resulting coordinates across plots. Thus, true and inferred causes were assigned to topics out of sample, but their two-dimensional display was fitted jointly. We estimated observation density on this display with a Gaussian kernel density estimate fitted to observation coordinates (bandwidth = 0.75 UMAP units); contours mark the 5th, 25th, 50th, 75th and 95th percentiles of the observations' estimated densities. The display was qualitative; all analyses use the original embeddings (see Predicting cause-to-global-observation-distribution distance).

True-cause recovery

We tested whether the LLMs accurately recovered the true causes of single-cause observation sets. We analyzed each combination of LLM, embedding model and number of observations separately.

We tested whether inferred causes were more similar on average to the true causes of their own observation sets than expected under shuffled true-cause assignments. We computed the cosine similarity between each inferred cause and its observation set's true cause, averaged these similarities within each observation set, and then averaged them across observation sets within each condition. We obtained a right-tailed P-value by randomly permuting true causes across observation sets and recalculating the mean similarity 5,000 times.

We also tested how well similarity discriminated same-set pairs (an inferred cause and its own true cause) from other-set pairs (the same inferred cause and each other set's true cause in that condition). We calculated the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) over these pairs; this is the probability that a randomly drawn same-set similarity exceeds a randomly drawn other-set similarity (1 = perfect discrimination;0.5 = chance). We obtained 95% percentile confidence intervals for mean similarity and AUC from 5,000 bootstrap resamples of complete observation sets (sampled with replacement), retaining all inferred causes and their similarity scores from each sampled observation set.

Delusion-like content

Delusionality scoring

We scored the delusionality of the LLM-inferred causes using a set of items that we developed to capture superordinate features that are common across delusions. We developed this score because existing scales (e.g., the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale [PANSS] 42 , the delusion subscale of the Psychotic Symptom Rating Scales [PSYRATS] 43 , the Scale for the Assessment of Positive Symptoms [SAPS] 44 and the Peters Delusions Inventory [PDI] 45 ) measure the severity or impact of delusions (e.g., distress or functional impact) or count their categories, whereas we wanted to measure their content. We therefore developed these items to reflect typical delusional content across various delusion categories, focusing on the following descriptive elements: agent (who), intent (why), method type and characteristics (how), impact (what), and locus (where). Although these items do not capture characteristics common to all delusion categories (especially less common ones such as delusions of jealousy, nihilism, erotomania, guilt, etc.), they capture key elements typical of some of the most common delusions in schizophrenia spectrum disorders (persecutory, reference, somatic, grandiose, religious, paranormal, technology, misidentification, control, mind reading and thought insertion 46, 47 . These include classically described aspects of delusions such as invoking an "unseen agency" (e.g., an external agent acting remotely and/or secretly 27 ), the negative valence of many delusional phenomena (e.g., subjugation, violation of privacy), and the involvement of extraordinary elements (e.g., preternatural methods such as magic or advanced technologies).

We scored each full explanation (i.e., the inferred cause and the sentences explaining how it accounts for each observation; see Generation of inferred causes), to capture how the inferred cause relates to the observations it was inferred to explain, rather than the inferred cause in isolation. For simplicity, we refer to this as inferredcause delusionality throughout.

The LLM that generated an explanation also rated it (e.g., GPT-4o rated GPT-4o generated explanations; temperature = 0), on the following seven items (each on a 0-9 scale):

Rate the following explanation on these questions, using a scale from 0-9. If a question is not relevant to the explanation, rate it as 0.

1. How much does the explanation feature an external causal agent or an external causal force outside of the belief holder's control? 2. How much does the agent or force intend to exert influence on the belief holder?

3. How much does the agent or force make use of extraordinary or preternatural methods? 4. How much does the agent or force make use of hidden processes that are highly difficult to detect? 5. How much direct control does the agent or force have on the belief holder's internal emotions, thoughts, or actions? 6. How much does the agent or force broadcast or transmit the belief holder's internal emotions or thoughts to the outside world? 7. How much does the explanation reference the simultaneous existence of multiple dimensions or realities?

Delusionality was the mean of the seven item ratings (range = 0-9), which is directly interpretable in terms of typical delusional content. Inferred causes with high delusionality scores had content consistent with several delusional subtypes (Fig. 3D), and our main conclusions held with alternative measures of delusion-like content, including real-world delusion similarity and similarity to items from delusion-related scales. The observation sets themselves had near-zero delusionality on average (Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Fig. S3).

Real-world delusion similarity

Real-world delusion similarity was the maximum cosine similarity between an inferred cause's embedding and the embeddings of the 383 real-world delusions in the real-world delusion corpus. We used the maximum rather than the mean to capture whether the inferred cause closely resembled at least one real-world delusion, rather than its average similarity to real-world delusions with diverse delusional content. We estimated the association between real-world delusion similarity and LLM-scored delusionality across individual inferred causes using Pearson's r, estimated as a standardized OLS coefficient, a two-sided 95% confidence interval and a right-tailed P-value from standard errors clustered by observation set.

High-delusionality threshold

For analyses and visualizations requiring a binary classification, we labeled inferred causes with a delusionality score greater than 2.5 as high-delusionality and the remainder as low-delusionality. We selected this threshold based on qualitative assessment of the inferred causes, choosing a threshold that demarcated the text-level emergence of delusion-like themes. The threshold classified 2,158 GPT-4o inferred causes (15.6%) as highdelusionality and 11,682 (84.4%) as low-delusionality.

Delusional theme labels and subtype selection

A trained clinician (EL) manually coded each real-world delusion (n = 383) for 18 delusional themes. Twelve came from the Scale for the Assessment of Positive Symptoms 44 (SAPS), a widely used clinician-administered instrument for assessing patient populations: persecutory, jealousy, guilt or sin, grandiose, religious, somatic, reference, control, mind reading, thought broadcasting, thought insertion, and thought withdrawal. To capture other common forms of delusional content that the SAPS does not cover, we added six themes from the Present State Examination 48 (PSE): erotomania, misidentification, catastrophe, nihilism or death, paranormal, and technology 46 . We coded each theme as "present" or "absent". For cases where the themes were uncertain (~1/3 of total cases), a second trained clinician (GH) reviewed the case, and the two raters reached consensus by discussion. We coded a theme as "maybe" when substantial uncertainty remained about whether a case met the theme definition.

To automate theme coding, we constructed a separate LLM prompt for each theme, containing theme definition, instructions distinguishing it from related themes, and examples of content that should and should not receive the label (Supplementary Data 2). We used GPT-4o (temperature = 0) to estimate the probability that each theme was present in each case. These probabilities discriminated the clinician's present from absent codes ("maybe" counted as absent) with a mean AUC of 0.96 across themes (minimum = 0.89). We then applied the same procedure to the full explanation of each high-delusionality LLM-inferred cause (GPT-4o; n = 2,158), coding a theme as present when its probability was at least 0.5. Theme prevalence across the high-delusionality inferred causes was varied (note that inferred causes could have multiple themes): paranormal (83%), control (47%), reference (40%), persecutory (39%), technology (33%), catastrophe (27%), somatic (18%), thought insertion (14%) and grandiose (9%), misidentification (3%), erotomania (2.3%), thought withdrawal (2.1%), mind reading (1.4%), nihilism/death (0.42%), guilt (0.37%), religious (0.23%), jealousy (0.05%) and thought broadcasting (0.05%).

To compare kinds of delusional content, we grouped themes into broader delusional subtypes. Paranormal and technology themes describe the means by which a cause acts rather than its effect, so they overlap substantially with other themes (e.g., a persecutor or a controlling agent can act through supernatural forces or devices). Consistent with this, 92-99% of inferred causes with the other themes were also labeled paranormal or technology. We therefore did not focus on these themes to define main subtypes. We pooled control and thought insertion into a passivity subtype (52% of inferred causes) because both involve an external agent acting on a person's thoughts or actions.

Passivity (control + thought insertion), persecutory and reference were the three largest groups among the inferred causes, but qualitative inspection suggested that reference-like inferred causes resembled real-world reference delusions less closely than passivity-like or persecutory-like inferred causes did. Real-world cases with pre-delusional observations

Case selection and preparation

We manually searched through relevant literature on psychosis and prodrome, including our corpus of cases (see Real-world delusion corpus), for reports of salient events preceding delusion formation (pre-delusional observations). We searched for cases that specifically met the following criteria: (1) at least two distinct predelusional observations that were unusually salient, uncanny or hard to explain; (2) at least one clearly stated delusional belief that emerged after at least some of the observations; and (3) enough temporal and other detail to distinguish the pre-delusional observations from the delusional belief(s). We found five cases meeting these criteria. Two cases came from Conrad 10 and one each from Varsamis & Adamson 66 , Chadwick 67 , and Connors 68 . For the Conrad cases, a native Spanish speaker (GH) translated the relevant passages from a Spanish-language edition into English.

We represented each pre-delusional observation and delusional belief as a short sentence. For observations, we kept the individual's report of what happened but removed any delusion-like interpretations and overt hallucinations (percepts without an external source, such as hallucinated speech). For example, Chadwick 67 reported that he observed simultaneous changes in the rain and his writing and that this was evidence of a deep connection between them; we represented the observation itself as: "The intensity of the rain changed at the same time his writing intensity changed." We split a belief into separate delusional beliefs when its content changed or developed over time. In all sentences, we removed patient names and clinical references, standardized to male pronouns (so that shared pronouns could not inflate within-case similarity), and matched the LLM dataset's short, simple declarative sentences. Together, the five cases contained 18 delusional beliefs and 83 pre-delusional observations; the full lists appear in Supplementary Data 4. Three authors (MS, EL and GH) extracted and cross-checked the items.

GPT-4o might reproduce these cases' delusions from its training data. Given each case's pre-delusional observation set and asked to identify the published case, it identified none and reproduced no delusion. The sources are unlikely to be well represented in its training data: Conrad 10 , originally in German, has no complete English translation, and Connors 68 postdates GPT-4o's training cutoff in 2023.

We treated each delusional belief as a candidate cause of a pre-delusional observation set. We calculated residual observation similarity and positive-relevance rate as above, then averaged each measure across the candidate case's delusional beliefs.

LLM recovery of delusional content

Using the same common-cause prompt as for the LLM dataset, we generated 100 causes per pre-delusional observation set (GPT-4o, temperature = 0.5). For each delusional belief, recovery was its maximum cosine similarity to this set of inferred causes; we averaged these maxima within case. We repeated this analysis after removing each case's pre-delusional observation subspace (obtained by singular value decomposition) from both its delusional beliefs and the inferred causes from every observation set, so that same-case and other-case observation sets were scored in the same residual space. Recovery after removing the observation subspace was the maximum dot product between the residual delusional-belief and inferred-cause embeddings.

Within-observation-set mixed-effects analysis

Because delusion-like inferred causes arose disproportionately from disparate observation sets, we compared causes inferred from the same observation set, holding fixed the observations they were inferred to explain. We analyzed all 10 inferred causes from each of the observation sets (530 sets for 5,300 total inferred causes) with at least one high-and one low-delusionality inferred cause, so that comparisons within each set included both high-and low-delusionality inferred causes. The threshold determined only set eligibility; delusionality was modeled as a continuous predictor.

We fitted a separate linear mixed-effects model for each observation condition, with a random intercept for observation set 69, 70 . We standardized delusionality across included causes and entered both the observation-set mean and each inferred cause's deviation from that mean. The deviation coefficient estimated the within-set association, comparing causes inferred from the same observations. Models also included standardized observation distance and number of observations.

The within-set coefficient is the difference in negative-relevance rate, in percentage points, between causes inferred from the same observation set that differ by one overall SD of delusionality; negative values indicate lower negative-relevance rates for more delusion-like inferred causes. We used Wald z confidence intervals and tests. For display only (Supplementary Fig. S7), we divided each coefficient and its confidence limits by the SD of the negative-relevance rate in that observation condition, placing the three conditions on a standardized scale.

Observation conditions

Original observations

Each inferred cause was evaluated against the observations from which it was inferred, which were identical for all causes from the same set.

New observations

For each observation set, we sampled 10 observations without replacement from the global observation distribution, excluding its original observations. All inferred causes from that observation set were evaluated against the same new observations.

Challenge observations

Challenge observations were intended to reflect the type of "counterevidence" (or "conflicting evidence" or "reasonably contradictory evidence", in the DSM-5) used to assess whether beliefs exhibit enough rigidity (or fixity) to be considered delusional 19 . For each inferred cause from the 530 analyzed observation sets, we prompted GPT-4o to generate ten challenge observations with the following instruction (temperature = 0.5):

Generate 10 observations that would make the candidate cause least likely.

The full prompt (Supplementary Data 2) required each challenge observation to describe an ordinary event that a person could perceive or measure, and excluded supernatural, magical, impossible, or purely logical events. We rated the relevance of the challenge observations to the inferred cause and computed the negative-relevance rate. Because challenge observations were specific to each inferred cause, within-set comparisons in this condition held only the original observations fixed.

Supplementary Note 5. Common-cause-like geometry

Supplementary Note 5. Common-cause-like geometry

Coverage of the global observation distribution

We tested whether observation sets with greater observation distance captured more variance in the global observation distribution, particularly when they contained more observations. To quantify this, we defined variance coverage as the fraction of the variance of the global observation distribution that lies within the span of a set's within-set-centered observations. This centered span describes variation within the set and differs from the uncentered span used for cause-to-own-observation-subspace distance. Coverage is high when the directions in which a set's observations differ from one another are directions of high variance in the global observation distribution. A centered set of 𝑛 observations has rank at most 𝑛 -1, so larger sets can span more directions. We therefore tested whether coverage increased more steeply with observation distance in larger sets.

As in the main analyses, we treated the 2,920 unique unit-normalized observation embeddings (text-embedding-3-small) as the global observation distribution. We used principal component analysis to decompose their empirical covariance matrix, 𝜮 𝒐 , into orthogonal directions, 𝒗 A (eigenvectors), with variances 𝜆 A (eigenvalues); larger 𝜆 A indicates more variance along 𝒗 A . For an observation set 𝑶, let 𝐏 u = denote the orthogonal projector onto the span of its within-set-centered observation embeddings. Variance coverage weights each principal component's variance by the fraction of that component lying in this span, summing over all K components:

Across all observation sets, we regressed variance coverage on observation distance, number of observations and their interaction (OLS regression; standardized variables, as in the main text). Variance coverage increased more steeply with observation distance for larger observation sets (interaction β = 0.19, 95% CI = [0.17, 0.20], P < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. S6A). Cause-to-global-observation-distribution distance showed the same interaction pattern (main text; Supplementary Fig. S6B), as expected if larger, more disparate observation sets leave less of the variation in the global observation distribution outside their subspace.

Delusional theme labeling

Persecutory delusion

The patient believes that he/she is being conspired against or persecuted in some way. A clinician assessing for this delusion type may ask the patient: Have people been bothering you in any way? Have you felt that people are against you? Has anyone been trying to harm you in any way? Has anyone been watching or monitoring you? This is a distinct delusion type from delusions of reference (which may in some ways appear similar but not give clear evidence of malintent of some agent toward the patient that is definitional of persecutory delusions). If malintent is strongly implied but not explicitly stated (e.g. in the case of a belief that someone is 'following' or 'watching' the patient) this can be considered moderate evidence of persecutory delusion presence. An example of clear evidence of malintent is a belief that someone is 'monitoring' or 'spying on' or 'spreading rumors about' the patient. The belief that 'someone is gossiping about or laughing at me [the patient]' should not be considered evidence of persecutory delusion, as there is no explicit evidence of malintent. Another example of only moderate evidence for this delusion type presence is a belief that SOMEONE is being targeted/persecuted, but it is not clear if the patient themself is the object of this targeting/persecution.

Delusion of jealousy

The patient believes his/her spouse is having an affair with someone. A clinician assessing for this delusion type may ask the patient: Have you ever worried that your husband/wife might be unfaithful to you? What evidence do you have?

If it is not clear whether the person having the ostensible affair is ACTUALLY the patient's partner/spouse, this could be considered only moderate evidence of jealous delusion presence. If the patient believes that someone 'is involved' with their partner, do NOT assume that this means romantically involved; this is NOT sufficient evidence on its own to infer presence of a jealous delusion.

Delusion of guilt

The patient believes that he/she has committed some terrible sin or done something unforgivable. A clinician assessing for this delusion type may ask the patient: Have you ever felt that you have done some terrible thing that you deserve to be punished for?

Do not count instances where someone else things or accuses the patient of doing something bad if it is not clear that the patient themself believes they are guilting or bad or sinful (and do not infer that they feel this way).

Grandiose delusion

The patient believes he/she has special powers or abilities. A clinician assessing for this delusion type may ask the patient: Do you have any special or unusual abilities or talents? Do you feel you are going to achieve great things?

There must be explicitly stated evidence for this to be counted; do not infer grandiose self-beliefs based on implicit information.

Religious delusion

The patient is preoccupied with false beliefs of a religious nature. A clinician assessing for this delusion type may ask the patient: Have you had any unusual religious experiences? What was your religious training as a child?

To count as a religious delusion, there should be evidence that a specific religion is the source of some part of the patient's belief (e.g., Christianity, Judaism, Islam, Hinduism, etc). Vague references to supernatural entities that are not likely associated with a specific religion should not be counted.

Somatic delusion

The patient believes that somehow his/her body is diseased, abnormal or changed. A clinician assessing for this delusion type may ask the patient: Is there anything wrong with your body? Have you noticed any change in your appearance?

The belief that a body part is missing count. The belief that (some part of) the patient's body is being controlled should not be counted automatically as somatic, as this is really a delusion of control. It COULD count as somatic as well if there is a substantial independent somatic theme (e.g. as a result of the control, something about the patient's body has been substantially altered, or there is extensive discussion of different specific body parts affected by the control as central part of the delusion). Somatic should generally be considered a delusion about something INTRINSIC to the patient's body (part) being changed/afflicted. Beliefs involving implanted chips or other objects in the patient's body should be considered moderate evidence of this delusion type; only give full confidence if there elaborate discussion of the impact of the implant on the body.

Delusion of reference

The patient believes that insignificant remarks or events refer to him/her or have special meaning. A clinician assessing for this delusion type may ask the patient: Have you ever walked into a room and thought people were talking about you or laughing at you? Have you seen things in magazines or on TV that seem to refer to you or contain a special message for you? Have people communicated with you in any unusual ways? This is a distinct delusion type from persecutory delusions (which are defined as having to do with malintent of some agent toward the patient). They can co-exist, but if all instances of things 'referring' to the patient are clearly persecutory, then rate lower than 1. Only give a full rating of 1 if a substantial independent part of the delusion has to do with referential content. Beliefs that 'someone is gossiping about or laughing at me [the patient]' or that 'someone is following or watching me [the patient]' should be considered an instance of this delusion. One example of a clear referential delusion is the belief that 'everything is staged for the patient'; in cases where the patient believes that 'everything is staged', but there is no clear evidence that it is FOR THEM, this could be considered MODERATE evidence for referential delusion.

Delusion of being controlled

The patient believes that his/her feelings or actions are controlled by some outside force. A clinician assessing for this delusion type may ask the patient: Have you ever felt that you were being controlled by some outside force?

Instances of the patient's body being moved without their volition or of emotions being inserted/influenced without their volition count. Other forms of influence (e.g. influence on the patient's thoughts or physical sensations) do not count. Use of the term 'control' (e.g., 'mind control') is not sufficient to count for this delusion type; it must specifically be about control of emotion or action.

Delusion of mind reading

The patient feels that people can read his/her mind or know his/her thoughts. A clinician assessing for this delusion type may ask the patient: Have you ever had the feeling that people can read your mind? This is distinct from delusions of thought broadcasting; if the patient believes that others are aware of their thoughts due to a process by which their thoughts are actually broadcast ALOUD outside of their head AND AUDIBLY PERCEIVED (i.e. "listened to", "heard" etc) this does not count as mind reading. Mind reading is present when the phrasing is active on the part of the other person who is becoming aware of the patient's thoughts. Use of the term 'mind reading' itself in the patient's belief is not sufficient to count as this type of delusion, because instances in which the patient is only reading OTHERS' mind(s) do NOT count. If the term 'telepathy' is explicitly used without further specification, this should not be counted as mind reading unless other information points strongly to mind reading. If others are aware of the patient's thoughts and it is not explicitly noted as auditorily mediated, this should be considered mind reading.

Delusion of thought broadcasting

The patient believes that his/her thoughts are broadcast so that he himself or others can hear. A clinician assessing for this delusion type may ask the patient: Have you ever heard your own thoughts out loud, as if they were a voice outside your head? Have you ever felt your thoughts were broadcast so other people could hear them? This is distinct from delusions of mind reading; to count as thought broadcasting, the patient must believe that others are aware of their thoughts due to a process by which their thoughts are actually sent out of their head (diffusion) AND AUDIBLY PERCEIVED (i.e. "listened to", "heard" etc). It does not count as thought broadcasting if such a process is not occurring, but rather the other agent is the one actively reading the patient's mind with physical auditory mediation. If the term 'telepathy' is explicitly used without further specification, this should not be counted as thought broadcasting unless other information points strongly to thought broadcasting. If others are aware of the patient's thoughts and it is not explicitly noted as auditorily mediated, this should not be considered thought broadcasting.

Delusion of thought insertion

The patient believes that thoughts that are not his/her own have been inserted into his/her mind. A clinician assessing for this delusion type may ask the patient: Have you ever felt that thoughts were being put into your head by some outside force? Have you ever experienced thoughts that didn't seem to be your own? References to 'influence' or 'thought transmission', e.g. via hypnosis, can be considered MODERATE evidence of the presence of this delusion type. Vague reference to 'mind control' or 'influence' of one's mind or thoughts can only be considered moderate evidence of thought insertion. Reference to others 'reading' or 'monitoring' the patient's mind/thoughts or 'telepathy' do NOT count as thought insertion, as these are a different type of delusion. The patient inserting their thoughts into someone else does not count. There must be explicit evidence that the patient actually believes thoughts THAT ARE NOT THEIR OWN have come into their head; counterfactual beliefs about what would happen if thoughts were to come into their head should not be counted. Only insertion of thoughts should be counted for this category, not insertion of other things (e.g., feelings, emotions, impulses, etc), nor should made actions (i.e. the patient feels they are being made to do something or physically controlled) be counted in the absence of additional mention of inserted thoughts.

Delusion of thought withdrawal

The patient believes that thoughts have been taken away from his/her mind. A clinician assessing for this delusion type may ask the patient: Have you ever felt your thoughts were taken away by some outside force?

Withdrawal of 'ideas' can also be considered thought withdrawal. Thought withdrawal should be considered possibly present if there is vague reference to hypnotic influence but ONLY if the influence is used to take something AWAY from the patient (e.g. 'someone was making their mind go blank with hypnotism'), do NOT count cases where something is added or inserted into the patient's mind. Withdrawal or absence of physical parts (e.g. the patient's brain) does not count. In addition, absence of thoughts alone does not necessarily imply that the thoughts were withdrawn unless the action of withdrawing is explicitly mentioned. Reference to others 'reading' or 'monitoring' the patient's mind/thoughts or 'telepathy' do NOT count as thought withdrawal, as these are a different type of delusion. The patient withdrawing others' thoughts does not count. Only withdrawal of thoughts should be counted for this category, not withdrawal of other things (e.g. feelings, emotions, impulses, etc), nor should made actions (i.e. the patient feels they are being made to do something or physically controlled) be counted in the absence of additional mention of withdrawn thoughts.

Erotomania delusion

The patient believes that another person or people is romantically interested in them or in love with them. A clinician assessing for this delusion type may ask the patient: Do you have a secret admirer? Have you noticed that someone has taken particular sexual or romantic interest in you?

No special principles required for this delusion type.

Delusion of nihilism/death

The patient believes that he/she has no head, has a hollow instead of a brain, cannot see him-/herself in the mirror, has a shadow but no body, does not exist, or is dead. A clinician assessing for this delusion type may ask the patient: Have you noticed that any part of you is missing, absent, or not where it should be? Do you feel that you or someone else in your life is no longer alive?

The belief that a body part is missing counts. Belief that someone other than the patient has died also count for this type; the belief that someone else (not the patient) does not exist is MODERATE evidence for this delusion type. Do not count instances of thoughts being withdrawn (this is rather a thought withdrawal delusion, not a nihilistic delusion). Do not usually count instances of patient beliefs that are vague and non-specific or future-oriented such as 'I may one day die/cease to exist'.

Delusion of catastrophe

The patient believes that the world is about to end, some enormous catastrophe has occurred or will occur, or everything is evil and will be destroyed. Delusion of misidentification

The patient has a mistaken belief about the identity of themself, or other people, places or objects. They may believe that other people, such as close relatives, have been replaced by doubles or impostors; they may believe that strangers are in fact known people in disguise; they may believe that someone has changed physically and psychologically into another person, or that they themselves have physically and psychologically changed into another person; they may believe that there are doubles of known people or places. A clinician assessing for this delusion type may ask the patient: Have you observed that anyone in your life has been replaced by "doubles" or imposters? Have you felt that someone you know is actually pretending to take on the appearance of a stranger? Be conservative. Mistaken beliefs about the patient's own identity do not usually count for this (eg the patient believes that they are a very important person; this does not count). Instances of the patient's own identity counting for this delusion type would be a delusion involving belief that they have a doppelganger or beliefs that their mirror reflection is another person. The core definition is about a mismatch between one's APPEARANCE and their true identity. Grandiose identity delusions do not meet this criterion, as there is no misleading/deceptive appearance involved or a mismatch between true/observed in the delusional logic. There must be change/mismatch of perceived vs. real identity. (Example explaining how grandiose identity doesn't count: One could say they are a reincarnation of Gandhi even if they have never changed (i.e., they were always Gandhi without knowing it) and even if they look nothing like the original Gandhi.) The patient believes he/she is influenced by hypnotism, telepathy or the occult. A clinician assessing for this delusion type may ask the patient: Have you been in contact with or influenced by any supernatural or magical forces? Do you believe in aliens, ghosts, vampires, werewolves, or other non-human creatures?

Clear cases should involve explicit mention of supernatural beings/forces; instances of simply unexplained force or actual physical force exerting influence (e.g., causing a sensation in the patient), should not be counted unless there is explicit mention of the paranormal/supernatural force being responsible. If the supernatural forces derive from the beliefs of a major religious group (e.g. Christianity, Judaism, Islam, Buddhism, Hinduism, etc), this should NOT be counted as paranormal/supernatural. Reference to 'evil spirits' should be considered moderate evidence for paranormal/supernatural ONLY in the absence of evidence that the spirits are part of a specific religious belief the patient holds. In general, if the belief contains any religious content, it likely should NOT be counted. The belief that the patient can read others' minds should be considered telepathy and should be counted, however, the belief that the patient's mind is being read by others does NOT count. The belief that someone is not in fact dead should be considered MODERATE evidence of this delusion type; note that the opposite does NOT count (i.e., the belief that someone IS dead).

These questionnaires differ in ways that have nothing to do with the meaning of the items: the PDI and PANSS ask second-person questions, the SAPS describes a patient for a clinician rater, and the OCI and BDI-II use firstperson statements. In six items the grammatical subject is not the person, such as the person's thoughts or an outside force (e.g., "Unpleasant thoughts come into the person's mind against their will"). Some differences in phrasing between items therefore remain. The 103 statements were 4 to 24 words long (median = 12), within the 25-word limit of the LLM-inferred causes.

The common-cause inference prompt broadens the range of observation-dependent inferred causes

Our common-cause inference prompt (see Generation of inferred causes in Methods) had two design goals: that the LLM (1) infer a single cause that can account for the full observation set and (2) consider a wide range of possible causes in doing so. The second goal matters because many real-world beliefs involve causes that are conventionally considered unlikely: 41% of U.S. adult citizens endorse the statement that a single group secretly controls world events 71 , 83% say that things happen that "cannot be explained by science or natural causes" 72 , 39% believe in ghosts and 27% believe in communication with the dead 73 . To allow such causes, we included the phrase "the cause does not need to be likely" (hereafter, the relaxed prompt). We tested whether this phrase broadened the range of observation-dependent causes that GPT-4o inferred (i.e., causes whose content depends on the observations they explain) or instead shifted its outputs toward unusual content largely independent of the observations. We selected 100 observation sets above the median observation distance (large-observation-distance sets) and 100 below it (small-observation-distance sets). For each set, we compared the 10 inferred causes in the main dataset, which were generated with the relaxed prompt, with 10 inferred causes generated with a baseline prompt that was identical except that it omitted the phrase. This gave 2,000 inferred causes per prompt across the same 200 sets; identical inferred causes were retained.

First, we asked whether the phrase was necessary for delusion-like content to emerge. If the phrase simply broadened the range of inferred causes, high-delusionality causes should still appear under the baseline prompt, only less often. They did occur in some instances; examples included "Julia is a vampire" and "A time-traveling helper is secretly aiding your community." The phrase was therefore not necessary for delusion-like content.

Second, we asked whether the phrase increased variation among the causes inferred from the same observations, measured as inferred-cause dispersion: the mean pairwise cosine distance among a set's inferred causes. The relaxed prompt increased inferred-cause dispersion (mean relaxed-minus-baseline increase = 0.09 cosinedistance units, 95% CI = [0.07, 0.11], P < 0.001; right-tailed paired t-test with 199 degrees of freedom), more so for sets with large rather than small observation distance (difference in dispersion increase between large-and small-observation-distance sets, b = 0.10 cosine-distance units, 95% CI = [0.06, 0.14], P < 0.001; OLS regression, two-sided t-test with 198 degrees of freedom).

Third, we asked whether the two prompts produced overlapping inferred causes from the same observation sets. Qualitatively, some inferred causes were identical under both prompts ("John is very stressed," "Katie is going on a vacation," "You overslept and missed your alarm," "A volcano erupted nearby"), and others were close paraphrases ("A massive snow hit the town" and "A major snowstorm hit the town"). To quantify this overlap, we computed for each observation set the centroid (unit-normalized mean) of its inferred-cause embeddings under each prompt. Centroids from the two prompts were more similar when they came from the same observation set than from other observation sets with the same observation-distance category and number of observations (mean same-set minus other-set difference = 0.54 cosine-similarity units, 95% CI = [0.50, 0.58], P < 0.001; right-tailed within-set permutation test). The same held when we compared individual inferred causes from the relaxed and baseline prompts instead of centroids (mean same-set minus other-set difference = 0.45 cosine-similarity units, 95% CI = [0.41, 0.48], P < 0.001; right-tailed within-set permutation test). Each permutation test pooled each set's same-set score with its other-set scores, randomly relabeled one of them as same-set and compared it with the set's other-set mean (5,000 permutations). The two prompts therefore produced inferred causes with observation-set-specific content in common.

The main text further shows that inferred causes remain anchored to observation geometry even at large observation distances (Emergence of extraordinary content from common-cause inference) and that observation content predicts whether delusion-like inferred causes are passivity-like or persecutory-like (Observation content predicts delusional subtype). Observation dependence therefore extended to the unusual content itself, not only to names, events or other surface details. Together, these results are consistent with the phrase relaxing plausibility constraints and broadening the range of observation-dependent causes sampled by the LLM. This approach thus successfully produced varied LLM-inferred causes whose content remained related to their observations.

Observation distance and inferred-cause variability

We tested whether causes inferred from the same observation set varied more when the observations were more disparate. For each observation set, we calculated inferred-cause dispersion (the mean pairwise cosine distance among its 10 inferred causes; Supplementary Note 1) and the standard deviation of their delusionality scores, and regressed each on observation distance (OLS regression, controlling for the number of observations). Greater observation distance was associated with greater inferred-cause dispersion (β = 0.60, 95% CI = [0.57, 0.64], P < 0.001) and a greater standard deviation of delusionality (β = 0.50, 95% CI = [0.46, 0.54], P < 0.001).

For example, the causes inferred from one observation set ranged from "You are recovering from a mild illness" to "Aliens have secretly taken over Earth." This range parallels the pre-delusional state, in which people are uncertain about the meaning of their experiences and entertain multiple hypotheses before settling on a single fixed belief 74.
