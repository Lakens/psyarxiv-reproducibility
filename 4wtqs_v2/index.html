We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p=0.45 In addition, a meta-regression comparing studies that used Prompts/cues alone to studies pairing Prompts/cues with another behavior change technique did not show a significant difference in medication adherence effects (95% CI=-0.47-1.05; p=0.45).

For metascientific articles demonstrating the rate of misinterpretations of non-significant results is high, see:

Aczel B, Palfi B, Szollosi A, Kovacs M, Szaszi B, Szecsi P, Zrubka M, Gronau Q, van den Bergh D, Wagenmakers E (2018). “Quantifying Support for the Null Hypothesis in Psychology: An Empirical Investigation.” Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science, 1(3), 357–366. doi:10.1177/2515245918773742.

Murphy S, Merz R, Reimann L, Fernández A (2025). “Nonsignificance misinterpreted as an effect’s absence in psychology: Prevalence and temporal analyses.” Royal Society Open Science, 12(3), 242167. doi:10.1098/rsos.242167.

For educational material on preventing the misinterpretation of p values, see Improving Your Statistical Inferences.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Background: Medication adherence is critical for chronic disease management, yet poor adherence remains an unsolved challenge. Digital health interventions are promising to support medication adherence but are limited by poor user engagement with the tools. Understanding which strategies effectively support user engagement could improve the efficacy and sustainability of these interventions. Purpose: This systematic review and meta-analysis characterized strategies used to support user engagement in digital adherence interventions with the well-established Behavior Change Technique Taxonomy and evaluated whether these strategies were associated with medication adherence and user engagement outcomes. Methods: Electronic databases (Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), Embase via Ovid, MEDLINE, PsycInfo, and Web of Science Core Collection) were searched (4/18/2025) to identify randomized controlled trials of app-based digital adherence interventions for people with chronic medical conditions taking oral medications, published in English between 2020 and 2025. User engagement strategies were characterized with the Behavior Change Technique Taxonomy. Meta-regression models evaluated associations between specific techniques and study outcomes. Risk of bias was characterized with the Risk of Bias-1 measure.

Results: The 60 studies included for review predominantly included adults with cardiovascular disease, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes (median N=117, IQR=72.0-204.5). All but one included at least one user engagement strategy appropriate to characterize with the Behavior Change Technique Taxonomy. The most common techniques were Prompts/cues (90%), Instruction on how to perform the behavior (70%), Adding objects to the environment (35%), Feedback on behavior (28%), Self-monitoring of behavior (25%), Credible source (22%), Goal setting (behavior) (20%), and Self-monitoring outcome(s) of behavior (20%). Prompts/cues were frequently paired with another behavior change technique (54%). User engagement was rarely measured as an outcome, and thus, it was not possible to evaluate this outcome in the metaanalysis. In the 44 studies included in the meta-analysis of medication adherence outcomes, there were no statistically significant associations with user engagement techniques. Conclusions: Digital adherence interventions commonly include strategies to support user engagement, particularly prompts/cues, but it remains unknown whether these strategies translate to actual user engagement. No user engagement strategy was associated with medication adherence outcomes. This may reflect variability across study samples and medication adherence measurement approaches, threats to internal validity, or reliance on ineffective strategies. Further research should continue to evaluate which engagement strategies support sustained digital adherence tool use and improved adherence outcomes.

: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Medication adherence, or the extent to which a person's medication-taking behavior aligns with their prescribed regimen [1], is essential for effective long-term management of chronic disease [2]. However, poor medication adherence is extremely common [3], with at least 50% of patients demonstrating suboptimal adherence [2]. Nonadherence has consistently been linked to accelerated disease progression, poorer health outcomes, increased hospitalizations, and higher healthcare costs [4-6]. For many patients, adherence is complicated by personal barriers, such as forgetfulness, limited social support, and complexity of managing multiple medications. Adherence promotion interventions are critically needed to help patients overcome barriers, consistently take medicine, and support positive health outcomes [7]. Digital health interventions are promising tools to support medication adherence and disease self-management [8-10]. Digital health involves the use of information and communication technologies, such as wearable and non-wearable devices, smartphone apps, and text messaging [9], to help people manage illness and promote wellness [11]. These tools have the potential to help patients more easily engage in their own care [9], by providing real-time adherence intervention and support integrated in patients' daily home routines. Moreover, over 90% of Americans own a smartphone, indicating that digital health interventions could help increase access to empirically-supported adherence-promoting strategies [12].

However, a major limitation of digital health interventions is poor user engagement with these tools [13] [14] [15] [16] [17] [18]. User engagement includes both behavioral engagement, or interactions with and use of the technology, and the subjective experience of using the intervention tools [19, 20].

Higher behavioral engagement with digital adherence interventions is associated with improved medication adherence outcomes [21].

[div-01]

Thus, if behavioral engagement with a digital health intervention is inconsistent or insufficient, users may not experience meaningful adherence benefit. However, little is known about how to best support sustained use of digital health tools, or how much engagement is necessary to produce behavioral or health benefit [18, 21, 22].

Consistent with the limited empirical literature on user engagement with digital health tools, including those targeting medication adherence [21], the specific strategies used to support behavioral engagement remain poorly understood. Characterizing user engagement strategies in existing digital adherence interventions could help to identify which techniques are most effective. Specifically, applying the Behavior Change Technique taxonomy, a standardized framework of 93 discrete techniques [23], to existing digital adherence interventions may help identify which strategies have been used to support user engagement and whether they are associated with better medication adherence and engagement outcomes. To our knowledge, the Behavior Change Technique taxonomy has not been used to characterize strategies for supporting user engagement in digital adherence interventions.

Thus, the objectives of this systematic review and meta-analysis are to answer the following research questions: (1) what proportion of user-facing digital adherence interventions for people with chronic medical conditions include strategies to support user engagement with digital tools? (2) which behavior change techniques characterize the user engagement strategies used in these interventions? (3) do interventions with user engagement strategies have stronger effects on medication adherence and user engagement outcomes than those that do not? and (4) which user engagement strategies (characterized as behavior change techniques) are associated with stronger effects on medication adherence and user engagement outcomes?

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was preregistered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (registration number masked for review) and conducted and reported in accordance with PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis) guidelines [24]. The review team, comprised of the Principal Investigator (masked for review) and five Research Assistants (masked for review), established a standardized workflow documented in a Manual of Operating Procedures for literature screening and data extraction. This systematic review and meta-analysis of published research studies did not require Institutional Review Board approval.

Search Strategy

The search strategy was developed by the Principal Investigator and an Informationist at the (masked for review) Library. The databases searched include the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), Embase via Ovid, MEDLINE, PsycInfo, and Web of Science Core Collection, restricted to manuscripts published between 2020-2025 (date of search: 4/18/2025). See Supplemental Figure 1 for search query details.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Manuscripts were evaluated for the following inclusion criteria: (1) empirical study involving original data collection, (2) evaluation of a digital health intervention in a randomized controlled trial, (3) intervention targeted oral medication adherence, which was quantitatively measured, (4) intervention was used by people managing a chronic medical condition and/or their caregivers, and (5) the study was published in a peer-reviewed journal between 2020-2025.

Although user engagement was a main outcome of interest, we included studies that did not quantitatively measure user engagement if all other inclusion criteria were met given that user engagement is not consistently measured in digital health research [21]. Exclusion criteria were (1) not published in a peer-reviewed journal, (2) not available in English, (3) not published between 2020-2025, (4) does not include participants following an oral medication regimen for a chronic medical condition (excludes acute illness and short course medication regimens), (5) does not evaluate a patient or caregiver-facing digital health intervention for adherence promotion (excluded interventions used by healthcare providers only), (6) single group experimental design, qualitative-only or descriptive only study, (7) systematic review, metaanalysis, think piece, conference abstract, or published protocol.

Selection Process

Citations returned from the search strategy were imported into Covidence [25], an online collaborative platform for streamlined systematic review and meta-analysis study selection and data extraction. Duplicate citations were removed, followed by title and abstract screening and full-text review (detailed below). At each level of review, manuscripts were evaluated by two blinded review team members. Discrepancies were resolved through discussions between the Principal Investigator and review team members at regular research meetings.

Title and Abstract Screening. Titles and abstracts were reviewed to identify manuscripts that appeared to meet inclusion criteria or needed further investigation to determine qualification at the full-text review phase. Manuscripts that clearly did not meet inclusion criteria were excluded at this screening stage.

Full Text Review.

The review team conducted a full-text review of all studies that appeared to meet inclusion criteria based on the title and abstract screening phase to confirm if inclusion criteria were met. Manuscripts meeting inclusion criteria at this level of review progressed to the Data Extraction phase and were included in the final systematic review and meta-analysis.

Data Extraction.

The review team extracted key participant and intervention characteristics, quantitative measures of medication adherence and user engagement, and, when available, data necessary for calculating a standardized effect size (e.g., mean, standard deviation). Any data that were unavailable or not reported were coded as "999" or "no/not reported." Medication adherence was operationalized as the extent to which a person followed their prescribed oral medication regimen. User engagement was operationalized as users' behavioral interactions with or use of the intervention technology (i.e., how often a user logs into the app or module completion) rather than the subjective experience of interacting with the technology [20, 26].

If the study authors described discrete strategies to support behavioral user engagement and interaction with the digital adherence intervention, the review team characterized the strategies with the Behavior Change Technique Taxonomy [23] following a decision algorithm developed by the Principal Investigator and review team. Reviewers first determined whether the intervention supported, elicited, or encouraged behavioral engagement or interactions with any component of the digital health tools, such as prompting users to enter medication adherence data in an app or setting weekly app usage goals. If the intervention supported, elicited, or encouraged behavioral engagement or interactions with the digital health tools, the review team characterized the strategies used to support user engagement using the Behavior Change Technique Taxonomy. Multiple behavior change techniques could be assigned to an intervention. After independent reviewer coding, behavior change technique assignments underwent additional review for accuracy and agreement by the Principal Investigator and two other team members (masked for review), and discrepancies were resolved through group discussion.

Risk of Bias Assessment

For each included study, the methodological quality was evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias-1 tool (ROB1) [27], which evaluates six domains: selection bias (random sequence generation and allocation concealment), performance bias (blinding of participants and personnel), detection bias (blinding of outcome assessors), attrition bias (incomplete outcome data), reporting bias (selective outcome reporting), and other potential sources of bias not captured in the other domains. Each study was independently assessed by two blinded coders, with discrepancies resolved by the review team in consultation with the Principal Investigator to characterize the risk of bias. Studies were characterized as (1) meeting all criteria; (2) failing to meet one criterion or had at least two criteria rated as "unclear" but were considered unlikely to have introduced significant bias; or (3) failing to meet at least one criterion or had at least two criteria that were "unclear" and likely introduced significant bias. Additional information was not sought by the review team if the study authors' reporting was unclear or unavailable in the manuscript. Given that this systematic review and meta-analysis focused on user engagement strategies rather than overall intervention effectiveness, no further certainty assessment beyond risk of bias was conducted.

Synthesis Methods

Study characteristics were descriptively reported (frequencies, percentages). When available, standardized effect sizes were calculated for medication adherence and user engagement outcomes to measure the magnitude of difference between randomized groups.

Effect sizes were initially expressed as Hedges' g or log odds ratios depending on the available outcome data; log odds ratio effect sizes were then converted to Hedges' g to provide a standardized effect size comparable across studies. When studies reported multiple outcomes, the primary medication adherence or user engagement outcome was selected for effect size calculation. If no primary outcome was identified, the outcome that was objectively measured was selected first, followed by the outcome most directly reflective of medication adherence or user engagement. If outcomes were measured at multiple time points, the measurement closest to the end of the intervention phase was selected. These effect sizes were used in the planned random-effects models to evaluate whether intervention effects differed between interventions targeting user engagement versus not and by user engagement-behavior change technique used.

Publication bias was characterized using a funnel plot. Heterogeneity was characterized with the I² test and 95% confidence intervals were reported as appropriate. Data analyses were conducted in RStudio with the metafor package [28].

Results

Search Results

The initial search returned 2,313 studies, of which 1,020 duplicate citations were removed. During title and abstract screening, 1,142 studies were removed (did not meet inclusion criteria) resulting in 151 studies that underwent full-text review. Of these 151 studies, 91 (60%) were excluded because the study was a conference abstract, pre-print, or protocol only. Other reasons for exclusion were that the intervention, outcomes, patient population, or study design did not meet inclusion criteria or the manuscript was not available in English, resulting in 60 studies included in the systematic review (see Supplemental File 1 for all included studies and key characteristics). Of these 60 studies, only three quantitatively measured user engagement and reported sufficient data for effect size calculation, thus it was not possible to evaluate user engagement effects by user engagement-behavior change technique. For medication adherence, 45 (75%) studies reported data appropriate for effect size calculation; however, one study was a significant outlier [29] and was, therefore, excluded from meta-analysis. Thus, 44 studies were included in the meta-analysis of medication adherence outcomes. See Figure 1 for a flow diagram of the initial search, screening, and reasons for exclusion.

Study Characteristics

Out of the 60 studies included in this systematic review, 75% (n=45) were performed outside of the United States. Consistent with the inclusion criteria, all studies were randomized controlled trials. The median sample size at randomization was 117 (IQR 72.0-204.5), and the median sample size allocated to the treatment group was 57.5 (IQR 35.0-102.0). The most common medical conditions were cardiovascular and related diseases (n=12, 20%), hypertension (n=11, 18%) Type 2 Diabetes (n=9, 15%), cancer (n=7, 12%), and HIV (n=7, 12%). Consistent with inclusion criteria, all included studies evaluated an app-based intervention. Over half involved human support to engage with the intervention (n=37, 62%). Only 20% (n=12) reported providing incentives for study participation. See Table 1 for more details.

Risk of Bias

Nearly all the studies (n=56, 93%) failed to meet at least one risk of bias assessment criterion or had at least two criteria that were "unclear" and likely introduced significant bias, primarily due to challenges blinding participants and personnel to intervention group assignment.

The remaining four (7%) studies failed to meet one criterion or had at least two criteria rated as "unclear" but were considered unlikely to have introduced significant bias. As a sensitivity analysis to remove blinding from the assessment criteria, 67% (n=40) still failed to meet at least one risk of bias assessment criterion or had at least two criteria that were unclear and likely introduced bias, 30% (n=18) failed to meet one criterion or had at least two criteria rated as "unclear" but were considered unlikely to have introduced bias, and 3% (n=2) met all criteria (low risk of bias).

Discussion

Discussion adherence, but their efficacy and scalability remain limited by poor and inconsistent user engagement [21, 30].

Digital health interventions have promise for addressing suboptimal medication

Further, little is known about how to optimally support user engagement with digital adherence tools to, in turn, support improved medication adherence or sustained use of the tools [21, 31, 32]. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to address this gap as the first to characterize the discrete strategies used to support user engagement with digital adherence interventions, using the well-established Behavior Change Technique Taxonomy [23], and to evaluate whether these strategies were associated with stronger effects on medication adherence and user engagement outcomes.

Nearly all studies reviewed incorporated at least one strategy to encourage user engagement, which enabled us to systematically characterize these strategies as discrete behavior change techniques [23]. The most common technique was Prompts/cues, often as push notifications and text messages, which served as reminders for users to engage with the digital adherence intervention. Over half combined Prompts/cues with another behavior change technique (e.g., delivering a push notification prompting the user to record taking their daily medication dose in the app (self-monitoring of behavior)). Given that inclusion criteria required the intervention to include a smartphone app, it is not surprising that features intrinsic to smartphones, like push notifications and text messages, dominated.

Instructions on how to perform the behavior, typically in the form of structured guidance (human, video, in-app) on how to navigate and use the digital tools after enrolling in the study, was also common. Onboarding and uptake challenges are well-known in digital health tools regardless of behavioral target [13, 14, [16] [17] [18]. Thus, it may be beneficial to support users' success with using the tools from the start, thereby helping to support continued engagement.

Notably, over one-third of reviewed studies incorporated Adding objects to the environment by providing participants with a connected device, such as an electronic pill bottle or Bluetooth blood pressure monitor, to use at home. Integrating devices into participant's home environments may help make the intervention more visible and accessible. In turn, this strategy could encourage more consistent engagement and provide an alternative digital health-based approach to promoting intervention engagement beyond push notifications or text messages.

Other frequently used techniques included Feedback on behavior, Self-monitoring of behavior, Credible source, Goal setting (behavior), and Self-monitoring of outcome(s) of behavior. These techniques, as well as Prompt/cues, overlap with those identified in effective digital adherence and self-management interventions [10], highlighting similarities in strategies designed to both promote engagement with the technology itself and strategies intended to target engagement in treatment adherence and self-management behavior(s). It is important to note that this review characterized strategies intended to support behavioral engagement with the digital intervention tools, but some strategies, such as Prompts/cues, may have concurrently served as a reminder to take medication. This duality is important to consider in digital health intervention development, particularly to clearly delineate techniques functioning to support user engagement with the digital tools, taking medicine or engaging in self-management behaviors, or both.

Less than 10% of studies used rewards or incentives to encourage engagement with digital health tools. This may reflect practical and financial challenges of providing compensation for digital tool use. It is also possible that relatively small engagement behaviors, such as logging into an app or using a daily electronic pillbox, are not sufficiently motivating targets for small monetary rewards, even when rewards and incentives may increase over time with continued user engagement.

Consistent with prior research showing that user engagement has not been regularly evaluated in digital adherence and self-management interventions [21], we were not able to conduct a meta-analysis of user engagement outcomes because very few studies included appropriate outcome measures. Thus, it remains unknown whether the user engagement techniques characterized in this systematic review effectively encourage sustained use of the digital tools. Compounding this challenge, comparison groups often did not involve a digital intervention (e.g., treatment as usual) indicating a need for intervention engagement measures that are applicable to both digital-and human-delivered modalities.

Despite the common use of strategies to support user engagement with digital tools, none of the techniques evaluated via meta-regression were significantly associated with medication adherence outcomes. One possible explanation is that reliance on Prompts/cues contributes to "alert fatigue," which occurs when people become desensitized to excessive notifications and alerts and stop responding to them [33, 34]. Because people are often inundated with push notifications and text messages, digital health notifications may be easily ignored. This suggests that future digital adherence interventions should advance beyond simple, generic, static push notifications and text messages. A transformative approach that could reduce alert fatigue is developing prompts that adapt to users' contexts, preferences, and behavior [35, 36]. In addition, the second most common user engagement-behavior change technique, Instruction on how to perform the behavior, was typically used at the beginning of the study to assist participants with learning how to use the technology; this strategy may be most helpful for initial engagement rather than supporting longer-term medication adherence. Continued research is needed to understand how to leverage user engagement strategies to also support medication adherence. This systematic review and meta-analysis has some limitations. The majority of studies failed to meet all risk of bias criteria primarily due to challenges with blinding. There was high heterogeneity in medication adherence outcomes across studies, likely reflecting variability in chronic medical conditions represented and differences in how medication adherence was measured. These are all inherent limitations in digital health and medication adherence research but are important to consider when interpreting findings, particularly the null meta-regression results. Finally, a limitation of systematic reviews is that research continues to be published during the data extraction process, a challenge compounded by rapid advances in digital health including artificial intelligence (AI) and how AI could affect user engagement. As such, this field is expected to continue evolving and an updated review will be warranted in the future.

The findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis show that digital adherence intervention developers have incorporated a range of strategies aligned with the well-established Behavior Change Technique Taxonomy [23], though heavily reliant on Prompts/cues, to encourage user engagement with the tools. However, it remains unknown which strategies optimally support both user engagement with the tools or improved medication adherence, a critical gap warranting further empirical investigation. Notably, AI, which was not part of any user engagement strategy characterized in this review, is increasingly integrated into digital health interventions [37] and has potential to transform engagement strategies (e.g., AIpersonalized prompts/cues and proactive feedback about behavior or outcomes of behavior).

However, AI may also introduce unintended negative effects on digital health users [38] and does not guarantee increased interactions with the digital tools. Future researchers should advance both the design and evaluation of digital health interventions for medication adherence by carefully selecting strategies aimed at supporting user engagement and prospectively evaluating whether the strategies support meaningful user interactions with the tools as well as improved medication adherence outcomes. the Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
