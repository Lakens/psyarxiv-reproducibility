Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 51 papers with 87 statements, this module correctly identified 38 statements (true positives) and incorrectly flagged 22 statements (false positives). It failed to detect 27 statements. Thus, among all statements flagged by the module, 63% were genuine cases (positive predictive value). However, the module missed 42% of all true statements (false negative rate).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

We try to exclude figure and table notes like “* p < .05”, but may not succeed at excluding all false positives.

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. It subsequently checks different ways in which Cohen’s d, g, ηp2, and ωp2 can be computed against the reported value. If effects are missing, or might be incorrect, you the module provides a warning. The module was validated on APA reported statistical tests, and might miss effect sizes that were reported in other reporting styles. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

Within two years, an infant who can barely lift her head learns to sit, reach, and walk.

Developmental science found that this growth is both continuous and phased: skills improve smoothly, yet the pace of improvement changes at identifiable ages, and the field built statistical tools to locate those changes. Language models also transform across training, and whether their capabilities grow smoothly or in jumps is actively debated. The present study argues that the developmental answer applies to artificial learners as well, and that the developmental tools can show it. We apply an analysis pipeline built for infant motor development to the training trajectories of 16 open-source language models, each tested at 27 points during training on five benchmarks. The results mirror the infant findings. Growth is continuous but organized into phases, and transitions cluster at a small set of shared points that are deployed differently across domains. Growth proceeds slowly, then rapidly, then slowly, and models differ reliably in developmental rate. Unlike infants, those rate differences are almost entirely explained by model size. The transitions also survive changes of measurement scale, and on a continuous measure they appear earlier and more sharply than on accuracy. The paper contributes a formal test for phase structure in capability growth, a construct mapping between infant development and language model development, and evidence that phase structure may be a shared property of learning systems.

Trajectories in Language Model Training

A Debate About the Shape of Growth

At some point between the first and the last of 143,000 training steps, a large language model (LLM) that cannot complete a sentence becomes one that answers science questions and follows a story to its final word. That path is observable. Language models are trained by practicing next-word prediction on enormous amounts of text, and copies of the model, called checkpoints, can be saved along the way. Each checkpoint can be tested on standardized benchmarks, fixed sets of questions administered identically every time, so a capability can be traced as a curve across training. The shape of that curve is now the subject of an active debate.

One side describes the curve as smooth. Scaling laws show that performance improves as a predictable function of a model's size, its training data, and the computation spent on training (Kaplan et al., 2020). A single equation summarizes an enormous range of training runs, but it cannot represent periods of faster and slower change, and later work found that scaling curves often contain breaks (Caballero et al., 2023). The other side describes the curve as discontinuous.

Studies of emergent abilities catalogue capabilities that appear absent in smaller or less-trained models and then present, sharply, in larger or further-trained ones (Srivastava et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2022). The question has practical consequences, since an ability that arrives abruptly, including an unwanted one, arrives without warning. A prominent critique answered that many of these jumps are illusions of measurement. A test that scores each answer simply right or wrong can turn smooth improvement into an apparent leap (Schaeffer et al., 2023). Case studies of models' internal structure show that some transitions are real. Small models abruptly acquire an internal circuit for reusing patterns from earlier in a passage (Olsson et al., 2022). Networks trained on arithmetic suddenly shift from memorizing to generalizing (Nanda et al., 2023; Power et al., 2022), and language models acquire syntax-sensitive internal structure in a brief window (Chen et al., 2024). Each of these studies, however, built its own instruments for its own case, and the findings do not combine into a general method.

A notable feature of this exchange is that both sides argue about whether trajectories contain discontinuities without a shared method for detecting discontinuities in trajectories. No existing approach specifies how many phases a capability's growth contains, where the boundaries fall, or whether different capabilities follow different schedules. At present, whether a curve is smooth or phased is judged plot by plot, by visual inspection.

Developmental Science Resolved a Parallel Debate

The argument has a precedent. For most of a century, developmental science asked whether infant development unfolds in discrete stages or continuously. Stage theories treated each new skill as a sudden acquisition (Gesell, 1928; McGraw, 1943; Piaget, 1952). Dynamic systems theories treated new behaviors as gradual accumulations of strength, control, and attention, with apparent jumps arising when change in one component tips the whole system. The newborn stepping reflex disappears as leg mass changes, and reaching advances and retreats as motor and perceptual systems come into alignment (Adolph & Robinson, 2008; Corbetta & Snapp-Childs, 2009; Thelen & Smith, 1994). The field settled on an answer that dissolves the dichotomy. Development is both, depending on the level of description. Skills improve continuously, with no jumps between discrete states, yet the pace of improvement changes at particular ages (Adolph & Hoch, 2019; Spencer et al., 2011). A phase, in this view, is a stretch of a continuous curve with its own rate of growth. The productive question is no longer whether growth is smooth or stage-like, but when its pace changes and by how much, and that question can be answered empirically.

Seen this way, infant motor development has three signatures. Growth reorganizes at a few identifiable ages, around independent sitting, walking onset, and locomotor consolidation (Adolph, 1997; Adolph et al., 2012; WHO Multicentre Growth Reference Study Group, 2006). Domains follow their own timetables, since each reorganization opens a new learning space (Iverson, 2021; Libertus & Hauf, 2017). Sitting frees the hands for object exploration (Soska et al., 2010), and walking changes what infants see and how caregivers talk to them, with consequences for language (Kretch et al., 2014; Schneider & Iverson, 2022; Walle & Campos, 2014). Finally, individual infants differ in pace along a continuous landscape rather than sorting into types (Adolph & Hoch, 2019; Smith & Thelen, 2003). Developmental science also built the statistics to detect these signatures: growth-curve models, changepoint models, and spline-based models of growth (Cudeck & Klebe, 2002; Grimm et al., 2011; Harring et al., 2021; Howe et al., 2016). These were recently combined into a single pipeline that, applied to a longitudinal infant sample, recovered transition ages from the data that matched known motor milestones and showed that individual differences in growth rate are substantial (An et al., 2026).

Machine learning and developmental science already trade ideas in both directions.

Developmental robotics has long drawn on infancy research (Cangelosi & Schlesinger, 2018; Oudeyer & Smith, 2016), and language models are now trained on child-sized or childperspective data (Frank, 2023; Hu et al., 2024; Vong et al., 2024; Warstadt et al., 2025). The exchange has not yet included developmental science's trajectory statistics, the methods built for precisely the curve-shape question on which the emergence debate turns.

The aim of the present study is to establish whether the capability growth of language models has the phase structure that developmental science found in infants, and to do so with the same statistical test. To that end, we take the infant pipeline of An et al. (2026) and apply it to language model training trajectories, treating models as participants, checkpoints as visits, and benchmarks as developmental domains. In essence, the current study is a transfer of a validated developmental method to a new population of learners. It follows the tradition of computational work that brings developmental data and methods to bear on language models (Frank, 2023; Vong et al., 2024). Three questions guide the work. First, does a phase-structured model describe the growth of capabilities over training better than smooth alternatives? Second, where do the transitions fall, and do they differ across domains? Third, how much do individual models differ in developmental rate, independent of their initial level?

Method Participants and Design

The data come from the Pythia suite (Biderman et al., 2023), a family of 16 language models released so that researchers can study how models change over training. The suite spans eight sizes, from 70 million to 12 billion parameters, each trained in two versions: one on a large public text collection and one on a copy with duplicate documents removed. Every model saw the same text in the same order, so the models differ principally in size, a stable and exactly measured constitutional variable of a kind unavailable in human samples. Model weights were saved at 154 checkpoints. The suite's released evaluations, computed with a standard opensource evaluation toolkit (Gao et al., 2023), cover 27 of them, densely spaced early in training and every 10,000 steps later on, with no evaluated checkpoint between steps 3,000 and 13,000.

One observation is one benchmark score for one model at one checkpoint, and each per-task analysis draws on 16 models × 27 checkpoints = 432 observations, close in size to the perdomain analyses of the infant study (433 observations).

In developmental terms, each model is a participant, each checkpoint is a visit, and each benchmark is a standardized assessment administered identically at every visit (Table 1). We take the logarithm of the training step as the developmental clock, since early training, like early infancy, contains a disproportionate share of change. Training steps index accumulated experience rather than maturation, since nothing changes in these systems except through learning. The mapping is therefore an analogy about the shape of learning trajectories, not a claim that the mechanisms are the same.

Measures

Five benchmarks serve as developmental domains. LAMBADA requires predicting the final word of a passage that only makes sense if the whole story has been followed (Paperno et al., 2016). SciQ probes accumulated science knowledge (Welbl et al., 2017). PIQA probes physical common sense (Bisk et al., 2020). ARC-Easy probes grade-school science reasoning (Clark et al., 2018). Winogrande probes commonsense reasoning through pronoun resolution (Sakaguchi et al., 2020). Like the motor domains of the infant study, these tasks tap distinguishable capacities that could develop on different schedules. A documented audit selected these five from the released evaluations, excluding tasks that never rise above chance or lack early coverage. One excluded near-chance task, ARC-Challenge, was retained as a comparison case in the robustness analysis.

Results

Growth Is Phased, Not Smooth

The phase-structured model outperformed all four conventional alternatives on every task (Table 2). The margins over the linear and logarithmic curves were decisive by any convention, and the logarithmic curve, which represents the hypothesis that capability simply accelerates early and then levels off, fit worst of all. The cubic polynomial was the closest competitor, as expected, since the spline is itself built from cubic segments. Nonetheless, on four of the five tasks the spline's margin over the cubic was roughly 10 times the corresponding margin in the infant study, and that margin survives a conservative correction for the knot search. Winogrande is the exception. Its small margin over the cubic does not survive the correction, so we classify four tasks, rather than five, as decisively phase-structured and defer Winogrande's status to the robustness analysis.

Where the Transitions Fall, and How Growth Moves Between Them

The data-selected transitions occupied a shared set of training steps, deployed domainspecifically (Table 3). Three tasks placed their transitions near steps 3,000 and 13,000. SciQ, the knowledge task, reorganized earliest, near steps 1,000 and 13,000, and Winogrande selected only a single, weakly determined late knot. The independent fits sharpened this picture (Table 4).

Nearly every individual curve preferred the phase-structured model, and the few exceptions were trajectories that remained near chance, precisely where no phase structure should exist. On SciQ, most models selected the identical configuration, and the four structured tasks concentrated their first transitions within the first few thousand steps and their second within the tens of thousands.

Winogrande's independent knots were distributed across the whole training run with no agreement, which converts the pooled analysis's uncertainty into a clear conclusion. This task has no robust transition within the training window, and it serves as an internal negative control. One size-related pattern also emerged. On two tasks, the models that exited their rapid-growth phase latest were the smallest. Within-phase rates followed a consistent shape (Table 3). On the four structured tasks, growth began slowly, accelerated to several times the initial rate in a middle phase, and then decelerated, with confidence intervals that did not overlap across phases. This is the slow-fastslow signature the infant study described as grounding, expanding, and mastering (An et al., 2026). The tasks ended training in different phases. SciQ decelerated almost to zero, the profile of a consolidating capability. Winogrande was still accelerating when training ended, the profile of a capability whose expansion phase was interrupted by the end of the training budget. Note. Two excluded ARC-Challenge curves, carried as a near-chance comparison, also failed to prefer the spline, giving 91 of 96 curves overall. Full knot distributions are given in Supplementary Table S4.

Learners Differ in Rate, and Size Explains Why

Individual differences in developmental rate exist and are reliable. On every task, a model that allowed each learner its own growth rate fit the data decisively better than a model that gave all learners the same rate (Table 5). The share of score variance attributable to stable differences between models varied by domain. It was smallest on SciQ, the knowledge task, where all 16 learners followed nearly the same track, and largest on the reasoning-weighted tasks, where the learners spread apart. Language models, like infants, do not all travel the same curve at the same speed (An et al., 2026).

Pace was independent of starting point. Before the first transition, the 16 models were nearly indistinguishable in accuracy, and the small variation in starting level that did exist did not predict a model's later rate on any task. The faster learners were not the ones with an early advantage. The infant study reached a parallel conclusion by a different route. Infants varied in their starting levels, and those starting levels did not predict how fast they grew (An et al., 2026).

In both populations, how fast a learner develops cannot be read from where it begins.

The source of the rate differences is where the two populations diverge. The infant study established that rate differences exist but could not identify their source, given the complexity of human development. In the language models, by contrast, parameter count and environment were strictly controlled. Each model's growth rate correlated almost perfectly with the logarithm of its parameter count on every task (Table 5). Larger models developed faster, and size alone accounted for nearly all of the variation in pace. The training variants make the same point from the other direction. Two models of the same size trained on different data, an environmental manipulation, developed at nearly identical rates, so the environmental difference affected pace far less than constitution did. A small residual remains. The share of rate variance that size does not explain is an estimate of individuality that the scaling-law framework does not represent (Kaplan et al., 2020). Infants and models therefore agree that learners differ in pace and that the difference is not a matter of starting point. They differ in how much of that pace can be attributed to a single measured cause, and this difference reflects the two study designs more than the learners themselves.

The Phases Are Not an Artifact of How Ability Is Scored

The measurement critique holds that apparent jumps come from scoring answers right or wrong (Schaeffer et al., 2023). Two checks address it. Refit on the logit of accuracy, the four interpretable tasks selected exactly the same transitions, with the same near-chance exceptions.

Refit on LAMBADA perplexity, the continuous measure, the phase structure did not weaken. It became more pronounced, with larger margins over every smooth alternative and nearunanimous agreement among the independent fits (Supplementary Tables S1 to S4). The one difference concerned timing. The perplexity transitions fell about a decade of log-steps earlier than the accuracy transitions (Figure 1). The reason is visible in the raw curves. Accuracy remains at zero until the model's best guess is correct often enough to register, so the threshold metric delays the appearance of a transition that is already underway in the underlying predictions. Threshold scoring therefore does not create the transition. It delays its detection. Note. The perplexity transitions precede the accuracy transitions by roughly one decade of log-steps.

Discussion

Interpreting the Findings

An analysis pipeline built for infants, applied to language models with only the time axis changed, paralleled the infant findings. Growth in these systems appears to be continuous, with no jumps between discrete states, yet its pace changes at a few identifiable points. It proceeds slowly while foundations form, rapidly during a period of expansion, and slowly again as a capability consolidates. Different capabilities keep different schedules, with knowledge reorganizing before reasoning. Furthermore, individual learners follow the same course at reliably different speeds. This is the resolution developmental science reached for infants. The productive question is not whether growth is smooth or stepwise, but when its pace changes and by how much, and the same resolution is available to the emergence debate.

This resolution speaks to both sides of that debate. The jumps are not illusions, since phase structure survives and becomes more pronounced on a continuous measure. Nor are the jumps discontinuities, since abilities do not appear instantaneously. What appears as sudden emergence on a capability test is the delayed expression of a gradual transition that began, on the underlying measure, well before the test could register it. An evaluation that relies on threshold scores alone will detect a capability's expansion phase only after it is already underway.

One finding had no infant counterpart. Rate differences in infants are substantial and unexplained. Rate differences in language models, by contrast, reduce almost entirely to a single measured cause, parameter count, with training data contributing far less. Human development takes place in a complex ecological system in which both biological and environmental factors play a role. Computational models, by contrast, develop with constitution measured exactly and environments held constant. The two types of learners therefore complement each other in design, and each answers a question the other cannot.

Learners with no body, no brain, no biological maturation, and no social partners nonetheless grow in phases. Shared trajectory shape, however, does not imply shared mechanism. Infants generate their own curriculum through action, and these models receive a fixed one. What the results show is that the growth of both is phase-structured, domain-timed, and individually variable in ways that one statistical framework captures.

Contributions

The paper makes three contributions. First, it gives the emergence debate a formal test with three criteria: whether a phase-structured model outperforms smooth alternatives, whether independent learners agree on where the phases fall, and whether the transitions survive changes of metric. The present data met all three criteria. Second, it provides a construct mapping from infant development to model training, so that participants, visits, domains, and constitution have counterparts on both sides. In turn, the same pipeline can be applied to both learners. Third, it reports a direct comparison of infant and language-model growth conducted with identical methods, including one result the infant design could not produce, the decomposition of developmental rate against an exactly measured constitutional variable.

Two implications follow for how models are evaluated. A training run may be better assessed as a trajectory than as an endpoint. A capability's phase at the end of training is itself diagnostic. A capability still accelerating when training stops, as one was in the present data, is a capability whose expansion the training budget cut short, and endpoint testing cannot detect this.

A capability also changes its growth rate as it enters the expansion phase, before the capability is fully present. Trajectory monitoring could therefore give advance warning that an ability, including an unwanted one, is approaching. For developmental science, the benefit flows in the opposite direction. Model suites offer dense, complete longitudinal data in a population where development can be rerun with the environment changed, so timing, cascade, and sensitiveperiod hypotheses that infant data can support only correlationally become directly testable.

Transitions here also partially align with a global change in training conditions, much as infant transitions align with re-gating events such as walking onset (Adolph & Tamis-LeMonda, 2014; Iverson, 2021). This alignment suggests a question the two fields can investigate together: how system-wide changes in the conditions of learning organize domain-specific reorganization.

Limitations and Future Directions

Each limitation of the present study points to a direction for future work. First, the sample is one model family with 16 members organized as eight near-duplicate pairs, so the recovered transition steps are properties of this training regime until replicated. The public release of the analysis pipeline makes replication immediately feasible for other openly released model suites.

Second, all five domains belong to a single modality, language, which is narrower than the infant battery's coverage of motor and perceptual domains. Expansion to other learning domains and types of learners is therefore the natural next step. Third, the released evaluations omitted the stretch of training between steps 3,000 and 13,000, which limits how precisely the second transition can be located. Evaluating the saved checkpoints within that gap would locate it more precisely. Finally, this study is observational. It describes the shape of development without intervening on it. Future studies could introduce small, well-timed training experiences and measure whether they redirect the trajectory, as an analogue of brief parent-guided motor practice (An & Libertus, 2025).

Conclusion

Whether the path from an untrained model to a trained one is smooth or phase-structured no longer needs to be judged by visual inspection. Applied to a population of 16 artificial learners, methods built for infants give a formal answer. Growth is phase-structured, by decisive margins, on every measure examined. Two very different kinds of learners grow in the same shape, and developmental science already possesses the tools to measure it.

change at specific points, called knots. Between knots the curve is smooth. At a knot, the growth rate is allowed to shift. The positions of the knots were not chosen by the researcher.
