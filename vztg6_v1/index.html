Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

What we found

We found 4 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Machiavellianism is linked to workplace competition, yet it remains unclear whether this association reflects personality-driven activity effects, environment-driven reactivity effects, or a combination of both. This study examined the reciprocal relationships between Machiavellianism and workplace competition across six years of early career development among 1,513 Belgian university graduates. Using both Cross-Lagged Panel Models and Random Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Models, we tested longitudinal activity and reactivity processes at the between-person and within-person levels, respectively. To this end, workplace competition was evaluated using both objective occupational characteristics and subjective perceptions. Regarding activity effects, results showed that higher levels of Machiavellianism at career start positively predicted increases in workplace competition (both objective and subjective), but only at the between-person level of analysis.

Additionally, results provided evidence for reactivity effects whereby higher workplace competition predicts increases in Machiavellianism, but only for subjective evaluations of competition and when analyzed at the between-person level. Implications for theory and practice are discussed.

Introduction

Originating in political philosophy, Machiavellianism describes a personality orientation shaped by beliefs about power, social systems, and interpersonal relations (Christie & Geis, 1970). Specifically, individuals high in Machiavellianism view social relationships as primarily competitive, which explains tendencies such as manipulativeness, cynicism toward others, and a strong focus on advancing one's own interests (Harms et al., 2024; Jones & Paulhus, 2014). In the work context, this orientation is associated with a range of detrimental outcomes, including workplace aggression (e.g., Linton & Power, 2013), bullying (e.g., Pilch & Turska, 2015), unethical conduct (e.g., Greenbaum et al., 2017) and more negative reactions to large-scale organizational change (e.g., Belschak et al., 2020).

Although the consequences of Machiavellianism have been well documented, understanding of the factors that shape Machiavellianism in organizational contexts remains limited. Most research conceptualizes Machiavellianism as a stable antecedent variable that predicts workplace behaviors and outcomes, while paying relatively little attention to the external factors that may shape the development, activation, or expression of Machiavellian tendencies over time. In contrast, contemporary personality research acknowledges that, alongside their relative stability, personality traits exhibit dynamic properties that allow them to adapt to environmental influences (Sosnowska et al., 2021; Tett et al., 2021; Wille et al., 2023), including features of the work environment (Woods et al., 2019). Some research suggests that work-related situational cues can influence how Machiavellian tendencies are expressed in workplace behavior (e.g., Castille et al., 2017; De Hoogh et al., 2021). Whether such work-related factors are also associated with changes in Machiavellianism over time remains unclear.

The early career stage following the transition from school to work represents a particularly formative period during which personality traits may adapt in response to new role demands and environmental pressures (McAdams & Olson, 2010; Woods et al., 2013; Woods et al., 2019). With respect to Machiavellianism, the present study argues that workplace competition constitutes a particularly relevant environmental factor, given the trait's emphasis on strategic self-interest and manipulative tactics (Jones, 2025). Specifically, workplace competition may function as a situational cue signaling that trait-relevant behaviors-such as prioritizing personal gain or exploiting others-are appropriate or rewarded (Tett & Burnett, 2003). However, little is known about how Machiavellianism and workplace competition are dynamically related over time, particularly during the early career period, when both individual characteristics and environmental contexts are relatively malleable.

To address this gap, the present study examines the reciprocal relationships between Machiavellianism and workplace competition across the first six years of early career development following graduation, using repeated measurements at two-year intervals. As an overarching theoretical framework, we draw on Person-Environment (P-E) fit theory (Kristof-Brown & Guay, 2011) which posits that the alignment between individual characteristics and environmental demands shapes work attitudes, behaviors, and outcomes (Van Vianen, 2018). When individuals experience a high degree of fit, positive outcomes such as greater satisfaction and well-being are likely to emerge, whereas misfit is associated with psychological strain (Edwards, 1996; Krick et al., 2016). Importantly, two complementary processes exist through which fit can be achieved or improved (Dawis & Lofquist, 1984; Edwards, 1996). First, activity refers to individuals' efforts to shape or select aspects of their work environment to better align with their needs and preferences. Second, reactivity refers to individuals adapting their own characteristics to better meet environmental demands. Building on this framework, the present study is the first to disentangle activity and reactivity processes linking Machiavellianism and workplace competition over the course of early career development.

In addition, the present study distinguishes between within-person and betweenperson effects to examine how activity and reactivity processes unfold over time (Hamaker et al., 2015). In this longitudinal design, with assessments every two years over a six-year period, between-person effects capture longer-term, developmental processes, reflecting how stable interindividual differences in one variable (e.g., workplace competition) predict enduring changes in the other (e.g., Machiavellianism). In contrast, within-person effects capture more dynamic processes, indicating how intraindividual changes in one variable, Machiavellianism and Workplace Competition relative to a person-specific baseline, predict subsequent changes in the other over time.

Therefore, disentangling within-person and between-person effects is essential for theory building, as it allows us to determine whether activity and reactivity processes operate through stable interindividual differences, dynamic intraindividual changes, or both (Hamaker, 2012).

Finally, the present study conceptualizes workplace competition using both subjective perceptions and objective occupational characteristics, as each captures a distinct aspect of the interplay between individuals and work environments over time. Subjective perceptions reflect individuals' experienced sense of competition (Fletcher et al., 2008), whereas objective characteristics capture structurally embedded competitive demands within occupations (Peterson et al., 2001). This distinction is critical for clarifying the mechanisms underlying both activity and reactivity processes. With respect to activity, objective indicators allow for the assessment of whether individuals high in Machiavellianism systematically select into occupations characterized by stronger competitive demands, reflecting preferences for particular types of environments. Subjective indicators, in contrast, capture how such individuals perceive and construe their work environment, potentially reflecting heightened sensitivity to competitive cues or the attribution of competitive meaning without corresponding changes in objective conditions. With respect to reactivity, objective competitive structures represent relatively stable environmental contingencies that, through sustained exposure, may contribute to enduring differences in personality development via reinforcement and internalization processes (Roberts et al., 2003). Subjective perceptions, by contrast, function as more proximal situational cues that directly activate trait-relevant psychological states, thereby eliciting Machiavellian tendencies in response to perceived competitive demands (Tett & Burnett, 2003).

Taken together, the present study aims to make three key contributions to the literature on Machiavellianism in the work context. First, it advances a more dynamic perspective on Machiavellianism by examining its developmental interplay with workplace characteristics. Specifically, we focus on longitudinal interactions with workplace competition, given the conceptual relevance of this environmental demand for Machiavellian tendencies (e.g., Woods et al., 2019). In doing so, the study is the first to investigate reciprocal relationships between Machiavellianism and workplace competition across a substantively meaningful time frame, namely the first six years of individuals' careers. Second, to deepen understanding of these reciprocal relationships, we distinguish between between-person and within-person processes. Both activity and reactivity mechanisms may operate at one or both levels, and disentangling these effects provides more precise insight into the longitudinal dynamics at play. Importantly, the chosen time frame aligns with the theoretical mechanisms under investigation (Ployhart & Vandenberg, 2010). Trait change at the between-person level is expected to unfold gradually and accumulate over extended periods, justifying the six-year longitudinal design. At the same time, although within-person dynamics in personality can unfold across a range of temporal intervals, two-year lags were selected to capture meaningful changes in workplace competition and their potential implications for Machiavellianism. Specifically, such intervals allow sufficient time for competitive norms to emerge, stabilize, or decline, and for individuals to perceive and internalize these dynamics.

Third, the present study distinguishes between objective and subjective indicators of workplace competition. By integrating both operationalizations, the study clarifies the extent to which person-environment dynamics linking Machiavellianism and workplace competition are driven by perceptual, as opposed to structural, mechanisms (or vice versa).

Adopting a more dynamic and developmental perspective on Machiavellianism in relation to workplace competition also yields important practical implications. Evidence for reactivity effects would challenge the assumption that Machiavellianism is fixed once individuals enter the labor market, instead suggesting that the trait remains malleable over the course of career development. Moreover, the present study identifies a situational factorworkplace competition-that may either dynamically activate Machiavellian tendencies (i.e., at the within-person level) or contribute to more enduring changes in the trait (i.e., at the between-person level) over time. To date, the predominant approach to managing dark personality traits in organizations has focused on employee selection, such as by screening for maladaptive characteristics during hiring (e.g., De Fruyt et al., 2009). Although valuable, this approach is limited in its ability to identify individuals who may develop higher levels of Machiavellianism over time in response to workplace risk factors (e.g., Heyde et al., 2024).

Understanding which aspects of the work environment can temporally elevate or more durably reinforce Machiavellianism opens the door for targeted job design interventions aimed at mitigating these risks (Wu & Wang, 2025). In this regard, distinguishing between objective occupational demands and subjective appraisals of the work environment is particularly important

Toward a Dynamic and Developmental Perspective on Machiavellianism

Machiavellianism, originating from Niccolò Machiavelli's political treatise The Prince, has been conceptualized in career and organizational literatures as one of the Dark Triad personality traits (Paulhus & Williams, 2002). It is defined as a strategic orientation toward social behavior characterized by the manipulation of others for personal gain, often at others' expense (Wilson et al., 1996). Individuals high in Machiavellianism tend to endorse manipulative tactics as effective, hold a cynical view of human nature, and prioritize expediency over moral principles (O'Boyle et al., 2012; Spain et al., 2014).

Following the traditional emphasis on trait stability in personality research (e.g., Roberts & DelVecchio, 2000), Machiavellianism has predominantly been conceptualized in the work context as a relatively fixed individual difference. However, contemporary personality psychology increasingly recognizes that traits, alongside their relative stability, also exhibit dynamic and developmental properties. Process-oriented frameworks such as Trait Activation Theory (Tett & Burnett, 2003), the TESSERA framework (Wrzus & Roberts, 2017), and the DATA model (Woods et al., 2019) illustrate that personality traits continue to develop in adulthood through mechanisms such as social learning, environmental shaping, and cumulative life experiences (Roberts et al., 2006). Empirical research further shows that personality traits and occupational experiences are reciprocally related, with individuals both shaping and being shaped by their work environments (e.g., Nieß & Zacher, 2015; Roberts et al., 2006; Wille et al., 2014). Importantly, personality change can occur across multiple temporal scales, ranging from short-term fluctuations to longer-term developmental trajectories unfolding over multiple years or even decades (Woods et al., 2013).

On the one hand, dynamic properties reflect relatively short-term, within-person fluctuations in trait expression that arise either from a trait's natural variability or in response to situational triggers. At the most granular level, such fluctuations have been observed within days, with momentary state expressions of the Big Five predicting work outcomes beyond the effects of stable trait levels (e.g., Abrahams et al., 2023; Debusscher et al., 2016 Debusscher et al., , 2017)). Extending this perspective to dark personality, recent studies demonstrate that Dark Triad traits also fluctuate on a daily basis in response to situational cues in and beyond the workplace (e.g., Heyde et al., 2023; Kuijpers et al.,2025; Nübold et al., 2022; Walczak & Rogoza, 2025). Although dynamics can be examined across a range of time scales (i.e., hours, days, months or years), they share a common feature: they capture true within-person variability, referring to deviations from an individual's baseline level of a given trait.

On the other hand, developmental properties reflect longer-term, cumulative changes in trait levels that unfold over extended periods. Longitudinal research shows that Big Five traits exhibit both relative stability and systematic mean-level change across adulthood (e.g., Bleidorn et al., 2022). A substantial body of work further indicates that workplace characteristics, experiences, and indicators of success contribute to individual trajectories of trait change (e.g., Nieß & Zacher, 2015; Wille et al., 2014). More recently, research has begun to document similar patterns of stability and change in dark personality traits across the lifespan (e.g., Orth et al., 2024; Zettler et al., 2021), although the role of work-related factors in shaping these trajectories remains underexplored. Focusing on narcissism, Wille et al. (2019) first showed that upward career mobility is associated with increases in this trait. Further, Heyde et al. (2024) found that engagement in communal work activities (e.g., helping colleagues) can buffer against such increases. In contrast, longitudinal research on Machiavellianism remains scarce, and knowledge about the work-related factors that may shape its developmental trajectory is largely absent (Götz et al., 2020).

The limited insight into the dynamic and developmental properties of Machiavellianism stands in contrast to seminal conceptualizations of the trait. Early socialcognitive conceptualizations (e.g., Christie & Geis, 1970) portray Machiavellianism as rooted in a cynical belief that others are fundamentally self-interested, reflecting learned expectations and social schemas. Because such schemas are shaped through experience, repeated confirmation or disconfirmation of these expectations in the workplace may lead to shifts in the underlying belief system. Similarly, within the Dark Triad framework, Machiavellianism is characterized by high environmental attunement-particularly compared to traits such as psychopathy-implying heightened sensitivity to situational cues and greater behavioral flexibility (Bereczkei et al., 2015; Bereczkei, 2018; Jones & Mueller, 2022).

Individuals high in Machiavellianism are thought to monitor environmental payoff structures and adjust their behavior accordingly. When such strategies are consistently rewarded or penalized, trait-relevant patterns may become reinforced or attenuated over time. Taken together, these theoretical perspectives point toward a more dynamic and developmentally sensitive view of Machiavellianism in the work context, which the present study seeks to advance.

Reciprocal Relationships between Machiavellianism and Workplace Competition

Workplace competition represents a contextual feature with complex implications for employee functioning (Swab & Johnson, 2019). Competitive work environments are characterized by negatively interdependent goals and relative performance evaluations, such that individuals' success often comes at the expense of others (Brown et al., 1998; Deutsch, 1949). Although such environments can stimulate performance and innovation, they also foster conditions that encourage strategic and self-interested behavior, as individuals navigate inherently zero-sum dynamics (Fletcher et al., 2008).

Importantly, workplace competition can be conceptualized at both the objective and subjective level. Objective competition reflects structural characteristics embedded in jobs, organizations or occupations, such as formal performance metrics, ranking systems, and reward structures that emphasize relative outcomes. These features exist independently of employees' interpretations of their work environment (DeVaro, 2006). In contrast, subjective competition refers to employees' perceptions of interpersonal rivalry and the extent to which they feel compelled to outperform colleagues to succeed. Psychological climate research conceptualized such perceptions as individually constructed interpretations of situational demands, rather than direct reflections of objective environmental conditions (James & James, 1989; Spurk et al., 2019).

Both individuals' perceptions of workplace competition and their exposure to objectively competitive environments are closely intertwined with personality characteristics.

For example, individuals low in agreeableness or high in dominance and hostility are more likely to construe their work environment as competitive, even under comparable objective conditions (Brown et al., 1998; Fletcher et al., 2008; Ryckman et al., 1996). Beyond such perceptual processes, personality traits are also systematically related to individuals' presence in objectively competitive environments. Vocational development research shows that individuals lower in agreeableness are more likely to enter enterprising occupations characterized by selling, persuading, and relative performance demands, whereas more agreeable individuals tend to select people-oriented occupations emphasizing cooperation (Brown & Hirschi, 2013; Wille & De Fruyt, 2014).

Given its emphasis on strategic self-interest, cynicism, and instrumental interpersonal tactics, Machiavellianism is particularly relevant for understanding how individuals navigate competitive work environments (e.g., Bereczkei, 2017). Empirical research indicates that Machiavellianism is positively associated with competitive orientations, including hypercompetitive motivation and rivalry (Houston et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2021).

Moreover, individuals high in Machiavellianism are more likely to perceive workplaces as competitive (Jonason et al., 2015), consistent with their cynical view of others and heightened sensitivity to relative advantage (Bereczkei, 2017). Importantly, a major limitation of this body of research is its predominant reliance on cross-sectional designs, which preclude conclusions about the direction of effects. Moreover, the limited longitudinal research on Machiavellianism and workplace competition (Spurk & Hirschi, 2018) does not distinguish between within-and between-person processes of reciprocity and considers only subjective indicators of workplace competition.

Method

Participants and Procedure

The data for this study were drawn from a six-year longitudinal project tracking the transition of Belgian university graduates into the labor market and their subsequent early career development. The baseline assessment (Time 1; T1) was organized in March 2014, approximately three months prior to degree completion. The initial sample comprised 1,513 final-year students from 11 different faculties at XXX (institution blinded for peer review) who indicated that they were willing to participate in this longitudinal project. Of these 1,513 students, 1,317 effectively completed the T1 survey (64% female; M_age = 22.84, SD = 1.44). Follow-up data were collected at 2-year intervals: in 2016 (T2; N = 709), 2018 (T3; N = 567), and 2020 (T4; N = 577). At each wave, all baseline participants were invited to complete the survey on a voluntary basis with informed consent. Participants were informed that all data would be treated confidentially and that contact information would only be used to organize biannual follow-up assessments. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Reference number blinded for peer review).

Workplace competition.

Given that participants had entered the workforce by T2, workplace competition was assessed at T2, T3, and T4. Subjective workplace competition was measured using a four-item scale from Fletcher et al. (2008). A Dutch version of the scale was obtained through back-translation. Items assess the extent to which employees perceive competition as normative and salient in their work environment (e.g., "Employees here often compete with each other to get ahead") and are rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree,7 = strongly agree). Cronbach's alphas ranged from .78 to .81 across waves. The scale demonstrated strict scalar invariance across waves, and latent factor scores were extracted for use in the structural models (see Table 2). Objective workplace competition was operationalized using the Level of Competition descriptor from the Work Context domain of the O*NET database (Peterson et al., 2001; see https://www.onetcenter.org). This descriptor reflects the structural requirements inherent in an occupational role, such as the necessity of competing with colleagues or external rivals.

Based on participants' self-reported job titles and a brief description of their primary work tasks, two trained research assistants independently assigned O*NET-SOC occupation codes for T2, T3 and T4 measurement points. The absolute agreement between the coders was 87.3% across the three waves. For the remaining cases where coders identified different occupations, discrepancies were reconciled by averaging the associated Level of Competition scores. Because these standardized descriptors reflect occupational-level characteristics rather than individual psychological interpretations, objective competition was treated as a manifest variable and required no longitudinal measurement invariance testing. Scores were standardized within each wave to facilitate longitudinal comparison.

Analytical Strategy

To evaluate the activity and reactivity hypotheses, we estimated both cross-lagged panel models (CLPM; Figure 1) and random intercept cross-lagged panel models (RI-CLPM; 2021), we implemented cross-wave equality constraints on all autoregressive and cross-lagged paths. This approach was deemed appropriate given the equally spaced time intervals and facilitated a more parsimonious and statistically powerful test of the reciprocal relationships. Separate models were specified for subjective and objective workplace competition. All models were estimated in Mplus 8.3 using maximum likelihood estimation with robust standard errors (MLR). Missing data were handled using full information maximum likelihood (FIML), and model fit was evaluated based on standard criteria (CFI/TLI ≥ .90, RMSEA ≤ .08).

Results

Preliminary Analyses

Table 1 presents descriptive statistics based on observed raw-scale scores and correlations based on latent factor scores. Test-retest correlations indicated moderate-to-high stability for Machiavellianism (r = .65 to .73), moderate stability for subjective workplace competition (r = .46 to .53), and high stability for objective workplace competition (r = .62 to .77). Machiavellianism at Wave 1 correlated positively with subsequent subjective (r = .17 to .25, p < .001) and objective (r = .11 to .19, p < .001) workplace competition. Objective and subjective competition showed small correlations (r = .16 to .24, p < .001), indicating limited overlap (Cohen, 1988) and supporting separate analyses.

Attrition analyses revealed no significant differences in earlier MACH, S_WPC, or O_WPC scores between later participants and nonparticipants (all ps ≥ .111, |ds| ≤ .12; see Appendix Table A1).

Model Fit

The CLPMs demonstrated acceptable fit for both subjective workplace competition (χ²(14) = 217.69, p < .001; CFI = .96, TLI = .95, RMSEA = .10, SRMR = .09) and objective workplace competition (χ²(14) = 214.36, p < .001; CFI = .96, TLI = .94, RMSEA = .10, SRMR = .08). The RI-CLPMs showed good fit for subjective competition (χ²(11) = 45.34, p < .001; CFI = .99, TLI = .99, RMSEA = .05, SRMR = .02) and objective competition (χ²(11) = 48.66, p < .001; CFI = .99, TLI = .99, RMSEA = .05, SRMR = .02). Parameter estimates for the CLPM and RI-CLPM are presented in Table 3 and Table 4, respectively. Table 5 provides a summary of the hypothesis-testing results across models.

Hypothesis Testing: Activity Effects

CLPMs indicated that between-person trait differences in Machiavellianism significantly predicted increases in subjective (β = .11, p < .001) and objective workplace competition (β = .08, p < .001). Hence, hypothesis 1a was confirmed.

In contrast, RI-CLPMs which tested for activity effects at the within-person level, revealed non-significant cross-lagged paths from Machiavellianism to subjective (β = .00, p = .974) and objective competition (β = .05, p = .370). Hence, hypothesis 1b was not supported.

Hypothesis Testing: Reactivity Effects

Hypothesis 2a proposed a positive effect from workplace competition on subsequent changes in Machiavellianism at the between-person level. In line with this expectation, the CLPM for subjective workplace competition showed a small but significant positive effect on subsequent Machiavellianism (β = .01, p = .017). In contrast, no significant reactivity effect was found for objective workplace competition at the between-person level (β = -.00, p = .622). Hence, hypothesis 2a was only partially supported.

No significant reactivity effects were identified in the RI-CLPMs (see table 5). Hence, hypothesis 2b was not supported.

Discussion

Machiavellianism reflects a strategic, goal-oriented interpersonal style focused on personal gain. Because social relationships are viewed in instrumental and competitive terms, individuals high in Machiavellianism tend to function well in competitive work environments (e.g., Czibor & Bereczkei, 2012). Building on this perspective and P-E fit theory, the present study examined reciprocal associations between Machiavellianism and workplace competition in a longitudinal panel of college alumni followed across their first six career years. Results supported bidirectional effects, although these differed by level of analysis (within-vs. between-person) and by whether workplace competition was operationalized objectively or subjectively.

Machiavellianism as a Driver of Environmental Selection or Modification

First, the results of this study provided clear evidence of activity effects whereby Machiavellianism positively predicted subsequent workplace competition at the betweenperson level. Specifically, higher trait levels of Machiavellianism predicted gradual increases in competition over the course of the study. These effects emerged for both objective and subjective measures of workplace competition, suggesting that individuals high in Machiavellianism progressively shape both their work environment and their perceptions thereof to become more competitive during the early stages of their careers.

The finding that Machiavellianism predicted both objective and subjective workplace competition suggests that the observed activity effect cannot be attributed solely to perceptual mechanisms, but rather operates through multiple pathways. At the individual level, those high in Machiavellianism may actively seek out and shape roles and responsibilities that align with their strategic and self-interested goals (Swab & Johnson, 2019). However, part of these activity effects can also be driven or strengthened by factors at the level of organizations, where Machiavellian characteristics such as strategic thinking may be valued and actively recruited because they align with structural demands for instance in competitive industries (Castille et al., 2017). Finally, these dynamics are likely reinforced by differential retention processes: once embedded in competitive environments, individuals high in Machiavellianism may experience greater fit and thus be more likely to stay, whereas those lower in Machiavellianism may perceive misfit and be more likely to exit (Schneider, 1987).

Findings also indicated that activity effects emerged only at the between-person level and not at the within-person level. Thus, no evidence was found for a dynamic adaptation process whereby intraindividual changes in Machiavellianism predict subsequent changes in workplace competition. Instead, the influence of Machiavellianism on competition appears to reflect a gradual and cumulative process, in which stable individual differences-already present at career entry-shape subsequent selection into and/or modification of work environments.

Workplace Competition Fosters Machiavellianism

The current study also provided evidence for reactivity effects, indicating that workplace competition can foster subsequent Machiavellianism. Notably, this effect only emerged at the between-person level, such that stable differences in competition predicted gradual increases in Machiavellianism over time. This finding suggests that competitive work environments may provide reward structures that encourage the expression of Machiavellian tendencies, which, through repeated trait activation (e.g., Wrzus & Roberts, 2017), eventually become reinforced and consolidated. This interpretation is consistent with the demands-affordances transactional (DATA) model (Woods et al., 2019), which posits that traits such as Machiavellianism can be activated and developed in response to stable situational demands.

From this perspective, striving for P-E fit represents a key mechanism underlying reactivity, as individuals adapt their behavior, skills and attitudes to meet environmental demands and facilitate effective functioning (Dawis & Lofquist, 1984; Rossetti et al., 2025).

Importantly, the results further indicated that the between-person effect of workplace competition on subsequent Machiavellianism was limited to subjective perceptions of the competitive work climate, whereas no such reactivity effects emerged for objective indicators of workplace competition. Previous research on how work affects personality development has produced mixed findings on the relative importance of objective and subjective work characteristics. Heyde et al. (2024), for instance, showed that only objective work activities focused on personal achievements fostered trait narcissism over time, whereas the diminishing effects of work activities centered around helping others were significant for both objective and subjective operationalizations. Accumulating research findings will provide further insight into the status of objective and subjective characteristics depending on the specific trait that is being studied and the aspect of work (e.g., work conditions, work activities, work outcomes, etc.) that is considered.

Finally, no reactivity effects were observed at the within-person level. Thus, intraindividual changes in workplace competition did not predict subsequent changes in Machiavellianism. To our knowledge, this study is the first to examine reciprocal relationships between personality and work characteristics using the combination of CLPM and RI-CLPM approaches, whereas prior research has typically relied on approaches that confound between-person and within-person effects (Orth et al., 2021). Taken together, these findings suggest that the influence of workplace competition on Machiavellianism reflects a relatively stable developmental process, rather than a dynamic transactional process in which changes in the work environment translate into corresponding trait-level adaptations.

Theoretical Contributions

Over the past decade, applied personality research has broadened the conceptualization of traits to better account for a range of dynamic and developmental personality processes in work and career contexts (e.g., Wille & De Fruyt, 2014; Woods et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2026). Yet, with few exceptions (e.g., Heyde et al., 2024; Spurk & Hirschi, 2018; Wille et al., 2019), this reconceptualization has been largely restricted to the so-called bright side of personality, such as the Big Five traits. By showing that individual experiences of workplace competition contribute longitudinally to differences in Machiavellianism levels, the current study provides initial evidence that such reconceptualization of (dark side) Machiavellianism is also warranted.

The reconceptualization of Machiavellianism as a dynamic and developmental trait in work and career contexts represents an integration between early social-cognitive views of Machiavellianism (Christie & Geis, 1970) and contemporary process models of personality development (e.g., Woods et al., 2019; Wrzus & Roberts, 2017). According to the socialcognitive view, Machiavellianism is construed on the basis of cynical beliefs about the intentions of others, which themselves are based on learned social schemas. Importantly, these schemas are shaped by experience, which means that if workplace environments repeatedly confirm or disconfirm cynical expectations, then the belief system itself could shift. It is here that process models of personality provide additional understanding of the mechanisms involved. Specifically, repeated experience of environmental demands, processed through cognitive and affective reactions resulting in behavior, and observation of behavioral consequences, leads to typical, characteristic response patterns (Woods et al., 2019). Through such processes, traits eventually become refined, strengthened or modified to better fit situational demands, providing a coherent theoretical foundation for the observed developmental (between-person) effect of workplace competition on Machiavellianism.

In addition to establishing such dynamic and developmental perspective on Machiavellianism, the present study also yielded first insights into some of the ways in which Machiavellianism and workplace competition reciprocally interact over time. Specifically, an important factor that needs to be taken into account when theorizing reciprocal relationships between Machiavellianism and work concerns the level at which these effects unfold. The current study suggests that the potential co-development of Machiavellianism and workplace competition resides primarily at the between-person level, whereby personal adaptation (in the case of reactivity effects) and environmental adjustment (in the case of activity effects) gradually accumulate over time, creating stable interindividual differences, rather than operating as highly dynamic and responsive intraindividual mechanisms.

Zooming in on reactivity, the distinction between within-person and between-person effects highlights a basic distinction between behavioral adjustment and trait change that can be integrated into theory of how Machiavellianism functions and unfolds in the work context.

Specifically, Machiavellianism combines a relatively stable cynical worldview about human nature with a flexible repertoire of instrumental tactics (Monaghan et al., 2020). This structure means that momentary changes in competitive pressures can largely be absorbed at the tactical level without altering the core disposition. For instance, when experienced competition increases, individuals may respond by deploying strategic tactics or becoming more selective in their cooperative interactions. These tactical adaptations satisfy immediate environmental demands while leaving the underlying worldview and cynical belief system largely intact. Trait change is likely only to emerge when such strategies consistently succeed or fail, and when the relevant disposition is repeatedly reinforced or challenged.

Interestingly, the reactivity effect of workplace competition on Machiavellianism was only significant for subjective perceptions of competition. P-E fit perspectives emphasize that environmental influences are psychologically mediated, such that objective characteristics become developmentally consequential as far as they are perceived as salient and personally relevant (Edwards et al., 2006). Moreover, work environments allow considerable room for construal, meaning that the same structural conditions may carry different meanings across individuals and over time (Deci & Ryan, 1987; Meyer et al., 2014).

Accordingly, subjectively experienced competition may more directly capture the functional input through which competitive contexts reinforce trait-congruent orientations. In contrast, objective competition reflects more distal structural features whose developmental influence depends on whether they are perceived and engaged with. In sum, our findings highlight that in theory on the impact of work on Machiavellianism, a distinction needs to be made between objective and subjective work characteristics, as both may capture distinct inputs into personality development.

Practical Implications

The current findings regarding activity and reactivity effects carry potentially important practical implications for individuals as well as organizations. From an individual perspective, our longitudinal study shows that Machiavellianism functions not only as an individual difference explaining work behavior (e.g., O'Boyle et al., 2012), but additionally as a career-shaping characteristic. That is, individuals high in Machiavellianism gradually select into, evoke, or create increasingly competitive work environments, indicating that career experiences are partly constructed through personality-driven environmental change.

Consequently, seemingly small dispositional differences at career entry can accumulate into substantially different career experiences over time. On the one hand, this process may be seen as positive from a P-E fit perspective, because individuals gravitate towards environments that align with their personality orientations. However, on the other hand it is relevant for individuals and their career coaches to bear in mind that competition at work also comes with potential trade-offs. Specifically, sustained exposure to workplace competition throughout one's career may increase the likelihood of adverse long-term health outcomes (Wittchen et al., 2013).

Moreover, the combination of activity and reactivity suggests that competitive environments can become self-reinforcing. Specifically, workers high in Machiavellianism may enter a developmental pathway in which they increasingly encounter competitive conditions. Such environments, in turn, may reward political skill, strategic behavior, and status striving, which could further strengthen competitive dynamics within teams and organizations. In terms of interventions, organizations can reduce harmful outcomes by setting clear behavioral standards and enforcing them consistently (Pilch & Turska, 2015; Shah et al., 2022). This includes defining unacceptable behaviors, increasing transparency in key decisions, and applying predictable consequences when standards are violated.

Finally, the current findings suggest that when assessing workplace climate or evaluating conditions associated with Machiavellian behavior, incorporating measures of perceived competition may provide more accurate insights than relying solely on structural indicators of competition (Houston et al., 2015). Indeed, the present results indicate that employees' subjective experience of competition may be at least as important as objective competitive conditions in shaping personality-environment dynamics. Consequently, interventions aimed at reducing dysfunctional competitive dynamics may need to address not only organizational structures, but also employees' perceptions and interpretations of those structures.

Limitations and Future Directions

This study was conducted in a cohort of Belgian college alumni who were followed across the first six years of their professional careers. Although this cohort design offers several advantages, such as ensuring that all participants face similar career challenges, it also comes with a number of limitations. First, all participants were highly educated, which could have affected the likelihood of activity effects. Specifically, one could argue that the careers of individuals with higher education offer greater flexibility to make choices and changes that accommodate personal preferences and strivings. Future research using a more educationally diverse sample could investigate whether activity processes are equally present in the careers of individuals with different educational backgrounds. Second, the present study focused on the initial career stage because (a) this period is often characterized by intense professional exploration, with starting professionals opening new doors in pursuit of P-E fit, and (b) personality traits are most malleable during this stage of adulthood (Bleidorn et al., 2022).

Future research is needed to examine whether activity and reactivity effects unfold similarly across different career and life stages. For instance, it could investigate the age at which Machiavellianism matures to the point where workplace factors no longer exert pervasive effects on the trait.

Relatedly, future research could adopt different timeframes to study reciprocal effects between Machiavellianism and work. A limitation of the current two-year intervals is that short-term within-person dynamics, occurring across months, weeks, or even days, cannot be captured. Personality dynamics research suggests that state-level variability differs substantially across traits and contexts (Nübold et al., 2022; Kuijpers et al., 2025). The absence of within-person effects in the current RI-CLPM analyses may indicate genuine trait stability, but it may also reflect that the measurement intervals were too long to capture shorter-term dynamics. Future research using intensive longitudinal designs, such as experience sampling or daily diary methods, could establish whether Machiavellianism shows meaningful state-level fluctuations and, if so, the extent to which workplace competition triggers such variation. Finally, future research could extend this dynamic and developmental perspective by examining other workplace characteristics and experiences. At the individual level, experiences of job insecurity, for instance, may lead people to prioritize self-preservation and engage in covert tactics to remain relevant. At the team or organizational level, toxic leadership may model Machiavellian behavior, which can spread through teams and create behavioral norms that employees internalize. In contrast, a strong ethical work climate may establish clear moral boundaries for appropriate behavior, thereby buffering the development of Machiavellianism among employees.

Conclusion

A defining characteristic of Machiavellianism is its sensitivity to environmental opportunities and constraints, accompanied by strategic behavioral adjustment. Therefore, understanding the role of this Dark Triad trait in work and career contexts requires a longitudinal perspective in which Machiavellianism both shapes and is shaped by the work environment. The present study provided evidence that workplace competition is one such environmental factor: individuals higher in Machiavellianism tend to seek out more competitive work environments, while experiencing workplace competition also contributes to the longitudinal development of this trait. Together, these findings underscore the dynamic interplay between personality and work environments and highlight the importance of adopting developmental and transactional perspectives in future research across different personality traits, workplace characteristics, career stages, and timescales.
