Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Standard models of bilingualism are primarily concerned with how words in multiple languages are stored and associated in the mind. However, there is a growing body of work in linguistic theory that language cannot be organized around words, given that meaning, syntax, and form do not always align at the word level. We propose the Multilingualism WithOut Words (M-WOW) model to realign models of bilingualism with current linguistic theory and account for a broader typology of languages. In contrast to other models, M-WOW allows more flexibility in how features are realized across languages and expands the possibilities for code-switching. Because the model does not rely on a word level, it accommodates a broader set of linguistic phenomena and provides new insights into bilingual language processing and the dynamic and variable processes of L2 acquisition and attrition.

[div-01]

Many prominent models of bilingualism are primarily concerned with how words in multiple languages are stored and associated in the mind. Because much of the literature on multilingualism focuses on English and other European languages, these models are built around the assumption that words are the "atoms" of language. This view entails that the lexicon is fully separate from syntax (in other words, that the dictionary of words we build for our language is distinct from the rules or principles which combine those words into a sentence), and that words are free-standing and independent, as Saussurean signs that bind forms and meanings together (de Saussure, 1916). Given the context of the field, these assumptions of atomicity and independence may seem like reasonable assumptions that should easily apply to all languages universally.

However, there is a growing body of work in linguistic theory that those assumptions are not correct (Bruening, 2018; Jackendoff, 2017; Haspelmath, 2017; Embick, 2015; Siddiqi, 2010; Starke, 2009; Harley, 2008; Halle & Marantz, 1993; among others); according to this research, based on data from a wide variety of languages in many language families, the two assumptions of atomicity and independence cannot hold for many linguistic phenomena, even in English.

These findings suggest that the principles of word formation are the same as the principles of phrase or sentence formation, so words cannot be atomic, and that the meanings and forms of words must be sensitive to their context, so words cannot be independent. Because the existing models of bilingualism primarily discuss word representations and often do not incorporate syntax, they struggle to account for a wide array of linguistic phenomena. For example, there are many languages where a single word can involve a great deal of morphological complexity, and can encode the meaning of what would be an entire sentence in English, as in this example from Inuktitut (Briggs et al., 2015): havauti -tuq -ti -taq -niaq -tara medicine -drink -cause -frequently -going.to -PTCP.1S/3S 'I'm going to give her medicine frequently'

You may be familiar with the myth that Eskimoan languages have "150 words for snow" (Kaplan, 2003; Pullum, 1989; Martin, 1986); in reality, Eskimoan languages like Inuktitut have about 6 morphemes for snow that can be combined with other morphemes to make an infinite number of words, in the same way that English speakers have several terms for snow and snow-like things that can be put into an infinite number of sentences. Representing a language like Inuktitut in a word-based model of language perpetuates something like this "150 words for snow" myth; it would mean that each word in Inuktitut has to be stored as a whole piece, despite the productivity of morphology in these kinds of languages, and would require an infinitely large vocabulary for every possible combination of morphemes in the language. Thus, when it comes to designing a model of bilingualism that can handle languages like Inuktitut, we need to rethink our central assumptions about human language. In doing so, we may stumble upon some new and different insights about languages that are already commonly studied.

To address these issues, we propose a new model of multilingualism that maintains the spirit of current models like Multilink (Dijkstra et al., 2019) and the Revised Hierarchical Model (RHM; Kroll & Stewart, 1994), but expands their capabilities for handling syntactic structure and morphological complexity, aligning it with current syntactic theory. In our proposed model, the "Multilingualism WithOut Words" (M-WOW) model, the language system is probabilistic and context-dependent, similar to Multilink; languages are not represented as a binary (e.g., language membership nodes in Multilink, separate lexicons in the RHM) but as a continuum, where representations and processes of the second language (L2) can piggyback on those of previously acquired languages. In addition, we propose a mechanism of "syntactic mitosis" to describe how languages in the bilingual mind become representationally distinct over time, extending and refining the RHM's developmental trajectory. These features allow us to account for languages that are underrepresented in bilingualism research, provide a more complete account for code-switching and language mixing, and also address other psycholinguistic findings in bilingualism.

This paper begins with a summary of the kinds of language phenomena that are challenging for existing models of bilingualism. Based on this data, supported by linguistic and psycholinguistic observations from the bilingualism literature, the second section outlines the desiderata of a non-lexicalist model of bilingualism. The third section is our proposal for the M-WOW model which fulfills those criteria, with an account of how sequential and simultaneous bilinguals would be instantiated in the model. We conclude with a series of testable predictions about bilingual language processing, language attrition, bilingual aphasia, and other areas of future research based on this proposed model.

1. Problems for existing models of bilingualism

One issue with standard models of bilingualism is the emphasis on words as the foundational representation of language. The notion of wordhood employed in these models involves two key assumptions: first, the assumption of atomicity, that these word representations are not syntactically complex, are distinct from the syntax, and do not participate directly in the syntax; and second, the assumption of independence, that a meaning or conceptual representation corresponds to one standalone segment of phonology or orthography in each language, regardless of the sentence context. These two assumptions present a number of challenges to current models of bilingualism, as described below. Because these models assume the atomicity and independence of words, they can only apply to only a limited set of linguistic phenomena and struggle to account for languages that are typologically different from those most commonly examined, such as English or Dutch. The evidence we discuss below will show that meaning, syntax, and form do not align at the word level. By decentering words as a primary unit of language, we move toward a view where human language processing involves more than just recognizing and combining words; it must involve complex, context-dependent transformations between independent representations of meaning, syntax, and form. For further discussion of these points and how they apply to models of language production, see Krauska & Lau (2023).

1.1 Atomicity

The atomicity of words is an assumption that emerged in the earliest theories of grammar, often implicitly. It has been formulated in a number of ways (Chomsky, 1970; Lapointe, 1980; Williams, 1981; among others), but it generally boils down to the idea that processes happening below the word level (the domain of morphology) are fundamentally different to processes happening above the word level (the domain of syntax). Some interaction exists between morphology and syntax, like when conjugating and inflecting verbs, but this is argued to be done in such a way that the rules and representations of morphology do not need to interact directly with the rules and representations of syntax.

Several pieces of evidence have been used to argue against the atomicity of words, showing that sub-word representations need to interact with the syntactic structure, and that syntactic relations also need to be able to be encoded below the word level:

1.1.1 Languages with rich morphology, like Inuktitut

In Inuktitut, many sentences can be composed of a single word with many highly productive morphemes, representing a high degree of complexity both semantically and syntactically. Importantly, many of the things that are considered syntactic relations in languages like English -such as phrasal argument structure -are encoded morphologically (examples from Cook & Johns, 2009; Briggs et al., 2015): 2) a. nivak -tuq shovel.debris -PTCP.3s 'She shovels debris, old snow [out of the door]' b. uqaalla-qattaq-tunga say -often -PTCP.1S 'I say that sometimes' c. havauti -tuq -ti -taq -niaq -tara medicine -drink -cause -frequently -going.to -PTCP.1S/3S

'I'm going to give her medicine more frequently'

To represent these Inuktitut sentences in a word-based model of bilingualism, one would be forced to assume that each of these is a single atom without internal complexity. This might not be problematic for example (2a), which is an inflected verb (something that any word-based model would have to account for anyway), but as the number of morphemes increases in (2b) and ( 2c), the word-based model becomes less and less tenable. One would have to assume that the entire sentence "I'm going to give her medicine more frequently" is stored in the lexicon as a whole and is associated with a single complex meaning, and furthermore that every minimally different sentence (e.g., "I'm going to give her vitamins more frequently") would also be stored as a whole with its own atomic meaning. As previously discussed, Inuktitut has highly productive morphology; the "150 words for snow" myth emerged from a misunderstanding of the morphosyntax of Eskimoan languages. Although it is generally known among language researchers that these myths are inaccurate, models that do not allow for morphological complexity inadvertently perpetuate that view.

The way that many models of bilingualism are formulated, there is no way to accommodate morphosyntactic complexity in the model. Even if the word representations were exchanged for morphemes, the models would not have a way to organize those morphemes syntactically or have them engage with the syntax because they often do not have a (morpho-)syntactic component. For German separable verbs like anfangen, which can appear as one piece or as two depending on their syntactic context, the model would not have a way to identify the correct form, unless one wants to argue that there is a sufficient conceptual difference between (3a) anfangen and (3b) fangen an to condition their use.

There is a growing body of research which has observed that morphosyntactic decomposition is an automatic process in language comprehension that is sensitive to syntactic structure and complexity (Wray et al., 2022; Stockall et al., 2019; among others), suggesting that morphological structure is not merely a description of the lexicon, but a property of the language faculty that is productive and engaged in on-line sentence comprehension and production.

Furthermore, contrary to previous work that claimed that code-switching only occurs at word boundaries or a particular phrase level (López et al., 2017; MacSwan, 2016; MacSwan & Colina, 2014; MacSwan, 2005; Belazi et al., 1994; Poplack 1981; among others), many speakers in multilingual communities have been observed to switch between languages at morpheme boundaries, not just at word boundaries (Alexiadou & Lohndal, 2018). For example, it has been observed that speakers in trilingual communities speaking Turkish, Anatolian Arabic, and Northern Kurdish regularly switch between all three languages within a single word (Akkus, 2025). These speakers are not memorizing whole words, but constructing them on-line from their individual morphemes. This phenomenon is not something that word-based models of bilingualism are equipped to capture.

1.2 Independence

The assumption that words are independent -that context is not required to determine a word's meaning or form -is a common notion in language sciences, descended from semiotics.

In semiotics, the signifier (the form) and the signified (the meaning) are directly linked, with a one-to-one correspondence (Shaumyan, 1987; de Saussure, 1916; among others). There are many examples from natural language which defy this description (homophones like herd and heard, homonyms like bank (edge of a river) and bank (financial institution), and close synonyms like couch and sofa), and there are number of examples which demonstrate that the context of a syntactic object is often required in order to determine its meaning or form.

There are many cases in natural language where the meaning of a word cannot be determined without referring to its syntactic context. Some obvious examples are idioms (e.g., "let the cat out of the bag", "kick the bucket", etc.), where the phrase as a whole has a different meaning from the combination of its parts. Although existing models often have mechanisms to handle this kind of polysemy, they are forced to treat them as exceptions from the norm, where one mechanism performs context-free combination or translation (e.g., "kill" in its default sense means "to cause a living thing to die") while another generates the non-literal meaning; or it must treat all of these verbs as semantically "bleached", despite these verbs not falling into the category of typical light verbs. However, if we assume that all linguistic meanings are calculated with reference to context, that would provide a more natural account which does not treat this kind of meaning as exceptional, while also allowing for novel meanings to arise more flexibly (e.g., in current slang, "John cooked with that Halloween costume" means that John had an amazing costume, while "John is cooked" means that he is or will be in trouble).

The issues with models of bilingualism representing solely words in the architecture of their model, and assuming both atomicity and independence of words, become particularly clear in cases where a word in one language translates to two separate elements in another language, or vice versa (English: "The rain doesn't stop" → German: "Der Regen hört nicht auf"). Even if multi-word phrases were incorporated into the model through a buffer, where a single concept could relate to multiple words or a single word could relate to multiple concepts, it would still be missing an important generalization about the syntactic properties and configuration of those elements; the relationship between the two elements in the German verb "aufhören" would be just one of association, lacking a particular structure or order.

To summarize, there are a number of linguistic phenomena that current models of bilingualism struggle to account for because the models assume that words are atomic (that words are the minimal unit of combination, not anything smaller or larger) and that they are independent (that their form and meaning are insensitive to context). The models are unable to accommodate morphological complexity or context-sensitive form or meaning, which are incredibly common throughout the world's languages, without non-parsimoniously positing additional machinery specifically to handle those cases. These assumptions -whether implicit or explicit -mean that the models are limited in the kinds of languages they can account for, but it also means that they are unable to handle many phenomena that appear even in English.

2 Toward a non-lexicalist model of bilingualism

As suggested by the evidence above, adopting a non-lexicalist approach in the study of bilingualism will allow us to accommodate a wider variety of languages and better incorporate findings from current linguistic theory. The following section outlines three desiderata of a non-lexicalist model of bilingualism, and how they follow from the linguistic observations in section 1, the basic principles of a non-lexicalist framework, and existing psycholinguistic evidence. Each feature is described in more detail below, along with the evidence supporting it.

In addition to these desiderata, the model should also be able to accommodate the full array of bilingual experiences -across different modalities, levels of proficiency, contexts of use, etc.and should be easily testable and falsifiable, as any successful model of bilingualism should do.

1. The language system should be represented as probabilistic and context-dependent.

2. Language acquisition should not have to progress uniformly across all language mechanisms.

3. The distinction between two languages in a bilingual mind/brain should be continuous and gradient, rather than binary.

1. The language system should be represented as probabilistic and context-dependent.

As discussed above, the atomicity and independence of words in models of bilingualism is problematic for a wide variety of linguistic phenomena. Rather than encoding linguistic properties or representations as atomic symbols that are deterministically activated in the mind, without any reference to context, the process of producing or comprehending language should be framed as mappings between independent representations of meaning, syntax, and form (inclusive of written and signed modalities), where the relationship between meaning and syntax does not have to be symmetrical to the relationship between syntax and form (Krauska, 2024; Krauska & Lau, 2023). In this view, morphosyntax is fully abstract, meaning that syntactic representations have no inherent meaning or form, and are purely units of structural composition (Harley, 2014) 1 . The choice between different elements at any level of representation is not deterministic, but probabilistic, in that multiple representations may be available for any given mapping, but other constraints and production processes may modulate which of those options is most likely. These representations can be framed either as discrete symbols or as distributed representations (e.g., tensor product representations; Smolensky, 1990).

To walk through an example of what this means, in the sentence "fireworks went off", there are three meaning components: fireworks, an event in the past, and explosion, organized as a structured message. The meaning of fireworks is associated with the syntactic object 2 of FIREWORK.PL, an event in the past is associated with the syntactic object +PAST, and explosion is associated with the syntactic object corresponding to the phrasal verb GO OFF. These syntactic objects are organized into a syntactic structure (which may not be isomorphic with the message structure). When those syntactic objects are then mapped to forms, FIREWORK.PL is associated with the form "fireworks", GO and +PAST together are mapped to the form "went", and OFF is associated with the form "off". Importantly, in this sentence, the phrasal verb "go off" is associated with one meaning (explosion) but two forms ("went" and "off"), where one of those forms also refers to another syntactic object (+PAST), so the two sets of mappings are not symmetrical.

Because this approach makes no distinction between morphology and syntax, it can accommodate cases of morphological complexity, like the examples above from Inuktitut in (2) and German in (3). Furthermore, because the mappings between representations are context-dependent and are not required to be symmetrical, it can accommodate cases of suppletion like in the examples from Hiaki in (4) and Anishnaabemowin in (5) as well as cases of meaning variation like in the examples from English in (6). In short, in order to account for the examples discussed above, language production and comprehension should not be framed around "activating" or "retrieving" words from memory, but instead around separate mapping processes that link independent representations of meaning, syntax, and form in a context-dependent manner.

2. Language acquisition should not have to progress uniformly across all language mechanisms.

Given that meaning, syntax, and form are independent representations in a non-lexicalist framework, a learner may not necessarily acquire a second language uniformly across their different language mechanisms. Over the course of language acquisition, some aspects of the L2 will be more easily observable to a learner than others; for example, phonological forms will be more obvious than abstract syntactic structures because there is more direct evidence for them. If we assume that learners are generally conservative about the kinds of representations they posit during learning (i.e., they only posit a new representation when they have sufficient evidence to do so, akin to the Input Hypothesis; Krashen, 1982 Krashen, , 1992; or the interpretability hypothesis; Tsimpli, 2003; Tsimpli & Dimitrakopoulou, 2007), and that developing and storing an additional representation is cognitively costly, this means that learners are going to be generally reluctant to posit representations for their L2 if they have one in their L1 that could serve that function, particularly for more abstract kinds of representations. As a consequence, L2 language acquisition will interface heavily with the syntax of the L1 and be parasitic on it until an independent L2 syntax can be developed.

Some cognates, therefore, may share a syntactic representation, depending on how similar the syntactic features are across the L1 and L2. For example, the L1 English "sofa" and L2 Spanish "sofá" may use the same syntactic representation 3 , piggybacking on the English syntactic representation and perhaps modifying it to add the Spanish grammatical gender feature (this change would be inconsequential for the English syntax, given that it would never refer to that feature). Early stage learners may also be able to rely on simple heuristics to capture surface patterns rather than developing a separate representation for their L2 (akin to the Shallow Surface Structure Hypothesis : Felser & Clahsen, 2009; Clahsen & Felser, 2006).

3. The distinction between two languages in a bilingual mind/brain should be continuous and gradient, rather than binary.

Given that language systems are probabilistic and context-dependent, and that there can be shared representations between languages, two languages operating within a single language mechanism should be represented as a gradient rather than a binary. That language mechanism may become specialized in part for functions required in either language, but there can also be a great deal of overlap between them, as illustrated in Figure 1. Acquiring a second language does not leave the processing of the first language entirely unchanged (Román & Gómez-Gómez, 2022; Titone et al., 2011; Van Assche et al., 2009). Having a gradient between languages rather than a strict binary necessitates a control or monitoring system to ensure that an utterance is in the intended language, based on social and contextual factors; language control is already something that has been argued to play a central role in bilingualism (Garbin et al., 2010; Abutalebi & Green, 2007). The division between languages (or lack thereof) in a bilingual mind has been discussed at great length in the bilingualism literature. Something that current models of bilingualism tend to agree on is that lexical processing is language non-selective. Multilink was designed with this principle in mind, where the inputs to the model are not immediately filtered or sorted based on language, and although the original version of the RHM did not address non-selectivity, a later update does incorporate this feature (Kroll, Van Hell, Tokowicz, & Green, 2010; Kroll & De Groot, 1997).

However, even if models allow for non-selectivity at the level of lexical perception and production, they often ultimately imply a distinction between the two languages at more abstract levels of representation, instantiated as separate lexicons or language membership nodes. When moving toward a non-lexicalist framework, we must evaluate whether the evidence for language non-selectivity also applies beyond the lexical level. Furthermore, it has been noted that in second language acquisition, acquisition is easiest for elements of the L2 that are more similar to elements in the L1, such as cognates (de Groot & van den Brink, 2010; Dijkstra & Van Hell, 2003; Costa et al., 2000; Dijkstra et al., 1999; among others), and more difficult for elements that are different from those in the L1, like morphemes that have different functions across the two languages (Cho & Slabakova, 2017; Cho & Slabakova, 2014). Based on this evidence, it must be assumed that the L1 is the foundation for second language acquisition, and that the L2 relies heavily on the abstract properties of the L1. Although each piece of evidence individually has been interpreted to be consistent with a binary distinction between languages, they collectively suggest that such a distinction should not exist; if there was truly a strict division between languages, we would not predict all of these independent phenomena to occur.

The availability and ease of code-switching and code-mixing also suggests that there should not be a strict division between languages. Code-switching and code-mixing can occur in a variety of places in a sentence and can be used very productively; as previously mentioned, it has been observed that people in trilingual communities can regularly and productively switch between all three languages within a single word (e.g., Akkuş, 2025)foot_1 . One consequence of a model architecture which strictly separates the two languages at any level of representation is that it implies that language switching should be a relatively rare phenomenon, or involve high cognitive demands. Historically, it has been regarded in the literature as a costly task, but more recent evidence has suggested that for people that regularly code-switch, it can be generally effortless (Adamou & Shen, 2019; Blanco-Elorrieta & Pylkkänen, 2018) and may have beneficial pragmatic or attentional trade-offs, supported by extra-linguistic processing such as executive control (e.g., attention; Salig et al., 2026 a and b). Because speakers are readily able to combine syntactic elements from two different languages, this suggests further that the boundary between languages cannot be so strict that it inhibits speakers from switching between languages.

Finally, the neurological evidence suggests that bilinguals' languages are also not strictly divided in the brain. fMRI studies generally show that each language engages overlapping parts of the language network (see Indefrey, 2006 et al., 2006; among others). In addition, although patterns of impairment in aphasia are very diverse (Kuzmina et al., 2019), bilinguals with aphasia have been observed to have the generally same kinds of deficits in both languages, independent of the severity of the aphasia (Chen et al., 2025; Kiran et al., 2013; Goral et al., 2006; Fabbro, 2001). This may not be incompatible with a strict division between languages (perhaps if each language uses separate neural circuits in the same brain area); however, once again, that view would also predict that the functional localization of each language could be non-overlapping, a prediction which is not well-supported by existing evidence.

3. The Multilingual WithOut Words (M-WOW) model of language production

According to the desiderata described above, we propose the following Multilingual

WithOut Words (M-WOW) model. It is a multilingual adaptation of the WithOut Words (WOW) model of language production (Krauska, 2024), outlined below.

3.1 The WithOut Words Model of Language Production

The WOW model of language production assumes independent representations of meaning, syntax, and form, where each type of representation has its own structure and rules or constraints. In this model, syntactic representations are fully abstract, hierarchical representations, meaning that they have no inherent meaning, form, or linear order. There are also no lexical representations, no stage corresponding to word retrieval, and thus no area of the brain devoted to lexical processing 6 . Instead, the neural architectures are performing transformations between complex data structures, in the same way that the visual cortex transforms raw visual signals into a 3-D rendering of the world with cohesive shapes and objects.

In this way, the model can be interpreted as a connectionist model, where each mechanism is framed as a kind of neural network where one representation is the input and the next representation is the output. This is consistent with a movement in neuroscience to frame neural activity not as the activation of representations or symbols, but as information transfer between neurons (Lau, 2025; Gershman, 2023; Mollon et al., 2022; Akhlaghpour, 2022; Abraham et al., 2019; Gallistel & Balsam, 2014). 6 In this model, the processes traditionally associated with lexical retrieval and processing are distributed into different mechanisms, and do not exclusively apply to content previously viewed as "lexical". This is not to say that studies that have identified a lexical area of the brain are entirely wrong; rather, the WOW model provides an alternative explanation, that the observed neural activity corresponds to processes which are not unique to lexical items. For example, Wilson et al. (2018) observed that the anterior dorsal bank of the superior temporal sulcus (STS) responded to both visual (written) and auditory (spoken) modalities, including backwards speech and scrambled writing. They concluded that this modality-independent response in the absence of linguistic content or structure should be attributed to the activation of lexical nodes. We argue that this effect should be attributed instead to modality-independent phonological processing, which is well-known to occur during reading as well as speech, and is not unique to lexical representations. The architecture of this model was motivated by findings from psycholinguistics, neurolinguistics, aphasiology, and theoretical morphosyntax; for more details about the motivation and support for this architecture, see Krauska (2024)foot_2 . To briefly summarize, as seen in Figure 2, the model consists of seven mechanisms, organized into a "representation" stream The second representation mechanism maps that structured message representation to a syntactic structure in a context-dependent manner, where the choice of a single syntactic object within that structure can depend on a single component of the message, a larger segment of the message, as well as the way that other components of the message are realized syntactically. All aspects of the message may be involved in this mapping process, including discourse (discourse can license syntactic elements or structural relationships that may not otherwise be available; see Nieuwland & Van Berkum (2006) for an illustration of this in comprehension). The syntactic structure is not required to be isomorphic with the structure of the message, and is subject to rules of well-formedness that are not semantically motivated and have no bearing on meaning. It is hierarchically organized and fully abstract, without form or linear order. The third representation mechanism of the model maps that syntactic structure to a series of (unordered) phonological forms, inclusive of speech and sign (and in some cases, orthographyfoot_3 ). These form representations may correspond to a single syntactic unit (such as monomorphemic words in English), or it may correspond to a larger segment of the syntactic structure, including pieces that do not compose a single constituent (e.g., English contractions like "can't").

In the linearization stream, the first mechanism generates "pre-syntactic prosody", the prosodic contour related to the message that is generated, which may be meaning-bearing (e.g., in English a rising tone can be used to mark questions, even if the syntax has not changed). The second linearization mechanism applies "post-syntactic prosody", which calculates the linear order for the phonological forms generated by the syntax-form mapping mechanism, and perform other transformations based on those phonological forms; these transformations and reorderings do not change the truth-conditional meaning of the utterance, and are triggered solely by phonological content. Word order is determined via interaction between the syntax-form mapping mechanism and the post-syntactic linearization mechanism; the syntax-form mapping mechanism makes several orders available based on the associated syntactic positions of those forms, while post-syntactic prosody modulates the most optimal order based on phonological and prosodic well-formedness (e.g., heavy-NP shift 9 ). The next mechanism, the local phonology mechanism, coordinates the information from the two streams to apply local phonological transformations and organize the phonological forms into a linear order, in anticipation of the final mechanism, articulation, which transforms that linearized phonological sequence into a sequence of motor plans coordinated in space and time to produce fluent speech or sign.

3.1.1 Language comprehension in the WOW model

In this model, language comprehension involves a similar set of mechanisms: mapping incoming stimulus (visual or auditory) to phonological forms, mapping form to syntactic structure, mapping syntactic structure to meaning, and interpreting pre-syntactic prosodic contours to reconstruct the speaker's intended message. Each mechanism involves a noisy channel inference process (Levy, 2008), where the inferences and predictions are being made at multiple levels at once. In contrast to other noisy channel models, which are usually word-based, the inference process here can operate over elements smaller and larger than the word level, and makes independent inferences and predictions at each level of representation. As listeners perceive incoming stimulus, they generate an inference about the phonological representations that the speaker intended. Based on that phonological input, listeners infer syntactic objects and syntactic structure, make predictions about upcoming phonological content, and make predictions about upcoming syntactic elements or structure. At the same time, listeners are using syntactic and phonological information to infer the semantic content of the utterance, integrating discourse and social information to make inferences about the speaker's intended message (including elements that are not explicit), and to make predictions about upcoming semantic and 9 Heavy-NP shift moves longer noun phrases (NPs) to the end of the sentence or phrase. For example, when given the choice between the prepositional dative (PD) and the double object construction (DOC), listeners often prefer the one with the heavier NP later in the sentence: "John gave [Mary] [the expensive gift from the department store]" (DOC) is preferred over "John gave [the expensive gift from the department store] to [Mary]" (PD), while "John gave [the gift] to [the beautiful woman who worked at the department store]" (PD) is preferred over "John gave [the beautiful woman who worked at the department store] [the gift]" (DOC). phonological content. All of these inference processes are interacting with one another, optimized over the course of language acquisition to make comprehension as fast, efficient, and as accurate as possible.

3.1.2 First language acquisition in WOW

Because representations of meaning, syntax, and form are distinct in the WOW model, each kind of representation can be acquired more independently than is often assumed in word-based models. In early stages of language acquisition, the learner develops distinctions between speech sounds without requiring representations at other levels. Infants have very limited access to linguistic meaning, but they can learn a great deal from the acoustic information present in their speech community. This learning process can be framed as "perceptual space learning" (Feldman et al., 2021), which only relies on perceptual (acoustic) information to group speech sounds according to their similarity on different dimensions. Phonological categories are developed later as a product of how the learner carved up or transformed that perceptual space.

This contrasts with the long-held view that learners rely on minimal pairs in order to develop phonological categories, where a distinction in meaning suggests that there must be a distinction between sounds.

Different kinds of input become available to the learner as observable evidence over the course of language acquisition. For example, once the learner has developed some phonological knowledge, they can begin to identify consistent sequences of sounds, such as phonological words (standalone sequences of speech sounds which may not correspond directly to the colloquial notion of words) or common functional elements. From there, they can begin to identify the abstract structure of their language, and connect those linguistic elements to meaning 10 . The mechanisms of language production and comprehension develop incrementally, as the quality and quantity of evidence for the representations and operations of the language develops over time. 10 There are theoretically two possibilities for the acquisition of syntax: either children start with the assumption that syntax is present, or they start with a semiotic assumption and eventually develop syntactic representations when that assumption is shown to be insufficient for the complexity of human language. In either way, this ability for children to posit syntactic structures and representations (provided by the neural architecture of the human brain) could be understood as a very weak version of Universal Grammar.

3.2 Syntactic Mitosis

Given that meaning, syntax, and form are independent representations, that acquisition can progress non-uniformly across language mechanisms, and that representations can be shared between languages, it is important to discuss the process by which the relationships between representations shift over the course of acquisition based on accumulated evidence. This process that occurs over the course of language acquisition and use is what we will be calling "syntactic mitosis"foot_4 , in which mappings between different representations develop and change over the course of second language acquisition. As shown in the "interphase" stage in Figure 3, a sequential bilingual begins with a mapping between syntax and form in their L1, where both the syntactic representation and the form representation can be simple (e.g., "cat", a monomorphemic noun) or complex (e.g., "turn on", a structured representation of a verb and its particle), and can be context-sensitive (e.g., the Anishnaabemowin suppletive verbs shown in (5); for simplicity, this is not illustrated in the diagram below). When they are first introduced to their L2, the learner creates an association between one form in their L1 and its counterpart form in the L2 (e.g., "hello" and "hola") (similar to claims made previously, e.g., Grainger et al., 2010; Kroll & Stewart, 1994; Kroll et al., 2010)). This is the "metaphase" of syntactic mitosis, where the L2 form representation is piggybacking on the L1 form and syntax during language production and comprehension. For the learner, this would be experienced as a need to translate that element into their L1 in their head before producing or comprehending it.

In the following "anaphase", the learner begins to directly associate the L2 form with the L1 syntactic representation. This would mean that producing or comprehending the L2 form becomes more automatic, but it may lack grammatical features of the L2 beyond surface-level patterns; learners may rely on memorization or heuristics to perform syntactic processes like agreement, without actually using syntactic features (e.g., "it's 'el gato', not 'la gato', because everything that ends in '-o' goes with 'el'"; "when you add tense to a German separable verb, you move the prefix to the end, so 'anmachen' becomes 'macht [the rest of the phrase] an'").

The association between forms in the earlier metaphase does not vanish, but is no longer the primary route for language processing.

The next phase is "telophase", where the L2 syntactic representation begins to be differentiated from the L1 syntactic representation, while still heavily reliant on it. Syntactic features that are present in the L2 but not in the L1 are added to the L1 syntactic representation to allow more fluent syntactic processes during production or comprehension. This can be thought of as a kind of tag, where learners mark a particular syntactic object with a syntactic feature or property that it did not previously have, without re-writing or removing the syntactic features of the L1. For example, L1 English speakers learning L2 Spanish would add grammatical gender to their syntactic representations to facilitate agreement between nouns, adjectives, and determiners, as in the phrase "el gato blanco". This step in the process also provides an opportunity for cross-linguistic influence, if by adjusting the L1 syntactic representation to generate a more useful L2 syntactic representation, the changes influence the processing of both languages.

Finally, in the final "cytokinesis" stage of syntactic mitosis, the L2 form becomes directly associated with its own L2 syntactic representation. Once the learner has established that the two syntactic objects have their own properties and engage in different kinds of syntactic processes or structures, a shared or piggy-backed representation is not sufficient, leading to the separation of the L1 and L2 syntactic representations, completing the process of syntactic mitosis. Those two representations retain an associative link, but this link would likely not be a critical route for language processing. This can explain phenomena such as the reduction of cognate facilitation effects in advanced bilinguals (e.g., Bultena et al., 2014; Pivneva et al., 2014), where the syntactic and form representations in each language are more separate and thus do not rely on representations in the other language in order to be used.

Not every item in the learner's vocabulary will necessarily be at the same point in this process, so there may be significant variability in how automatically learners can produce or comprehend them. As the learner progresses, however, each item in the vocabulary may be able to more quickly advance to later stages, based on the features and processes that they have acquired and internalized. For an L1 English speaker learning L2 Spanish, learning grammatical gender and advancing an item to telophase may make it easier to do the same for other items.

The process of syntactic mitosis bears some similarity to the developmental trajectory of the RHM, but differs in several key ways. In the RHM, beginner L2 learners link lexical representations in the L1 and L2, so translation from the L2 to the L1 can occur without reference to meaning; as the speaker becomes more fluent in the L2, they develop direct links between the L2 and meaning. This is very similar in shape to the first three stages of syntactic mitosis, essentially concluding in anaphase. In contrast, because M-WOW has no lexical representations, and meaning, syntax, and form are independent representations, this kind of process involves more elements that can diverge at different points, allowing for a greater amount of detail in which representations are shared between the L1 and the L2 and how they become representationally distinct. This model also allows for the process to continue after anaphase, where the L2 syntactic representation can separate from the L1 so that the two syntactic representations are not identical. Furthermore, one could imagine an additional process of syntactic mitosis is available for the relationship between syntax and meaning, allowing for the meaning representation of the L2 to drift from that of the L1 as required by the language.

Syntactic mitosis makes the mechanisms of the developmental trajectory more transparent, providing an avenue for cross-linguistic influence and interference, while also expanding the set of phenomena that can be accounted for.

3.3 Sequential bilinguals

In the M-WOW model, learning a second language means knowing two grammars for each mechanism and its interfaces (with the exception of message generation and articulation mechanisms; different languages may engage them differently, but not as a principle of the grammars). These two grammars exist in each mechanism as a gradient, rather than a strict binary between languages, and can operate over the same basic sets of representations as long as those representations do not contradict each other. Over the course of learning a second language sequentially, the different language mechanisms may not uniformly acquire a second grammar.

Each mechanism will acquire a second grammar based on the amount of evidence that is directly available to it; however, the kind of evidence that can be considered "directly observable" will change over the course of language acquisition, as other mechanisms develop the representations and transformations required for the L2. For example, the mechanisms involved in phonological representations and linear order will likely acquire a second grammar first, whereas the mechanisms that can only be updated via indirect evidence (e.g., mapping between form and syntactic representations, mapping between syntactic representations and meanings, etc.) will acquire a second grammar later (or perhaps never).

The second grammar does not develop in the same way that it would for a first language.

The primary difference is that the second grammar is developed in reference to the first grammar and heavily relies on it, not only in early stages of second language acquisition, but throughout the learner's entire experience with the second language. Firstly, a speaker's L1 permanently shapes their perceptual space. Returning to the perceptual space learning approach to phoneme acquisition in section 3.1.2, the transformations in acoustic space that occurred during infant language acquisition make it much harder to perceive and produce non-native speech sounds; the relevant dimension for that non-native contrast may not be heavily weighted, or it may have been transformed in such a way to obscure the contrast. Because the perceptual dimensions are more established in adulthood, recovering the acoustic information and reshaping that perceptual space is much more difficult. This aligns with many observations about the acquisition of non-native sound contrasts, such as Japanese-speaking adults' difficulty in perceiving and producing the /ɹ/ -/l/ distinction (Saito et al., 2022; Bradlow, 2008).

In addition, a speaker's L1 serves as the foundation for the acquisition of their L2 grammar.

The elements that will be the most difficult to acquire are the ones that are more computationally complex from the perspective of the learner, or ones that have no correlate in the L1, such as additional syntactic elements or features that are not present in their first language (e.g., English has grammatical case in very limited contexts, namely pronouns: I ~ me ~ mine / she ~ her ~ hers / etc.; for L1 English speakers learning Russian or Hungarian, the more complex case system applied to nouns, pronouns, adjectives, and determiners will be more difficult to learn than some other aspects of the language that are more similar to English; akin to Cho & Slabakova, 2017). This difficulty may be alleviated in cases where those syntactic features have a clear semantic interpretation (e.g., formal and informal second-person pronouns, like French tu and vous), even if the distinction is not present in the L1. Another locus of difficulty could arise with what is known as feature reassembly, where different sets of syntactic features combine into a single phonological word in the L1 and the L2; this would be particularly relevant for people acquiring an L2 where morphosyntax is realized synthetically rather than analytically (Lardiere, 2008; see also Lohndal & Putnam, 2024).

Over the course of language acquisition, learners can use a property they have already acquired to make observations about other kinds of representations. For example, young infants pay a lot of attention to phonetic information, showing sensitivity to phonetic contrasts that adult speakers of the language are not consciously aware of; however, once they learn phonological categories based on that phonetic input, they show much less sensitivity to phonetic information.

Similarly, when learning an L2 as an adult, the first hurdle for many will be learning how to recognize unfamiliar speech sounds and pronounce phonological words and segments, but once they learn them, they can process those phonological sequences more automatically and focus on the more abstract syntactic representation (the context and distribution of syntactic objects, sentence structure, etc.). Consequently, the type of evidence that could be considered "directly observable" to a learner changes over time, based on the kinds of representations that the learner begins to assume and take for granted.

Another aspect of second language acquisition beyond learning the representations that are engaged in the L2 involves learning the parsing and production strategies that are optimized for the L2. For example, there are many verbs in English and Germanic languages that are made of two parts, like particle verbs (e.g., turn on, grow up, run into, etc.) or the German separable verbs in (3) above; meanwhile, verbs in Romance languages are often one phonological word (e.g., the corresponding verbs in Italian: accendere, crescere, incontrarsi). An L1 Romance speaker learning English or German may need to adapt their parsing strategy when listening to German or English sentences, to avoid committing to a semantic interpretation of the main verb until they reach the end of the sentence or phrase that could have the remaining particle (if they were to listen to the sentence, "John turned the light by the window on" and commit to a literal interpretation of "turn", they would consistently need to do a -costly -semantic reanalysis to arrive at the correct interpretation). Learning these parsing and production strategies contributes greatly to fluent speech and comprehension.

Stage 1

When the learner has their first exposures to the L2, the elements that are most directly observable in the perceptual input are the phonological forms of the language. The learner is able to recognize forms and some basic ordering patterns, and potentially map them onto a form in their L1 (for example, they can learn that "hola" in L2 Spanish corresponds to "hello" in L1 English). In the diagram above, the Local Phonology mechanism at Stage 1 now has a gradient of blue to green to illustrate how a small part of the mechanism has become specialized for handling the phonological forms of the L2. The key learning strategy here is in relating representations at one level in one language to representations at the same level in another language, recognizing differences between those two representations, and using heuristics to accommodate those differences.

At this stage, the learner does not have sufficient evidence to map between representations at different levels, and the learner has not acquired any syntax of the L2. All or most vocabulary items are at "metaphase" in syntactic mitosis, so they have not performed any morphosyntactic analyses of the L2 forms. They may have learned some basic linearization patterns, like word or morpheme order (adjectives and nouns: the black cat vs. the cat black; subject, object, and verb: cats eat mice vs. cats mice eat), but this underlyingly piggybacks on the L1 syntax.

Employing the linearization patterns of the L2 may be consciously effortful if they are different from the linearization patterns of the L1 (see Krashen, 1982 for discussion on the distinction between learning and acquisition). Furthermore, the learner at Stage 1 relies heavily on their L1 phonological inventory, so they may not be sensitive to phonological contrasts in the L2 if they do not exist in the L1, but new phonemes may be acquired depending on how well those phonemes can be distinguished and categorized perceptually. Because the L2 is piggybacking on representations in the L1, cognates between the L1 and L2 are easier to learn because they do not require the learner to posit a separate representation for the L2, or it can piggyback on more features in the L1 representation than non-cognates would.

Speaking in the L2 at Stage 1 is effortful and conscious, so another important process in this stage is developing control mechanisms to selectively inhibit processing and production strategies used in the L1 while using the L2, but only if they are different from the ones used in the L2. Importantly, this cannot be characterized as a universal inhibition of the L1, because the learner at this stage still relies heavily on representations and algorithms of the L1 as a scaffold for their L2.

Cross-modal language learning, in contrast to unimodal language learning, involves developing an entirely new phonological inventory and involves a completely different set of articulatory gestures. For an L1 speaker of English, learning American Sign Language (ASL) as an L2 may initially present similarly to L1 acquisition, given that phonological contrasts will not transfer to the new modality, and the learner will have to discover which features are contrastive and develop categories based on those contrasts. The difficulty of this will depend on how perceptually available those contrasts are. After acquiring the new phonological system, however, second language acquisition progresses along the same course as unimodal language learners.

A learner may be very advanced at Stage 1 without progressing to subsequent stages. For example, the winner of the French Scrabble competition in 2015 was an English speaker from New Zealand who was able to memorize the French Scrabble dictionary and learn enough forms in French to win the competition, but did not speak or understand French and would likely have difficulty putting together a sentence (Chappell, 2015). His knowledge of the phonological forms of French with essentially no knowledge of the syntax or semantics of the language is another illustration of the independence of form, syntax, and meaning representations within the language network.

Stage 2

As the learner becomes more advanced, vocabulary items progress to "anaphase" or "telophase" in syntactic mitosis, so they begin to relate L2 forms directly to either the L1 syntactic representation, or eventually their own L2 syntactic representation. The key process in this stage is the transition from an Extended System Hypothesis, where elements of the L2 are stored in the same network as the L1 and piggyback on those representations and mappings, to a Subset Hypothesis, where there are dedicated representations for the L2 with links between elements that are strengthened through use and co-occurrence (de Bot, 1992; Paradis, 1985).

Within the Subset Hypothesis, representations within a single mechanism cause the formation of subsets which tend to consist of elements of the same language; for bilingual speakers that regularly code-switch, and where code-switching is a regular conversational strategy, the links between elements in different languages become stronger, causing the boundary between languages to be less strict than it would be otherwise. Many learners may stay at this stage for many years, or may not progress fully into Stage 3 over the course of their lifetime.

As an important note, learning in one mechanism does not have to be complete in order for learning in other mechanisms to begin (e.g., a learner does not need to have a native-like phonological inventory in their L2 to begin learning their L2 syntax). Learning can progress in any mechanism as soon as the learner has sufficient evidence (direct or indirect) for what that mechanism needs to do to support the L2.

Because the learners are developing independent syntactic representations for their L2, this means that they must also learn new mappings between syntax and form. In Figure 4, the Syntax-Form Mapping mechanism at Stage 2 now has a gradient of blue to green to illustrate how part of the mechanism has become specialized for handling the mappings between syntactic representations and phonological forms in the L2. Mappings that are more reliably 1:1 between meaning, syntax, and form will tend to be acquired first (e.g., simple nouns like "cat" in English, where the meaning, syntactic representation, and form does not vary in most contexts), while elements that are more variable or rely on a larger piece of syntax for either mapping process will be acquired later (e.g., verbs that are inflected for tense, aspect, person, or number rely on larger segments of syntax and agreement processes to determine their form). The learner also develops and strengthens the post-syntactic prosody and control mechanism to accommodate the linear patterns in the L2, and to selectively facilitate the target language when speaking. Parsing and production strategies also become more specialized for the needs of the L2 (e.g., when learning Japanese, the listener needs to learn to wait for the end of the sentence to hear the verb and hold off on some structural commitments until that point, while the producer needs to learn to wait until the end of the sentence to produce the verb). The strategies used at Stage 1 are still available to these learners, so even if the learner has not developed an independent representation for some elements in the L2, they can still rely on rote memorization and heuristics like in earlier stages.

In this stage, the learner will also need to develop stronger control mechanisms to modulate the use of each language. This will likely still be primarily uni-directional control, where the L2 would need to be facilitated while the L1 would not. Because processing strategies and algorithms may not yet be optimized for the L2, there may be competition for cognitive resources with pre-and post-syntactic prosody mechanisms, creating a task bottleneck for some of these processes that may contribute to difficulties acquiring native-like prosody (melody and rhythm).

A very advanced learner will have learned all of the surface patterns of their L2, with a large vocabulary and well-developed L2 syntax. However, they may still not achieve native-like performance in some areas, for several different reasons. Firstly, there may be some elements that the learner did not develop an independent representation for the L2, either because there was not sufficient evidence for it to undergo mitosis from the L1 representation, or because it is a feature that the learner has not attended to or has not been able to attend to, it is a low-frequency feature or phenomenon, or it has especially uncommon or unpredictable mappings between representations in the L2 (e.g., idioms, as unpredictable mappings between meaning and syntax).

Those kinds of elements will still be dependent on the properties of the representations in the L1, and they may over-or under-generalize syntactic features and relations from the L1 to fill in any gaps. Because those linguistic mechanisms are all changed at a deep level, these speakers will have different linguistic performance in implicit measures from monolinguals in either language.

3.4 Simultaneous bilinguals

In the M-WOW model, first language acquisition develops along a similar trajectory as second language acquisition, where the most directly observable evidence informs learning first, and the less directly observable evidence must be inferred to develop more abstract linguistic representations. Children who grow up learning two languages simultaneously in a more-or-less balanced way ultimately develop a language system more similar to that shown for Stage 3 above, where each mechanism is able to become specialized for both languages while also sharing some representations and algorithms between the two. In this way, the model can account for the systemic ways that even simultaneous bilinguals differ from monolinguals in implicit psycholinguistic measures and other subtle measures (e.g., VOT : Macleod & Stoel-Gammon, 2005; relative clause attachment preferences: Jegerski et al., 2016).

Children are able to distinguish two languages at an early age, even from birth, relying on rhythmic cues in addition to acoustic and phonological cues (Bosch & Sebastián-Gallés, 2003 , 2001; Nazzi et al., 1998; among others). Thus, the key challenge for a child growing up in a bilingual environment is not determining that the languages are different, but determining how much information about their two languages can be shared and how much needs to be distinct, given that they have limited information about either language.

In the same way as adult sequential bilinguals, there are two cognitive pressures, one of economy, and one of accuracy and efficiency. Based on this, there are two possible hypotheses about the routes children take in bilingual language acquisition: either they start with the assumption that the two languages are fully distinct, but may have converging representations in some instances, thereby allowing them to maximize accuracy in each language (the Separate Development Hypothesis; De Houwer, 1990 Houwer, , 2009)); or they start with the assumption that, despite any surface-level differences between the two languages, the languages have sufficiently similar functions underlyingly that allow the learner to share representations and information across them (e.g., if they learn that one of their languages has grammatical categories like nouns and verbs, they generalize those grammatical categories to the other language, akin to the Ivy Hypothesis, Bernardini & Schlyter, 2004), and only develop separate representations when they have sufficient evidence to do so, maximizing cognitive economy (akin to the Interdependent Development Hypothesis; Silva-Corvalán & Montanari, 2008; Yip & Matthews, 2007; Döpke, 2000; Hulk & Müller, 2000; Paradis & Genesee, 1996).

Given the available evidence, it seems unlikely that infants treat the two languages as two completely separate phenomena, even children growing up as cross-modal bilinguals (e.g.,

children of deaf adults (CODAs)).

There is evidence that infants treat sign language as a language and not gesture, understanding early that language serves a particular communicative intent that is different from non-linguistic sounds or gestures (Novack et al., 2021). The primary difference between simultaneous and sequential bilinguals is that neither language can serve as the foundation for the acquisition of the other in simultaneous bilinguals, and thus the children may come to different conclusions about which information or representations can be shared across languages compared to sequential L2 learners.

3.5 Multilinguals and language consolidation

For each additional language learned, the number of distinct representations required (new forms, new syntactic objects, etc.) will likely reduce successively, assuming there is some redundancy between languages or features and representations that can piggyback on prior acquired languages (e.g., a speaker might only need one set of syntactic objects for tense, if we assume that the syntactic features +PAST or +FUTURE could be shared across languages, and would have a similar relationship to meaning even if their phonological realization is different across languages). Third language (L3) acquisition gives more possibilities for how representations are shared, depending on language typology (Puig-Mayenco et al., 2022) and similarity (Westergaard et al., 2017). Because both the L1 and L2 are available, the L3 could piggyback on representations from either of them, or for representations that are shared between the L1 and L2, it could also piggyback on those shared representations (Slabakova, 2017). The process of syntactic mitosis would become more complex in a variety of ways; for example, a given syntactic representation that is shared across all three languages could undergo mitosis between the L1 and L2, leaving the learner with a choice (albeit largely unconscious) about which representation the L3 should piggyback on.

As a consequence, the storage requirements decrease for each successive language, both due to the consolidation of representations, but also due to the consolidation of processing strategies and mapping algorithms between meaning, syntax, and form. Furthermore, control mechanisms developed in the course of second language acquisition can similarly apply to other languages, so no significant additional development in control networks would likely be required when learning an L3.

The neural evidence seems to support this view, where all languages use largely overlapping brain areas, but where the degree that a brain changes after learning an additional language decreases for each successive language learned; so learning an L3 involves less change than learning an L2, and learning an L4 involves less change than learning an L3, and so on (de & Jaensch, 2015; Goral, 2012; Osterhout et al., 2008; Vingerhoets, 2003) foot_5 . If we assume that these neural changes like increased cortical thickness and gray matter volume reflect the acquisition of new representations and algorithms/processes, then we must conclude that learning an L3 or L4 does not require the acquisition of as many representations or algorithms as would be required to learn an L2, or simply that the brain has optimized or consolidated the language production and comprehension mechanisms to require less additional neural substrate to support it. In models like the RHM or Multilink, where representations are not shared across languages, each additional language learned would require greater and greater storage requirements, given that they would need to store a new lexicon as well as links between items in the new lexicon and their correlates in the previous one(s). Assuming that storage requirements correlate with neuroanatomical measures, this would mean that this indication from the RHM and Multilink may not be supported by the data.

4. Testing the model and further research

This model presents a number of unique testable and falsifiable predictions. In general, adopting a language gradient rather than a binary categorization of languages in the bilingual mind generates different predictions about how bilinguals engage each language, given that all their languages are processed using the same networks and with overlapping sets of representations. By removing language membership nodes from the system, we expect that bilinguals engage metalinguistic awareness and social cues to choose the appropriate linguistic elements (in the same way that a monolingual speaker might switch between registers based on the situation), and then rely on language control to continue to facilitate the appropriate linguistic elements. The elements in one language will be more closely associated with each other than with the elements of another language, allowing the network to distinguish between the two languages without necessarily isolating them from each other. Because there is no strict divide between languages or binary language membership nodes, and the syntactic representations of each language are shared and mutually compatible, code-switching and code-mixing are made much easier; any cognitive costs associated with code-switching would likely be associated with the demands of an unfamiliar task, either because of the unnaturalness of an experimental design, or due to a speaker's limited use of code-switching.

The following sections discuss several groups of predictions generated by the model, including the effects of syntactic mitosis, a potential role of syntactic mitosis in language attrition, possible mechanisms for cross-linguistic interference, and a new perspective bilingual aphasia. There are many other predictions within these topics that merit further exploration, and additional predictions in other areas that may be generated. Other predictions may also emerge as research following a non-lexicalist approach continues.

4.1 The effects of syntactic mitosis

The process of syntactic mitosis generates testable predictions about how linguistic representations change with more language exposure, suggesting that changes should be identifiable in implicit measures like priming and production speed. For example, items that reach anaphase may exhibit a smaller phonological priming effect with cross-language primes compared to items at an earlier stage in mitosis, because the learner would have developed a direct link between syntax and form, and rely less on the form-form association from metaphase.

Once an item reaches telophase, its syntactic processing may exhibit faster production speeds compared to items at an earlier stage, or shorter gaze duration in eye-tracking, because the specific syntactic features required for that item are more readily accessible and involve less cognitive effort. Once an item reaches cytokinesis, phonological and syntactic priming effects may diminish relative to earlier stages because the two sets of representations are more separated, but still have some association through meaning and remaining form-form and syntax-syntax links. These different effects may also be observed in electroencephalography (EEG) and event related potentials (ERPs), where the timing of observed differences may index separation at the phonological, syntactic, or semantic levels, based on established time windows for phonological processing, lexical/syntactic processing, or semantic processing in the EEG literature.

In addition, because syntactic mitosis relies on multiple linguistic cues, this also generates predictions about the kinds of elements that advance more quickly through the process.

Those that have multiple interacting and conflicting cues may have more directly observable evidence for an element to reach later stages of mitosis. For example, in this model, the cognate effect can be seen as an effect of multiple shared or overlapping representations, facilitating the use of those representations in multiple language tasks. Conversely, as the degree of phonological and orthographic overlap decreases, the cognate benefit has been observed to diminish. In a syntactic mitosis view, the mismatch of multiple signals provides the learner with additional evidence that two representations should be separate, moving those representations to the next stage of syntactic mitosis, thereby reducing the cognate benefit, as shown by Comesaña et al. (2015), Rubina et al. (2026), and others. The more overlap there is between cognates, the less evidence there is to advance them to a later stage of mitosis.

There is also a question about how well syntactic mitosis can generalize across similar items; for example, an L1 English speaker learning Spanish may develop a separate representation for some nouns that includes grammatical gender, but in this view of syntactic mitosis, an advancement in one representation may not necessarily apply to others. This suggests that learners may perform inconsistently on the same variable before it is strongly established in the language network. The progress of this generalization process may be identifiable in longitudinal studies of L2 learners, using the same methods and analyses mentioned above, where behavior or responses for items in a similar category (e.g., grammatically gendered nouns) may diverge as some of those items reach telophase or cytokinesis, and then converge later as that syntactic feature generalizes across the entire group of items. This may be seen particularly in language acquisition beyond the L2 (i.e., L3A or Ln), which should show mediation from proficiency of languages with a relevant feature (rather than holistic proficiency). In fact, research on L2 proficiency has shown that L3Aers perform better on features that are shared with previously learned languages and for which they have stronger skills (Jaensch, 2009), but this performance is variable (Tasseva-Kurktchieva & Fahey, 2025).

4.2 Cross-linguistic interference / influence

Similarly, cross-linguistic interference/influence (CLI) can also be understood as a process of shifting representations. The process of CLI could arise through several mechanisms.

Firstly, the L2 input may update some of the learner's linguistic statistics, where one type of structure or construction becomes more or less frequent than it was before due to that input. For example, languages which feature relative clause attachment ambiguity often have a preference for one of the two interpretations (high or low attachment), where one interpretation appears more frequently than the other in natural language (modulated by a number of factors, such as the type of head noun), and the preferences and proportions of each interpretation can vary widely across languages. High and low relative clause attachment involves the same structure, but with different relationships with meaning. When learning an L2 with a different attachment preference, the probabilistic mapping process between syntax and meaning may be sensitive to the change in statistics presented by the new language, resulting in an overall shift in attachment preferences purely as a fact of statistics. The same effect could emerge in any linguistic phenomenon that can be influenced by statistics.

Another mechanism of CLI in M-WOW is a "tug-of-war" between representations during telophase in syntactic mitosis. Elements that share representations at anaphase or telophase may lack sufficient evidence to suggest that they should be fully separate representations, and so all new evidence about that element would apply to the single shared representation. The tug-of-war occurs when new evidence from the L2 leads the learner to shift that representation in one direction, while continuing exposure to the L1 provides evidence not to. Until the element advances to cytokinesis, the bi-directional pressures will continue. If it does move into the final stage of mitosis, the original representation may not immediately return to its original state, but remain there until enough evidence accumulates to shift it back.

The degree of conflict between representations in the L1 and L2 would also likely modulate CLI. In attrition, conflicting representations should be more vulnerable because continued exposure to the attriting language is limited, meaning that new evidence does not come in to reinforce that representation. In interference, conflicting representations may be less vulnerable depending on how crucial those representations are to the learner's communicative goals (comprehensibility, clarity, etc.) in each language. If that representation is has a large role in comprehensibility (e.g., word order in English, where an ungrammatical word order would significantly impact comprehension), both of the conflicting representations would be preserved; features with a smaller role (e.g., grammatical gender in Spanish, where the intended meaning of an utterance is often recoverable even with incorrect gender markers or agreement). When the elements that have a large role in comprehensibility in the L1 and L2 are in conflict, and both representations are reinforced through continued use, the learner would be more likely to preserve both, while those that have a smaller role in comprehensibility may be less likely to be preserved (e.g., conflicting word orders for an English speaker may be less vulnerable to CLI than conflicting grammatical gender for a Spanish speaker).

4.3 Language attrition

If a speaker goes long enough without speaking a language, that language can be gradually forgotten. Similarly to language acquisition, attrition in the M-WOW model may not be uniform across language mechanisms; the language can attrite either in its entirety, or just specific features, representations, mappings, or processing strategies of the language. Because the model assumes that each type of representation is independent, meaning, syntax, and form should be able to attrite dissociably; for example, the phonological realization of case or grammatical gender may be preserved even if the corresponding syntactic representations are not, or vice versa. The elements or processes that are most likely to attrite are largely dependent on those that are used in the dominant language, where those that are not used in the dominant language or that conflict with those in the dominant language will attrite first. For example, the loss or reduction of case morphology has been observed for speakers of several languages in environments where the dominant language had comparatively reduced case morphology (e.g., Finnish-English, Glodstaf et al., 2025; German-English, Altenberg, 1995; Swedish-English and Swedish-French; Håkansson, 1995).

Beyond just forgetting, an alternative avenue for language attrition made available in the M-WOW model is that the process of syntactic mitosis -normally used to acquire new representations and shift existing ones -is applied in such a way that one of the languages is not preserved (akin to the Attrition via Acquisition (AvA) model; Hicks & Dominguez, 2020). In second language acquisition, elements begin in metaphase, where the L2 form is associated with the L1 form directly, and they advance to anaphase, where the two forms are both associated with the L1 syntax. After anaphase, representations may advance to telophase, where the syntactic representation for the L2 begins to shift and separate from the L1 syntax. In the case of attrition, however, this could be a point of vulnerability, where instead of splitting the two syntactic representations, the single syntactic representation just shifts toward what is needed for the L2, shown in Figure 5 as "shifting". In this situation, there is not enough sustaining evidence for the learner to justify the existence of two separate representations, so they make small, incremental changes in the syntactic representation until it eventually adds up to being catastrophic for the attrited language. Elements that were acquired later may attrite first, assuming that the speaker may not have made as strong a commitment to those elements compared to other aspects of the language, making them more vulnerable to shifting or fading.

Thus, the speaker may fail to preserve specific grammatical features required for the attrited language, in a similar way to how in L2 acquisition learners may fail to acquire those same features.

For elements that make it to the final stage of syntactic mitosis, where the L2 syntax has fully separated from the L1 syntax, the mechanism of attrition would likely be different. One mechanism is forgetting, where a representation is dropped from memory (akin to Ecke, 2004; Ammerlaan, 1996). The syntax and form representations themselves may decay in memory due to lack of use, or their association with meaning and the representations of the dominant language become weakened, making them harder to retrieve but ultimately preserving their features. The forgetting process or weakening of associations is shown in Figure 5 as "fading".

Another mechanism could be regression, where the syntactic representations merge together again and return to anaphase, but since the learner had at one point sufficient evidence to suggest that there should be separate syntactic representations for the L1 and L2, it seems unlikely that they would merge those representations back together. Thus, the stage that each element reaches in mitosis may be a significant factor in language attrition, in that it influences which elements or features are more vulnerable to decay.

This process would occur at the level of individual elements, rather than the language as a whole, but grammatical features like gender or case could be more broadly affected. As shown in Figure 5, elements that are in anaphase or telophase stages of syntactic mitosis are more vulnerable to shifting, whereas elements that are at the cytokinesis stage (or have not undergone mitosis in the first place) would be more vulnerable to fading. Consequently, representations that are cognates, involve straightforward translations, or involve similar kinds of grammatical features may be more likely to stay at anaphase or telophase, so those representations would be expected to shift to be more similar to those of the dominant language, but would not be lost altogether (e.g., speakers of Greek that live in a German-dominant community would preserve grammatical gender features because both languages have grammatical gender, but they would come to rely more heavily on the German gender features over time). Meanwhile, representations or features that are very dissimilar from their parallel in the dominant L2 -or that do not have a parallel in the L2 -would likely have reached cytokinesis or not have gone through mitosis at all, and thus would be expected to fade due to underuse (e.g., speakers of Spanish in an English-dominant community would not be expected to preserve grammatical gender features). If we assume that fading is a much slower process than shifting, this would explain why beginning level learners may show little recall of that language later in life because those representations were shifted, while disused intermediate or more advanced languages can show retention even after years of disuse because those representations were only subject to a slow fading process (Bahrick, 1984).

According to the critical threshold hypothesis of language attrition (Neisser, 1984), the elements of a language that are learned best are the last to attrite, whereas more complex grammatical processes (e.g., case marking and agreement in languages like Estonian or Hungarian, which involves adding morphemes and/or linear reordering based on non-adjacent morphosyntactic relationships, as well as local agreement processes) are harder to learn and thus are the first to be lost. In a non-lexicalist framework, grammatical elements or processes that are more complex can be thought of as harder to acquire because they involve more steps to generate them or transform them appropriately. Conversely, these grammatical elements may also be more vulnerable to attrition because any step could individually be affected by the processes of shifting or fading, leading to catastrophic results for the whole process.

The effects of shifting and fading may be identifiable in implicit measures such as priming effects or ERP responses. For example, the process of shifting causes some representations to become more like that of the L2 than the L1, and thus those representations may be primed by different features, and may change which language is more dominant in those priming effects (e.g., the gender priming effect observed in Salamoura & Williams (2007) would shift to preference the gender feature of the L2 rather than the L1 over the course of attrition).

Fading may result in attenuated ERP responses or priming effects in the L1 compared to bilinguals that are not experiencing L1 attrition, similar to the decay of priming effects observed in monolingual priming studies (e.g., Hartsuiker et al., 2008). These predicted effects may be more observable in longitudinal studies than cross-sectional studies, as individual variation is assumed; a between-subject comparison may not provide clear evidence for a linear improvement or development in structure acquisition or processing, whereas it would be identifiable in a within-subject design.

This model of attrition that M-WOW makes available can account for some data from heritage speakers. For example, heritage Spanish speakers that have a dominant language of English often have difficulties with grammatical gender. If the syntactic representations for Spanish were shifted toward English, rather than split, they would ultimately lose the grammatical gender feature on nouns, perhaps with some variability across items. They also tend to struggle with the subjunctive in Spanish (Giancaspro, 2019), a feature that also appears in English, but it is not an inflectional form as it is in Spanish and has a somewhat different distribution (Waller, 2017). Because of the misalignment between the subjunctive in English and Spanish, bilinguals could not rely on the English subjunctive as a foundation for the Spanish subjunctive, and would likely need to develop separate representations; if heritage speakers' representation of the subjunctive has shifted toward English, or their representation of the Spanish subjunctive has faded, they will struggle to produce the subjunctive in Spanish like Spanish-dominant speakers. As another example, heritage Korean speakers with English as their dominant language use bare noun constructions less than Korean-dominant speakers, preferring the more English-like demonstrative (Seo & Kuza, 2024). However, as the Korean proficiency of the heritage speakers improves, their performance becomes more like Korean-dominant speakers, suggesting that the processes of shifting and fading are not irreversible, and that those states can become the starting point for a new process of syntactic mitosis.

Our model of attrition may also provide insights into language contact and change beyond the level of the individual. In one case study on the endangered languages of the Ryukyuan islands in Japan, it has been observed that younger generations of speakers do not use politeness markers, even though the dominant language of Japanese also has grammatical politeness markers (van der Lubbe et al., 2021). Although this initially would seem to be in conflict with our account of language attrition -both languages have grammatical politeness, so why should the non-dominant language lose its politeness markers? -it can be understood instead as the consequence of shifting rather than fading. Because Japanese provides a more dominant set of politeness markers, the younger speakers may lack sufficient input to progress through the stages of syntactic mitosis with those of their heritage language, and thus do not acquire them. The context of use also plays a role in this process, where Japanese is the language primarily used in formal contexts for those speakers, while their heritage language may be used in typically less formal contexts. Thus, the consequence of this process is the shifting of politeness markers at a community level to be more Japanese-like.

The possibility of "shifting" and "fading" as possible side effects in the syntactic mitosis process suggests that there may be general vulnerability in the process, and that learners may make a mistake in the process that could be catastrophic for their L1 or influence it in some other way; in normal second language acquisition, continued exposure to the L1 would help to prevent or correct those kinds of errors.

4.4 Bilingual Aphasia & Dementia

Individuals with post-stroke aphasia and language deficits in dementia (typically primary progressive aphasia (PPA), but also dementia more generally) have damage to the brain regions that support language processing and/or brain regions supporting speech, language controlfoot_6 , attention, or other related cognitive processes. However, there is little existing literature on bilingual aphasia; significant research is needed in this understudied topic (Arce Rentería et al., 2026; Beveridge & Bak, 2011; Goral, 2004; Jessner et al., 2021; Reifegerste, 2021). Aphasia research remains heavily concentrated on monolingual speakers of English and other western European languages, limiting the generalizability of findings across language pairs (Beveridge & Bak, 2011; Egia-Zabala & Munarriz-Ibarrola, 2024). Studies on bilinguals frequently involve small samples or individual cases (Goral et al., 2010; Kohnert, 2009), and findings concerning language control, recovery, and treatment generalization are heterogeneous (Goral et al., 2023; Kuzmina et al., 2019). Furthermore, the literature on impairments to syntactic representations in bilinguals is also limited (Egia-Zabala & Munarriz-Ibarrola, 2024; Goral et al., 2023; Grasso et al., 2023; Kuzmina et al., 2019), while much of the treatment literature examines lexical-semantic rather than syntactic impairments (Lee & Faroqi-Shah, 2024). Morphosyntactic treatment and cross-language generalization have been examined in individual cases (e.g., Goral et al., 2010), but remain insufficiently studied. The non-lexicalist approach of M-WOW offers testable predictions about patterns of impairment, assessment, recovery, and treatment that can guide future research in aphasia and dementia.

In M-WOW, impairment in aphasia or dementia does not necessarily affect the entire language system equally. Instead, patients can exhibit selective impairments to representations (e.g., phonology; Astell & Harley, 1996; Goodglass et al., 1976; Middleton & Schwartz, 2013), to the mapping processes between representations during processing, to language control, or some combination of the three. The effect of impairments to representations or mappings are illustrated in the middle panel of Figure 6 below. Damage to a representation should affect the tasks and languages that recruit that representation, while damage to a mapping should affect the processing operations that depend on that mapping, potentially producing dissociations between comprehension and production. Impaired language control should produce disproportionate difficulty under conditions requiring selection between languages, inhibition or facilitation of competing alternatives, or increased cognitive control. These deficits may not be immediately transparent; deficits that appear to reflect impaired linguistic representations may instead be caused by impairments in the mappings between meaning, syntax, and phonological form, particularly when these processes depend on cognitive control. Testing these predictions requires assessment of the same syntactic structures across languages and tasks that recruit different representations, mappings, and control processes.

Aphasia may not necessarily affect both of a bilingual's languages equally. Case reports have described the re-emergence of a seldom-used language after stroke (Leemann et al., 2007) and increased reliance on an earlier acquired language in dementia (McMurtray et al., 2009), though M-WOW suggests that there should also be effects at the level of individual linguistic consider the specific linguistic properties shared between languages rather than treating generalization as an all-or-none outcome.

Patients with impaired language control may instead benefit from treatment targeting language selection and competition. For example, Ansaldo et al. (2010) demonstrated improvement in a bilingual patient with pathological language mixing by teaching a strategy to recognize and correct involuntary language switching. Similar approaches could potentially be extended to sentence production by training patients to select and maintain the intended language when producing syntactic structures that differ across their languages. Treatment could also manipulate language switching demands and cross-language interference to determine whether improvements reflect better control over competing representations. However, the effects of these approaches on syntactic production and cross-language generalization have not been established.

The M-WOW framework also generates predictions about recovery patterns in bilingual aphasia. We propose that recovery may involve changes to the organization of the bilingual language system itself, rather than exclusively restoring the premorbid organization. During recovery, patients may shift their reliance on different syntactic representations or mappings to compensate for those that are impaired, potentially producing changes in the relative contributions of L1 and L2. This could be investigated through cross-language structural priming, interference, and patterns of generalization to untrained constructions, where we might expect to observe changes even on structures that were not directly treated. Change in the direction or magnitude of cross-language structural priming for untrained constructions could indicate changes in which representations are recruited, particularly if it persists across tasks and processing demands. Access to previously available representations may also be restored by recruiting existing mappings, allowing them to be accessed via a different route. These possible recovery pathways are illustrated in the right panel of Figure 6. Longitudinal changes in performance may reflect the recovery of previously impaired components, changes in access to preserved components, or representational reorganization during recovery, where the relationship between the L1 and L2 may change over time.

Recovery is also expected to vary depending on each element's degree of representational overlap, providing an account of how recovery may be non-uniform across languages or tasks even when some underlying representations are shared. If a particular syntactic representation is recruited in both languages, recovery of that representation should benefit both languages.

Consequently, recovery should be relatively parallel for structures with shared representations, although the magnitude of improvement may differ depending on premorbid proficiency, language use, and the status of the mappings supporting each language. In contrast, structures with independent representations may recover separately, resulting in differential recovery of L1 and L2. Partially overlapping representations may show intermediate or inconsistent patterns of recovery. Recovery of mappings or language control may produce other patterns; for example, recovery of the syntax-form mapping may improve production without equivalent changes in comprehension, while improvements in language control may benefit several structures or both languages.

5. Conclusion

In this paper, we have outlined three desiderata of a non-lexicalist model of multilingualism: that the language system should be probabilistic and context-dependent, that there should be no strict divide between languages in the bilingual mind, and that language acquisition should not have to progress uniformly, while also being able to accommodate a diverse set of experiences, and being testable and falsifiable. From these criteria, we developed the M-WOW model of bilingual language production, which is able to capture a wider range of linguistic phenomena from many different languages and language families, and also can also accommodate many aspects of the bilingual experience, including sequential and simultaneous second language acquisition, changes in representations over the course of language acquisition, and language attrition. We also describe the process of syntactic mitosis and how it may be involved during language attrition, as well as in cross-linguistic interference and bilingual aphasia. Although M-WOW is more computationally expensive and complex than other models like Multilink or the RHM, we think this is justified given the evidence that needs to be accounted for, as well as the explanatory power that this new model provides.

Bilingual aphasia assessment is frequently limited by the availability of appropriate assessment materials (Favis et al., 2026; Wallace et al., 2022), clinicians' knowledge of patients' languages (Arslan et al., 2026; Norvik et al., 2022), and access to qualified interpreters (Norvik & Goral, 2021; Roger & Code, 2011). Inadequate examination of bilingualism status may account for generalizations in the literature regarding L1/L2 status, proficiency, dominance, age of acquisition, etc. (Egia-Zabala & Munarriz-Ibarrola, 2024; Favis et al., 2026). For example, the majority of standardized assessments are normed for monolingual populations and not appropriate for bilingual patients (Goral & Conner, 2013; Wallace et al., 2022).

Adopting M-WOW could therefore improve assessment in bilingual aphasia. First, assessments should consider the likely status of individual representations prior to neurologic injury or disease, including premorbid proficiency and language history (see Fahey et al., 2026; Gray & Kiran, 2013; Kiran & Roberts, 2012), to estimate the degree of representational overlap in individual elements across the patient's languages. Second, assessments should examine the same representations through different processing pathways to distinguish damage to representations from damage to the mappings. For example, relatively preserved comprehension of a structure alongside impaired production could suggest that the syntactic representation is intact while the syntax-form mapping is impaired, while impairment across several tasks recruiting the same representation could be consistent with representational damage.

Assessments should also manipulate processing demands to identify possible impairments in language control. These could include tasks involving language interference, switching, or competing syntactic alternatives, as well as comparisons across levels of syntactic complexity. If performance deteriorates disproportionately with increased control demands, this may indicate that the deficits are not exclusively representational. In sum, appropriate assessment requires more than comparing general language impairment in L1 and L2, and should include converging evidence from multiple tasks analyzing individual elements. The resulting assessment profile should inform treatment selection by identifying whether intervention should target the impaired syntactic representations, the mappings required to access them, and/or language control.

Treatment following the M-WOW framework should target the specific locus of impairment. If syntactic representations are impaired, treatment could explicitly train the relevant syntactic structures, such as noncanonical sentences involving syntactic movement, as in Treatment of Underlying Forms (TUF; Thompson & Shapiro, 2005). Improvement should then be examined in untrained constructions that recruit the same syntactic representations. If the representations are preserved but their mappings are impaired, treatment could instead target the affected mappings. For example, Mapping Therapy targets the relationship between thematic roles and syntactic structure (Rochon et al., 2005), while impairments to syntax-form mappings may require practice in producing the appropriate language-specific forms from otherwise preserved syntactic structures. Similarly, for PPA, treatment may focus on maintaining access to preserved representations and mappings, and supporting the processing mechanisms that remain available.

Aphasia treatment has been shown to generalize across languages (Faroqi-Shah et al., 2010), but M-WOW may provide a more detailed account of this process.
