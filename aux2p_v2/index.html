Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Online collaborative projects like Wikipedia rely on sequential collaboration, where individual contributors iteratively revise a shared product. While previous research has examined wikis for collaborative writing and sequential collaboration for numerical judgments, little is known about the cognitive mechanisms underlying information sharing in such settings. The present study bridges research on collaborative writing, group information sharing, and cognitive models of sequential collaboration. Drawing on a computational model of sequential collaboration, we tested whether higher variation in contributors' expertise facilitates the integration of information into a shared text. In an experiment with 413 participants, we demonstrate that information accumulation increased throughout the sequential chain but at a decreasing rate.

Consistent with model predictions, higher expertise variation resulted in more information being incorporated into the shared text. Contrary to expectations, expertise variation did not affect the speed of information accumulation, nor did it increase information deletions. These findings demonstrate that predictions derived from a cognitive model of numerical judgment formation generalize to information-sharing contexts. More broadly, the results suggest that diversity in contributors' knowledge can improve collaborative knowledge construction in sequential environments. This research adds to evidence that sequential collaboration is an effective mechanism for integrating distributed knowledge in online collaborative systems.

Expertise Diversity Enhances Information Sharing in Wiki settings: Evidence from Sequential Collaboration

Online collaborative projects rapidly emerged about 25 years ago. These projects fundamentally reshaped how individuals collaborate allowing collaboration across place and time. Prominent examples are Wikipedia and OpenStreetMap which are highly successful considering their accuracy and extent (El-Ashmawy, 2016; García del Valle et al., 2018; Giles, 2005; Mesgari et al., 2015; H. Zhang & Malczewski, 2017) and have been extensively researched in the past (Okoli et al., 2014; Sun et al., 2026). These projects follow the same collaborative mechanism, namely sequential collaboration (Mayer & Heck, 2024). In sequential collaboration, one contributor starts a sequential chain by creating an entry. This entry is then sequentially adjusted or maintained by subsequent contributors encountering it.

Projects and interventions relying on a wiki structure have been extensively researched in the context of computer-supported collaborative learning (Jeong et al., 2019; Kimmerle et al., 2015; Zheng et al., 2015). Here, wikis are examined as a potential learning intervention for students. Even though not explicitly labeled as such, the examined projects and interventions typically relied on sequential collaboration.

Research on computer-supported collaborative learning typically focuses on individual learning and the generation of a common product. Individual cognitive processes determining which information contributors enter into a collaboratively written text under what conditions is typically not examined.

The most central component of online collaborative projects is information sharing. In a meta-analysis, Mesmer-Magnus and DeChurch (2009) demonstrated that information sharing is improved when tasks are highly demonstrable, discussions are structured, and group members are more cooperative, which overall leads to better group decisions. A specific paradigm investigating group information sharing and subsequent decision making is the hidden profile paradigm (Lu et al., 2012; Stasser & Titus, 1985). Research examining hidden profiles consistently showed that individuals consider unshared information only available to one group member too little and shared information available to all group members too much resulting in inaccurate group decisions (Lu et al., 2012). Thus, Mesmer-Magnus and DeChurch (2009) recommend highly demonstrable tasks, structured information sharing, and a cooperative climate to foster information sharing and subsequent decisions. This can potentially be achieved through sequential collaboration as it provides structured collaboration in which contributors can remain anonymous reducing unwanted biases and directing contributors attention and focus to the common product of their collaboration.

Recently, work on sequential collaboration emerged outside computer-supported collaborative learning, namely for rank order problems (Miller & Steyvers, 2011) and for numerical judgment formation (e.g., Mayer & Heck, 2024). These accounts use minimal sequential collaboration tasks in which contributors are only confronted with the most recent state of the common group product and receive no information about the (number of) previous contributors, no change history, and do not have access to discussion forums or comments. This research has more strongly focused on the accuracy of the collaborative estimate (i.e., rank order or numerical judgment, Mayer & Heck, 2024; Miller & Steyvers, 2011), features of current contributors (Mayer et al., 2023), information about previous contributors (Mayer & Kimmerle, 2025) and information about the sequential chain (Fischer et al., 2026) as influences on the contribution process and investigated variations of the collaboration process (Mayer et al., 2025). Based on this work, a cognitive model formalizing the findings and theoretical assumptions was developed (Mayer, 2026; Mayer & Heck, 2025). Other potentially more successful forms of information sharing were neglected even though they are more prevalent in online settings and often yield a shared product.

Finally, cognitive sequential-collaboration research has focused on numerical judgment formation and explored individual conditions of successful collaboration, but did not apply their results to more realistic sequential-collaboration settings.

This work seeks to take a first step in combining these strands of research and systematically examine sequential collaboration building on a cognitive model of sequential collaboration for numerical judgment formation but using more realistic scenarios of information sharing as found in online collaborative projects. Thereby, we focus on individual contributors and investigate cognitive mechanisms of sequential collaboration for information sharing, which in turn can help gain deeper insights into optimal conditions for collaboration on a shared product.

Sequential Collaboration: From Wikipedia to numerical judgment formation

From the perspective of computer-supported collaborative learning, collaborative writing tools have been examined in various educational contexts (Mindel & Verma, 2006; Wheeler et al., 2008; Zheng et al., 2015). Wikis allow learners to engage in a joint task, to communicate, share resources, engage in productive collaborative learning processes, engage in co-construction, monitor and regulate collaborative learning and find and build groups and communities (Jeong & Hmelo-Silver, 2016), which result in a medium-sized learning effect (Jeong et al., 2019). However, students typically need support for adequate learning (Zheng et al., 2015) and as also observed in online collaborative projects (Arazy & Nov, 2010; Ortega et al., 2008) there is severe inequality in the extent to which learners contribute (Judd et al., 2010). From a theoretical perspective, computer-supported collaborative learning was often considered as a co-evolution process (Cress et al., 2016; Cress & Kimmerle, 2008; Kimmerle et al., 2015). This process model assumes that knowledge is constructed socially in a collaboration process which in turn affects individual learning whereas individual knowledge shapes contributions. Thus, a co-evolution of individual knowledge and collaborative product emerges.

However, while examining collaborative writing, computer-supported collaborative learning strongly focuses on the product and its impact on group and individual learning neglecting individual contributions and the cognitive processes behind them. Thus, we argue that investigating and understanding information sharing with sequential collaboration on an individual level yields important insights into the processes shaping online collaborative projects.

When contributing to online collaborative projects, contributors mostly share information in these projects. Information sharing has been excessively investigated (Mesmer-Magnus & DeChurch, 2009), for example in the context of the hidden profile paradigm (Lu et al., 2012; Stasser & Titus, 1985). In the hidden profile paradigm, individuals perform a group decision task (e.g., a murder mystery or a hiring decision).

Each individual receives a set of information containing information shared among all group members and unshared information only available to one group member.

Information is distributed among group members such that shared information as well as the set of information available to each group member suggests a suboptimal decision but integrating all information, especially unshared information, suggests the correct decision (hence, hidden profile). The information sharing as well as individual decisions before and after group interaction and the group decision are primarily examined.

Overall, information sharing is insufficient such that groups tend to discuss shared information and neglect discussing unshared information (Lu et al., 2012). This is due to discussions primarily revolving around preferences rather than information (Gigone & Hastie, 1993) resulting in social validation (Mojzisch et al., 2014; Wittenbaum et al., 1999), repetition of shared information being socially desirable (Henningsen & Henningsen, 2004; Postmes et al., 2001), and individuals tending to keep their initial preference (Faulmüller et al., 2010). Transferring group discussion to online settings did neither improve information sharing nor subsequent group decisions (Lu et al., 2012).

Some of these difficulties may be remedied by using sequential collaboration for information sharing. In sequential collaboration, contributors cannot discuss preferences in the same setting as they share information reducing the chance of an unwanted focus on preferences, social validation cannot happen as information about other contributors is minimal, and information is unlikely to be repeated if it is already present in the current entry.

To improve information sharing, Mesmer-Magnus and DeChurch (2009) recommend highly demonstrable tasks, structured information sharing and a cooperative climate to foster information sharing in groups. This can potentially be achieved through sequential collaboration. First, it provides a structured collaboration due to the sequential collaboration format. Second, information about contributors is sparse which on the one hand allows contributors to remain anonymous reducing unwanted biases, but on the other hand directs contributors' focus toward the product and the collaborative task. Thus, sequential collaboration is potentially a suitable method to share information.

Collaborative writing and especially sharing information in online collaborative projects can be viewed as a naturally emerging situation often experimentally induced in hidden profiles with shared and unshared information among contributors. When creating and refining an article about the Eiffel Tower, all contributors probably know where it is located, but only few know how tall it is, when it was constructed, which materials were used, or how heavy it is. Thus, contributors hold shared as well as unshared information, which is typically more nuanced in its distribution among contributors compared to experimentally designed hidden profiles. Nonetheless, online collaborative situations mimic information distribution experimentally induced in hidden profiles, in which contributors combine shared and unshared information into a common product. Recently, the contribution process of Wikipedia and OpenStreetMap has been adapted to numerical judgment formation and termed sequential collaboration (Mayer & Heck, 2024). In these paradigms, the focus was on cognitive mechanisms allowing for accurate judgments and thus a high-quality product rather than on learning or information sharing. Instead of text production, contributors provide numerical judgments to questions such as "How tall is the Eiffel tower?". Thereby, one contributor starts a sequential chain by providing an independent judgment. Following contributors encounter the latest judgment in the sequential chain, decide whether to adjust or maintain it, and provide a revised judgment when deciding to adjust. In this paradigm, contributors do not know their position in the sequential chain and are not provided the change history above and beyond the latest judgment. Mayer and Heck (2024) demonstrated for sequential collaboration with numerical judgments that changes become fewer and smaller whereas judgments become more accurate over the course of a sequential chain of four or six contributors. Moreover, the final estimate at the end of a sequential chain was at least as accurate and in some cases even more accurate than the average of the same number of independent judgments (wisdom of crowds, Galton, 1907; Larrick & Soll, 2006; Surowiecki, 2005). Sequential collaboration for numerical judgment formation has been shown to be at least partially driven by contributors' expertise with higher expertise in contributors leading to more frequent and larger changes and more improvement compared to contributors with lower expertise (Mayer et al., 2023), which is in line with findings for hidden profiles (Franz & Larson, 2002). In line with assumptions by Wagner (2004) for wikis in general, both the sequential process of collaboration and the possibility to opt out of providing judgments contributes to judgment accuracy in sequential collaboration (Mayer et al., 2025). Information about irrelevant (i.e., gender or group membership in a minimal group paradigm, Billig & Tajfel, 1973; Brown, 2020) as well as relevant (i.e., expertise) features of the previous contributors showed overall no substantial effects on contribution behavior (Mayer & Kimmerle, 2025). This is in line with Ollesch et al. (2021) who found that information about other contributors' knowledge or participation had only minor effects on contribution behavior, especially on article quality in wiki settings. Finally, whereas information about other contributors showed no substantial effects, information about the sequential chain affects contribution behavior in sequential collaboration for numerical judgments (Fischer et al., 2026). Participants being informed about how often the presented judgment has been left unchanged by previous participants reacted accordingly with more and larger changes to recently changed judgments and fewer and smaller changes to longer maintained judgments.

Overall, this research was combined in a formalized cognitive model of sequential collaboration (Mayer, 2026; Mayer & Heck, 2025).

Even though yielding promising results and extending research on numerical judgment formation, sequential collaboration is typically not practiced to result in accurate numerical judgments. Rather contributors in online collaborative projects and learners collaborating with wiki methods, which inspired work on sequential collaboration as a collaborative process, share information with each other. Research, however, has focused on group discussions and has not yet investigated which and how information is shared using sequential collaboration for this task. This work therefore seeks to bridge the gap between using wikis for knowledge construction on the one hand and sequential collaboration as a mere judgment generation process on the other via information sharing with sequential collaboration. Thereby, these three research traditions are combined in a single experiment.

To derive our hypotheses, we used the recently developed cognitive model of sequential collaboration for numerical judgments (Mayer & Heck, 2025). This model assumes that contributors rely on an internal distribution of plausible values for each numerical judgment question which is defined by their expertise and the difficulty of the task. Based on this distribution, they decide whether to adjust or maintain the presented judgment of a previous contributor. When deciding to adjust, contributors are influenced by the judgment of a previous participant when generating the revised judgment. The model predicts that contributions become smaller over the course of a Note. This prediction is based on the simulation study presented by Mayer and Heck (2025). We used all simulations that had either a high or low variation in contributors' expertise and aggregated across other parameters manipulated in the simulation study, such as overall level of expertise or item difficulty. sequential chain while judgments become more accurate and that the accuracy of provided judgments as well as the success of sequential chains strongly depends on contributors expertise, which is in line with empirical findings (Mayer et al., 2023; Mayer & Heck, 2024).

We derived a previously untested prediction from the cognitive model of sequential collaboration and tested it in an information-sharing sequential-collaboration setting, namely we varied how much individuals difer in their expertise. The cognitive model predicts that numerical judgment formation is especially fruitful and yields highly accurate judgments if contributors have high variation in their expertise. Figure 1 displays the probability and magnitude of changes as well as the accuracy of judgments in sequential chains with high or low variation in contributors expertise.

These results demonstrate that changes are more frequent and larger resulting in more accurate judgments overall when contributors vary highly in their expertise compared to when they do not.

Translated to information sharing, contributors vary more strongly in expertise if they have access to various different pieces of information, whereas contributors vary only little in expertise if they are familiar with mostly the same pieces of information.

Based on the cognitive model prediction, we therefore expect that high variation in contributors' expertise leads to more pieces of information included into the common product than low variation in contributors' expertise (Hypothesis 1). Moreover, we expect that high variation in contributors' expertise leads more contributions especially early in the sequential chain as shared information is still relatively sparse and contributors still can contribute a substantial amount of information known to them but unshared in the common product. Thus, we expect that high variation in contributors' expertise leads to implementation of new information into the common product earlier than low variation in expertise (Hypothesis 2). Finally, high variation in expertise may also come with some cost. Contributors are more likely to encounter information that they do not have when variation in expertise is high. Thus, we expect that high variation in contributors' expertise leads to more deletions of content than low variation in expertise (Hypothesis 3).

To test our hypotheses, we report an empirical study manipulating expertise variation in participants sharing previously provided information sequentially on several topics. We analyze the effects of variation in contributors' expertise on sharing and deleting information and examine how information sharing progresses in sequential chains.

Methods

All hypotheses were tested experimentally and preregistered at https://aspredicted.org/z9er2u.pdf. Materials, data and analyses are available at LINK.

Participants and Design

To test our hypotheses, we implemented a 2 (expertise variation) x 5 (chain position) repeated measures design. Participants were assigned to either the high-variation or the low-variation expertise condition and a sequential chain position before performing sequential collaboration for six different topics. Sequential chains were formed with five participants, the first one starting the collaboration with a preset starting text. All participants in one sequential chain were assigned to the same expertise-variation condition.

We sampled 433 participants who were both university students and staff from University of Tübingen as well as university students recruited via a commercial German panel provider. One participant was excluded due to technical issues during participation; another 19 participants were excluded due to duplicates in chain position.

The final sample consists of 413 participants with a mean age of 27.73 (SD = 9.52) of which 67.80% were female, 29.06% were male, and 1.21% indicated a different gender; the remaining eight participants did not indicate their gender. In total, 84 sequential chains were formed whereas four chains remained unfinished, but were nonetheless included in the analysis.

Material and Procedure

For this study, thirteen pieces of information for six different topics, that is, giraffes, water supply in the roman empire, Moai, the Maya calendar, the Byzantine empire, and runes, were sources from Wikipedia. Three of these pieces of information were mentioned in the starting text that was presented to the first participant in a sequential chain, the other ten pieces of information were only distributed among participants. For each topic, participants in this study were presented with eight different pieces of information. Two of these pieces of information were already mentioned in the starting text. This procedure ensured that even participants beginning the collaboration could potentially make a deletion based on their available information. The other six pieces of information presented were filled with topic information according to participants' experimental condition: In the low-variation condition, all participants received the same five pieces of information and only one piece of information varied between participants. In the high-variation condition, the six pieces of information presented to participants were filled such that each of the remaining ten pieces of information for each topic was mentioned thrice throughout the sequential chain. All pieces of information presented throughout the study and their distribution in the sequential chain are available in the Appendix.

Participants were recruited for a 20-minute collaborative wiki-style study and provided their informed consent. Before starting with the task, they were informed that they would receive information about six different topics after which they were presented with the text of a previous participant each. Their task was to adjust the presented text according to the information they received. Moreover, they were randomly assigned to either the high-variation or the low-variation expertise condition. In each trial, participants were presented with eight pieces of information and were given 60 seconds to read them thoroughly. Information could not be copied and window changes (e.g., to screenshot programs) were tracked throughout the study to ensure participants did not copy the information to later paste it. Afterward, participants were automatically directed to the editing page where the text of a previous participant was displayed in an open text box ready for editing. To ensure that participants did not erroneously remove all text, a copy of the presented text was provided beneath the open text box and could be copied into it if necessary. Participants were required to stay for at least 30 seconds on the page before they could continue and could edit the text for a maximum of 5 minutes. In between these time restrictions, participants could continue to the next topic at will. After completing sequential collaboration for all six topics, participants provided demographic information, were debriefed and thanked for their participation.

Data analysis

To examine which information previously provided to the participants was mentioned in the texts participants produced, we used the coding framework by Mayer and Akef (2026). This data was coded using a supervised pair scoring approach. Here, the pretrained transformer encoder BERT was used to code data by receiving texts and information simultaneously and searching for the information in the text (T. Zhang et al., 2020). As BERT is a pretrained transformer encoder, it is necessary to provide a manually coded training dataset which is typically a representative subset of the data.

Thus, a manually coded subset of 128 texts containing a total of 1664 pieces of information was provided. If a piece of information had a probability of more than 50% to appear in the text, it was coded as included. Based on the manually coded subset, we computed sensitivity and specificity of this coding methods. Overall, the coding was highly accurate with a sensitivity of 100% and a specificity of 98.61%.

To test our hypotheses, we implemented generalized hierarchical linear models with crossed random effects to account for the nested structure of our data as multiple participants worked on multiple texts relying on multiple pieces of information. We added logit link functions as information was either included (1) or not included (0), and either deleted (1) or not deleted (0), respectively. The initial texts presented to the first contributor were excluded from all analysis as no empirical data was collected here. Additionally, expertise variation was effect coded with -0.5: low variation and 0.5: high variation. Chain position was polynomial coded to test a linear trend as well as a quadratic trend of chain position.

Results

Confirmatory analysis

First, we analyzed whether pieces of information were mentioned in each text for which the results are also displayed in Panel A of Figure 2. We estimated a hierarchical linear model with generalized hierarchical linear model with whether information was included or not as dependent variable and chain position and expertise variation as independent variables. The probability that a piece of information was mentioned in the text increased over the course of a sequential chain with a significant positive linear relationship ( β = 1.37, 95% CI [1.21, 1.54], z = 16.32, p < .001) as well as a significant negative quadratic relationship ( β = -0.29, 95% CI [-0.45, -0.12], z = -3.43, p < .001). This combination of trends indicates that across expertise-variation conditions contributors add more new information with every sequential step, but the number of newly added information decreases with every sequential step. This finding is in line with the hypothesis-generating simulation, showing that the accuracy of numerical judgments increases most in the beginning of a sequential chain and less with increasing chain positions. In line with Hypothesis 1, the model revealed a positive effect of expertise variation ( β = 0.30, 95% CI [0.15, 0.45], z = 4.01, p < .001) with more information mentioned by contributors in sequential chains with high expertise variation compared to contributors in chains with low expertise variation. However, there was no significant interaction of expertise variation and chain position neither for a linear trend ( β = -0.04, 95% CI [-0.37, 0.29], z = -0.23, p = .820) nor a quadratic trend ( β = 0.01, 95% CI [-0.32, 0.34], z = 0.07, p = .945). Thus, Hypothesis 2 was not supported.

Instead of analyzing whether a piece of information was present or not, we analyzed how many pieces of information were mentioned. To account for the dependent variable being integers between zero and thirteen, we used generalized linear mixed models with a poisson link function for this analysis. As in confirmatory analysis, a significant positive linear ( β = 0.44, 95% CI [0.39, 0.49], z = 17.05, p < .001) and negative quadratic trend ( β = -0.12, 95% CI [-0.17, -0.07], z = -4.62, p < .001) of chain position as well as a significant positive effect of expertise variation ( β = 0.09, 95% CI [0.04, 0.13], z = 3.89, p < .001) on the number of pieces of information included in the text were observed. For deleted information none of the tested effects reached significance.

Overall, these results demonstrate that higher variation in contributors' expertise increases information sharing, and texts contain an increasing number of pieces of information over the course of a sequential chain. Moreover, the robustness analysis indicates that there could also be more deletions with increasing chain position but this effect was not present in the confirmatory analysis.

Discussion

We derived and adapted hypotheses for the effect of expertise variation on information sharing in sequential collaboration from a cognitive model for numerical judgment formation with sequential collaboration. As predicted by the model, we expected both increased information sharing and more information deletion with higher expertise variation. We examined information sharing in sequential collaboration with an experimental paradigm manipulating the variation in contributors' expertise via provided information. The results demonstrated that increasing chain position and higher variation in expertise indeed leads to more shared information but not to more deletions.

Overall, with increasing chain position more information was entered into the collaboratively written texts, but the positive slope decreased over the course of a sequential chain. As expected, high variation in expertise led to more information sharing, but no moderation by chain position was found. Moreover, we did not find more deletions in the confirmatory analysis. However, we found increasing deletions with chain position in the robustness analysis when information already present in the starting text was removed from the analysis. Nonetheless, deletions were comparatively rare and therefore difficult to assess with sufficient power.

This experiment demonstrates a successful transfer of model predictions from a cognitive model of sequential collaboration built for numerical judgment formation to information sharing with sequential collaboration.

This extends the previous work on this theoretical framework to the more realistic settings of information sharing and opens a new direction for assessing cognitive mechanisms of sequential collaboration in information sharing. Participants in this experiment successfully shared information with sequential collaboration. In contrast to findings for hidden profiles, information sharing increased throughout the sequential chain indicating that encountering shared information did not substantially reduce group members' own information sharing. Nonetheless, in line with previous findings and the hypotheses of this research, information sharing is reduced if only single group members hold a piece of information. Sequential collaboration may thus be a valid alternative to group discussions for information sharing.

Furthermore, our results demonstrate that individual features of contributors and the way they are composed in a sequential chain shape contribution behavior in sequential collaboration. Thus, individual features such as contributors' expertise or the composition of contributors within sequential chains should be considered both when designing and analyzing learning environments with wikis.

Overall, we successfully combined a cognitive modeling approach and a collaborative writing approach to sequential collaboration by manipulating and assessing information sharing. Introducing model-based predictions to information sharing with sequential collaboration yielded mostly the expected results and showed that expertise variation in sequential collaboration indeed affects information sharing.

Thus, modeling approaches and their predictions can productively influence information sharing with sequential collaboration.

Even though yielding promising results and demonstrating that combining research on sequential collaboration for numerical judgment formation, group information sharing and decision making and collaborative writing, this research has also some limitations. First, the paradigm used in this research is memory-based.

Participants collaborated on topics they were most likely not familiar with and had to memorize the information presented to them to some degree before engaging in sequential collaboration. This can influence how much and which information was shared as contributors may have not remembered later pieces of information.

Insufficiently remembering information may also be the reason why participants overall only included 8.29 pieces of information at the final chain position. Thus, developing a memory-free paradigm to assess sequential collaboration can be helpful to avoid these influences. Second, the study had a limited sample of mostly university students and individuals employed at the university, thus having high levels of education. Even though cognitive mechanisms are not expected to vary significantly with educational background, the variability in participants' answers may increase which increases the statistical power required to detect effects. Similarly, deletions were comparatively rare in the experiment with an average of 0.38 deletions per participant and trial. Thus, the power for examining deletions was low and may have been amplified by a relatively homogeneous sample. Collecting a more diverse sample of participants may therefore improve statistical power and help to clarify the effects examined in this research.

Conclusion

Sequential collaboration successfully supported the integration of distributed information across contributors especially when expertise variation increased, demonstrating the value of heterogeneous knowledge sources for collective outcomes.

Thus, sequential collaboration may overcome some limitations commonly observed in group information sharing and leverages heterogeneous knowledge distributions for collaborative knowledge construction. This work provides a foundation for studying information sharing through cognitive models of collaborative behavior.
