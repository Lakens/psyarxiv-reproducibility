To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 24 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background and objectives: Animal-assisted interventions have been introduced in adolescent mental health care, but evidence for their added benefit in inpatient treatment remains limited. This study examined animal-assisted group psychotherapy across three assessment intervals: 1) the individual sessions, 2) the five-week intervention period, 3) the full inpatient stay.

Methods: This controlled study included 276 adolescents aged 12-18 years receiving inpatient psychosomatic treatment. The intervention group (n = 108) participated in five weekly 150-minute animal-assisted group psychotherapy sessions alongside standard multimodal treatment, whereas the control group (n = 168) received standard treatment alone. Group allocation was determined by the treating clinicians based on predefined clinical indications and safety criteria. Linear mixed-effects models examined 1) immediate pre-post session changes in depressive symptoms and mindfulness, 2) change in depressive symptoms, self-esteem, self-concept, and mindfulness across the intervention period, and 3) admission-to-discharge changes in depressive symptoms by group.

Results: Depressive symptoms decreased (b = -2.53 to -3.04, all p < .001) and mindfulness increased (b = 4.88 to 6.36, all p ≤ .001) immediately after individual sessions. Across the intervention period, depressive symptoms decreased, while self-esteem, self-concept, and mindfulness increased significantly (all p ≤ .031). Although depressive symptoms decreased substantially from admission to discharge in both groups, no significant time × group interactions were found for the BDI-II (b = 1.36, p = .371) or PHQ-9 (b = 0.65, p = .394).

The findings suggest that animal-assisted group psychotherapy may provide short-term psychological benefits for adolescents receiving inpatient mental health care, potentially supporting readiness, attention, and therapeutic engagement. However, these immediate benefits do not necessarily translate into additional improvements in depressive symptoms over the course of inpatient treatment.

Animal-Assisted Therapy in Adolescents with Mental Health Disorders: Effects on Depression, Self-Perception, and Mindfulness

Globally, approximately one in seven adolescents aged 10-19 years experiences a mental disorder, with depression among the leading causes of illness and disability during this developmental period (World Health Organization, 2025). Many mental disorders first emerge during adolescence or early adulthood and approximately half of all lifetime mental disorders have their onset before the age of 20, highlighting the importance of early detection and therapeutic interventions (McGrath et al., 2023; Solmi et al., 2022). Although evidence-based psychological and pharmacological treatments are available, not all adolescents respond sufficiently, and maintaining engagement and motivation can be challenging, particularly during intensive inpatient treatment. Consequently, there is growing interest in complementary approaches that can be integrated into established treatment programs.

Treatment of adolescents with mental health disorders aims not only to reduce psychological symptoms but also to strengthen positive aspects of psychological functioning, including selfconcept and self-esteem. Self-concept refers to the organized, multifaceted, and hierarchical system of perceptions individuals hold about themselves, whereas self-esteem represents the evaluative dimension of the self, particularly an individual's subjective evaluation of his or her worth as a person (Donnellan et al., 2011; Shavelson et al., 1976). Both undergo substantial development during adolescence and are closely related to depressive symptoms (Hards et al., 2020; Orchard et al., 2021; Van Der Cruijsen et al., 2023). Given their relevance to adolescent psychological functioning, selfconcept and self-esteem represent important outcomes when evaluating interventions for adolescents with mental health disorders.

Mindfulness is another relevant aspect of psychological functioning in adolescence. It is commonly conceptualized as the regulation of attention toward present-moment experience, thereby increasing awareness of mental processes and supporting more skillful responses to emotional distress and maladaptive behavior (Bishop et al., 2004). In a clinical adolescent sample, mindfulness-based treatment has been associated with reductions in anxiety, depressive, and somatization symptoms, and improved self-esteem (Biegel et al., 2009). Together, depressive symptoms, selfesteem, self-concept, and mindfulness capture both psychopathological symptoms and positive aspects of psychological functioning. Their combined assessment is consistent with models proposing that adolescent mental health should be evaluated using both negative indicators of psychopathology and positive indicators of psychological functioning (Magalhães, 2024) Animal-assisted interventions represent one complementary approach that may be beneficial in strengthening these symptom-and resource-oriented outcomes. Animal-assisted intervention is an umbrella term for structured, goal-oriented interventions that intentionally incorporate animals into therapeutic, educational, or human services. Within this framework, animal-assisted therapy (AAT) refers specifically to planned and documented interventions delivered by qualified health or humanservice professionals as part of their clinical practice (International Association of Human-Animal Interaction Organizations, 2018). Integrating animals into psychotherapy may offer additional opportunities for therapeutic engagement and experiential learning (Jones et al., 2019; Wilson et al., 2017). Through their responses to human behavior and nonverbal cues, animals may provide feedback that helps adolescents become more aware of their own emotional and behavioral responses (Pendry et al., 2018). Interacting with animals may provide opportunities for adolescents to experience competence and develop confidence and assertiveness, potentially contributing to positive self-esteem (Shen et al., 2018; Wilson et al., 2017). Furthermore, positive interactions with animals may also provide calming experiences that facilitate engagement in therapeutic activities (Jones et al., 2019; Shen et al., 2018). Systematic reviews suggest potential benefits for psychopathological symptoms, emotional well-being, and social and behavioral functioning among children and adolescents with mental health difficulties (Hoagwood et al., 2017; Jones et al., 2019).

More specific evidence from controlled studies suggests that canine-and equine-assisted interventions can have beneficial effects in children and adolescents, although research examining these interventions among inpatient populations with significant mental health needs remains relatively scarce. Two small randomized controlled trials examined canine-assisted therapy as an adjunct to standard inpatient psychiatric care. Stefanini et al. (2015) randomized 34 children and adolescents hospitalized for acute mental disorders to either 12 weekly dog-assisted therapy sessions in addition to treatment as usual or treatment as usual alone. Global functioning and school attendance improved significantly in the AAT group but not in the control group. A subsequent publication based on an expanded sample of 40 participants from the same study reported significant reductions in internalizing symptoms and improvements in total competence and global functioning in the AAT group, whereas no corresponding improvements were observed in the control group (Stefanini et al., 2016). Although internalizing symptoms encompass depressive and withdrawn symptoms, depression was not assessed as a distinct outcome in either trial. In a nonrandomized controlled study, Prothmann et al. (2006) found improvements in vitality, emotional balance, social extraversion, and alertness following dog-assisted psychotherapy among child and adolescent psychiatric inpatients, whereas no corresponding improvements occurred in the comparison group.

Two further nonrandomized studies examined immediate session-level effects. Brown et al. (2020) found immediate reductions in negative mood and increases in positive mood following a therapydog session in a psychiatric setting, although the sample included both patients and staff. Similarly, Fodstad et al. (2019) reported reduced subjective distress after canine-assisted activities among 94 child and adolescent psychiatric inpatients.

Randomized studies of equine-assisted interventions have primarily involved at-risk adolescents rather than adolescents receiving psychiatric treatment. Frederick et al. (2015 Although the intervention group reported a greater increase in perceived social support, no significant between-group differences were found for self-esteem or general self-efficacy. In contrast, the nonrandomized study of Trotter et al. (2008) found that at-risk youths receiving equineassisted counseling improved across more behavioral and psychosocial domains than those receiving classroom-based counseling, including significantly greater improvements in self-esteem.

Taken together, previous studies provide preliminary evidence that animal-assisted interventions may improve internalizing symptoms in adolescents. However, findings remain mixed, particularly for depression, self-esteem and other self-related outcomes. Several methodological limitations may contribute to these inconsistent findings. The existing evidence is limited by small samples in populations characterized by mental health symptoms varying in type, severity and progression, which increases the risk of Type II error and may contribute to nonsignificant findings.

Medication use has rarely been considered adequately, despite its potential influence on psychological symptoms. Furthermore, studies have often relied on single or broad measures of internalizing symptoms or those associated with depression rather than comprehensive assessments of depression. Most have also focused on simple pre-post comparisons, without distinguishing changes across individual therapy sessions, the intervention period, and the full inpatient stay. This distinction is important because treatment effects may develop through stepwise changes across different time frames, beginning with immediate responses within individual sessions and extending to broader changes across the intervention and treatment periods.

The present study addresses these limitations in several ways. The comparatively large sample provides greater statistical power to detect intervention effects, while matching based on medication helps reduce potential confounding. The study simultaneously assesses depressive symptoms, self-esteem, self-concept, and mindfulness, with depression measured using multiple validated instruments to provide a more comprehensive assessment of symptom severity and change.

Finally, in our study we examine changes across three distinct assessment intervals: (1) individual therapy sessions, (2) the intervention period, and (3) the full inpatient stay. This approach captures immediate session-level changes, assesses changes across the five-week intervention period within the intervention group, and compares admission-to-discharge changes in depressive symptoms between the intervention and control groups. Assessing outcomes across different assessment intervals helps distinguish immediate changes following individual sessions from broader changes.

Accordingly, the study addressed three research questions: 1.

Research Question (RQ1): Do depressive symptoms and mindfulness change from before to after individual AAT sessions, and do these changes vary across sessions? 2. Research Question (RQ2): How do depressive symptoms, self-esteem, self-concept, and mindfulness change across the intervention period within the intervention group? 3. Research Question (RQ3): Do changes in depressive symptoms across the full inpatient stay differ between the intervention and control groups?

We expected improvements in depressive symptoms and mindfulness immediately following individual sessions (H1), as well as improvements in depressive symptoms, self-esteem, selfconcept, and mindfulness across the five-week intervention period (H2). We further hypothesized that reductions in depressive symptoms from admission to discharge would be greater among adolescents receiving animal-assisted group psychotherapy in addition to standard inpatient treatment than among those receiving standard treatment alone (H3). Differences in immediate changes across sessions were examined exploratorily.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

The study used a nonrandomized controlled design with clinician-determined group allocation. Participants were recruited among adolescents receiving inpatient psychosomatic treatment at Schön Klinik Bad Arolsen, Germany, between March and November 2021. No prospective sample-size calculation was performed. The sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients recruited during the study period for whom the required outcome data were available.

Participants and Group Allocation

The initial sample comprised 279 patients. Three patients from the intervention group were excluded from the analyses because of extensive missing data or the absence of intervention-related data, resulting in a final sample of 276 adolescents (intervention group: n = 108; control group: n = 168).

General inclusion criteria were an age of 12-18 years at the start of treatment, informed parental consent, sufficient German language proficiency, and a clinical diagnosis of a mental health disorder, specifically major depressive episode, eating disorder, anxiety disorder, and/or obsessivecompulsive disorder, according to the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10; World Health Organization, 2004). While the registered study protocol specified an upper age limit of 17 years, it was extended to 18 years to reflect the age range of patients treated at the clinic. General exclusion criteria included acute suicidality, schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, bipolar disorder, substance use disorder, organic mental disorder, a current Axis I disorder other than the primary diagnosis, severe physical illness, insufficient physical fitness due to anorexia nervosa, and the need for ongoing therapeutically supervised meals within the clinic.

The individual patient was the unit of allocation. Treating psychotherapists and physicians determined eligibility for AAT according to predefined clinical indications, including promoting mindfulness and self-awareness; enhancing self-esteem and self-efficacy; developing assertiveness and boundary-setting skills; addressing self-image and identity; increasing activation; and strengthening treatment engagement. Patients were excluded from AAT if they exhibited pronounced emotional instability, had a fear of animals, or had animal allergies. Before enrollment, clinicians assessed each patient's physical health and emotional stability to determine suitability for participation in group therapy sessions held outside the clinic. Particular attention was given to the physical health of patients with eating disorders. These assessments were conducted in consultation with the head physician and/or the responsible ward physician.

The control group comprised patients treated during the same period who met the general eligibility criteria but did not participate in AAT. Antidepressant medication status was used as a stratification factor to obtain comparable proportions of medicated and unmedicated patients in the two groups.

Study Procedure

Before participation, patients and their legal guardians provided written informed consent.

Both groups received standard inpatient treatment, including pharmacotherapy, group therapy, sports therapy, art therapy, supervised group meals, mindfulness-based therapy, and other therapeutic services. The intervention group additionally participated in a five-week animal-assisted group psychotherapy program. During the corresponding time slots, the control group received no additional therapy. All patients completed a routine depression assessment at admission (T0) and discharge (T3). Participants in the intervention group completed additional measures at the beginning (T1) and end (T2) of the five-week intervention and brief assessments immediately before (S1) and after (S2) each therapy session (see Figure 1).

Because of the nature of the intervention, participants and the therapist delivering the intervention could not be blinded to group assignment.

The study protocol complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable German legal and regulatory requirements. The study was registered in the Dutch Trial Register and is available via the https://www.onderzoekmetmensen.nl/en/trial/19949. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the legal department of the Schön Klinik. All research methods complied with the relevant ethical and regulatory guidelines, and the study is reported in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of Evaluations with Nonrandomized Designs (TREND) statement.

Intervention

The animal-assisted group psychotherapy was conducted by a child and adolescent psychotherapist in training, who was simultaneously completing a certified program in AAT at an institute accredited by the International Society for Animal Assisted Therapy (ISAAT; https://isaat.org/). The intervention was supported through regular consultation and supervision by a senior physician and the project's medical director. This included ongoing monitoring to ensure adherence to the treatment manual.

The intervention manual was developed based on established group psychotherapy protocols used at Schön Klinik Bad Arolsen and grounded in cognitive behavioral therapy. All hygiene procedures related to animal contact and therapy sessions were implemented in accordance with the clinic's institutional standards. International Association of Human-Animal Interaction Organizations (IAHAIO, 2018) and the

The psychotherapy program aimed to use the potential anxiety-reducing, activating, and antidepressant effects of animal interactions to stabilize and improve patients' mood, enhance intrinsic motivation, promote mindfulness, and strengthen self-efficacy. The program comprised five weekly group sessions of 150 minutes each, conducted on the farm of the therapist delivering the intervention. Groups included a maximum of seven participants, and no minimum group size was required. Each session combined theoretical discussion, practical exercises with dogs and horses, and guided reflection. The theoretical components addressed human-animal relationships, mindfulness, non-verbal communication, body language, personal boundaries, and relationship experiences.

During the practical components, participants completed progressively structured exercises involving observation, communication, mindful leading, and direct interaction with the animals. For example, participants practiced guiding dogs and horses through deliberate body language while peers observed and described their behavior. The therapist provided individualized guidance, and participants reflected on how their posture, communication, and relational behavior influenced the animals and the interaction. Across sessions, the exercises progressed from becoming familiar with the animals and observing non-verbal communication to practicing mindful leadership, reflecting on relationships, and integrating the acquired skills during a group walk. An overview of the content and focus of each session is provided in Table 1.

Animals

The therapy program involved both dogs and horses (including ponies). Six therapy dogs participated in the program. Four belonged to the therapist and lived on the farm, while two belonged to a staff member working there and were present on most days. The dogs were a mix of breeds, including Hovawart/Bernese/Sheepdog, Labrador/Golden Retriever, Samoyed, Labrador mix, and Terrier mix. Ages ranged from 4 to 11 years. Three of the dogs were female (two intact, one neutered), and three were male (two intact, one neutered). All dogs were raised in a group setting, forming a social hierarchy that allowed each to develop a distinct personality. This diversity enabled each patient to connect with a dog that suited their individual needs. All dogs were trained by the therapist and assessed for therapeutic suitability by a veterinarian. Additionally, three horses and two ponies lived on the farm in a large, natural open stable. The horses included an English Warmblood mare, a Zweibrücker mare, and a Haflinger-Quarter-mix gelding, aged between 10 and 22 years. The ponies were Shetland mares aged 4 and 5 years. To ensure animal welfare, all sessions were accompanied by a certified animal welfare practitioner. Animal participation was entirely voluntary; dogs, horses, and ponies were free to leave the sessions at any time.

Measures

Depressive Symptoms

Depressive symptoms were assessed in all participants at clinic admission (T0) and discharge (T3) using the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II; A. T. Beck et al., 1996; Hautzinger et al., 2009) and the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9; Kroenke et al., 2001). In the registered study protocol, the BDI-II and PHQ-9 were designated as primary outcomes, whereas all other measures were designated as secondary outcomes.

The BDI-II is a 21-item self-report measure of cognitive, affective, somatic, and vegetative symptoms of depression in adolescents and adults. Items are rated from 0 to 3, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 63, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. Scores of 0-13 indicate minimal, 14-19 mild, 20-28 moderate, and 29-63 severe depressive symptoms. The BDI-II has demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach's α = .91) and validity (A. T. Beck et al., 1996).

The PHQ-9 is the nine-item depression module of the Patient Health Questionnaire and assesses the frequency of depressive symptoms during the previous two weeks. Items are rated from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 27. Scores of 0-4 indicate minimal, 5-9 mild, 10-14 moderate, 15-19 moderately severe, and 20-27 severe depressive symptoms. A score of 10 or higher is commonly used to indicate clinically relevant depressive symptoms. The PHQ-9 has demonstrated excellent internal consistency (α = .89) and validity (Kroenke et al., 2001).

In the intervention group, depressive symptoms were additionally assessed at the beginning (T1) and end (T2) of the intervention period and immediately before (S1) and after (S2) each session using the German version of the Beck Depression Inventory-Fast Screen (BDI-FS; A. T. Beck et al., 2000). The BDI-FS comprises seven cognitive and affective items derived from the BDI-II. Items are rated from 0 to 3, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating greater depressive symptom severity. Scores of 0-3 indicate minimal, 4-8 mild, 9-12 moderate, and 13-21 severe symptoms. A score of 4 or higher has been proposed as an indicator of clinically relevant symptoms. The BDI-FS has demonstrated good internal consistency (α = .84) and satisfactory discriminant validity (A. T. Beck et al., 2000).

Depressive symptoms in the intervention group were additionally assessed at T1 and T2 using the depression scale of the Beck Youth Inventories-Second Edition (BYI-2; J. S. Beck et al., 2019) and the General Depression Scale-Longform (Allgemeine Depressionsskala-Langform) (ADS-L; Hautzinger et al., 2012). The Beck Depression Inventory for Youth (BDI-Y) is a 20-item self-report scale assessing affective, cognitive, and motivational symptoms of depression in children and adolescents aged 7-18 years. Items are rated from 0 (never) to 3 (always) and summed to obtain a total score ranging from 0 to 60, with higher scores indicating greater depressive symptom severity.

Age-based normative data permit the interpretation of individual scores relative to clinically meaningful ranges. The BDI-Y has demonstrated excellent internal consistency in adolescents (α = .90-.94) and convergent and discriminant validity (J. S. Beck et al., 2019).

The ADS-L is a 20-item self-report measure of affective, cognitive, somatic, and behavioral symptoms of depression during the previous week. Items are rated from 0 (rarely or never) to 3 (most or all of the time), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 60. Higher scores indicate greater depressive symptom severity, and a score of 23 or higher is commonly used to indicate clinically relevant symptoms. The ADS-L has demonstrated good internal consistency (α = .82-.88) and validity in clinical and research settings (Hautzinger et al., 2012).

Self-esteem and Self-concept

Self-esteem in the intervention group was assessed at T1 and T2 using the Multidimensionale Selbstwertskala (MSWS; Schütz et al., 2016), the German adaptation of the Multidimensional Self-Concept Scale (Fleming & Courtney, 1984). The MSWS comprises 32 items across six subscales: emotional self-esteem (ESWS), social self-esteem related to interpersonal contact (SWKO), social self-esteem related to criticism (SWKR), performance-related self-esteem (LSWS), perceived physical attractiveness (SWPA), and perceived athletic competence (SWSP). Items are rated on seven-point scales, with higher scores indicating higher self-esteem. All scale scores were calculated as mean scores (ranging from 1.00 and 7.00), with higher scores indicating higher levels of selfesteem. The higher-order Social Self-Esteem scale (SSW) was calculated by combining the items from the SWKO and SWKR subscales. The higher-order Body-Related Self-Esteem (KSW) was calculated by combining the items from the SWPA and SWSP subscales. In the present sample, internal consistency ranged from α = .76 to .93 at T1 and from α = .79 to .93 at T2, indicating acceptable to excellent reliability.

Self-concept was assessed at T1 and T2 using the Beck Self-Concept Inventory for Youth (BSCI-Y) from the BYI-2 (J. S. Beck et al., 2019). The BSCI-Y comprises 20 items assessing positive self-perceptions, such as pride, competence, and perceived personal strengths. Items are rated from 0 (never) to 3 (always), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 60 with higher total scores indicating a more positive self-concept. The scale has demonstrated good internal consistency in adolescents (α = .81-.87) and convergent and discriminant validity (J. S. Beck et al., 2019).

Mindfulness

Mindfulness in the intervention group was assessed at the beginning (T1) and end (T2) of the intervention period, as well as immediately before (S1) and after (S2) each session, using the German version of the Freiburg Mindfulness Inventory (Freiburger Fragebogen zur Achtsamkeit; FFA; Walach et al., 2006). The 30-item FFA assesses mindful awareness and acceptance of presentmoment experiences, including attention to current experience, nonjudgmental acceptance, and openness toward internal states. Items are rated on a scale from 1 (rarely) to 4 (almost always), yielding a total score ranging from 30 to 120, with higher total scores indicating greater mindfulness.

The scale has demonstrated excellent internal consistency (α = .93) and construct validity in both meditating and nonmeditating samples (Walach et al., 2006).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.1 (R Core Team, 2025). Model assumptions were evaluated graphically using residual-versus-fitted plots, scale-location plots, and normal Q-Q plots of the conditional residuals. No substantial violations of the model assumptions were identified. All models converged without warnings and showed no evidence of singularity.

Outliers were assessed using both Z-score transformation (|z| > 3.00) and visual inspection of boxplots. Only a very small proportion of data points were identified as outliers (0.24% using the Zscore criterion and 1.08% using the boxplot/IQR criterion). As all identified values fell within the expected and interpretable range of the respective questionnaire scales, no observations were excluded or modified. Missing item-level responses occurred only in the MSWS. For this questionnaire, missing responses were replaced using person-mean imputation when at least 50% of the items within the respective scale were available. Overall, 0.4% of MSWS item-level responses were imputed.

The individual patient was the unit of analysis. Repeated measurements were nested within patients and accounted for using linear mixed-effects models fitted with the lmer() function from the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015). Models were estimated using restricted maximum likelihood. Separate models were fitted for each outcome. Primary analyses were conducted following the intention-to-treat principle, with participants analyzed in their assigned condition regardless of intervention attendance. In addition, sensitivity analyses were performed including only participants who attended at least three animal-assisted group psychotherapy sessions to assess whether the findings remained consistent after excluding participants with only limited intervention exposure.

To examine immediate changes in depressive symptoms and mindfulness within individual AAT sessions (RQ1), separate linear mixed-effects models were estimated for each outcome. Fixed effects included within-session assessment point (pre-session vs. post-session), session number (sessions 1-5, reference session 1), and their interaction. The interaction tested whether the magnitude of the within-session pre-post change differed across sessions. Session number was modelled as a categorical factor, allowing a separate pre-post difference to be estimated for each session without assuming a linear trend across sessions. Primary models adjusted for age and gender, and sensitivity models additionally adjusted for length of clinic stay. Random intercepts were specified for participants and for sessions nested within participants to account for repeated sessions and the dependence between the pre-and post-session assessments. Estimated marginal means were calculated using the emmeans package (Lenth, 2024), and pre-post contrasts were estimated separately for each session. The resulting p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni method across the five session-specific pre-post contrasts for each outcome.

To examine changes in depressive symptoms, self-esteem, self-concept, and mindfulness across the intervention period (RQ2), separate linear mixed-effects models were estimated within the intervention group. Assessment time (pre-intervention vs. post-intervention) was included as a fixed effect, and a participant-specific random intercept accounted for repeated observations. Primary models adjusted for age and gender, and sensitivity models additionally adjusted for length of clinic stay. Exploratory models included the number of sessions attended and its interaction with assessment time to examine whether pre-post changes were associated with intervention attendance.

None of the primary models showed a singular fit, and intraclass correlations ranged from .69 to .89, supporting participant-specific random intercepts.

To compare changes in depressive symptoms across the full inpatient stay (RQ3), separate linear mixed-effects models were estimated for the BDI-II and PHQ-9. Fixed effects included assessment time (clinic admission vs. discharge), group (intervention vs. control), and the time × group interaction. Primary models adjusted for age and gender, and sensitivity models additionally adjusted for length of clinic stay. Additional exploratory models replaced group with the total number of AAT sessions attended, coded as zero for control participants. These models included assessment time, number of sessions attended, and their interaction and adjusted for age and gender.

The assessment time × session attendance interaction tested whether admission-to-discharge changes in depressive symptoms were associated with the number of sessions attended. Unstandardized model coefficients (𝑏𝑏) were used as effect-size estimates and are reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The significance level was set at 𝛼𝛼 = .05.

Results

Sample characteristics and intervention implementation

Descriptive statistics for demographic, clinical, and outcome variables are presented in Table 2. The intervention group comprised 108 adolescents with a mean age of 15.84 years (SD = 1.26), and the control group comprised 168 adolescents with a mean age of 15.49 years (SD = 1.41).

Intervention implementation and attendance

The intervention was successfully implemented in the routine inpatient setting. Based on regular consultation and supervision, no major deviations from the treatment manual were reported.

No adverse events or unintended effects related to the animal-assisted intervention were observed or reported. No corresponding adverse events were recorded in the control group. One participant attended six sessions because one of the five standardized session modules was attended twice, all other participants attended a maximum of five sessions. This participant was excluded from the session-level analyses.

Session-specific pre-post changes within the intervention group

Regarding RQ1, we examined whether depressive symptoms decreased and mindfulness increased immediately from pre-to post-session and whether the magnitude of these changes differed across sessions. Session-specific contrasts based on estimated marginal means showed that depressive symptoms (BDI-FS) were significantly lower after each of Sessions 1-5, with estimated reductions ranging from 2.53 to 3.04 points (all p < .001; Table 3). None of the assessment point-by-session interaction terms was significant (all p ≥ .482), providing no evidence that the magnitude of the reduction differed across sessions.

Mindfulness scores (FFA) were significantly higher after each of Sessions 1-5, with estimated increases ranging from 4.88 to 6.36 points (Sessions 1-4: all p < .001; Session 5: p = .001; Table 3). None of the assessment point-by-session interaction terms was significant (all p ≥ .585), indicating no evidence that the magnitude of the increase differed across sessions. Age and gender were not significantly associated with BDI-FS (age: p = .429; gender: p = .860) or FFA scores (age: p = .181; gender: p = .351). Sensitivity analyses additionally adjusting for length of clinic stay yielded the same pattern of significant session-specific contrasts, and length of clinic stay was not significantly associated with BDI-FS (p = .571) or FFA scores (p = .664; Supplementary Table S1).

Discussion

The present study examined outcomes associated with a five-week animal-assisted group psychotherapy program delivered alongside standard multimodal inpatient treatment for adolescents with mental health disorders across three assessment intervals. The first hypothesis, which predicted immediate decreases in depressive symptoms and increases in mindfulness from pre-to post-session, was supported, with significant changes observed in all five sessions. The second hypothesis, which predicted decreases in depressive symptoms and increases in self-esteem, self-concept, and mindfulness across the intervention period of five weeks within the intervention group, was also supported. Hypothesis 3, which predicted greater admission-to-discharge reductions in depressive symptoms in the intervention group than in the control group, was not supported. Thus, the changes observed during individual sessions and across the intervention period were not reflected in a detectable additional reduction in depressive symptoms relative to standard inpatient treatment alone.

The session-level findings provide the most direct insight into adolescents' immediate responses to individual AAT sessions. Depressive symptoms decreased and mindfulness increased from pre-to post-session in each of the five sessions. The repeated reductions in depressive symptoms and increases in mindfulness across the sessions suggest a relatively consistent pattern of short-term change associated with session participation. The reductions in depressive symptoms converge with previous studies reporting immediate improvements in mood and reductions in subjective distress following therapy-dog activities in psychiatric and pediatric behavioral-health settings (Brown et al., 2020; Fodstad et al., 2019). However, these earlier studies did not examine session-specific changes across a standardized multi-session intervention. The present study extends this evidence by repeatedly assessing depressive symptoms before and after five structured therapy sessions in a clinical adolescent sample.

The immediate increases in mindfulness represent a particularly novel finding. Trujillo et al. (2020) incorporated a therapy dog into mindfulness and communication exercises for adolescents with psychiatric and substance use disorders. However, that study assessed treatment attendance, school engagement, and general well-being rather than mindfulness itself. Similarly, Künzi et al. (2022) evaluated animal-assisted psychotherapy incorporating mindfulness and self-compassion among adults undergoing neurorehabilitation. Although mindfulness itself was not assessed as an outcome, participants in the animal-assisted condition showed greater immediate improvements in several emotional states, including feeling secure, accepted, motivated, and at ease, than those receiving the same intervention without animal assistance. Direct evidence regarding changes in mindfulness has mainly been obtained from adults with recurrent depression. In a small pilot study, Schramm et al. (2015) reported improvements in overall mindfulness skills following nature-and animal-assisted mindfulness training. However, a subsequent randomized trial found benefits for relapse prevention but no additional improvements in mindfulness compared with usual care (Schramm et al., 2022). The present findings therefore provide direct evidence that mindfulness may increase immediately following AAT sessions among adolescents.

Several processes might contribute to these immediate changes. Animals may direct attention toward sensory, emotional, and relational cues and provide nonverbal feedback to adolescents' behavior, body language, and emotional state. The experiential nature of the sessions may therefore temporarily interrupt maladaptive thought patterns and facilitate greater engagement with presentmoment experience. Such proximal changes may be clinically relevant if they increase emotional readiness, motivation, or engagement in subsequent therapeutic activities.

Consistent with our second hypothesis, the intervention group showed significant improvements across several psychological outcomes over the five-week intervention period.

Depressive symptoms decreased, while self-esteem, self-concept, and mindfulness increased. The reductions in depressive symptoms are consistent with previous controlled studies. Frederick et al. (2015) found greater reductions in depression following a five-week equine-assisted learning program than following treatment as usual among at-risk adolescents. Muela et al. (2017) similarly reported greater reductions in depression and sense of inadequacy among adolescents receiving animal-assisted psychotherapy compared with those receiving usual residential care without AAT.

Evidence from adult populations also suggests potential beneficial effects of animal-assisted interventions on depression, although substantial heterogeneity in populations, interventions, and study quality limits conclusions (Hediger et al., 2021; Souter & Miller, 2007). The present findings extend this literature to adolescents receiving intensive inpatient psychiatric treatment, a novel finding.

Our findings supporting improvements in self-esteem and self-concept add to a limited and mixed evidence base. Trotter et al. (2008) reported significantly greater improvements in self-esteem among at-risk youths receiving equine-assisted counseling than among those receiving classroombased counseling. In contrast, Hauge et al. (2014) found no significant between-group differences in self-esteem following a four-month equine-assisted intervention, while Bachi et al. (2012) found no clear treatment-specific improvement in self-image relative to the comparison group among adolescents in residential care. More recently, Rodrigo-Claverol et al. (2026) compared a structured therapeutic activity conducted with a therapy dog with the corresponding activity conducted without a dog among adolescent psychiatric inpatients. The animal-assisted group showed a small but significantly greater improvement in self-efficacy. Although self-efficacy is conceptually distinct from self-esteem and self-concept, this finding provides related evidence that AAT may support adolescents' perceptions of their own competence.

The improvements across multiple dimensions of self-esteem and self-concept are clinically relevant because negative self-evaluation is closely associated with adolescent depression and may contribute to its persistence (Hards et al., 2020; Orchard et al., 2021). The intervention in this study included experiential exercises involving communication, body language, boundary setting, and guiding animals. Successfully interacting with or influencing an animal may provide concrete experiences of competence, agency, and self-efficacy, which could contribute to more positive selfevaluations. An important contribution of the present study is the simultaneous assessment of depressive symptoms, multidimensional self-esteem, self-concept, and mindfulness, which provides a broader picture of changes during the intervention than studies focusing only on general symptoms or functioning.

Depressive symptoms decreased substantially from admission to discharge in both groups, but, contrary to our third hypothesis, the improvement was not significantly greater in the intervention group than in the control group. Thus, the controlled comparison provided no evidence that animalassisted group psychotherapy produced additional improvement in depressive symptoms beyond standard inpatient treatment. This finding should be considered within the context of the intensive multimodal treatment received by both groups. Moreover, the admission-to-discharge assessments captured overall changes in depressive symptoms across the entire inpatient stay, potentially obscuring short-term improvements associated with individual AAT sessions. Reporting these nonsignificant findings contributes to a more balanced understanding of the potential benefits and limitations of AAT and strengthens the broader evidence base, particularly given the methodological rigor of the present study.

The absence of an additional effect on depressive symptoms is consistent with the mixed evidence concerning small sample studies on animal-assisted interventions. Small randomized studies involving child and adolescent psychiatric inpatients have reported improvements in internalizing symptoms, competence, and global functioning following animal-assisted therapy compared with standard treatment (Stefanini et al., 2015 (Stefanini et al., , 2016)). Conversely, other controlled studies have found no significant between-group differences for relevant psychological outcomes, such as depression or anxiety (Berget et al., 2011; Rodrigo-Claverol et al., 2026). These differences may partly reflect variations in study design, sample characteristics, and outcome measures. Stefanini et al. (2015 Stefanini et al. ( , 2016) ) used small inpatient samples and assessed broader outcomes, whereas the present study included a substantially larger sample and focused specifically on depressive symptoms. The findings are not necessarily contradictory: AAT may be associated with improvements in broader aspects of functioning or internalizing symptoms without an additional effect on depressive symptoms specifically. The absence of an overall additional effect may also indicate that not all adolescents benefit equally. Future research is needed to identify which patients are most likely to benefit from AAT as an adjunct to standard care.

Strengths and Limitations

The study has several strengths. Its comparatively large clinical sample allowed intervention outcomes to be examined with greater precision than in many previous animal-assisted intervention studies. Conducting the study in a naturalistic inpatient setting enhanced its clinical relevance.

Efforts to balance the intervention and control groups with respect to antidepressant medication use helped reduce potential medication-related confounding. Multiple validated measures of depression were used, and depressive symptoms, self-esteem, self-concept, and mindfulness were assessed simultaneously during the intervention period. This enabled the examination of both psychopathological symptoms and positive psychological resources. The study combined a comparison-group analysis across the full inpatient stay with repeated within-intervention-group assessments across the five-week intervention period and individual therapy sessions. In particular, the session-level assessments provided information about immediate changes that would have been obscured by relying solely on admission and discharge assessments. Finally, the intention-to-treat analyses and sensitivity analyses based on intervention attendance generally supported the robustness of the main findings.

Several limitations must also be considered. First, participants were not randomly assigned to conditions. This approach was chosen to ensure that the intervention was clinically relevant and could be delivered safely within routine inpatient care. Although it strengthens the ecological validity of the study, it creates a risk of selection bias and confounding by indication. The comparable baseline levels of depressive symptoms and the balancing of antidepressant medication use across conditions may reduce this concern. Second, although validated self-report instruments and standardized assessment procedures supported data quality, responses may have been influenced by treatment expectations. Including clinician ratings, behavioral observations, or physiological indicators of stress would provide a more comprehensive assessment. Third, generalizability may be limited by the single-site, predominantly female inpatient sample and the specific farm-based intervention. The findings may therefore not apply to other populations, settings, or intervention formats, and the absence of follow-up precludes conclusions about longer-term effects.

Implications and conclusions

AAT may support proximal therapeutic processes, with immediate reductions in depressive symptoms and increases in mindfulness potentially facilitating adolescents' readiness and engagement in subsequent therapeutic activities, particularly among those who initially struggle to engage in conventional psychotherapy. Clinically, this study suggests that AAT sessions could be scheduled before demanding individual or group interventions. This work also demonstrates that repeated session-level assessments may capture changes overlooked by conventional pre-post designs and as such could be incorporated into treatment and assessment protocols. That said, although AAT was associated with improvements in depressive symptoms, self-esteem, self-concept, and mindfulness across the intervention period, it did not yield significantly greater reductions in depressive symptoms from admission to discharge than standard inpatient treatment. These findings suggest that AAT's benefits may be most evident at the session level rather than in overall treatment outcomes. Future randomized controlled trials with these understudied inpatient populations are needed to strengthen causal inference.
