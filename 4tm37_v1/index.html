Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Psychological traits and social identities have been linked to misinformation sharing, but most studies measure passive or low-effort engagement. Less is known about high-effort engagement that requires users to write a response. We examined whether cognitive reflection, motivated reasoning variables (political orientation, religiosity, trust in medical scientists) and personality traits are differentially associated with liking, retweeting, replying to, and quoting COVID-19 misinformation and public-health-unaligned tweets. Methods US MTurk participants (N = 858; September 2021) engaged with real COVID-19 tweets in a simulated Twitter feed. Replies and quotes were manually coded as endorsive or combative. We fit OLS regressions for each engagement type (N = 851) and estimated relative importance using Shapley (LMG) decomposition of R².

Endorsive replies and quotes were rare (7.3% and 4.3% of participants for misinformation). Political conservatism and religiosity were associated with endorsive misinformation replies, and motivated reasoning variables were the largest contributors in all reply and quote models, though these models explained little variance (adjusted R² ≤ .03). Cognitive reflection was negatively associated only with liking, and grandiose narcissism was the largest contributor to retweeting. Conclusion Classical reasoning, personality and motivated reasoning explanations of misinformation propagation were each supported, depending on the type of engagement. Simulation tasks that measure multiple engagement behaviors can help clarify inconsistent findings in the misinformation literature.

Introduction

Circulation of misinformation on social media has become a critical concern, especially during public health crises like the COVID-19 pandemic. Misinformation is defined as unverified rumors and false claims that spread widely and deceive audiences (Kumar & Geethakumari, 2014). In recent years, misinformation is largely spread through platforms like Twitter and Facebook (Houston et al., 2015; Simon et al., 2015) and the overabundance of misinformation can complicate public health responses, as seen with the COVID-19 pandemic (Romer & Jamieson, 2021). Previous work reports that misinformation and fake news stories on Twitter propagate significantly farther, faster, and more broadly than true stories (Vosoughi et al., 2018). During crisis events, the volume of misinformation often exceeds the corrections (Haupt, Li, et al., 2021; Starbird et al., 2014). The prevalence and speed of misinformation propagation during global events highlights the need to understand the variety of ways people can engage with misinformation. Recent studies show that distinguishing types of online behaviors can provide nuanced insights into propagation dynamics. Fang et al. (2022) report that on Twitter, different engagement behaviors such as liking, retweeting, replying and quoting all differ in effort in both input and output mechanisms. For instance, liking and retweeting require no original content from the user, while replying and quoting both require additional original user input (Fang et al., 2022).

Liking is also a more passive form of propagation since it does not produce new tweets on the user's profile (Fang et al., 2022). Haupt et al. (2024) further show that user characteristics are associated with different types of engagement tendencies: users higher in cognitive reflective thinking (CRT) were less likely to like a misinformation post while those higher in grandiose narcissism and religiosity had a higher tendency to retweet misinformation. While Haupt et al. (2024) examine how factors related to classical reasoning, motivated reasoning and personality traits are associated with passive engagement (i.e., liking) and low-effort active engagement (i.e., retweeting), there is currently no work examining how these factors may be associated with high-effort active engagement types (i.e., replying and quoting). Therefore, the current study will build on the findings from Haupt et al. (2024) by identifying factors that are most predictive of high-effort engagement behaviors.

In addition to misinformation, we also examine engagement with posts that express unsafe public health practices with regard to COVID-19, even if they do not make an explicit false claim. We classify these tweets as public health guideline "Unaligned" (hereby referred to as just "Unaligned") if they did not contain misinformation but still expressed sentiment that was not aligned with public health guidelines. Unlike misinformation, which contains a discrete, verifiably false claim, unaligned tweets do not make falsifiable factual assertions; instead, they express skepticism, dismissiveness, or opinion-based rhetoric which promoted harmful practices during the pandemic. Examples of guideline unaligned tweets include expressing negative stances towards wearing a mask, toward vaccines, or a positive stance towards using hydroxychloroquine to treat COVID-19. This distinction follows broader work cautioning that not all content contributing to public misperception is straightforwardly false or fabricated but can still promote distrust in public health guidance through framing, selective emphasis, or misguided opinions (Jack, 2017). In this study, we include unaligned tweets alongside misinformation tweets because both may function similarly in shaping attitudes and engagement behavior on social media, even though only misinformation involves a verifiably false claim.

Classical Reasoning

The present study will extend findings from previous work (Haupt et al. 2024) to assess how factors related to classical reasoning, motivated reasoning, and personality traits are associated with high effort engagement behaviors (i.e., replying, quoting). One widely used explanation of misinformation spread is the classical reasoning, or "inattention," account, which holds that people share false content unintentionally, due to insufficient careful thought and relevant knowledge (Pennycook et al., 2016; Pennycook & Rand, 2019 , 2021).

People simply lack time or mental energy to carefully evaluate the sheer volume of content on social media, thus relying on 'heuristics' (or mental shortcuts) and 'illusory truths' (encountering the same information repeatedly can be judged as true), which in turn contributes to misinformation propagation (van der Linden, 2022).

The Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT) is a widely used measure to assess analytical thinking skills (Frederick, 2005). While previous research has shown negative associations with CRT and misinformation engagement (i.e.https://paperpile.com/c/yoECXc/HXFCX+zgDAB+oftmF liking, retweeting) (Haupt et al., 2024; Pennycook & Rand, 2019 , 2021) , some studies determined that higher CRT mitigates misinformation only in liberal political identities, while not affecting conservative identities' tendency to spread misinformation (Bayrak et al., 2025). Similarly, another study found that political bias influenced how CRT affected misinformation engagement, but only with hard news (news that is factual and policy relevant), and not soft news (news that is more informal, and entertainment-oriented) (Lois et al., 2026). Thus, these studies suggest that misinformation spread may be due to an interplay between the classical reasoning and motivated reasoning (discussed below) frameworks. However, to our knowledge, there is currently no work that separately examines this effect for high effort engagement behaviors that require writing original responses. Considering that both high CRT and high-effort active engagements require deliberate reflection (Pennycook et al.https://paperpile.com/c/yoECXc/75Vxw 2016) , users engaging actively have more time to discern the veracity of the tweet. Thus, we propose the following hypotheses which extend findings reported in Haupt et al. (2024) by examining high effort engagement with misinformation (replying and quoting) and engagement with unaligned sentiment. We further distinguished whether a reply/quote endorsed the misinformation or was in opposition.

H1: CRT is negatively associated with higher unaligned sentiment engagement (liking, retweeting).

H2: CRT is negatively associated with high effort endorsements of misinformation and unaligned sentiment engagement (replies, quotes).

Motivated Reasoning

Another explanation for how misinformation spreads is the motivated reasoning account.

This view holds that people evaluate information according to pre-existing goals, choosing to endorse and share content that fits those goals (Kahan, 2015; van der Linden, 2022). Such goals might include protecting one's self-image (Dunninghttps://paperpile.com/c/yoECXc/3rgBD 2003) https://paperpile.com/c/yoECXc/Xh6gV avoiding the discomfort of unwelcome news (Dawson et al., 2002) , or justifying self-serving behavior (Hsee, 1996).

Further, social media users may be influenced by the filter bubble created by their engagement behaviours, they bring their political influences and beliefs to scientific information (Rhodes, 2022; Spohr, 2017). As a result, social media users may be inclined to share posts that are factually wrong but consistent with their beliefs and group identities, while disregarding posts that contradict them (Weeks, 2015).

Social factors like political orientation and identity-based motivations (religiosity)

further influence misinformation engagement and propagation. For politically motivated reasoning, the underlying goal is thought to be safeguarding one's identity, since people adopt beliefs that preserve their ties to a social group whose values they share (Kahan, 2015). Supporting this, studies show that people's political identities shape how readily they detect misinformation on divisive topics (Kahan et al., 2017), as well as how willing they are to share fake political news (Osmundsen et al., 2021). Studies confirm that individuals tend to accept information that aligns with their political beliefs and scrutinize or reject information that contradicts their already existing beliefs (Weeks, 2015). For misinformation, this means partisan users might share a false claim that confirms their side's narrative while dismissing fact-based corrections from the opposing side. In the UShttps://paperpile.com/c/yoECXc/udFdY where the COVID-19 pandemic quickly became a polarizing political issue (Haupt, Jinich-Diamant, et al., 2021) , conservatives were more likely to oppose public health guidelines (Haupt et al., 2024; Lawson & Kakkar, 2022; Pereira et al., 2018; Romer & Jamieson, 2021; Van Bavel et al., 2021).

According to the motivated reasoning account, conservatives may engage with COVIDrelated misinformation because the content resonates with their group identity. Bayrak et al. 's (2025), and Lois et al. 's (2026) accounts of how CRT varies with political ideology and type of news also align with how motivated reasoning affects misinformation propagation, through mediating classical reasoning framework. Thushttps://paperpile.com/c/yoECXc/d9e41+HXFCX+xxcVj based on how conservatism has been linked to misinformation propagation (Haupt et al., 2024; Lawson & Kakkar, 2022; Romer & Jamieson, 2021) , we hypothesize that politically motivated conservative individuals may readily and actively propagate misinformation.

H3: Political conservatism is positively associated with higher unaligned sentiment engagement (liking, retweeting).

H4: Political conservatism is positively associated with high effort endorsements of misinformation and unaligned sentiment engagement (replies, quotes).

In addition to political identity, other social factors could evoke motivated reasoning.

Trust in information sources is one such factor: if people fundamentally distrust authorities or medical experts, they may be more inclined to believe fake news and misinformation or conspiracy theories. During the COVID-19 pandemic, for instance, individuals with low trust in medical scientists and public health institutions were observed to be more susceptible to conspiracy theories about the virus (Hunt et al., 2022). Conversely, higher trust in scientists and experts might protect people against health misinformation by making them more likely to seek confirmation from credible sources. We believe that this trust can influence active engagement with misinformation.

H5: Trust in medical scientists is negatively associated with higher unaligned sentiment engagement (liking, retweeting).

H6: Trust in medical scientists is negatively associated with high effort endorsements of misinformation and unaligned sentiment engagement (replies, quotes).

Religiosity can also influence misinformation engagement (Bronstein et al., 2019; Haupt et al., 2024; Lee Rogers & Powe, 2022). Strong religious or moral worldviews might predispose individuals toward certain misinformed beliefs (for example, preferring faithbased explanations or distrust of secular authorities), although these relationships are complex and under-studied (Bronstein et al., 2019; Lee Rogers & Powe, 2022). A study finds that strong religious beliefs were tied to COVID-19 misperceptions (Druckman et al., 2021).

Moreoverhttps://paperpile.com/c/yoECXc/TkPui research in the US has shown that higher religious belief (specifically Christianity) is associated with greater skepticism toward science (Azevedo & Jost, 2021) , and higher conspiracy thinking and belief in misinformation (Bronstein et al., 2019; Frenken et al., 2023). Thus, we posit that highly religious individuals may be more actively propagating misinformation, spreading their beliefs within their communities and on social media. H7: Religiosity is positively associated with higher unaligned sentiment engagement (liking, retweeting).

H8: Religiosity is positively associated with high effort endorsements of misinformation and unaligned sentiment engagement (replies, quotes).

Personality Traits

Recent research suggests that many psychological factors influence the likelihood of sharing misinformation. For example, factors like empathy -encompassing both cognitive perspective-taking and emotional concern -may act as a buffer against spreading misinformation on social media (Hangartner et al., 2021; Haupt et al., 2024; Moore-Berg et al., 2022). Individuals high in empathy may be less likely to like or share false content on platforms like Twitter since they are more likely to consider the potential harm misinformation could inflict on others. Similarly, interventions that evoke empathy, such as empathetic messaging, can correct mistaken beliefs and even curb harmful online content (Hangartner et al., 2021; Moore-Berg et al., 2022). These findings suggest that users with strong perspective-taking abilities and compassionate concern are less inclined to engage with misinformation (Haupt et al.https://paperpile.com/c/yoECXc/HXFCX 2024) , which can further extend to high effort engagement.

H9: Empathy is negatively associated with higher unaligned sentiment engagement (liking, retweeting).

H10: Empathy is negatively associated with high effort endorsements of misinformation and unaligned sentiment engagement (replies, quotes). Narcissism-both grandiose and covert (vulnerable) variants -have been linked to greater engagement with misinformation on social media. Both types share entitlement and self-focus, but differ mainly in neuroticism, extraversion and insecurity (https://paperpile.com/c/yoECXc/HS9I+4n2r. Kaufman et al., 2020; Miller et al., 2011). Grandiose narcissism is characterized by an inflated self-image, entitlement, and low regard for others, and it is associated with heavy social media activity and higher belief in conspiracy theories (Gnambs & Appel, 2018; Sternisko et al., 2023). This could indicate an increased propensity to spread misinformation: for instance, Haupt et al.'s (2024) study found that users high in grandiose narcissism were significantly more likely to retweet false or misleading posts (Haupt et al., 2024). Grandiose narcissists may share misinformation to attract attention, bolster their ego, or reinforce narratives that flatter their worldview (Haupt et al., 2024; Sternisko et al., 2023). Covert (vulnerable) narcissism, marked by insecurity, hypersensitivity, and self-victimisation (Dickinson & Pincus, 2003; Fegan & Bland, 2021; Wink, 1991) also appears conducive to misinformation engagement (Cosgrove & Murphy, 2023; Kay, 2021). Those higher in covert narcissism prefer online interactions over in-person interactions, and report more problematic social network use than those higher in grandiose narcissism (Casale et al., 2016; Musetti et al., 2022).

Oversensitivity, a common experience in covert narcissism, is also associated with greater concerns about others' comments and reactions online (Fegan & Bland, 2021). Research during the COVID-19 pandemic found that narcissistic individuals showed lower compliance with public health guidelines and a preference for conspiracy explanations and COVID-19 conspiracy theories (Sternisko et al., 2023). Thus, narcissistic traits tend to increase one's likelihood of engaging with or amplifying misinformation on social networks, as these individuals are more driven by personal or ideological gratification (Haupt et al., 2024; Sternisko et al., 2023). To build on the findings from Haupt et al. (2024), we hypothesize: H11: Narcissism (Grandiose and Covert) is positively associated with higher unaligned sentiment engagement (liking, retweeting).

H12: Narcissism (Grandiose and Covert) is positively associated with high effort endorsements of misinformation and unaligned sentiment engagement (replies, quotes).

In contrast, those higher in openness, a Big Five Inventory (BFI) personality trait, have been shown to be less likely to take information at "face value" and may be better at discerning false from true content (Lawson & Kakkar, 2022; Sindermann et al., 2020). Other research indicates that actively open-minded thinking correlates with lower susceptibility to fake news (Munusamy et al., 2024). However, some studies find that low cognitive reflectiveness moderates higher openness into sharing more misinformation (Ahmed & Tan, 2022). Conscientiousness, another BFI personality trait reflecting self-discipline, caution, and deliberation, has consistently been associated with lower engagement with misinformation online: highly conscientious social media users are more likely to verify information and consider consequences before acting, making them less prone to like or share false news (Lawson & Kakkar, 2022; Sindermann et al., 2020). Empirical studies support this buffering effect: for example, people higher in conscientiousness were found to share significantly fewer fake news stories during the COVID-19 pandemic, especially when compared to their less conscientious counterparts (Lawson & Kakkar, 2022). In Haupt et al.'s (2024) study, conscientious individuals showed a reduced tendency to passively endorse misinformation (e.g., by liking posts), though their rate of actively retweeting false content was not significantly lower, suggesting that conscientiousness mainly curbs passive engagement with misinformation (Haupt et al., 2024). Thus, based on previous research, we hypothesize that open individuals are more likely to communicate their beliefs through active engagement, and are less susceptible to misinformation, leading to less endorsive active engagement with misinformation. Similarly, conscientious individuals who are less susceptible to misinformation would show lower endorsive active engagement with misinformation.

Present Study

Though prior work suggests various potential predictors of misinformation engagement and propagation, the relative contributions of these factors have not been studied together extensively. The present research aims to fill this gap by systematically investigating individual factors (e.g., classical reasoning, motivated reasoning, psychological traits) associated with different types of engagement behaviors on social media (e.g., reply). The present study is an extension of (Haupt et al.https://paperpile.com/c/yoECXc/HXFCX 2024) , which examines the influence of individual characteristics on liking and retweeting misinformation. By measuring different engagement behaviors in the same participants, we also assess whether the main explanations for misinformation propagation (classical reasoning, motivated reasoning and personality traits) apply to different types of engagement. Methodology

Participants and Procedure

Data was collected in prior work, which tested how the factors examined in the present study were associated with low effort and passive engagement types (i.e., retweeting, liking) (Haupt et al., 2024). The present study expands on this work by examining more active forms of misinformation engagement (i.e., replying, quoting). Participants (N = 858) were recruited via Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) during September 2021 and were compensated based on standard survey-taking rates. The survey was administered via Qualtrics and divided into two primary sections. In the first section, participants were presented with a series of real tweets on controversial topics related to COVID-19, including the COVID-19 vaccine, mask usage, and hydroxychloroquine (Table 2). The subset of tweets used for this study included Unaligned tweets (opinion-based negative attitudes to COVID-19 public health recommendations) or COVID-19 Misinformation tweets. As shown in Figure 1, participants were instructed to interact with each tweet as if they were using Twitter by selecting one or more of the following options: "Like," "Reply," "Retweet," or "Quote Tweet." Input text responses were required for "Reply" and "Quote Tweet" selections. This design aimed to simulate real-world engagement behavior with social media content. We further manually categorised replying and quoting as endorsive (positive sentiment/ agreeing with original tweet) vs non-endorsive (negative sentiment/disagreeing with original tweet) replies or quotes, with endorsive/affirmative interactions suggesting misinformation propagation, while non-endorsive interactions were actually corrective in nature, and minimized misinformation propagation (and are therefore not explored in this paper). Each reply and quote was coded separately for each tweet, and the endorsive reply and quote outcomes are the number of replies (or quotes) to misinformation or Unaligned tweets that were coded as endorsive. Following this simulated newsfeed exercise, participants completed a battery of questionnaires assessing individual traits and attitudes (Haupt et al., 2024). Only relevant measures included in this study are described below.

Cognitive Reflection

Cognitive Reflective Thinking (a measure of classical reasoning) was assessed using the three-item Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT) (α = 0.79) (Blacksmith et al., 2019; Frederick, 2005). These word problems required participants to override common heuristics in thinking in favor of more deliberate, reflective answers (e.g., the classic "bat and ball" problem). This is scored on a scale of 0 to 3, with 0 being the minimum, where no questions are correctly answered, followed by 1, 2 and 3 for the number of questions correctly answered respectively.

Political Ideology

Participants self-reported their political orientation on a 6-point scale from1 = Very Conservative to6 = Very Liberal. For analysis, higher scores indicate more liberal ideologies.

Trust in Medical Services

Trust was assessed using a single-item measure (from a 4-item scale adapted from the 2019 Pew Research Center's American Trends Panel survey (Funk et al., 2019)) embedded within a broader "Trust in Institutions" scale from the Pew survey. Participants were asked how much confidence they had in medical scientists to act in the best interests of the public.

Responses were given on a 4-point Likert scale:1 = No confidence at all,2 = Not too much,3 = A fair amount, and4 = A great deal.

Religiosity

Religiosity was measured using the Centrality of Religiosity Scale (Huber & Huber, 2012) which had 7 items that assessed participants' religious affiliation, frequency of religious practices (e.g., prayer, meditation), and experiences of spiritual connection (α = 0.87). Response formats varied by item type but generally used a Likert-type scale.

Empathy

Empathy was measured using a series of items adapted from the Interpersonal Reactivity Index. Perspective-taking (PT) empathy refers to the capacity to shift one's perspective to others' intentions, goals, and motives (Frith & Frith, 2005; Stietz et al., 2019).

Emotional-concern (EC) refers to emotions that are elicited by the perceived emotional wellbeing of others (Batson, 2009). Participants responded to 14 items on a 5-point scale ranging from0 = "Does not describe me well" to4 = "Describes me well." Both the PT (α=0.60) and EC (α = 0.71) subscales were taken directly from the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (Davis, 1983). For analysis, the two subscales were averaged into a single empathy score (range 0-4).

Narcissism

Two variants of narcissism were assessed:

Grandiose Narcissism. was measured using the Narcissism Personality Inventory (NPI) (Ames et al., 2006) (α = 0.81), a forced-choice format in which participants selected one statement from each of 16 pairs that best described them (e.g., "I am an extraordinary person" vs. "I am much like everybody else").

Covert Narcissism.

was assessed using the 10-item Hypersensitive Narcissism Scale (Hendin & Cheek, 1997) (α = 0.89), where participants indicated how characteristic each statement was of their feelings and behavior on a 5-point scale (1 = Very uncharacteristic/strongly disagree to5 = Very characteristic/strongly agree).

Openness

Openness was measured using the Openness subscale from the Big Five Inventory (BFI) (α = 0.65) (John et al., 2008). The subscale assessed imagination, intellectual curiosity, and appreciation for art and abstract ideas (e.g., "Loves to read challenging material").

Participants rated each statement on a 5-point scale from1 = "Disagree strongly" to5 = "Agree strongly."

Conscientiousness

Conscientiousness was also measured using the conscientiousness subscale from the Big Five Inventory (α = 0.72) (John et al., 2008). This subscale assessed self-discipline, organization, and goal-directed behaviors (e.g., "Completes tasks successfully," "Pays my bills on time").

Participants rated each statement on a 5-point scale from1 = "Disagree strongly" to5 = "Agree strongly."

Data Analysis

Six regression models were run with the dependent variables being engagements in the context of COVID-19 tweets: 1) Positively Replying to/Endorsing misinformation Tweets, 2) Positively Quoting/endorsing misinformation Tweets, 3) Liking Unaligned tweets (which included liking tweets that were against the vaccine or against masks, or liking tweets which supported hydroxychloroquine use), 4) Retweeting Unaligned Tweets, 5) Positively Replying to/Endorsing Unaligned Tweets, and 6) Positively Quoting/Endorsing Unaligned tweets. Two additional models (liking and retweeting misinformation tweets) from the previous study (Haupt et al., 2024) are reported in the results for comparison purposes.

For each model, the independent variables (IV) listed in the measures section were used (all classical reasoning, motivated reasoning and personality trait variables). Shapley scores were used to identify relative importance of each independent variable in the regression models. This method accounts for multicollinearity between the IVs and gives the relative importance of an IV while controlling for the others. Relative importance was computed as the Shapley decomposition of R² (lmg; (Lindeman & Merenda, 1980 https://paperpile.com/c/yoECXc/rUJa), consistent with Haupt et al. (2024), and normalised to sum to 100%; 95% confidence intervals were obtained from 1,000 bootstrap resamples (Supplementary Table A2). All models used participants with complete data on the independent variables (N = 851); 7 participants were excluded from the regression due to incomplete data. To compare endorsive replying with replying in general, the same predictors were also used to model all replies to misinformation (endorsive and non-endorsive), non-endorsive replies to misinformation (all misinformation replies minus endorsive replies), and replies to all tweet types (Supplementary Table A1).

The average age of the participants was 37.26 (SD = 10.22) years old. Most of the participants identified as male (60.49%), and a large part of the overall sample considered themselves at least slightly liberal (65.73%).

Discussion

The present study examined the role of psychological and social factors in predicting high effort forms of misinformation engagement. Taken together, the findings offer a nuanced picture of how individual differences shape the ways in which people interact with misinformation online, extending the work of Haupt et al. (2024) to high effort engagement behaviors. On average, participant replies and quotes counts for all tweets were lower than the like and retweet counts, suggesting that not many users are willing to exert the effort needed to write responses. Overall, the main explanations for misinformation propagation were each supported, depending on the type of engagement: classical reasoning (CRT) was an inhibitor of passive engagement (liking), personality (grandiose narcissism) was associated with low-effort active engagement (retweeting), and motivated reasoning variables were most associated with high-effort active engagement (replying and quoting).

Classical Reasoning

CRT was significantly negatively associated with liking Unaligned tweets, and was a large contributor to the model based on Shapley-weighted variance. This pattern suggests that lower reflective thinking is a meaningful driver of passive misinformation endorsement. This is in-line with previous literature that indicates that individuals who lean towards quickheuristic thinking are more susceptible to misleading content (Haupt et al., 2024; Munusamy et al., 2024). However, no other form of Unaligned engagement was significantly associated with CRT. Thus, H1 is only partially supported for liking Unaligned tweets (passive engagement).

CRT was not associated with endorsive replies or quotes to misinformation or Unaligned tweets. Thus, H2 is not supported. This finding suggests that higher cognitive reflective thinking may not reduce high-effort active engagement with misinformation; individuals who choose to author a reply may already spend more time deliberating on the tweet, regardless of their reflective nature (Pennycook et al., 2016). Notably, CRT was negatively associated only with liking; it was not associated with retweeting, or with endorsive replying or quoting. In contrast, political orientation and religiosity were the largest contributors to endorsive replying to misinformation (see Motivated Reasoning). This suggests that the reflective-thinking account (Pennycook & Rand, 2019 , 2021) may best explain quick, low-effort judgments such as liking, while endorsements that users write themselves may depend more on pre-existing beliefs and group identity (Kahan, 2015).

However, because endorsive replies were rare, small effects of CRT on these behaviours cannot be ruled out.

Motivated Reasoning

Political orientation did not significantly predict liking or retweeting Unaligned tweets; thus, H3 is not supported. However, political conservatism was positively associated with endorsive replies and quotes to misinformation and was the largest contributor to both models, partially supporting H4. This is consistent with prior work suggesting that partisan identity alignment can increase susceptibility to misinformation consistent with one's worldview (Bayrak et al., 2025; Lois et al., 2026; Rhodes, 2022; Spohr, 2017; Van Bavel & Pereira, 2018; Weeks, 2015). Since political ideology was not associated with passive or low effort engagement once psychological and social covariates were controlled for, political identity may matter most when users author their own endorsement of a claim. Our results are somewhat consistent with Pennycook and Rand's (2019) finding that reflective thinking was more predictive of misinformation discernment than political orientation, since CRT was negatively associated with liking unaligned tweets. However, in the present study, political conservatism, rather than CRT, was associated with positively replying to misinformation, which suggests that motivated reasoning may play a larger role when users write a reply to a post that aligns with their pre-existing beliefs.

Trust in medical scientists was negatively associated with liking Unaligned tweets. This finding is consistent to previous research showing that institutional trust in scientists mitigates susceptibility to misinformation (https://paperpile.com/c/yoECXc/d8GBF. Kaufman et al., 2022) https://paperpile.com/c/yoECXc/d8GBF (Haupt et al., 2024; Hunt et al., 2022). While we found higher trust in medical scientists to be positively associated with replying to Unaligned tweets and negatively associated with quoting them, engagement (e.g., liking or judging the accuracy of headlines), or did not separate passive and active engagement. However, the reply and quote models explained little variance, so these weights show which variables mattered most within these models, rather than strong effects.

Personality Traits

Among the personality variables, empathy was not significantly negatively associated with high-effort active engagement with misinformation or with any of the Unaligned tweets models. These results were not consistent with previous literature (Hangartner et al., 2021; Haupt et al., 2024; Moore-Berg et al., 2022). H9 and H10 are therefore not supported in the present study. The reduced significance in this study may be attributable to the reduction of empathy subscales into the overall scale, or due to the extended list of covariates includedespecially some of the other psychometrics which highly correlate with empathy (particularly Openness (r=0.57) and Conscientiousness (r= 0.54)) (Figure A1 in the supplementary material).

Based on Haupt et al. (2024), grandiose narcissism was specifically a strong predictor in passive and low effort active engagement models. The analysis in the present study shows that Grandiose Narcissism is also significantly positively associated with liking and retweeting Unaligned tweets. Notably, grandiose narcissism was also the largest contributor to the retweeting Unaligned model, consistent with prior research linking grandiose narcissism to social media amplification behavior and conspiracy endorsement (Gnambs & Appel, 2018; Haupt et al., 2024; Sternisko et al., 2023). However, in contrast, grandiose narcissism was not associated with positively replying to or quoting misinformation, further highlighting differences between low effort and high effort active engagements. Retweeting may better fit the approval-seeking need of grandiose narcissists (https://paperpile.com/c/yoECXc/HS9I. Kaufman et al.https://paperpile.com/c/yoECXc/HS9I 2020) , using third-person claims as evidence to bolster their ego, and promote their own worldview while avoiding refutation by not attaching their own words to the claim. Therefore, H11 is supported, but H12 is not supported.

Covert narcissism was positively associated with liking Unaligned tweets, but was not significantly associated with endorsive replies or quotes to misinformation or Unaligned tweets. These results are broadly consistent with prior findings linking covert narcissism to heightened social media engagement (Fegan & Bland, 2021; Haupt et al., 2024). However, H12 is not supported for covert narcissism. Notably, Haupt et al. (2024) found no significant effect of covert narcissism on liking or retweeting misinformation. Our findings also reveal meaningful differences in engagement behaviors between covert and grandiose variants of narcissism. As shown in Haupt et al., (2024), grandiose narcissism was the strongest predictor of retweeting across tweet types while the present study shows covert narcissism was associated with replying in general (Supplementary Table A1). There was no significant association between grandiose narcissism and high effort engagement. These findings suggest that those higher in covert tendencies may be more likely to write responses despite the higher efforts, whether or not they agree with the tweet, while those with grandiose tendencies may be more averse to high effort responses and prefer low effort propagation. This may be because those higher in covert narcissism prefer online interactions (Casale et al.https://paperpile.com/c/yoECXc/GjRO+8QvI 2016https://paperpile.com/c/yoECXc/MBOL Musetti et al., 2022) , and are sensitive to how others respond to them (Fegan & Bland, 2021) ; replying would allow them to express themselves and draw responses from others. However, these effects were small, so replicating these findings in larger samples is crucial.

Openness was negatively associated with liking Unaligned tweets, consistent with the expectation that intellectually curious individuals are better at evaluating information critically (Lawson & Kakkar, 2022; Sindermann et al., 2020). Thus, this offers support to H13. However, openness was not associated with positive replies or quotes to misinformation or Unaligned tweets, unlike Haupt et al.'s (2024) finding that openness was negatively associated with passive misinformation engagement. Thus, H14 is not supported for openness. This may be due to the low number of repliers to tweets and may reflect heterogeneous effects of Openness on misinformation engagement. In an additional model, openness was positively associated with non-endorsive replies to misinformation (Supplementary Table A1), suggesting that open individuals do reply to misinformation, but mostly to disagree with it rather than endorse it. Highly open individuals tend to engage broadly across a range of content and explore multiple perspectives, rather than uncritically accepting claims (https://paperpile.com/c/yoECXc/xxcVj+PAEWx+nhQaD. Kaufman et al., 2025; Lawson & Kakkar, 2022; Sindermann et al., 2020). Taken as a whole, openness appears to function as a protective factor against passive unaligned tweet acceptance.

Conscientiousness was significantly negatively associated with liking Unaligned tweets. This supports H13. These results align with research demonstrating that conscientious individuals are more likely to verify information before acting and less prone to reflexive, passive endorsement of misinformation (Calvillo et al., 2021; Lawson & Kakkar, 2022; Sindermann et al., 2020). However, Conscientiousness was not associated with high effort active engagement models suggesting that conscientiousness primarily curbs passive rather than active engagement. Instead, conscientiousness was positively associated with nonendorsive replies to misinformation (Supplementary Table A1), suggesting that conscientious individuals do reply to misinformation, but not to endorse it. H14 is therefore not supported for conscientiousness.

Strengths & Limitations

A strength of our study was our distinction between passive engagement, low-effort active engagement, and high-effort active engagement metrics. As evidenced by many variables discussed, there were significant differences in how these engagement types differed across variables. For example, CRT was only associated with liking, while motivated reasoning variables contributed the most to replying and quoting, and grandiose narcissism to retweeting. Another strength of this study was the fact that we manually distinguished between positive replies (and quotes) (endorsive active engagement) and negative replies (and quotes) (non-endorsive active engagement) to misinformation and Unaligned tweets. A strength and limitation of this study is the use of a simulation exercise rather than using real interactions on Twitter/X. While a simulated newsfeed exercise allows for measurement of engagement behaviors aligned to true Twitter interface (which may be better than simple Likert-type questions), it still lacks ecological validity as participants' interactions with tweets might be altered as their engagement behaviours are not reflected on their actual public profile. Another limitation is the demographic composition of the sample; being sourced from MTurk, it may not represent the broader population of Twitter users, especially considering cultural and geographical variations in social media (Buhrmester et al., 2011).

Endorsive replies and quotes were also rare, which limited statistical power for the higheffort models. Because these models explained little variance, the large Shapley weights of the motivated reasoning variables reflect their importance relative to the other predictors, not large effects. Future research should ensure sample sizes large enough to draw conclusions from differences in types of active engagement with tweets (i.e., endorsement vs combatant).

New research could extend this study by incorporating real-world Twitter data to validate findings in naturalistic settings, enhancing external validity (Hargittai, 2020). In addition, examining platform-specific behaviors (e.g., Twitter vs. Facebook vs. TikTok) could shed light on how platform dynamics influence misinformation propagation.

Conclusion

This study extends prior work on misinformation propagation by moving beyond passive and low-effort active engagement to examine how classical reasoning, motivated reasoning, and psychological factors shape high-effort active engagement (replying and quoting) with both misinformation and public-health-unaligned content. The findings highlight that engagement type matters: classical reasoning, motivated reasoning, and personality traits did not operate uniformly across passive, low-effort active, and high-effort active behaviors, and in several cases (e.g., CRT, openness, grandiose narcissism, political orientation) their effects on higheffort engagement differed from their established effects on liking and retweeting, even from the previous paper linked to this study (Haupt et al., 2024). Motivated reasoning variables, rather than CRT, contributed the most to high-effort engagement, suggesting that findings based mainly on passive engagement may not extend to replying and quoting. By measuring multiple engagement behaviors, we found that classical reasoning, personality and motivated reasoning explanations of misinformation propagation were each supported depending on the type of engagement, suggesting that simulation exercises measuring multiple engagement types can help clarify disparities in the misinformation literature. Future work that can investigate different misinformation user types, examine social media data from naturalistic settings, and test differential responses to user-specific interventions will be necessary to translate these psychological and behavioral distinctions into practical tools for reducing misinformation propagation.
