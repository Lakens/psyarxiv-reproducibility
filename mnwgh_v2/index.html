Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p < 0.01 Preterm children obtained average intelligence scores, but scored significantly lower on all three intelligence measures compared to full-term peers (all p < 0.01; Table 1).
p > 0.19 All other interaction effects were non-significant (all p > 0.19).
p > 0.23 All other interaction effects were non-significant (all p > 0.23).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p > 0.19 All other interaction effects were non-significant (all p > 0.19).
p > 0.23 All other interaction effects were non-significant (all p > 0.23).

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Abstract

Background: Preterm birth has been linked to atypical social development and difficulties with social orientation similar to those observed in autism. A fundamental component of social interaction is adequate gaze toward an interaction partner's face and eyes. Preterm individuals have been reported to display atypical social gaze patterns, characterized by more frequent but shorter looks toward faces. Methods: We compared looking times to the face, as well as eyes versus mouth during face-to-face interactions in preterm (N = 45) and full-term (N = 29) five-yearolds, using a video-based eye-tracking set-up. Secondary, we examined number of dwells and mean dwell duration. Each child participated in four interaction tasks, including silent looking at their mother, silent looking at a female stranger, and a positive and negative conversation with their mother. Results: No statistically significant group differences were found between preterm and full-term children in percentage of total dwell times to either the face or the eyes, however small differences were observed with preterm children showing less dwells per minute with longer mean durations than full-term children. We observed a marginal effect of interaction partner, with children spending proportionately more time gazing at their mother's face than at a stranger's face. Conclusion: Preterm and full-term five-year-olds showed no differences in total social gaze to the face and the eyes of their interaction partner during face-to-face interactions, although subtle differences were observed in other gaze measures. Speculatively, these findings may reflect a developmental catch-up in social orientation following initial delays in infancy.

Alternatively, advances in neonatal medical care, including infant-directed interventions in the neonatal intensive care unit, may have been successful in improving social gaze after preterm birth. Given the context-dependent and individual nature of gaze behavior, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Future longitudinal studies employing consistent methodological approaches are needed to investigate how social gaze develops over time following preterm birth.

Main text Introduction

Atypical social gaze is one of the core characteristics of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) (1-3).

However, a range of other socio-emotional factors can influence the way individuals look at faces and eyes, including social anxiety, and emotional and behavioral problems. Such difficulties are common following premature birth, highlighting the importance of investigating the development of social gaze in individuals born preterm. Social gaze and eye contact are particularly important when interacting with others. However, the field has only recently started to shift from studying social gaze in artificial contexts using static pictures or short videos of faces (4, 5), to studying gaze behavior during face-to-face interactions (6) (7) (8) (9). This shift is relevant as gaze behavior has been shown to be strongly influenced by the presence and behavior of an interaction partner (10) (11) (12). Thus, the presence of an interaction partner is crucial to properly investigate typical and atypical gaze toward faces and eyes.

Preterm birth is defined as birth before 37 weeks of gestation, and has many developmental consequences (13, 14). As survival rates and physical health outcomes have steadily improved over the years, research has increasingly shifted to long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Many individuals born preterm show a so-called preterm behavioral phenotype, characterized by heightened inattention, anxiety, and socio-emotional difficulties (15) (16) (17). Moreover, a three-tofour-fold increased prevalence of psychiatric conditions has been reported, most notably attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), ASD, and social anxiety (18) (19) (20).

Research has demonstrated poorer social skills in individuals born preterm compared to termborn peers (21) (22) (23). These social difficulties might be related to reduced social preference or altered social gaze patterns (24). Several studies have reported that preterm infants orient less toward social scenes, faces and eyes (25) (26) (27). In a recent review, we described a delayed and fluctuating trajectory of gaze behavior in preterm individuals (aged 0-5 years) during face-to-face interactions (28). Overall, research suggests reduced gaze to faces for preterm infants compared to full-term peers (29) (30) (31). Importantly, this reduced gaze to faces seems to be characterized by a pattern of more frequent but shorter gazes (29, 32). In other words, preterm infants look at faces more frequently but disengage more quickly, resulting in a shorter total looking time to faces. This gaze pattern has been observed both in infancy and in later childhood, including in very preterm (gestational age ≤32 weeks) school-aged children (33). It has been proposed that these short, frequent social gazes in preterm individuals may reflect more immature attention regulation skills (29, 33) or may serve as a self-regulatory strategy during socially demanding interactions (32).

Evidence for altered visual attention following preterm birth has indeed been reported (34), suggesting a potential mechanism underlying these atypical gaze patterns.

Despite these findings, the preterm population is highly heterogeneous and findings are fluctuating, with a substantial proportion of preterm individuals showing social functioning and social gaze comparable to full-term peers. For example, no differences in face-directed gaze were reported between preterm and full-term infants at 2-3 months of age (35, 36). Interestingly, Dean et al. (2021) reported reduced gaze to social information in preterm infants at 7-9 months, an effect that was no longer present at five years of age (37). Furthermore, Sekigawa-Hosozawa and colleagues (2017) identified distinct subgroups of very preterm children based on gaze patterns at social scenes, with one group exhibiting gaze patterns similar to typically developing full-term peers, and another resembling children with ASD (38).

Overall, knowledge regarding social gaze following premature birth, especially beyond infancy, is rather limited. Moreover, much of the existing evidence is either based on selected stimuli (i.e. pictures or short videos of faces) or based on observational studies of face-to-face interactions with time intensive manual coding. While these approaches have yielded valuable insights, they either lack generalizability to behaviors in actual face-to-face interactions or they lack the spatial accuracy of eye-tracking methods. The present study addresses these limitations by using a video-based eye-tracking set-up to investigate gaze toward the face and eyes during face-to-face interactions between five-year-old children and either their mother or a female stranger (i.e. one of the experimenters). Specifically, we compared the percentage of time children spent looking toward the face (eyes, nose and mouth) versus the background, and toward the eyes versus the mouth, in preterm and full-term preschoolers across different interaction conditions. We also investigated the effect of familiarity of the interaction partner, by comparing gaze behavior during silent looking conditions with their mother versus a female experimenter. Silent looking tasks were chosen to minimize potential confounding effects of speaker versus listener roles on gaze behavior (39, 40), especially since these dynamics might differ between mother-child and stranger-child dyads. We hypothesized that preterm children would show reduced gaze toward the face versus the background and reduced gaze to the eyes versus the mouth, compared to fullterm peers, and that all children would exhibit less face and eye gaze toward a stranger than toward their mother. Lastly, we explored the potential confounding role of autism traits on social gaze by repeating the analyses while excluding preterm children with a formal ASD diagnosis or with clinically relevant scores on the Autism Observation Schedule (ADOS-2).

This research is part of the Resilience Study (41), which monitors preterm children born between 2016-2018 since initial admission to the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) at University Hospital Leuven, Belgium. The present work is embedded within a larger research protocol consisting of three laboratory sessions. The first involved frequency-tagging EEG measurements reported elsewhere (42, 43). The second session implemented the experimental tasks described below, while clinical assessments and questionnaires were completed in the third session (44).

Participants

In total, 102 children (mean age 5.5 years) participated in the current study. All children were native Dutch speakers with normal or corrected-to-normal hearing and vision. Of these, 37 were born moderate-to-late preterm (MPT; GA 33-36w), 21 very preterm (VPT; GA 29-32w), and 12 extremely preterm (EPT; GA ≤28w). Experimental set-up

The experimental set-up (Figure 2) employed a two-way video configuration, adapted from Hessels et al. (2017, 2018) (6,7). The child and a female adult (either mother or female stranger) were seated in the same room, each in front of a wooden box containing a half-silvered mirror, which reflected the screen of a 23.8-inch LED-backlit LCD monitor using a resolution of 1920x1080 pixels lying at the bottom of the box.

A Logitech BRIO 4k Stream Edition webcam, placed at eye level behind the half-silvered mirror in each box, was used to record the respective participant's face and head at 30 Hz and a resolution of 800x600 pixels. The live video feed from each box was concurrently saved to disk and displayed on the computer monitor of the other box (scaled to be vertically filling, horizontally centered, thus maintaining the same aspect ratio), such that each participant had a direct view of the other participants' face and head. The latency between video capture and presentation at the other end of the set-up was 121 ms (SD = 13.9 ms).

Using the same calculations as in Hessels et al. (2017) (6), this means that the latency was below the minimum of 130 ms for noticing lag in speech-video asynchrony (50). During video presentation, two Tobii Pro Spectrum eye-trackers, tilted at a 21.7° angle, were used to collect eye-tracking data at 600 Hz from each participant. Light grey panels were placed behind each participant, ensuring a neutral background.

In addition to (dual) eye-tracking, the dyadic set-up enabled simultaneous recording of heart rate, brain activity, galvanic skin response and audio/video. The current report focuses solely on the eye-tracking measurements of the child.

Procedure

Upon entering the lab, participants were familiarized with the room and equipment. Once they felt comfortable, they were seated in the dyadic set-up at a distance of 70 cm from the eye-tracker, such that one cm on the screen corresponds to a visual angle of 0.75°. Using a height-adjustable table, the webcam in the box was positioned at eye height, allowing eye contact when participants looked straight ahead. Daylight was blocked using window blinds and constant lighting was provided by diffuse artificial lights attached to the ceiling. Once the participant was ready, a fivepoint calibration and four-point validation was performed using the Tobii Pro Lab software V1.171.

Next, children performed multiple tasks with their mother and a stranger, with the order of interaction partner counterbalanced. Mothers were informed on the protocol prior to testing, ensuring procedural adherence. The mother-child tasks included two minutes of looking at each other without any verbal interaction, and a three-to-five minute positive and negative conversation (based on Holleman et al. (2023) (51)). Finally, participants performed a 15-second validation task, during which participants were instructed to consecutively look at each other's mouth, nose and eyes for five seconds each. With the stranger, children performed two minutes of silent looking at each other, similar to the first task performed with their mother.

Data processing

Data processing was performed using custom-built scripts in MATLAB R2021b (RRID:SCR_001622) and Python (v3.9.12; RRID:SCR_008394) in Spyder (v5.1.5; RRID:SCR_017585), unless otherwise specified.

Data quality

The goal of our analysis was to quantify the time the child spent looking at the other's face and facial features. To ensure adequate sensitivity of the gaze measures, we first performed several data quality assessments.

Eye-tracker calibration. First, we checked the calibration quality of the eye-tracker to ensure high accuracy of the gaze point estimation. The average calibration accuracy over the four-point validation was calculated per participant using a custom-built Python script. A calibration procedure was considered good if the mean accuracy was ≤1.5° and the data-loss rate was ≤ 5%.

In the current set-up at 800x600 resolution, an accuracy of 1.5° corresponds to 40.5 pixels (~2 cm), which is approximately 2.5 times smaller than the area of interest radius of 100 pixels (see below). Mean calibration accuracy of included participants did not differ between preterm (0.65° ± 0.29°) and full-term (0.61° ± 0.20°) children (𝐹(70.66) = 0.37, 𝑝 = 0.55).

Data loss.

Next, we estimated the amount of data loss per task to assess eye-tracker performance and participant's task compliance. A data loss estimate was calculated by subtracting the total fixation duration over the task from the total task duration. This means that any gaze behavior that is not a fixation (such as blinks, saccades, or fixations outside the screen), as well as any moment when the eye-tracker cannot provide a gaze estimate, is classified as data loss. The estimated amount of data loss during the 15-seconds validation task of consecutive mouth, nose and eye gaze was used to assess the overall quality of eye-tracking recordings.

Participants having 50% or more estimated data loss during the validation task were excluded from further analyses. This applied to three preterm children (see Figure 1). Head rotation. Lastly, we wanted to differentiate between data loss due to participant behavior or due to poor eye-tracking performance. Therefore, the participant's head rotation angles (i.e. pitch and yaw), as estimated by OpenFace 2.2.0 (52), were used to measure whether participants oriented their head toward the screen. OpenFace reports head orientation angles in radian with the camera as the origin. High eye-tracking data quality can thus be expected when the reported orientation angles are small, indicating that the participant is looking straight ahead (i.e. toward the camera behind the half-silvered mirror). Based on the video data, a vertical orientation range of [-0.6 -0.6] and a horizontal orientation range of [-0.3 -0.3] was used as an operationalization for looking at the screen. The proportion of frames exceeding the range for looking straight ahead in either direction (i.e. gazing away from the screen) were computed over the total amount of frames per video. These proportions of gazing away from the screen were generally very low and similar for preterm and full-term children (Table 4). Therefore, any potential group differences in looking times could not be attributed to gazing away from the computer screen.

Data processing

Raw webcam videos and eye-tracking files were imported into MATLAB. Using OpenFace 2.2.0, facial landmarks were assigned to each video frame (52, 53). Areas Of Interest (AOI) for eyes, nose and mouth were delineated using the Limited-Radius Voronoi Tessellation (LRVT) method (54), with predefined cell centers identified by OpenFace and a radius of 100 pixels. The size of the radius was set to correspond roughly to the mean eye-to-eye distance of the female adults (95.8 ± 11.9 pixels), and roughly to 1.5 times the AOI span, with AOI span defined as the mean distance from each AOI cell center to the cell center of its closest neighbor (54). Importantly, varying the radius size did not significantly alter the amount of identified dwells per AOI. The area outside the facial features AOIs (i.e. outside the LRVT radius) was categorized as the background AOI. This background AOI included the shoulders and the grey panel behind the person.

Fixations were identified using the Identification by Two-Means Clustering algorithm V2.0.7, described by Hessels et al. (2017), using default parameters (55). Fixations were then mapped onto the AOIs for the video frame closest in time to the middle of the fixation. Hereafter, dwells were computed using a custom-built python script. Dwells were operationalized as the time that a gaze remains within a particular AOI, from entry to exit. Therefore, a dwell may include multiple fixations and saccades within this AOI and short blinks. The maximum time allowed between two consecutive fixations within the same AOI was set to 150 ms, based on estimates of the duration of blinks (100 ms) and saccades (20-40 ms).

Additionally, we derived two secondary outcome variables from the total dwell duration for the face and eyes. First, we calculated the number of dwells per minute for the face and eyes by dividing the total number of dwells by the task duration in milliseconds and multiplying the result by 60,000 to express the in minutes. Second, we calculated the mean dwell duration for the face and eyes by dividing the total dwell duration by the total number of dwells within the respective AOI.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses related to participant characteristics were conducted in R (v4.2.0; RRID:SCR_000432). To quantify and describe potential group-and task-related differences in percentage of total dwell time, we performed two-way analyses with group as a between-subject factor and either task (mother-child interactions: silent looking, positive conversation, and negative conversation) or interaction partner (silent looking at mother and silent looking at stranger) as a within-subject factor. Parametric two-way ANOVAs were used when assumptions were met, otherwise Kruskal-Wallis tests were applied. Additionally, we employed Bayesian statistics with a binomial prior using JASP 0.96 (RRID:SCR_015823) (56). We report Bayes Factors (BF) provided by JASP, which represent evidence in favor of a specific hypothesis given the data.

BF < 0.33 indicate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis, whereas BF of 0.33-3 are inconclusive.

BF of 3-10, 10-30, 30-100 correspond to moderate, strong, and very strong evidence, respectively, in favor of an alternative hypothesis (57, 58). The full results of the Bayesian statistics exported from JASP are included in the Supplementary Materials.

Similar three-way ANCOVAs were used to quantify and describe potential group-and task-related differences in number of dwells per minute, where AOI (face vs. background, or eyes vs. mouth) was added as a third within-subject factor, and data loss was included as a covariate since this measure is more sensitive to data loss.

To quantify potential group-and task-related differences in mean dwell duration, we fitted linear mixed-effect models in R (LMM; 'Afex' package, v1.2-1) (59). This approach allowed to account for the missing values, as mean dwell duration could not be reliably estimated when there were no dwells on a specific AOI. The models were fitted with group as a fixed between-subject factor and either task or interaction partner as a fixed within-subject factor. A random intercept per participant accounted for repeated measures. As mean dwell duration is also more sensitive to data loss, it was added to the model as a covariate. Post-hoc tests ('emmeans' package, v1.8.5) were performed using the Kenward-Roger method to approximate the degrees of freedom (60), and Tukey-correction was applied for multiple comparisons.

We explored the potential confounding role of autism traits on social gaze by repeating the analyses while excluding preterm children with a formal ASD diagnosis or with clinically relevant scores on the ADOS-2.

Discussion

We investigated whether prematurely born five-year-olds spent less time looking at faces during face-to-face interactions than full-term peers, and whether they look less at the eyes when they do look at a face than full-term peers. For this, we used a dyadic video-based eye-tracking set-up.

Contrary to our hypothesis, we found no statistically significant group differences in the percentage of total dwell time to face versus background, as well as the eyes versus mouth, between children born preterm compared to those born at term, despite preterm children showing more social difficulties on the ADOS-2. However, the pattern of this gaze behavior, operationalized by number of dwells per minute and mean dwell duration, does slightly differ between preterm and full-term children.

Similar total dwell times in preterm and full-term preschoolers during face-

to-face interactions

The absence of a statistically significant group difference between preterm and full-term children in the percentage of total dwell times to the face and eyes can be interpreted in several ways.

Although the findings indicate similar patterns of gaze behavior across groups, a nonsignificant result should not be taken as evidence that the groups did not differ, as smaller effects may have gone undetected. Nonetheless, one possibility is that, by five years of age, preterm children may no longer differ from full-term peers in the amount of time they spend looking at the face and eyes of their interaction partner, whether this is their mother or a stranger. This hypothesis is supported by previous work suggesting that early differences in social preferential looking between preterm and full-term infants attenuates by five year of age (37). Indeed, the majority of research suggesting atypical social gaze in preterms is limited to infancy (25, 27, (29) (30) (31) (32). Similar to our work, Dean et al. (2021) reported no group differences in overall social gaze in five-year-old children born preterm, despite persistent neurodevelopmental differences in early childhood (37).

Importantly, they suggested that observed social difficulties may be driven primarily by languagerelated challenges rather than by impairments in visual social orienting. Consistent with this notion, preterm children in the present study scored significantly lower than full-term peers on the Verbal Comprehension Index and showed significantly more social difficulties as measured by the ADOS-2. Furthermore, previously reported neural measures from this same cohort demonstrated intact implicit neural tuning to faces in preterm children (42). In contrast, group differences were observed in neural tuning to voices, further supporting the notion that the observed social difficulties in preterm children may be more closely related to auditory processing rather than visual attention.

Alternatively, social difficulties in preterm children may not result from atypical social gaze per se, but rather from differences in higher-order processing or interpretation of social information (61).

While Dean et al. (2021) found no group differences in overall social gaze duration in five-yearolds, they did report qualitative differences in looking patterns toward socially informative stimuli (37). Interestingly, we also found small group differences on the number of dwells per minute and mean dwell durations, particularly in eye gaze, with preterm children showing less dwells per minute but longer mean dwell durations than full-term peers. However, this pattern is in contrast to previous literature reporting more frequent, but shorter moments of social gaze reminiscent of immature attention skills or a self-regulating strategy (29, 32, 33). If these group differences reflect a true difference in gaze behavior, we could speculate that preterm children may find disengaging from a social stimulus more difficult. Previous research indeed suggests that toddlers with a lower gestational age are slower to disengage and shift their attention between different stimuli (62). On the other hand, it is important to note that the effect sizes of these detected group differences between preterm and full-term children are very small. Furthermore, an important consideration is that the group differences during the silent looking tasks no longer persist when specifically excluding children with significant autism traits from the analyses. In sum, the groups did not differ significantly in the percentage of total dwell time directed toward faces or eyes, however, differences were observed in other dimensions of gaze behavior. Specifically, children born preterm showed fewer dwells per minute and longer mean dwell durations. These findings suggest that similar overall looking time may coexist with differences in the temporal organization of visual attention. However, the functional significance of these differences remains uncertain and requires further investigation.

An important consideration when interpreting these findings relative to previous research is the time period during which each cohort was observed. Most studies reporting reduced gaze to faces in preterm infants during face-to-face interactions were conducted no later than 2012 (29) (30) (31) (32).

Methodological considerations

Another explanation for the absence of group differences in total dwell durations between preterm and full-term children is that our experimental set-up and task design may not have been sufficiently sensitive to capture differences in overall social gaze behavior. Previous research reporting differences in social gaze behavior in face-to-face interactions between preterm and full-term children showed a pattern of shorter but more frequent gazes to faces (29, 32, 33). These studies suggested that immature visual attention mechanisms could be underlying this atypical social gaze pattern. Our approach suggested a different pattern of less frequent but longer dwells.

However, careful interpretation is warranted as the effect sizes of the group differences were small and our set-up may have been limited in detecting gaze aversions, as no eye-tracking data were available when children gazed away from the screen. As a result, our analyses were restricted to dwells occurring on the screen, which were either classified as either directed to facial features or elsewhere on the screen. Although we estimated time spent gazing outside the box set-up (i.e. away from the screen) based on their head orientation measures, these estimates did not differ significantly between groups and showed substantial individual variability. One way to reduce information loss of 'gazing away from the target' would be to use mobile eye-tracking glasses. However, this approach introduces additional challenges, including more complex and time-intensive data analysis, particularly with respect to defining AOIs.

A further methodological consideration concerns the definition of facial AOIs. In the current study, face-related AOIs (eyes, nose and mouth) were large, as recommended by Hessels et al. (2016) (54). This is advantageous for noisy data, such as the current eye-tracking data including children in multiple face-to-face interactions. However, the downfall of such large AOIs is that it limits more spatially fine-grained assessments. For example, gazes toward the eyebrows or forehead are currently attributed to the eye region. Although it is technically possible to define separate, smaller AOIs, the spatial accuracy of the eye-tracking data would also need to be substantially better to resolve gaze to such smaller AOIs. Thus, the current AOI set is most appropriate for the quality of our eye-tracking recordings.

Relatedly, earlier studies that reported group differences in overall face gaze relied on manual video-coding rather than eye-tracking. As a result, such research investigates gaze behavior to the face more globally, whereas we focused on specific facial features. This is an important distinction, as the eyes and mouth can be considered as among the most relevant facial features during interactions and conversations (11, 73, 74). Moreover, while video-coding offers flexibility, it is inherently more subjective and biased than using an eye-tracking system (75-77). For example, gaze directed broadly to the interaction partner may be coded as face gaze even when fixation is actually directed toward non-facial regions such as hair or body, potentially inflating estimates of face looking and contributing to false positives.

Finally, experimental context itself may have influenced gaze behavior. Interactions took place in a rather empty room, with bare, white walls. Positioned behind the box set-up, children were exposed to very limited distractions and the only salient stimulus was the face of their interaction partner. In contrast, previous studies, especially those using free-play interactions (29, 30, 32), typically provided richer environments with more meaningful ways to disengage from the interaction partner (for example, by looking at toys or surrounding objects). The lab setting and lack of potential distractors may have influenced the children's behavior, promoting sustained visual engagement with the interaction partner. Also in regard to the small group differences in number of dwells per minute and mean dwell durations, it is important to consider the interactional context and dynamics between partners. The current research does not take into account for example turn-taking behavior, which may potentially explain individual and group differences in the observed social gaze patterns particularly relevant for eye gaze.

It would be interesting to assess whether similar results would be observed in more unrestricted settings, while also considering the conversational dynamics. However, such contexts, particularly those employing mobile eye-tracking glasses, pose additional methodological challenges, including reduced precision in facial features discrimination and potential gaze biases introduced by the presence of eye-tracking glasses on the interaction partner in case of dual eye-tracking approaches (78).

Effect of interaction partner familiarity

Although no group differences were observed in percentage of total dwell times, nor an effect of interaction type when children interacted with their mother, we did observe a small effect of interaction partner during the silent looking tasks. First, all children proportionately gazed more toward their mother's face than toward the face of a female stranger. This was expected, as previous literature has also reported preferential gaze toward the mother's face versus a stranger's face (79, 80). Surprisingly, when assessing eye-versus-mouth gaze, children proportionally gazed more toward the eyes of a stranger than toward the eyes of their mother. One potential explanation is that children may feel more shy when asked to look at a stranger, which somewhat counter-intuitively has been shown to result in increased eye gaze during face scanning (81, 82). An alternative explanation relates to the behavior of the adult interaction partner during the silent looking task. Although both mothers and strangers were given the same instructions ("Look at each other without any verbal interaction"), the nonverbal behavior differed considerably. Prior to the task, both mothers and strangers were instructed that they were allowed to shortly react to nonverbal initiatives of the children (e.g. making a silly face) but not to initiate such nonverbal behavior themselves. Female strangers (i.e. experimenters) strictly adhered to these instructions, resulting in minimal nonverbal interaction in stranger-child dyads (i.e. both child and stranger mostly maintained neutral expressions). In contrast, mother-child interactions contained substantially more nonverbal engagement including smiling, laughing, sticking out tongues, and making other silly faces. These expressive behaviors may have biased children's gaze toward their mother's mouth, since gaze to happy or smiling faces is often characterized by enhanced mouth gaze (83) (84) (85).

Limitations

Certain limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of our study. First of all, gaze behavior is very individual-dependent, meaning that each individual has their own preferential gaze pattern (86) (87) (88) (89). This complicates the interpretation of gaze behavior, especially at a group level, and makes it harder to detect overall group differences.

Second, the preterm population is markedly heterogeneous in gestational age, ranging from late to extremely preterm, and in neurodevelopmental outcomes, which span from typical development to subclinical or even severe difficulties, with some children already diagnosed with ASD. Excluding children with significant autism traits attenuated the detected group differences solely in eye gaze during the silent looking tasks, whereas most findings remained unchanged.

Intelligence scores and autism traits mostly did not differ between the different degrees of prematurity (see Supplementary Materials). It is also important to note that ten preterm children did not interact with the female stranger (six through excessive crying or repeatedly refusing, and four due to no parental consent for this part of the study), whereas all full-term children completed the stranger interaction. Moreover, a total of 25 preterm children had to be excluded either due to technical failure (N = 11) or due to poor eye-tracking quality (N = 14). Although included and excluded preterm children did not significantly differ on general characteristics, this may still have led to a selection bias, including only preterm children willing to complete the tasks and adhering to the instructions such as sitting as still as possible for proper data quality. This is particularly relevant given that the six preterm children who refused themselves on average had lower scores for Working Memory. Moreover, 50% of the refusing preterm children showed clinical traits of autism. The results of the current study should thus be interpreted with caution when trying to generalize to the whole preterm population.

Third, gaze behavior is highly task dependent and viewing patterns differ between for example a silent free viewing task and a conversation. Even within conversations, a large variability can arise depending on the topic (90) and whether a person is speaking or listening (39, 40). Variability in gaze behavior may also emerge depending on the interaction partner, even when task instructions are identical. This was particularly evident when comparing children's gaze behavior toward their mother versus a stranger, resulting in a significant effect of interaction partner on the percentage of gaze to the eyes. This points to another limitation that is particularly relevant for the study of unrestricted face-to-face interactions, namely that, it leaves participants with a lot of freedom.

This freedom may translate into variability in behavior and topics of discussion, which might in turn complicate the interpretation of results.

Fourth, the cross-sectional design does not allow us to draw conclusions about how this similar total social gaze duration between preterm and full-term children developed by five years of age.

Future research should investigate whether improvements in medical care at NICU and interventions such as NIDCAP have led to enhanced social gaze, even in infancy. Alternatively, future work could examine whether overall social gaze differs between preterm and full-term infants early on, and gradually converges throughout late infancy and early childhood.

Lastly, it is important to note that our video-based interaction set-up is specifically engineered to capture eye-tracking data with high spatial accuracy while preserving the face-to-face social experience. Participants view full-frontal video recordings of one another, with the partner's eyes rendered in a position that aligns precisely with the camera located behind the half-silvered mirror. This configuration ensures that when participants look directly at the screen, they also appear to establish direct eye contact with their interaction partner. A limitation of this set-up is that the face-to-face interaction is physically restricted by the equipment positioned between participants. As a result, participants were unable to touch each other, and there was little opportunity for distractions or object-based interactions. We did, however, estimate the amount of time children spent gazing away from the screen based on head rotation, and this did not differ between preterm and full-term children.

Conclusion

In this sample, preterm and full-term five-year-olds showed no statistically significant differences in total social gaze duration directed toward faces or eyes, although subtle differences were observed in other gaze measures, including less frequent dwells with a longer mean duration in preterm children. Speculatively, these findings may reflect a developmental catch-up in overall visual social orienting during early childhood, potentially as a result of advances in medical care at NICU primarily with a strong focus on infant neurodevelopment. However, the results of the current study should be interpreted with caution as our methodological approach vastly differed from that of previous research and allowed for considerable inter-individual variability. Future research with larger samples should adapt longitudinal designs to assess the developmental trajectory of social gaze over time, and apply consistent methodological approaches across measurement points to minimize potential confounding effects.

Since then, neonatal intensive care practices have changed substantially. The preterm children in the current study were born between 2016-2018, during a period in which the focus of NICU care has increasingly shifted from survival alone toward optimizing infant well-being and long-term developmental outcomes. Contemporary NICU interventions place strong emphasis on improving socio-communicative development, particularly through enhancing the parent-infant bond. Examples include the Family Nurture Intervention (FNI) (63-65) and the Newborn Individualized Developmental Care and Assessment Program (NIDCAP) (66-68). The preterm children in this sample received care in a NICU that integrates NIDCAP principles into standard clinical practice and is staffed by NIDCAP-trained professionals, and approach that has been associated with improved long-term socio-communicative outcomes (69-71). In line with this, Aija et al. (2024) found that greater exposure to parental speech during neonatal care was associated with stronger face preference later in infancy among children born preterm (72). It is thus plausible that early developmental care practices may have contributed to the absence of a group difference in overall social gaze behavior at preschool age, as observed in our study.
