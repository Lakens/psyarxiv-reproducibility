How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Nonexperimental cross-sectional data are frequently used to evaluate mediation and serial mediation hypotheses despite well-established limitations for such tests. Associational Variable Analysis (AVA) was introduced to address this problem by reframing mediational language in terms of conditional associations and incremental variance. This paper extends the framing, analysis options, and interpretation guidance in AVA to help researchers improve the analysis and interpretation of studies exploring serial mediation type theories with cross-sectional data. Two supplemental analyses are proposed to aid in the interpretation of such data. One symmetric conditional attenuation, measuring redundancy among the proposed set of variables. The second conditional centrality focused on the proposed mediating variables to examine if they account for a disproportionate share of the conditional dependence relative to the putative independent and dependent variables. Two empirical illustrations are used to demonstrate the framing, set of additional analyses needed to help frame alternative interpretations, and the utility of the two analytic summaries in terms of fostering both the completeness and accuracy of interpretation of such tests and foster incremental science. The first example draws on the data used in the initial AVA paper and the second evaluates a published serial mediation model linking family atmosphere to suicidal ideation through attachment and cognitive flexibility. Code and details for conducting the analyses are provided in supplemental materials. Findings illustrate how the suggested estimates help clarify redundancy, differentiation, and structural organization within multivariable theories thereby providing interpretive guidance for cross-sectional analyses where serial mediation conclusions are not defensible.

Nonexperimental cross-sectional data (NECSD) continue to be widely used to test hypotheses with mediational and serial mediational goals, despite extensive theoretical and empirical work demonstrating that such designs cannot support causal or temporal claims about intervening processes (Cole & Maxwell, 2003; Maxwell & Cole, 2007; O'Laughlin et al., 2018; Weems, 2025). While the myriad problems are well-detailed in the literature, one main issue worth noting here is that framing cross-sectional analyses as mediation risks creating a misleading depiction of a particular literature, by incorrectly identifying causal or temporally intermediary processes. Despite the reasons to not frame such studies as mediation, its likely authors will continue if viable alternatives are not available (Weems, 2025). Indeed, initial crosssectional tests of mediational type theories can have value but need clear framing and complementary analytic tools. Weems (2025) presented Associational Variable Analysis (AVA) as an alternative conceptual framework and set of steps that retain a set of common analytic tools, while explicitly constraining inference to patterns of association and overlap that are identifiable from contemporaneous data. By reframing mediational language in terms of incremental validity and conditional associations, AVA provides a way to frame, analyze and reduce inferential overreach.

The present paper extends Weems (2025) by defining and exploring ways to estimate a potential associational variable's influence that may help capture distinct properties of multivariable dependence in cases where there are more than three variables of interest. The Weems (2025) paper used the term associational variable for mediator and indirect association to denote a non-causal version of the indirect effect but left how to deal with theories involving Conditional Associations 4 chain or serial mediation largely unaddressed. The issues are ones of both terminology and analysis. Serial or chain mediation is said to occur when the effect of an independent variable on a dependent variable is conveyed through multiple mediators (causal intermediary processes) that operate in a hypothesized causal and or temporal order. In an NECSD study, such a process cannot be truly discovered. A set of cross-sectional associations may be more or less consistent with the theory and thereby provide initial evidence that conducting a more difficult longitudinal or experimental study may be useful. The goal in this paper is to further extend the reframing of mediational language in terms of conditional associations and provide an improved framework for evaluating serial mediation theories using NECSD. A set of complementary analytic techniques to augment such studies are outlined with their logical basis and examples of framing and interpreting such data with the techniques are provided.

It is useful to start with the concept of partial correlation which is often defined as the correlation between X and Y conditional on Z. From this definition, an associational variable in AVA is a conditional variable or conditioning variable (CV) and indirect association, the conditional association (CA) 1 . Starting with three variables, let (𝑋𝑋, 𝐶𝐶𝐶𝐶, 𝑌𝑌) we can denote three observed variables measured contemporaneously, with covariance matrix Σ and correlation matrix 𝑅𝑅. The basic question that a NECSD can address is to quantify change in zero-order association between 𝑋𝑋 and 𝑌𝑌 once the CV is conditioned on it. A typical conditional association in a mediational theory can be defined as the directional difference between the zero-order association and the conditioned association, and so can have positive (conditional association is smaller), zero (no difference), or negative (conditional association is larger) values. Positive values indicate attenuation of the focal association when conditioning on the CV, whereas negative values indicate amplification or suppression (i.e., cases in which the conditioned association exceeds the zero-order association in magnitude) which is standard partial correlation math in regression analysis (MacKinnon et al., 2000).

In hypotheses involving two or more variables as intermediary processes (serial mediation), a common approach is to conduct a structural equation model testing the relative fit of different configurations and/or conduct indirect effects decomposition with software such as the PROCESS macro with the intermediary variables between the theoretically primary independent variable and main outcome (Hayes, 2022; JASP Team, 2025; Kline, 2023). If the study uses NECSD, one may simply frame this test as associational; however, only testing the single hypothesized set of associations leaves several alternative interpretations of the findings untested. Thus, the results and their interpretation are ambiguous (MacCallum et al., 1993).

This ambiguity and failure of causal or temporal logic has been understood and noted for some time. Because of this, authors have suggested 1) conducting a set of alternative model analyses to help rule out alternative interpretations (see e.g., Hayes, 2022; Tomarken & Waller, 2003). For example, Kline (2023) provides detailed coverage of how to and the importance of comparing alternative models fitted with the same data as part of standard SEM practice. In addition to alternative model fit and path coefficients 2) a simple approach is to conduct and compare the set of all possible partial correlations and examine relative patterns of change from the zero-order to the conditional association (similar to the suggestion in Weems, 2025 for a three variable structure). Both are important to interpret the results of NECSD. However, neither 1) alternative-model comparisons within SEM nor 2) inspection of partial-correlation patterns directly summarizes two potentially informative features of a multivariable set of associations implied by serial mediation theories. These are (A) the overall degree of symmetric conditional attenuation across the full set of variables and (B) the relative concentration of conditional association within particular variables. These features are currently not common targets of conventional mediation analyses, alternative-model comparisons, or pairwise partial-correlation approaches, but such estimates may provide useful information for interpreting the viability of serial mediation type theories with NECSD.

Theoretically, in terms of (A) an affirmative test of a serial mediation theory with NECSD might be characterized by moderate symmetrical overlap. This is because some redundancy is expected if the mediators account for the predictor-outcome association. In terms of (B) the putative mediators should exhibit conditional centrality comparable to or greater than that of the predictor or dependent variable -should not appear structurally peripheral within a conditional dependence system implied by a theoretical model. In other words, a favorable associational pattern for a serial mediation theory in NECSD would be one in which conditional centrality is concentrated in the proposed mediating variables rather than primarily in the predictor or outcome. Thus, a serial mediation theory receives stronger associational support: (A) when the variables exhibit a moderate degree of symmetric conditional attenuation, indicating meaningful but not overwhelming redundancy among the proposed variables, and (B) when conditional centrality is focused on the proposed mediating variables such that they account for a disproportionate share of the model's conditional dependence relative to the putative independent and dependent variables. Such a pattern is consistent with the theoretical claim that associations between the predictor and outcome are organized around intermediary constructs. General Discussion

The present study extends the AVA framework (Weems, 2025) to multivariable data sets implied by serial or chain mediation theories. The motivation for this work derives from a longstanding methodological problem: nonexperimental cross-sectional data (NECSD) continue to be used to evaluate mediation and serial mediation hypotheses despite the inability of such designs to establish temporal ordering or causal intermediary processes (Cole & Maxwell, 2003; Maxwell & Cole, 2007; O'Laughlin et al., 2018). Although mediation analyses, indirect-effect decompositions, and SEM-based approaches remain useful for describing patterns of association, support for a hypothesized serial mediation model does not uniquely support the proposed ordering of variables because alternative explanations and equivalent models often remain viable (MacCallum et al., 1993; Tomarken & Waller, 2003; Hayes, 2022; Kline, 2023). The goal of this Conditional Associations 24 paper was to extend AVA by highlighting the importance of patterns of partial correlations and introducing two complementary descriptive summaries to help characterize features of associational results implied by serial mediation theories.

Within an AVA framework, SCA and CC provide estimates to aid in evaluating serial mediation-type theories with NECSD. SCA summarizes the extent to which observed associations reflect shared versus conditionally specific dependence across the full set of variables. CC summarizes where the remaining conditional dependence is concentrated once shared dependence has been removed. Viewed together, SCA and CC move beyond the logic of pairwise attenuation by describing both the degree of redundancy in a set of variables and the relative structural involvement of individual variables. These estimates can help guide interpretations by providing information that complements traditional common analyses.

Theoretically, stronger associational support would be expected when variables exhibit a moderate degree of shared dependence and when conditional dependence is concentrated in the proposed mediating variables.

The examples illustrate how SCA and CC can inform the interpretation of serial mediation-type theories in NECSD. Across the two examples, SCA values were in the moderate range, suggesting that the variables shared meaningful but not overwhelming overlap, consistent with the expectation that intermediary variables account for part of the predictor-outcome association. However, CC demonstrated that the organization of conditional dependence can differ across results. In Illustration 1, conditional dependence was concentrated in verbal ability, broadly consistent with the proposed intermediary role of that variable. In Illustration 2, by contrast, suicidal ideation emerged as the most central variable, whereas fearful attachment was comparatively peripheral.

The empirical illustrations demonstrate how the summaries emphasized here can alter interpretations over "serial model" with cross sectional data. For example, in the second illustration, reanalysis of Yang et al.'s (2023) serial mediation model also produced moderate SCA, indicating meaningful overlap among the variables. However, CC revealed that suicidal ideation accounted for the largest share of conditional dependence within the set of variables, whereas fearful attachment occupied a comparatively peripheral position. However, they provided additional information about the organization of the associational structure that was not apparent from the mediation model alone.

The proposed supplemental tests should be distinguished from existing diagnostics for common method variance. As noted, The SCA formula draws directly on established calculations with partial correlations that have been used to characterize residual pairwise associations after accounting for shared dependence with other variables and redundancy-focused diagnostics for multivariate association (Lauritzen, 1996; Waldorp & Marsman, 2022; Timme et al., 2013). The CC calculations also draw directly from strength-centrality indices based on partial correlations obtained from the precision matrix, used as centrality measures in Gaussian graphical models and psychological network analysis (Epskamp et al., 2018; Waldorp & Marsman, 2022). In contrast, single-factor, marker-variable, and latent method-factor approaches are intended to evaluate whether covariance among measures may be attributable to a shared measurement method rather than to the constructs represented by those measures (Podsakoff et al., 2003; Richardson et al., 2009; Williams et al., 2010). For example, a marker-variable technique estimates method-related variance using a measured variable that is theoretically unrelated to the focal constructs and evaluates whether the substantive relationships change after that variance is controlled. Latent method-factor approaches similarly model a common factor across indicators to estimate covariance potentially attributable to the shared measurement method (Podsakoff et al., 2003; Richardson et al., 2009; Williams et al., 2010). In contrast, SCA quantifies the aggregate reduction in observed pairwise associations when each association is conditioned on the remaining variables in the set. Similarly, conditional centrality describes how the remaining conditional associations are distributed across variables. Thus, common-method diagnostics evaluate a possible source of covariance, whereas the proposed measures characterize how the observed association changes under mutual conditioning. The approaches suggested here can be considered complementary with the diagnostics for common method variance as part of an analytic pipeline.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The illustrative examples were selected to demonstrate interpretation and should not be taken as establishing normative values for SCA or CC. The practical meaning of low, moderate, or high redundancy will likely vary across domains as a function of construct overlap, measurement characteristics, and typical effect sizes.

Additional work is needed to examine the sampling properties of the estimates, investigate robustness to measurement error and suppression effects, and establish domain-specific benchmarks through replication. Future work might also explore extensions to latent-variable SEM frameworks, longitudinal designs, and alternative forms of conditional dependence estimation. The proposed interpretation framework should be regarded as descriptive rather than inferential. Neither SCA nor CC provides evidence regarding temporal ordering, causal processes, or mediation. Instead, the estimates summarize properties of observed correlational results and are therefore consistent with the central purpose of AVA: constraining interpretation to inferences that are supported by contemporaneously measured data (Weems, 2025).

Ultimately, NECSD tests of mediational theories should be descriptive first steps about the viability of mediational theories. Consistent with this perspective, the value of the suggested supplemental tests is likely to through cumulative comparisons across studies, rather than through specific thresholds.

In conclusion, this paper extends the framing, analysis options, and interpretation guidance in AVA to help researchers improve the analysis and interpretation of tests of chain mediation type theories with cross-sectional data. Theoretically, a serial mediation theory receives stronger associational support: (A) when the variables exhibit a moderate degree of symmetric conditional attenuation and (B) when conditional centrality is focused on the proposed mediating variables relative to the putative independent and dependent variables. SCA quantifies the degree to which association is shared across a set of variables, whereas CC identifies where conditional dependence is concentrated. When used alongside partial correlations, regression analyses, or SEM model comparisons, examining the patterns of partial correlations and the supplemental tests proposed may help researchers more completely characterize the associational structure implied by serial mediation theories within the limitations of NECSD.
