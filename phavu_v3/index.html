Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Asking crowds of stakeholders to generate and prioritize ideas has become commonplace, from citizen assemblies to corporate open-innovation challenges. Although the reliability of the results of such consultations is taken for granted, their consistency across independent groups has received little empirical attention, largely because such processes are typically one-off events that cannot easily be repeated. In this exploratory study, we introduce the concept of parallel-crowds reliability, the consistency of outcomes when the same collective intelligence process is applied to the same problem by different but demographically equivalent crowds, and we provide an initial empirical test of it. Two separate but equivalent groups of MBA students in the same course were asked to independently propose and prioritize ideas to improve their MBA program, with the same decision-maker curating and selecting winning ideas in both groups. We assess reliability at three successive stages using semantic similarity measures based on two independent embedding models. We find that the two groups independently proposed semantically similar ideas, with convergence concentrated among valuable ideas and divergence among poor ones. They also prioritized similar ideas similarly, though this correspondence was concentrated among the ideas they judged most valuable, enabling the decision maker to select similar sets of winning ideas. Given the single-domain design, we frame these results as an initial evidence base for future investigations.

Introduction

Collhttps://www.zothttps://www.zothttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?wpg7uCro.org/google-docs/?o3ONyWro.org/google-docs/?BwH4Rfctive intelligence consultations such as citizen assemblies (e.g.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?BIfSBL Landemorehttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?xc97mx 2026) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?nLR9mw corporate open-innovation challenges (e.g.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?MeFGgL Cao et al., 2024) , participatory budgets (e.g., Zheng et al., 2026) , or forecasting competitions and prediction markets (e.g., Sell et al., 2021) , are increasingly used to inform consequential decisions in public and private organizations (Boucher et al., 2023). Despite their growing adoption, a fundamental question about these consultations remains unaddressed: are their results reliably consistent?

We draw on the concept of parallel-forms reliability from measurement theory (Nunnally & Bernsteinhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?TABZcJ 1994) , in which two equivalent versions of the same instrument are expected to produce consistent outcomes when applied to the same subject. We adapt this framework to collective intelligence consultations by asking whether the same crowd-consultation process, applied to the same problem with the same objectives, produces consistent outcomes when carried out by two different but demographically equivalent crowds. We call this "parallelcrowds reliability". It is arguably a stricter test than classical parallel forms, since convergence must emerge despite variation in both the individuals involved and the possibly open-ended outputs (e.g., policy or product ideas) they generate.

Reliability, thus defined, is distinct from accuracy. The wisdom of crowds literature has established that aggregating the judgments of many individuals can produce accurate estimates, provided certain conditions such as diversity and independence of opinion are met (Galton, 1907; Larrick & Soll, 2006; Surowiecki, 2004). However, accuracy does not entail reliability. For instance, two crowd estimates may have the same absolute accuracy while one is an overestimation and the other an underestimation. Similarly, independent crowds may each produce similar-quality sets of policy or product ideas with little overlap between them. For a real-world practitioner (a company, a government) who typically runs a single crowd consultation with intent to act on the results, the concern is not only whether the crowd's output is smart, but whether a different but similar crowd would have led to similar actionable outcomes. If not, the results of any single consultation may be more contingent than they appear.

The concern about reliability is grounded in a broad body of research on noise in human judgment. Decades of work in psychology and cognitive science have documented that individual decision-making is inherently variable: the same person, faced with the same information, can arrive at different conclusions on different occasions (Gilovich et al., 2002; Kahneman et al., 2021; Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). This variability, also termed "occasion noise", has been demonstrated in domains ranging from judicial sentencing (Englich et al., 2006) to clinical diagnosis (Koran, 1975) to hiring decisions (Highhouse, 2008). In the statistical literature, such variability is understood as a component of measurement error that reduces the reproducibility of assessments (Lord & Novick, 2008).

The wisdom of crowds is often positioned as an antidote to individual noise, because aggregation can cancel out random individual errors (Larrick & Soll, 2006; Surowiecki, 2004). This cancellation effect is well established for estimation tasks with a verifiable ground truth (e.g., guessing the weight of Galton's ox). However, many other types of crowd consultations involve open-ended ideation followed by relative prioritization or vote-based selection which are tasks with no single correct answer. In such contexts, it is less clear how much individual variability is absorbed by aggregation, or how much noise remains in the collective outputs. Given the open-ended nature of ideation, different groups may explore different regions of 'idea space' and may or may not converge on the same core set of valuable ideas. Moreover, because prioritization or voting is inherently relative, in that ideas or candidates are judged better or worse than the listed alternatives, the composition of the set of alternatives itself impacts the crowd's classifications (Stewart et al., 2003; Trueblood, 2022). These considerations suggest that the reliability of crowd consultations deserves empirical investigation, rather than being assumed as a corollary of crowd wisdom.

This might imply a rather large research program, given the variety of collective intelligence consultations. In this study we take a modest first step and explore how to assess the reliability of just one popular kind of open-ended crowd consultation, sometimes called "crowdstorming"; a sort of large-scale brainstorming where many stakeholders are invited to propose ideas, and then to prioritize them in order to help a decision-maker identify the most promising ones for further action (Afuah & Tucci, 2012; Chesbrough, 2003; Majchrzak & Malhotra, 2013; Terwiesch & Xu, 2008).

Crowdstorming consultations typically start with an ideation phase in which participants propose ideas on a shared platform. This first step aims to leverage the cognitive diversity of a crowd to generate quality ideas. However, not all ideas are of equal quality, so, as the number of ideas grows, surfacing the most valuable ones becomes a significant challenge (Ghezzi et al., 2018; Piezunka & Dahlander, 2015). One practical solution is to crowdsource the prioritization of ideas, thereby reducing the complexity of the decision-maker's ultimate selection task (Klein & Garcia, 2015; Lykourentzou et al., 2018; Toubia & Florès, 2007). This crucial prioritization step can be approached in several ways, including ratings, votes, or bets. Klein & Garcia (2015) showed that a "multi-voting with incentives" approach (in fact closer to betting than voting) yields significantly better results than scaled ratings. Instead of voting for their preferred ideas, participants are asked to bet on which ideas an expert committee will select based on explicitly stated criteria. Klein and Garcia showed that, when accuracy is measured against an expert rating gold standard, this approach can yield faster and more accurate crowd judgments than a conventional Likert rating scale. By comparing positive judgments ('bag of stars') against negative judgments ('bag of lemons' or 'BOL'), they also showed that negative judgments alone provide a more accurate filter than positive judgments alone, as participants were better at identifying poor ideas than at spotting good ones. The BOL condition achieved roughly 33% greater filtering accuracy than Likert ratings while requiring only about one third of the participants' time.

The crowdstorming process used in our study was developed by two of the authors (MB and ESS) through fifteen years of real-world commercial practice in public and private organizations (Servan-Schreiber, 2018). The platform was originally deployed for InterContinental Hotels Group in 2007 (Lohr, 2008) and has since been used by public and private organizations such as the US Air Force, Johnson&Johnson, Électricité de France (EDF), to tap into the collective intelligence of their employees. Other organizations such as the French Ministry of Defense, a Spanish regional government, or the bank Société Générale have used it to solicit the creativity and wisdom of vetted external panels.

The process mostly resembles Klein and Garcia's approach but differs in two important ways. First, it imposes a strict cap on the number of tokens that may be placed on any single idea, forcing participants to spread their bets across the idea set. Second, it gives participants equal quantities of stars (positive blue tokens) and lemons (negative red tokens), asking them to bet on both the ideas most and least likely to be retained by the decision-maker. This balanced design preserves BOL's filtering power (participants identify poor ideas with red tokens), but also enables the identification of controversial ideas, which BOL alone cannot achieve. Using the Bayes factor (Jarosz & Wileyhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?xIDwM4 2014) , the statistical classification distinguishes ideas with a genuine positive or negative token imbalance, classified as High-Priority or Low-Priority, from ideas that attract substantial and balanced engagement of both kinds, classified as Controversial. Ideas that attract too few tokens to be confidently assigned to any category remain Unclassified. In practice, surfacing Controversial ideas is valuable to decision-makers because they signal high but divergent interest among stakeholders, including disagreement that may reflect differences across functional, demographic, or geographic cohorts, and that can point to serious flaws in an otherwise promising idea or suggest modifications that could build broader support. A pure BOL filter would discard these ideas based on their high volume of negative judgments, potentially eliminating high-potential but disruptive proposals.

In this paper, we present an exploratory empirical investigation of the reliability of this crowdstorming process. The opportunity for a natural experiment arose when a field engagement at HEC Paris (Hautes Études Commerciales de Paris) Business School required running the same crowdstorming exercise with two separate but equivalent groups of students in the same course (morning or afternoon sessions). The students were tasked with proposing and prioritizing ideas to improve their MBA program. The two groups of students were of similar size, had been randomly assigned at the start of the semester, and worked independently. The same decision-maker (the course professor) curated and selected winning ideas in both groups. This setting provides a natural test of parallel-crowds reliability, with the same process, same problem, same decision-maker, but two crowds composed of different individuals randomly drawn from the same population.

It allows us to address three questions that could operationalize parallel-crowds reliability at successive stages of the process. The first two assess whether the crowds themselves converge in ideation and in prioritization, while the third examines whether this convergence is sufficient to yield similar actionable outcomes when filtered through the decision-maker's judgment.

1. Did the two groups generate similar ideas? We use semantic similarity measures based AI embedding models (Mikolov et al., 2013) to quantify the degree of semantic convergence between ideas produced by the two crowds.

2. Did the two groups classify similar ideas similarly? For ideas with a sufficiently similar counterpart in the other group, we examine whether they received the same classification (High-Priority, Controversial, Low-Priority, or Unclassified). 3. Did the decision-maker select similar winning ideas across groups? We assess the correspondence between the sets of winning ideas chosen by the decision-maker in each group.

This study should be viewed as exploratory and suggestive rather than conclusive. It is based on a single crowdstorming method, a unique domain (MBA program improvement), a particular decision-maker, and relatively small crowds of a few dozen participants. These constraints limit generalizability. However, as the first empirical probe (to our knowledge) of the reliability of crowdstorming outcomes, this study provides initial evidence and perhaps a methodological template for future investigations across domains, scales, and contexts.

Methods

Our crowdstorming process was conducted in five phases illustrated in Figure 1: (1) ideation by the crowd, (2) curation by the decision-maker, (3) prioritization by the crowd, (4) statistical classification of ideas, and (5) selection of winning ideas by the decision-maker. The crowds drove phases 1 and 3; the decision-maker drove phases 2 and 5; and phase 4 was fully algorithmic. Throughout this paper, we refer to the course Professor who curated and selected ideas as the "decision-maker", to the students who proposed and prioritized ideas as "participants", and to the groups of students as "groups" or "crowds".

The ideation, prioritization, and classification phases were conducted on a commercial-grade cloud-based crowdstorming platform 'Storm', developed and operated by Hypermind.com, a company governed by two of us (MB and ESS) since 2000.

Participants and setting

Two groups of MBA students at HEC Paris Business School took the same strategy course from Professor Olivier Sibony in the fall semester of 2022, but in separate sessions (alternating morning and afternoon). The groups were of similar size (Group A: 62 students; Group B: 65 students) and had been randomly assigned to one session or the other at the beginning of the semester. The two groups worked independently throughout the process. Both were tasked with proposing and prioritizing ideas for improving the MBA program. The same decision-maker (Professor Sibony) operated across both groups at the curation and selection phases, creating a natural test of parallel-crowds reliability: same process, same problem, same decision-maker, but two crowds composed of different individuals randomly drawn from the same population.

Because the decision-maker intervened at two of the five phases (curation and selection), we specify below what information he had access to at each phase and what role his judgment played. As we will discuss below, curation serves a different purpose than selection, as it prepares a clean input for the crowd's judgment rather than acting on its output. The implications of the decision-maker's involvement in the selection phase for interpreting our reliability findings are addressed in the Discussion.

Phase 1: Ideation by the crowds

During the ideation phase, participants had one week to propose ideas to improve the MBA program on the crowdstorming platform. All proposals were anonymous and consisted of a brief title and a short text-only description. Participants could submit as many ideas as they wished and could see the proposals of other participants in their own crowd but had no access to the ideas proposed by the other crowd. The decision-maker did not intervene during this phase.

Letting participants see the ideas of others in their own crowd was meant primarily to help participants avoid unwittingly submitting near duplicates, so as to reduce the workload of the decision maker in the subsequent curation phase. This is a crucial consideration in field practice which could not be sidestepped for experimental purposes. Another benefit is that it helps stimulate complementary ideas. As a result, participants in the same group may have influenced each other's ideas, potentially producing greater thematic convergence within each crowd and more divergence between crowds than might be observed with a fully-blind ideation design. If anything, this only raises the bar for parallel-crowds reliability in real-world settings that is the focus of our study.

We cannot exclude the possibility that students from different groups discussed their ideas informally, as they shared the same program, campus, and possibly housing. Such cross-group contamination might be problematic for our purposes to the extent that it produced nearduplicate proposals across groups. However, any cross-group similarities in the proposals more likely reflected pre-existing shared concerns across the student population, which is precisely what the crowdstorming process is designed to surface.

Phase 2: Curation by the decision-maker

In our crowdstorming process, the curation step is meant to be deliberately minimal, as its purpose is to prepare a clean set of distinct ideas for the prioritization phase, not to evaluate or rank them. It is these curated sets that participants are then asked to prioritize.

Before the prioritization phase, the decision-maker reviewed the raw ideas in each group to remove obvious non-starters which are ideas outside the scope of what could realistically be changed (e.g., "Move the campus to Paris"), and to merge closely-related proposals into single, well-differentiated ideas. For instance, "Include different options for leadership activities" and "Leadership credits should be truly encouraging us to lead" were merged into "Redesign all the leadership activities". Closely-related ideas include cases where proposals address the same underlying concern from different angles, not only verbatim overlap.

The decision-maker curated each group's ideas independently, working through one group's full set before turning to the other. He was therefore aware that a parallel process was underway but did not systematically compare ideas across groups during curation. No feedback from the curation phase was shared with participants.

This curation step is a standard and necessary component of any crowdstorming process that involves crowd-based prioritization, particularly at scale. Without curation, the expressive power of the crowd's limited token budget for prioritization would be degraded in at least three ways. First, closely-related ideas might split the judgments of participants who share the same concern, since rather than concentrating positive tokens on a single well-formulated version of an idea, participants would scatter them across multiple similar ideas, diluting the signal for what may be a high priority. Second, ideas that address complementary components of a broader issue may benefit from consolidation, as the decision-maker may have domain knowledge about why those components should be considered together. Third, retaining nonstarters or off-topic ideas wastes negative tokens that participants would otherwise allocate to on-topic ideas, reducing the informativeness of the prioritization. Curation thus protects the reliability of the crowd's prioritization rather than introducing a confound.

Phase 3: Prioritization by the crowds

The prioritization phase lasted 40 minutes during a class session. As illustrated in Figure 2, each participant connected to the platform on their own device and was presented with their group's curated ideas in a randomized order. Participants received equal and limited quantities of positive (blue) and negative (red) tokens, calibrated to represent approximately 15% of the number of curated ideas in their group. The token budget was scaled to the number of curated ideas rather than fixed across groups, so that participants in both groups faced a comparable level of selectivity. Although the 15% ratio was chosen based only on validation from more than a decade of commercial field practice with Hypermind's platform, it is consistent with estimates that 10-30% of crowd-generated ideas are typically of high quality (Blohm et al., 2011) while remaining conservative enough to avoid the accuracy degradation that occurs when token budgets substantially exceed the number of valuable ideas (Lukumon & Klein, 2023).

The task was framed as a predictive judgment rather than a personal preference, with participants asked to bet their blue tokens on the ideas "most likely to be retained" for further consideration by the decision-maker, and their red tokens on the ideas "least likely" to meet that criterion. Accuracy was incentivized: participants would earn one point per blue token correctly placed on a winning idea, and one point per red token correctly placed on an idea that did not win. A strict cap of two tokens of either color per idea forced them to distribute their bets across at least several ideas rather than go all-in on just a few. To reward accuracy, the three highest-scoring participants in each group would receive a copy of the book "Noise" (Kahneman et al., 2021) co-authored by their professor.

Participants were blind to the token allocations of all other participants throughout this phase, preserving the independence of judgment essential to crowd aggregation (Surowiecki, 2004). The decision-maker had no involvement in the prioritization and did not see the token distributions until after all participants had completed their allocations.

Phase 4: Statistical classification

The aggregated token distributions were used to classify each idea along two dimensions. The first is the token balance, defined as the difference between the number of blue (positive) and red (negative) tokens received by an idea. A positive token balance indicates a preponderance of positive bets; a negative token balance indicates a preponderance of negative bets.

The second dimension uses the Bayes factor (Jarosz & Wiley, 2014) to assess whether the observed token imbalance is genuinely directional or could plausibly result from balanced bets (i.e., roughly equal numbers of blue and red tokens). In this context, the Bayes factor can be interpreted as the ratio of the likelihood of balance over the likelihood of imbalance. Thus, a Bayes factor below 1 increasingly favors imbalance as it trends towards 0, while a Bayes factor above 1 increasingly favors balanced bets as it rises with no upper limit. For instance, a Bayes factor of 0.5 means that the probability that the token allocation is genuinely imbalanced is twice as large as the probability that the token allocation is well balanced. Inversely, a Bayes factor of 2 means that the probability that the token allocation is well balanced is twice as large as the probability that the token allocation is genuinely imbalanced. Based on field practice, we chose these thresholds to guide our classification algorithm.

Critically, the Bayes factor inherently incorporates the total number of tokens placed on an idea: an idea that receives 5 blue and 5 red tokens has a Bayes factor of 2.71 while an idea that receives 10 blue and 10 red tokens has a larger Bayes factor of 3.70, even though the blue/red ratio is identical in both cases. This distinguishes the Bayes-factor classification from a simple ratio-based filter.

Based on these two dimensions, each idea was assigned to one of four classes: • High-Priority: Bayes factor within ]0, 0.5[ with a positive token balance, indicating strong evidence of genuine positive preference.

In practice, these ideas are immediately actionable because they have significant buy-in from stakeholders.

• Low-Priority: Bayes factor within ]0, 0.5[ with a negative token balance, indicating strong evidence of genuine negative preference. In practice, these ideas can be discarded because there is near consensus on their lack of value.

• Controversial: Bayes factor > 2, indicating that the balanced distribution of positive and negative tokens is statistically meaningful (the idea attracted strong but opposite reactions). In practice, these ideas demand deeper discussion with stakeholders, and analysis of which organizational cohorts favor or reject them and why.

• Unclassified: Bayes factor within [0.5, 2], indicating that they attracted too few tokens to be assigned to any of the above categories. In practice, these ideas can be ignored for lack of interest from stakeholders.

This classification was fully algorithmic and required no judgment from the decision-maker.

No information about the other group's classifications was used in this phase.

Phase 5: Selection by the decision-maker

After the classification phase, the decision-maker reviewed the classified idea sets for both groups and selected the "winning" ideas, defined as those he judged to deserve further investigation or action (i.e., presentation to the relevant academic or administrative committee). The decision-maker was provided with the full classification results and the underlying token distributions for each group. He was not blind to the other group's ideas or classifications during this phase, meaning that, having reviewed both groups' outputs, his selections in one group may have been influenced by having seen similar or different ideas in the other group.

We acknowledge this as a potentially problematic methodological feature and consider its implications for our third question (whether the decision-maker selected similar winning ideas across groups) in the Discussion.

Measuring semantic similarity

A key methodological challenge in assessing ideation reliability is that text-based ideas cannot be directly correlated across groups in the way that numerical test scores can. We addressed this challenge using embedding-based semantic similarity. Each idea's title and description were converted into high-dimensional numerical representations (embeddings) using two independent models. The cosine similarity between embedding vectors was used to quantify the semantic similarity between any two curated ideas in both groups.

To guard against the possibility that our findings might reflect the particularities of any single embedding model, we used two models with different architectures and training approaches.

The first is OpenAI's Ada-002 (text-embedding-ada-002; Neelakantan et al., 2022), which achieved an average score of 80.97% on semantic textual similarity (STS) tasks in the Massive Text Embedding Benchmark (MTEB; Muennighoff et al., 2023). The second is Google's Universal Sentence Encoder 4 (USE; Cer et al., 2018), which achieved a score of 72.04% on the same benchmark. While Ada demonstrates higher benchmark performance, USE has seen broader adoption across research domains, including healthcare and social sciences (Fang et al., 2022; Fischer et al., 2024; Joshi et al., 2019; Ul Haq et al., 2024). To validate the consistency of these two measures, we computed the Pearson correlation between their similarity scores across the idea pairs used in our analysis (the results of this validation are reported in the Results section).

The similarity scores were used in three ways, corresponding to our three study questions. First, for each of the curated ideas we identified its most similar counterpart within its own group and its most similar counterpart in the other group. To assess whether the two groups generated semantically similar ideas, we examined these pairs using a 2×4 ANOVA with comparison type (own-group vs. other-group) and idea classification (High-Priority, Controversial, Low-Priority, or Unclassified) as factors. This design allows us to test not only whether cross-group similarity differs from within-group similarity overall, but critically whether this difference varies by idea classification. That is, whether the two groups converged more strongly on some types of ideas than on others.

Second, to assess whether the two groups classified similar ideas similarly, we needed to define which cross-group pairs were sufficiently similar to be included in the analysis. This requires a similarity threshold that balances two competing demands: it must be high enough that paired ideas are genuinely semantically close (since cosine similarity scores below a certain level reflect superficial or incidental overlap rather than meaningful correspondence), yet low enough to retain a sufficient number of matched pairs for a meaningful comparison. We set this threshold at the median of the cross-group most-similar-counterpart cosine scores. For each matched pair, we then examined whether an idea and its cross-group counterpart received the same classification. We used permutation tests to verify whether classification correspondence exceeded chance. For both embedding models, we computed the proportion of qualifying pairs in which an idea and its counterpart shared a classification, then generated a null distribution by randomly reassigning counterpart classifications across ideas 10,000 times (seed=42). This procedure preserves the observed frequency of each class, so the resulting null accounts for the uneven distribution of ideas across classifications. One-sided p-values report the proportion of permutations in which chance agreement equaled or exceeded the observed value. The same procedure was applied to each class.

Third, to assess whether the decision-maker selected similar winning ideas across groups, we examined whether the most similar counterpart of each winning idea in one group was also selected as a winner in the other group.

Validating the semantic similarity measures

To validate our similarity measurements, we compared the cosine similarity scores computed from the embeddings generated by two models (Ada and USE) across the 216 most-similar idea pairs used in our analysis: 108 pairs matching each idea to its most similar counterpart in the same group, and 108 pairs matching each idea to its most similar counterpart in the other group. As shown in Figure 4, the similarity scores computed by the two models showed strong agreement (Pearson's r = 0.767, 95% CI [0.705, 0.817], p < 0.001), providing confidence in the robustness of our semantic similarity measurements. To illustrate how these similarity scores correspond to actual idea content, Figure 5 presents three group B ideas at varying levels of semantic similarity to a single Group A anchor idea (Idea 39: "Improve elective selection and choice process"). The most similar counterpart, Idea 98 ("Improve system to select and allocate electives"), proposes nearly identical changes using different wording. At a medium distance, Idea 102 ("Review the list of electives and add more interesting ones") shares the same topic but shifts the focus from the selection mechanism to the content of electives. Even further, Idea 95 ("Double bed for rooms for people with partner") concerns campus housing and shares no meaningful overlap with the anchor. (full idea texts are provided in Table S1).

Did the two groups generate similar ideas?

To assess whether the two groups generated similar ideas, we examined the semantic similarity of each idea to its closest counterpart in its own group and in the other group. The results are plotted in Figure 6. We performed a 2×4 ANOVA with comparison type (Own-group vs.

Other-group) and idea classification (High-Priority, Controversial, Low-Priority, or Unclassified) as factors. While formal assumption tests indicated some departures from ANOVA assumptions (Levene's test: p = 0.004; Shapiro-Wilk: W = 0.982, p = 0.007), visual inspection of diagnostic plots confirmed that these departures were not severe enough to invalidate the analysis. Residual plots showed reasonably random scatter without pronounced heteroscedasticity (see Figure S3), and Q-Q plots demonstrated acceptable conformity to normality with only minor deviations at the tails (see Figure S2). The complete distributions of similarity scores are presented in Figures S1A and S1B. Both embedding models revealed significant main effects of comparison type (Ada: F(1, 208) = 10.993, p = 0.001, ηp² = 0.050; USE: F(1, 208) = 4.077, p = 0.045, ηp² = 0.019), indicating that, overall, ideas were more similar to their closest counterpart in the other group than to their closest counterpart within their own group (Ada: Other M = 0.854, Own M = 0.843; USE: Other M = 0.449, Own M = 0.427). This likely results from, on the one hand, the curation step which was designed to eliminate or merge closely related ideas within each group so as to present a set of well-differentiated ideas for prioritization. That would mechanically depress the amount of semantic similarity within a group. On the other hand, the amount of semantic similarity across the groups would increase to the extent that the two groups converged independently on a core set of concerns.

The ANOVA also revealed significant main effects of classification (Ada: F(3, 208) = 10.667, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.133; USE: F(3, 208) = 5.220, p = 0.001, ηp² = 0.070), and significant interactions between comparison type and classification (Ada: F(3, 208) = 2.674, p = 0.048, ηp² = 0.037; USE: F(3, 208) = 2.660, p = 0.049, ηp² = 0.036), indicating that the degree of cross-group convergence varied across idea classifications.

Post-hoc Tukey HSD tests clarified these effects. For the main effect of classification, High-Priority ideas showed significantly higher semantic similarity than Low-Priority ideas in both models (Ada: p < 0.001; USE: p = 0.004), and Controversial ideas were also significantly more similar than Low-Priority ideas (Ada: p = 0.006; USE: p = 0.046). This is consistent with the intuition that the universe of poor ideas (eventually classified as Low-Priority) is much larger than the universe of valuable ideas (eventually classified as High-Priority or Controversial).

For the interaction, the relatively higher cross-group similarity was driven by High-Priority ideas (Ada: Other M = 0.873, Own M = 0.849, p = 0.0002; USE: Other M = 0.505, Own M = 0.429, p = 0.001), while this pattern was not significant for other classifications. For Low-Priority ideas, the pattern was even slightly reversed, with marginally higher within-group than between-group similarity (Ada: Other M = 0.833, Own M = 0.836; USE: Other M = 0.394, Own M = 0.417), though these differences were not statistically significant.

These results, consistent across two methodologically distinct embedding models, suggest that the two independent crowds converged on similar High-Priority and Controversial ideas while diverging in their lower-priority proposals. The effect sizes range from small to medium-large by conventional standards (Richardsonhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ZVID8G 2011) , with the classification effect showing the strongest magnitude (ηp² = 0.133 for Ada).

Did the two groups classify similar ideas similarly? To answer this question, we restricted the analysis to the ideas that had a sufficiently similar counterpart in the other group, using the median of the 108 inter-group best-match similarity scores as the threshold (Ada: 0.851; USE: 0.454). As explained in the Methods sections, this threshold balances the competing demands of ensuring meaningful semantic correspondence while retaining a sufficient number of matched pairs for analysis. For both models, this approach qualified 54 ideas, with the two models yielding similar proportions within each classification: 23-25 High-Priority ideas (62-68% of that class), 12-13 Controversial ideas (57-62%), 9-12 Unclassified ideas (39-52%), and 6-8 Low-Priority ideas (22-30%). The fact that High-Priority ideas are disproportionately represented among those with close cross-group counterparts is itself consistent with the convergence finding from the previous analysis.

Figure 7 shows how ideas in each classification have their most similar counterparts classified in the other group, with results from both embedding models displayed side by side. High-Priority ideas show strong classification stability: 73.9% of High-Priority ideas in Ada (17 of 23) and 80.0% in USE (20 of 25) have their most similar counterpart also classified as High-Priority, while most of the remainder are classified as Controversial (17.4% in Ada, 12.0% in USE).

Ideas in other classifications show less stability. In the Controversial class, both models show that a substantial share of ideas have High-Priority counterparts in the other group (46.1% in Ada, 33.3% in USE), suggesting fluidity between these two classifications. This pattern is interpretable: since a "counterpart" is more like a sibling than a twin, small variations in how an idea is expressed or in the composition of the surrounding idea set can shift a Controversial idea toward High-Priority (by attracting fewer red tokens or more blue tokens) or vice versa.

The Low-Priority classification shows the least stability, with neither model showing a significant pattern of Low-Priority ideas matching to Low-Priority counterparts. This is consistent with the finding from the ANOVA that Low-Priority ideas are the most idiosyncratic across groups.

We note an overall bias toward High-Priority counterparts across both models: while High-Priority ideas account for 43-46% of the qualified sample, they represent 51-56% of the most similar counterparts. This surplus is especially evident in the Controversial class, where ideas are more likely to have High-Priority counterparts than Controversial ones. Since students were all trying to propose good ideas rather than bad ones, the counterparts of Controversial ideas are more likely to be better (High-Priority) than worse (Low-Priority).

To verify that these correspondences exceed what the distribution of classifications would produce by chance, we ran permutation tests in which counterpart classifications were randomly reassigned across ideas (10,000 iterations; see Supplementary Materials). High-Priority stability was significantly above chance in both embedding models: 73.9% observed versus 51.9% expected under random reassignment for Ada (p=0.006), and 80.0% versus 55.6% for USE (p=0.001). Agreement across all four classes taken together was weaker, reaching significance in USE (48.1% observed, 33.9% expected, p=0.011) but not for Ada (40.7% observed, 32.4% expected, p=0.102). None of the other three classes showed stability distinguishable from chance in either model. Classification correspondence across parallel crowds is therefore concentrated in the High-Priority class rather than distributed across the classification scheme.

The practical bottom line, confirmed by both embedding models, is that the two crowds proposed to the decision-maker similar sets of valuable ideas, predominantly High-Priority ideas, complemented by a few Controversial variants.

Did the decision-maker select similar winning ideas across groups?

After reviewing the classifications, the decision-maker selected 15 winning ideas in Group A and 10 in Group B for further investigation or action (i.e., presentation to an administrative or academic committee). As shown in Figure 8, 80% of the winning ideas were drawn from the High-Priority ideas (64%) and Controversial ideas (16%). The remaining 20% were selected from among the Low-Priority (8%) and Unclassified ideas (12%), indicating that the decisionmaker did not simply follow the crowd's classifications but exercised independent judgment. Table 1 shows the cross-group matches between winning ideas. For each winning idea, we checked whether its most similar counterpart in the other group was also selected as a winner. That was the case for a substantial proportion of winning ideas: 72% with Ada encodings (18 of 25) and 48% with USE encodings (12 of 25).

As noted in the Methods, the decision-maker was not blind to the other group's ideas or classifications when selecting winners. It is therefore likely that this correspondence reflects both the crowds' convergence in surfacing similar high-value ideas and the decision-maker's consistency in recognizing them. If the decision-maker selected a winning idea in one group and a highly similar idea existed in the other group, he would naturally be inclined to select it as well. However, the decision-maker could only select similar winners across groups if both groups had independently surfaced similar sets of quality ideas for consideration.

Discussion

Our study set out to provide an initial empirical probe of parallel-crowds reliability in a crowdstorming consultation. Two demographically equivalent crowds independently proposed and prioritized ideas on the same problem, and the same decision-maker selected winning ideas from each group's output. We asked three questions: (1) whether the two crowds generated similar ideas, (2) whether the two crowds classified similar ideas similarly, and (3) how similar the winning ideas were across groups. In the limit case of perfect reliability, every idea in one group would have an identical counterpart in the other group with the same classification, and the decision-maker would select the same winning ideas. Our results unsurprisingly deviate from this ideal, but the answers we got are generally positive and consistent across two methodologically distinct semantic similarity embedding models.

Did the two crowds generate similar ideas?

Yes, but this convergence was concentrated among ideas that the crowds subsequently classified as High-Priority or Controversial. Low-Priority ideas were more idiosyncratic across groups. The significant interaction between comparison type and classification suggests that the two crowds independently gravitated toward a shared core of valuable proposals while exploring different regions of the space of lower-quality ideas. This asymmetry is consistent with a structural feature of idea spaces: in open-ended ideation, the set of possible poor ideas is vastly larger than the set of valuable ones. Innovation research documents that partial or complete failure rates in innovation projects range from 40% to 90% (Rhaiem & Amarahttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?MB0Ad1 2021) , and creativity research establishes that valuable ideas must simultaneously satisfy multiple criteria (originality, utility, and surprise), whereas ideas can fail to meet this threshold through numerous distinct pathways (Simonton, 2018). When two independent groups engage in ideation, they are therefore likely to sample different poor ideas from this large space while converging on the smaller set of genuinely valuable proposals.

Did the two crowds classify similar ideas similarly?

When we restricted the analysis to ideas with a sufficiently similar counterpart in the other group, High-Priority ideas showed strong classification stability. The convergence on valuable ideas is in part shaped by two features of the process. First, the curation step, by merging close neighbors and removing non-starters within each group, mechanically reduces within-group similarity while preserving cross-group similarity for ideas that both groups generated independently. Second, the relativity of prioritization where ideas are judged better or worse than the listed alternatives, not on absolute merit, means that the composition of each group's idea set can shift classifications even for semantically similar ideas (Stewart et al., 2003; Trueblood, 2022). This context-dependence is a plausible source of the classification fluidity we observed, particularly between the Controversial and High-Priority categories. That the crowds nonetheless converged on similar sets of valuable ideas, despite this relativity effect pushing toward divergence, is a meaningful result. From a practical standpoint, this convergence is precisely what crowdstorming is designed to deliver, namely a reliable signal about the ideas a given stakeholder population considers most important. Importantly, this convergence is selective and concentrated among High-Priority ideas rather than uniform across all classifications.

An important feature of the crowdstorming process examined here is the use of equal quantities of positive (blue) and negative (red) tokens in the prioritization phase. This balanced design, combined with the Bayes factor classification, enables the identification of Controversial ideas (those attracting substantial and roughly balanced engagement from both positive and negative tokens). A pure "bag of lemons" (BOL) approachhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?5PbKZb as proposed by Klein & Garcia (2015) , would eliminate such ideas based on their high volume of negative tokens, potentially discarding innovative but polarizing proposals.

Our results provide suggestive evidence that this would be a costly loss. We observed substantial fluidity between the Controversial and High-Priority classifications across groups, with 33-46% of Controversial ideas having their closest counterpart classified as High-Priority in the other group. This pattern suggests that Controversial and High-Priority ideas may often represent different crowd reactions to ideas of similar underlying quality, with small differences in expression or in the surrounding idea set tipping the balance. If so, decisionmakers would be ill-advised to summarily dismiss ideas with mixed-token distributions. The practical implication is that the balanced token design surfaces a category of ideas that merits the decision-maker's attention even though it would be filtered out by standard BOL methods.

A methodological choice in our analysis deserves comment. To assess classification correspondence across groups, we used the median of the inter-group best-match similarity scores as a threshold for defining "sufficiently similar" idea pairs. This threshold balances the demands of ensuring meaningful semantic correspondence and retaining a sufficient sample for analysis. We acknowledge that this choice is somewhat arbitrary and that the median threshold mechanically selects the more similar half of idea pairs. The fact that High-Priority ideas are disproportionately represented among those exceeding the threshold is itself consistent with our convergence finding, but it also means that the classification stability results should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Permutation tests confirm that High-Priority stability exceeds chance under this threshold in both embedding models, but future work could explore sensitivity to thresholds to assess robustness.

How similar were the winning ideas across crowds?

A substantial proportion of winning ideas selected by the decision-maker had their closest counterpart also selected as a winner in the other group. However, because the decision-maker intervened at two phases (curation and selection), our design cannot fully isolate the crowd's reliability from the decision-maker's consistency. At the curation stage, this concern is mitigated by the nature of the task, since curation operates on the input to the crowd's prioritization (removing non-staters and merging closely related proposals) rather than on its output. Here, curation serves to protect the informativeness of the crowd's token allocations rather than to evaluate idea quality. At the selection stage, the concern is more substantive. Because the decision-maker was not blind to the other group's output, the correspondence between winning ideas across groups likely reflects both the crowd's convergence in surfacing similar ideas and the decision-maker's consistency in recognizing them. These two sources cannot be fully disentangled. That said, we note that the decision-maker could only select similar winners across groups if both groups had independently surfaced similar sets of good ideas. So, the correspondence at selection presupposes convergence at ideation and prioritization, which is independent of the decision-maker's involvement.

Overall, our findings support the idea that meaningful parallel-crowds reliability can be achieved with a well-designed crowdstorming process, particularly for the ideas that matter most to a decision-maker (those classified as High-Priority). However, some broad limitations constrain the generalizability of our results, so they are best viewed as proof of concept rather than conclusive demonstration. Firstly, this study is not a perfectly controlled experiment but an exploratory analysis of a natural experiment that arose from a field engagement. Secondly, we used a single crowdstorming method, to solve one specific problem (MBA program improvement), with one population type (MBA students), and a particular decision-maker (the course Professor).

The ideas generated in this context may be relatively constrained in scope and complexity compared to a citizen assembly, a corporate innovation challenge in a technical domain, or a prediction market on a contested election. We cannot know whether the convergence we observe would hold in domains where the idea space is larger, more complex, or more polarized; or where the stakeholders are more heterogeneous. Controlled studies of parallelcrowds reliability are needed to establish which features of our findings may generalize and which are setting specific. Such studies would be especially valuable for practitioners and decision makers who need to appreciate how reliable the results of a targeted crowd consultations might be.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that it is possible to empirically test the reliability of a crowdstorming process. The methodological toolkit we propose operationalizes parallel-crowds reliability through three related questions: ideation convergence, classification correspondence, and winner overlap. We used embedding-based semantic similarity as a bridge between independent text-based outputs, providing a template for future investigations. Our main finding is that parallel crowds tend to converge on valuable ideas while diverging on poor ideas. This asymmetry is encouraging as it suggests that the noise inherent in collective intelligence processes may be largely benign, affecting the parts of the idea space that the process is designed to filter out. As collective intelligence practice increasingly seeks to inform consequential real-world decisions, from citizen assemblies shaping public policy to enterprisewide innovation challenges, practitioners need to be able to offer guarantees about not only the quality of the process outputs but also about their reliability. Our findings provide a first, promising indication that such reliability is achievable. Our study being more exploratory than strictly controlled doesn't offer definitive answers, but it is a first step toward the kind of evidence base that can underpin trust in collective intelligence processes.

Assembling this evidence base is an ambitious research challenge. Within the restricted domain of crowdstorming, we need to test parallel-crowds reliability in domains beyond educational program improvement, for instance corporate innovation challenges where idea spaces are larger and more technical. Scale is also an obvious challenge. A consultation involving hundreds or thousands of stakeholders canvassing one or two orders of magnitude more ideas would require more automated processeshttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?VaU3na for instance using LLMs (Page, 2025) , or more complex classification algorithms, that may impact convergence.

More broadly, all other kinds of consequential crowd consultations would not only benefit from basic reliability testing, but also enable other important dimensions of collective intelligence to be stress tested. Citizen assemblies on public policy, where stakeholders are more heterogeneous and opinions more polarized, would test reliability under conditions of sortition, cognitive diversity, disagreement, expert input, professional moderation and richer participant interactions. While such assemblies are increasingly popular (Landemorehttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?rfyPzK 2026) , no research has directly tested whether their substantive outputs are reproducible across different instantiations. The complexity and cost of such consultations -some may cost up to $2 million to conduct over several months with just a hundred participants -obviously pose significant hurdles.

Prediction market imputed probabilities are also increasingly heeded (Arrow et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?nYG7LY 2008) , but research into the reasons why two comparable prediction markets may diverge on the same questions is lacking. Market maker algorithms, population demographics, incentives (e.g., realmoney, cryptocurrency, play money), geography, anonymity, etc. could all play a role.

Crowd consultations, from democratic elections to smaller scale instances using all forms of structured collective judgment, all share the same vulnerability, namely that they are typically run once (because of their complexity and cost) while their results are implicitly assumed to be stable. Systematic reliability testing, applied at every stage of a collective intelligence process, could help uncover where variability originates and suggests ways to reduce it. Our hope is that this modest exploratory study encourages such testing across the many domains in which collective intelligence is called upon to inform decisions that matter.
