Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Generative artificial intelligence (AI) can take away valued parts of work, such as learning through one's own effort or the sense that a result is one's own. I define AI grief as sadness and longing related to what generative AI takes away from people's lives and present a self-report scale of AI grief at work. In a preregistered online survey, 304 adults, nearly all employed and mostly in the UK, rated how often they had 15 such experiences in the past month. The items formed one strong scale with a dominant general factor, which justifies a single total score. AI grief experiences were on average infrequent. Scores were associated with the number of losses that participants attributed to AI and were related to, but distinguishable from, anxiety, depression, and fear of AI. Their negative association with acceptance of AI was stronger than predicted. The scale needs confirmation in an independent sample.

[div-01]

Alongside its benefits, generative artificial intelligence (AI) can take away valued parts of work, such as tasks people used to enjoy, learning through their own effort, the feeling that a result is truly their own, and trust that what colleagues send them was written by those colleagues. Once access was removed, high school students who had practiced with unrestricted access to a chatbot performed worse than students who never had it (Bastani et al., 2025). People do not feel that text generated by AI for them is their own (Draxler et al., 2024), and managers who write with substantial AI assistance are seen as less trustworthy (Cardon & Coman, 2026). These studies document losses but not whether people grieve them. A computing teacher has described experiencing these changes as grief (Geller, 2024), and qualitative health researchers have mapped their field's reactions to AI onto stages of grief (Sibeoni et al., 2026). However, these accounts of grief are personal or conceptual. I define AI grief as sadness and longing related to what generative AI takes away from people's lives. Such losses may arise in many areas of life, from personal relationships to creative activities, and the present study concerns work and studies. The definition includes a reaction and its object. The reaction is grief in the broad sense used for losses other than death; it includes longing for how things were, sadness, a shaken sense of who one is at work, and a loss of meaning in one's own effort. Its object is a concrete loss that the person attributes to generative AI. For example, a translator who now mostly edits machine translations may keep their job and income but miss finding the right words themselves, and if they feel sadness and longing about this, they experience AI grief. AI grief also includes concrete losses that a person expects, such as a part of the work that AI will soon take over. AI grief differs from grief supported by AI, such as the use of chatbots that simulate the deceased (Hollanek & Nowaczyk-Basińska, 2024), and from grief after the loss or alteration of an AI companion (De Freitas et al., 2026). It also differs from fear or anxiety about AI, which are reactions to threat, because it is sadness and longing over losses, including losses that a person expects. AI grief is not the same as losing a job, because what is lost is often what work meant to the person. Finally, AI grief is not a disorder but a normal reaction to loss, as is grief over other non-death losses.

AI Grief as a Non-Death Loss

Grief is not limited to bereavement, as people also grieve losses of health, work, abilities, and ways of life, which share with bereavement the loss of possibilities that gave their lives structure (Harris, 2020; Ratcliffe & Richardson, 2023). Papa and Maitoza (2013) showed that grief after job loss was distinguishable from depression and anxiety and was higher after involuntary than after voluntary loss. Scales of grief over job loss, impending job loss, and health loss have adapted items on longing, preoccupation, identity, and meaning from measures of complicated grief (van Eersel et al., 2019 (van Eersel et al., , 2024 (van Eersel et al., , 2026)). The loss of a work-related identity has also been modeled with concepts from grief research (Conroy & O'Leary-Kelly, 2014). Grief related to large-scale change has been described for the environment, where ecological grief concerns experienced or anticipated losses in the natural world (Cunsolo & Ellis, 2018). Similar reactions have been reported for digital environments, where users of social media platforms that had changed for the worse reported solastalgia, a sense of loss about a place they still inhabit (Cipriani, Menicucci, & Grassini, 2026).

Four features may make AI grief distinctive. First, the grief can be disenfranchised (Doka, 1989), which means that the loss is not openly acknowledged, for example because others see AI as progress or note that the person uses AI too. Second, the loss is often ambiguous (Boss, 1999), because it can be unclear what exactly is gone, whether it will return, and whether the next version of AI will take more. Such unacknowledged and ambiguous losses can be harder to work through because they lack social recognition and clear boundaries (Boss, 1999; Doka, 1989). Third, because AI keeps changing, people can expect to lose further parts of their work, so the grief is partly anticipatory. Fourth, the loss can come together with gains, so a person can grieve and benefit at the same time.

Drawing on this literature and on measures of prolonged grief (Prigerson et al., 2021), I specified seven facets of AI grief before data collection, namely longing, sadness, identity, meaning, preoccupation with the loss, anticipatory grief, and avoidance. Stages of grief, which Sibeoni et al. (2026) applied to reactions to AI, were not used because stage models lack sound empirical support (Stroebe et al., 2017).

The Need for a New Measure

Existing measures related to AI capture threats, evaluations, or self-conscious emotions. They assess anxiety about AI (Wang & Wang, 2022), fear of generative AI (Corradi et al., 2026), threat to work identity (Mirbabaie et al., 2022), attitudes toward AI (Sindermann et al., 2021), and shame and guilt about using AI (Cipriani, Menicucci, Greco, & Grassini, 2026). Measures of job insecurity similarly concern the possibility of losing the job itself (Vander Elst et al., 2014). None of them asks about grief over losses, which may occur whether or not the job is at risk and together with acceptance of AI and its benefits.

Because grief after job loss has correlated strongly with anxiety and depression (van Eersel et al., 2019), a measure of AI grief should be distinguishable from general distress. It should also be distinguishable from attitudes toward AI, because a person can value AI and still grieve what it has taken. Finally, the measure should be related to losses that people actually perceive.

The Present Study

The aim of this study was to develop a self-report scale of AI grief in work and studies, the AI Grief Scale, and to provide initial evidence of its validity in a preregistered study in which nearly all participants were employed. In an online survey, I tested 15 items together with measures of perceived losses and gains, distress, and attitudes toward AI. The study addressed three research questions, the first of which was whether the AI grief items form one dimension or a general factor with more specific components. The second was whether a short scale covering both milder and stronger experiences can be built from the items, and the third was whether AI grief can be distinguished from anxiety and depression and from attitudes toward AI. Because the scale should measure AI grief equally well in people who use AI a lot, a little, or not at all, I also examined whether the items function differently across levels of AI use.

The confirmatory hypotheses were that the items share one dominant general factor (H1) and form a Mokken scale (H2). AI grief was expected to correlate positively with anxiety and depression, but not so strongly as to suggest the same construct (H3), and people who perceive more losses were expected to report more AI grief (H4). AI grief was expected to correlate positively, but not too strongly, with fear of AI and at most weakly with acceptance of AI (H7). The exploratory hypotheses were that two optional short scales outside the AI Grief Scale, one on unrecognized loss and one on ambivalence, each have two factors (H5) and that anticipatory grief has variance of its own beyond the general factor (H8).

Method

Participants and Procedure

I conducted a preregistered cross-sectional online survey of a nonprobability sample of adults who work or study, including those who do not use AI. Participants came from a paid Prolific panel of UK and U.S. residents and from volunteers recruited online, and the panel was restricted to full-or part-time employment because Prolific does not allow screening participants out within a study without its in-study screening feature. Panel members took part on October 22-23, 2026, and received £0.67 or £0.70, which corresponds to about £6 per hour. The study was advertised as a survey on what AI gives to and takes from people's work or studies, without mentioning grief, and all responses were in English.

Participants gave informed consent online after an information page that described the age requirement of 18 years or older, anonymity, voluntary participation, questions about sadness, and data handling. No names, email or IP addresses, or Prolific IDs were stored. The core part of the questionnaire had a fixed order, starting with background questions, followed by perceived losses and gains, the AI grief items, attitudes toward AI, and distress. A personal summary shown at the end of the questionnaire (to the two volunteers already after the core measures) introduced the term AI grief, suggested ways of coping, pointed to a directory of helplines, and recommended professional help when scores on the Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4) were elevated (total ≥ 6 or a subscore ≥ 3; Kroenke et al., 2009). Ethics review was not required for this anonymous observational survey under the statute of my institution's ethics committee, and none was sought.

The preregistered exclusion criteria were a failed attention check, implausibly fast or uniform responding, too few answered items, and neither working nor studying. Because AI agents can pass attention checks (Westwood, 2025), responses with signs of AI agents were excluded from the main analyses and kept for sensitivity analyses (rules and counts in Supplement, Section 1). Of 326 submissions to the final questionnaire, 20 were excluded by the preregistered criteria and 2 others were flagged as possible AI agents, which left 304 participants. Of these, 54% were women, most were aged 25-44 (age was collected in bands), and 91% were in the UK according to their connection. Measures

AI Grief Scale

I drafted a bank of 84 items, 31 of them for AI grief. The AI grief items adapted the content of the PG-13-R (Prigerson et al., 2021) and the Brief Solastalgia Scale (B. K. Christensen et al., 2024) to losses attributed to AI and drew on published personal accounts and grief theory. During preselection, I added one item on avoidance, which the bank lacked, and one on anticipatory grief. Language-model ratings, item sorting, simulated cognitive interviews, and synthetic responses were used only to shorten the item bank and are not evidence of validity (Supplemental Table S3). A clarity pilot with the first 16 volunteers, whose responses were not analyzed, used comments and web probes (Behr et al., 2017). After the pilot, six AI grief items were reworded, mostly into plainer language, and an instruction was added to choose never if a statement does not apply.

The final 15 items (Table 2) represent the seven facets described in the Introduction, and respondents rate from 0 (never) to 4 (very often) how often each statement was true in the past month, with an unscored attention check (select often) after the eighth item. The score is the mean of at least eight answered items.

Perceived Losses and Gains

Two checklists ask what AI has taken away from or weakened in one's work or studies (10 areas) and then what it has given or strengthened (six of these areas); the areas appear in random order, and each list has an exclusive None of these option. I counted the marked losses (H4) and gains.

Distress

The PHQ-4 (Kroenke et al., 2009) combines two anxiety items from the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 and two depression items from the Patient Health Questionnaire-9. Items are rated for the past 2 weeks from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), so the total score ranges from 0 to 12 and each subscore from 0 to 6. In this sample, McDonald's omega total (ω t ) was .92 for the total score, and the Spearman-Brown coefficient was .88 for both subscores.

Attitudes Toward AI

The Attitude Towards Artificial Intelligence scale (ATAI; Sindermann et al., 2021) measures fear of AI with three items (e.g., "I fear artificial intelligence") and acceptance of AI with two items (e.g., "I trust artificial intelligence"), rated from 0 (strongly disagree) to 10 (strongly agree). In this sample, ω t was .84 for fear, and the Spearman-Brown coefficient was .86 for acceptance.

Other Variables

Background questions covered work or study status, field, frequency of generative AI use, share of work done with AI, age band, and gender. A continuation, completed by 296 participants, asked about education, teaching or leading others, required AI use, duration of regular use, work lost because of AI, and two new items on preferring work done by people (0-10). It also included two optional short scales (H5): one on unrecognized loss (ω t = .60), which participants who marked at least one loss answered together with two items on the nature of the loss, and one on ambivalence and complicity (ω t = .75), answered by AI users.

These scales and items were rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Participants who teach or lead others also marked what AI had weakened for the people they teach or lead. All variables collected are listed in Supplement, Section 1.

Data Analysis

Each analysis is labeled as confirmatory, preregistered exploratory, or not preregistered. Confirmatory tests were not corrected for multiplicity because each tested a different claim.

Dimensionality

I estimated the number of dimensions with parallel analysis (Horn, 1965) of polychoric correlations, using common factors, 100 simulated data sets of normally distributed variables, and the 95th-percentile criterion. I also used exploratory graph analysis (EGA; Golino & Epskamp, 2017) and assessed its stability in 500 parametric bootstrap samples (A. P. Christensen & Golino, 2021). To evaluate whether a total score is justified, I fitted a Schmid-Leiman bifactor model (Schmid & Leiman, 1957) to polychoric correlations, with as many group factors as parallel analysis suggested but at least three. H1 (confirmatory) required an omega hierarchical (ω h ) of at least .80 and an explained common variance (ECV) of at least .70 (Rodriguez et al., 2016). Omega hierarchical is the share of total-score variance due to the general factor. When it is computed from polychoric correlations, it refers to the continuous responses assumed to underlie the ratings (Flora, 2020). Exploratory H8 required all three anticipatory grief items to have their highest absolute group loading (≥ .30, not preregistered) on the same group factor.

Scalability and Precision

Mokken scale analysis tested whether the items order respondents consistently from mild to strong AI grief (Sijtsma & van der Ark, 2017). The scalability coefficient H indicates how consistently the items order respondents, and H i indicates this for each item. Automated item selection with a lower bound of .30 was followed by the removal, one at a time, of items with monotonicity violations or H i < .30. H2 (confirmatory) required H ≥ .40 and at least 10 items with H i ≥ .30.

A scale with H ≥ .50 is considered strong. Stricter lower bounds (.35-.50) were examined descriptively, as was invariant item ordering, that is, whether the items keep the same order of endorsement at all levels of AI grief (H T ; Ligtvoet et al., 2010). In preregistered exploratory analyses, a graded response model (GRM; Samejima, 1969) estimated precision as marginal reliability, and model fit was evaluated with the C2 goodness-of-fit statistic (Cai & Monroe, 2014), its root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), the standardized root mean square residual (SRMSR), the comparative fit index (CFI), and the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI). Ordinal logistic regression (lordif; Choi et al., 2011) tested differential item functioning (DIF), that is, whether participants with the same level of AI grief answer an item differently depending on their group. Groups were defined by frequency of AI use, by share of work done with AI in two groupings, and, as indicative only, by field. Items were flagged by the preregistered criterion (Holm-corrected p < .05) and, more strictly, when the change in McFadden's pseudo-R² (ΔR²) was also at least .02 (not preregistered).

Validity

H3 (confirmatory) required a positive Pearson correlation below .60 with the PHQ-4, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) excluding zero, and H4 required a Spearman's ρ above .30 with the number of losses, with CIs following Bonett and Wright (2000). H7 required a positive correlation below .60 with fear of AI and an absolute correlation below .30 with acceptance of AI. Preregistered exploratory analyses included a partial ρ controlling for fear of AI, Kruskal-Wallis comparisons, and factor analyses of the optional scales (H5). The correlation with the PHQ-4 corrected for unreliability was not preregistered but was specified before the analysis.

Reliability, Sensitivity, and Additional Analyses

Reliability was estimated with ω t from Pearson correlations, which refers to observed scores (Flora, 2020), or with the Spearman-Brown coefficient for two-item scores (Eisinga et al., 2013). Sensitivity analyses repeated the tests of H1-H4, H7, and H8 with the two possible AI agents included and with panel participants only (Table 3). Analyses of the AI grief items and the partial correlation used complete cases, and other correlations used pairwise deletion. Analyses that were not preregistered (Supplemental Table S1) included effect sizes (ε² [Tomczak & Tomczak, 2014] and Cohen's d), the reliability of the other measures, the floor-effect criterion, correlations with gains and with the PHQ-4 subscores, an exploratory 7-item version with one item per facet, and three sensitivity checks described with the results. Further exploratory analyses, including a network of all items and a search for subgroups with markedly different AI grief, are reported on OSF (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/EXHS6). I used R 4.6.1 (R Core Team, 2026) with psych 2.6.9 (Revelle, 2026), EGAnet 2.4.1 (Golino & Christensen, 2026), qgraph 1.10.1 (Epskamp et al., 2012), mokken 3.1.2 (van der Ark, 2007), mirt 1.48 (Chalmers, 2012), and lordif 0.4.2 (Choi et al., 2011).

Transparency and Openness

I preregistered the study on OSF on October 21, 2026 (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/ZRBC5), before data collection with the final questionnaire. Before testing the hypotheses, I uploaded a Transparent Changes document with the deviations and analysis specifications, following the template of Willroth and Atherton (2024), to the OSF project of the study (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/EXHS6) and later updated it; the timing of each change is stated there. I report how I determined my sample size, all data exclusions (if any), all manipulations, and all measures in the study (Simmons et al., 2012).

Results

Descriptive Statistics

No AI grief item responses were missing. The mean AI grief score was 1.31 (SD = 0.95, Mdn = 1.23), which lies between rarely and sometimes (Supplemental Figure S1). Overall, 8.9% of participants answered never to all items, which is below the 15% often used to indicate a floor effect (Terwee et al., 2007). Items about sadness over losses still to come were endorsed most often, and items about being close to tears and avoiding reminders were endorsed least often (Figure 1 and Table 2). The areas most often marked as lost were trust that what others write was written by them, learning through one's own effort, and the feeling that a result is truly one's own (Table 1). The creative part of work was most often marked as gained, but it was also often marked as lost.

Scalability and Measurement Precision

All 15 items formed one Mokken scale at the lower bound of .30 and remained in it up to .50. The scale was strong, H = .65, with H i values from .58 to .71, so H2 was supported. Invariant item ordering was inaccurate (H T = .18, below .30), so the items should not be read as a fixed sequence from mild to severe. The reliability of the total score was high (ω t = .97). In the GRM, marginal reliability was .94, and precision was lower at the lowest and highest levels of AI grief (Supplemental Figure S4). Model fit was not adequate, C2(90) = 498.8, p < .001, RMSEA = .12, SRMSR = .06; the SRMSR exceeded the .05 bound for adequate fit proposed by Maydeu-Olivares and Joe (2014). CFI was .97 and TLI .96. The precision estimates are therefore approximate.

Differential Item Functioning

By the preregistered criterion (Holm-corrected p < .05), the two items on sadness about future losses (Items 4 and 12) were flagged in every comparison by AI use. Items 2 and 7 were also flagged by frequency of AI use, and Item 8 by share of work done with AI, both with the registered categories and with the median split. With the additional threshold of ΔR² ≥ .02, Item 4 was flagged by frequency of AI use, and Items 4, 8, and 12 were flagged by the registered categories of share of work done with AI. No item reached this threshold with the median split. By field (indicative only), Item 2 was flagged. ΔR² values ranged from .013 to .043, and the groups had 32-199 participants, which limits power (Scott et al., 2009). Items flagged by both criteria are marked in Table 2, and detailed results are reported in Supplemental Table S5.

Discussion

The present study developed a self-report measure of AI grief at work and evaluated it in a preregistered study of 304 adults, nearly all of them employed. Four of the five confirmatory hypotheses were supported. However, H7 was not supported because, although AI grief correlated with fear of AI as predicted, its correlation with acceptance of AI exceeded the predicted bound. The preregistered item selection retained all 15 items, so it did not produce a shorter scale, and the second research question remains open. Exploratory H8 was not supported, and H5 was not supported by the criterion specified before the analysis. Because error rates were not controlled across the exploratory analyses, their results should be treated as hypotheses.

Items covering all seven facets of AI grief measured one dominant dimension, so a total score summarizes them well. The group factors added little reliable variance. Contrary to H8, the three anticipatory grief items did not form a separate component, although the two items on sadness about future losses were endorsed most often. This suggests, although it does not show, that grief over expected losses is part of the same experience as grief over losses that have already occurred (Cunsolo & Ellis, 2018).

AI grief experiences were on average infrequent in this sample, although this level does not indicate how common AI grief is, because the sample was not representative. Scores were associated with the number of perceived losses, even when fear of AI was controlled, and were higher among participants who had lost part of their work. More AI grief was also reported by participants who were unsure whether what they were losing would come back and by those who felt that each new version of AI brought another loss. These two items fit the idea of ambiguous loss, in which it is unclear what is gone and whether it will return (Boss, 1999), although single items cannot show that AI grief is a form of it.

AI grief was related to anxiety and depression, with correlations of .32 and .33, but was distinguishable from them. Grief before an impending job loss correlated .44 and .37 with these symptoms (van Eersel et al., 2024), and grief after job loss correlated. 63 and .71 (van Eersel et al., 2019). These studies, however, used other instruments and concerned other losses. The structure of the scale was similar to that of scales of grief over job loss and health loss, which fitted one factor after correlated residuals were allowed (van Eersel et al., 2019 (van Eersel et al., , 2024 (van Eersel et al., , 2026)). Like these scales, the AI grief items had one dominant factor with small content clusters, which may explain the poor fit of the graded response model.

AI grief was also associated with attitudes toward AI. Its correlation with fear of AI was within the predicted range, and its negative correlation with acceptance of AI was moderate. Grieving what AI takes may lower acceptance of AI, low acceptance may make losses more visible, or both may reflect a broader negative view of AI, and cross-sectional data cannot tell these apart. Participants who reported more gains reported only slightly less AI grief, and the creative part of work was the area most often marked as gained but was also often marked as lost.

Implications for Research and Practice

The loss measured by the AI Grief Scale can be ongoing and partly anticipated. Whether it also goes unrecognized by others, as theorized for disenfranchised and non-death losses (Doka, 1989; Harris, 2020), remains open, because the short scale on unrecognized loss had low reliability. The scale can be used to study who grieves what AI takes from work and, once its stability and sensitivity to change are established, to evaluate support in counseling and psychotherapy or in the workplace. The areas most often marked as lost, such as learning through one's own effort and the feeling that a result is truly one's own, suggest topics for such support. In this sample, these experiences were on average infrequent, so a grief frame may be relevant mainly to people who report them often, not to everyone whose work AI changes.

The total score should be used without subscores, and the scale is not a diagnostic measure. The exploratory 7-item version, with one item per facet, kept most of the precision and associations of the full scale and may be useful when questionnaire length matters. Nevertheless, because it was selected and evaluated in the same sample, I recommend the 15-item scale until the 7-item version is confirmed in an independent sample.

Limitations and Future Directions

First, the structure, item selection, and validity were evaluated in one cross-sectional sample, so confirmatory factor analysis in an independent sample, test-retest reliability, and external criteria such as functioning or help seeking are still missing. Discriminant validity was examined only against distress and attitudes toward AI, not against job insecurity, AI anxiety, or an established grief scale. Because every item attributes a loss to AI, the score may also partly reflect a negative view of what AI does to one's work.

Second, content validity rests on items developed with language-model assistance and on a clarity pilot with 16 volunteers, without concept elicitation in the target population, an expert panel, or cognitive interviews (Terwee et al., 2018). Third, although the preregistered population was adults who work or study, the panel was restricted to employed people, so nearly all students also worked. Fourth, all measures were self-reports, which may have inflated the associations (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Perceived losses were always reported before the AI grief items, and three areas of loss overlap in content with three items. Both the fixed order and this overlap may have inflated the association tested by H4, although a sensitivity check without these areas led to the same conclusion. Fifth, the answer never also meant that a statement did not apply, so many responses were zero, which may inflate indices of a dominant factor. Among participants who marked at least one loss, however, H1 and H2 were still supported. Sixth, the graded response model did not fit adequately, so its estimates of measurement precision are approximate. The groups tested for DIF were small, so small DIF may have gone undetected. Items 4, 8, and 12 functioned differently by share of work done with AI, so total scores may not be fully comparable across these groups. Finally, the thresholds for H3, H4, and H7 were set in advance but are arbitrary.

The findings apply to losses at work among employed adults, mostly in the UK, who were recruited through an online panel in 2026 and answered in English. It is not yet known whether they generalize to students, people without work, other languages and countries, or later stages of AI adoption (Simons et al., 2017), and AI grief in other areas of life, such as personal relationships, remains to be measured. A planned preregistered second study will test the structure with confirmatory factor analysis in an independent sample, add test-retest reliability and measurement invariance across AI use and language, include students, and test the 7-item version against criteria set in advance. The short scales on unrecognized loss and on ambivalence need revision before further use.

Conclusion

The AI Grief Scale gives a single score for how often people experience grief over what AI takes from their work. In this sample, these experiences were moderately related to distress and fear of AI and were more frequent among people who attributed more losses to AI. Before the scale is used to evaluate support, its structure and the 7-item version need confirmation in an independent sample.
