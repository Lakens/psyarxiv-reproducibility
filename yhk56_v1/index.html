Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 18 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Anhedonia is a core symptom of depression linked to poorer outcomes in young people. The Anhedonia Scale for Adolescents (ASA) is a 14-item multidimensional measure validated in 11-18-year-olds. We examined its psychometric properties in 1,199 individuals aged 16-25 years, with and without clinical diagnoses. Confirmatory factor analysis supported the original three-factor structure over one-and two-factor alternatives. Internal consistency was excellent for the total scale (α = .92), acceptable to good for the subscales (α = .71-.88) and comparable across age and clinical groups; five-month test-retest reliability was moderate (ICC = .69).

The ASA correlated with the Snaith-Hamilton Pleasure Scale (SHAPS; r = .66) and ₛ depressive symptoms (r = .80), explained variance in depressive symptoms beyond the ₛ SHAPS (ΔR² = .32), and predicted depressive symptoms five months later beyond baseline symptoms. The ASA is a reliable, valid measure of anhedonia across adolescence and young adulthood.

Introduction

Anhedonia, defined as a reduced capacity to experience pleasure, is a core symptom of Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) (American Psychiatric Association [APA], 2013). It is highly prevalent among young people with depression, affecting approximately 50-78% (Malhi & Mann, 2018), and is associated with greater illness severity and poorer outcomes (Gabbay et al., 2015). Despite its clinical importance, relatively few measures of anhedonia have been developed specifically to reflect how it is experienced and expressed by young people and existing measures do not always capture the multidimensional nature of anhedonia (Ma et al., 2026).

The Anhedonia Scale for Adolescents (ASA) was developed to address these limitations (Watson et al., 2021), informed by qualitative research exploring young people's lived experiences of anhedonia (Watson et al., 2020). The ASA is a 14-item self-report measure assessing three dimensions of anhedonia: (1) Enjoyment, excitement, and emotional flattening; (2) Enthusiasm, connection, and purpose; and (3) Effort, motivation, and drive (Watson et al., 2021). Items are rated on a four-point Likert scale (0-3), ranging from "never" to "always", with higher total scores indicating greater anhedonia. The language and content of the ASA were developed to be appropriate and meaningful for young people and to capture experiences of anhedonia that may not be fully represented by existing measures. In the initial validation samples, ASA scores demonstrated excellent internal consistency (α = .93-.94), good test-retest reliability and convergent validity and indicated that the ASA outperformed an existing measure of anhedonia in predicting depression severity (Watson et al., 2021). More recently, a validation of a Chinese translation of the ASA in community and clinical adolescent samples supported the original three-factor structure, demonstrated good reliability, and established cut-offs distinguishing depressed from healthy adolescents (Kuan et al., 2026). A separate Chinese translation of the ASA has also shown good reliability and validity in adolescents aged 13-18 years, although with a different factor structure, which the authors attributed to cultural and sample differences (Ye et al., 2025). Kuan et al. (2026) included clinically diagnosed participants up to 22 years of age, but whether the original Englishlanguage ASA and its three-factor structure extend beyond adolescence, and whether its structure is comparable across older adolescents and young adults, has not yet been examined. In parallel, we have derived empirically-based clinical cut-off scores for the original English-language ASA in the present sample of older adolescents and young adults (16-25 years), extending cut-off determination beyond the adolescent age ranges examined in prior work (Demir-Kassem et al., 2026).

As the ASA was originally developed and validated in adolescents aged 11-18 years, it remains unclear whether its psychometric properties extend into young adulthood. This is important because the transition from adolescence to young adulthood is accompanied by substantial developmental, social, and functional changes, and the experience and expression of anhedonia may vary across this period (Ma et al., 2026). A measure developed specifically from adolescents' experiences cannot necessarily be assumed to retain the same factor structure, reliability, and validity as individuals transition into young adulthood. Establishing whether the psychometric properties of the ASA extend across this developmental period is therefore important for determining its applicability to a broader age range.

It is also important to establish whether the ASA performs consistently across individuals with and without clinical diagnoses. Anhedonia is particularly relevant to depression, but considerable variation exists in its severity and presentation. Individuals with a clinical diagnosis may differ from those without a diagnosis in symptom severity, functional impairment, and experiences of anhedonia, which could influence the performance and interpretation of the measure. Examining the ASA across clinical and non-clinical groups is therefore important for determining its broader research and clinical utility. The present study aimed to extend the validation of the ASA into young adulthood using a large sample of older adolescents and young adults aged 16-25 years, including individuals with and without clinical diagnoses. We examined whether the original three-factor structure of the ASA was supported in this broader age range and assessed the internal consistency and test-retest reliability of the total scale and its subscales. Convergent validity was examined in relation to the Snaith-Hamilton Pleasure Scale (Snaith et al., 1995), and concurrent validity was examined in relation to depressive symptoms measured using the Mood and Feelings Questionnaire (Angold et al., 1987). We additionally examined whether ASA scores and psychometric properties were comparable across older adolescents and young adults and across clinical and non-clinical groups. Finally, we examined the predictive validity of the ASA over time, including its ability to predict subsequent depressive symptoms.

Together, these analyses provide a comprehensive evaluation of whether the psychometric properties and utility of the ASA extend from adolescence into young adulthood.

Methods

Power

Sample size adequacy for the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was considered using both previous simulation-based recommendations and a formal model-based power analysis. CFA models with moderate factor loadings (.50-.70) and at least three items per factor generally require samples of about 300-400 participants to achieve good statistical power (Wolf et al., 2013). In the previous ASA validation study the weakest standardized factor loading was approximately .64, indicating moderately strong item-factor relationships. In addition, a power analysis was conducted using semPower (Moshagen & Bader, 2024). For the specified 14item, three-factor CFA model (df = 74), assuming α = .05, power = .80, and an RMSEA of .05 as the degree of model misspecification to be detected, approximately 194 participants were required. The available CFA sample of 1,199 participants therefore substantially exceeded both the model-based requirement and previous simulation-based recommendations.

The Mood and Feeling Questionnaire (MFQ)

The MFQ is a self-report questionnaire commonly used to screen for depressive symptoms in children and adolescents (Jarbin et al., 2020). It consists of 33 items rated on a three-point scale (0 = not true,1 = sometimes,2 = true), with higher scores indicating higher levels of depression and is one-dimensional. Scores range from 0 to 66. Previous research has reported excellent internal consistency for MFQ scores (Cronbach's alpha .91 to .93), together with evidence of validity and reasonable diagnostic accuracy, with a suggested clinical depression cut-off score of 27 (Thabrew et al., 2018).

Anhedonia The Snaith-Hamilton Pleasure Scale (SHAPS)

The SHAPS is a self-report tool designed to assess hedonic capacity, or the ability to experience pleasure (Snaith et al., 1995). It comprises 14 items, each rated on a four-point scale, covering various aspects of pleasurable experiences in four domains: interest/pastimes, social interaction, sensory experience, and food/drink. Previous research has reported good internal consistency for SHAPS scores (Cronbach's α = .86) (Snaith et al., 1995). Originally, it was scored using a dichotomous system with a total score ranging from 0 to 14. However, a four-point Likert scale has been suggested to better distinguish levels of severity for each item (Franken et al., 2007). In the present study, items were scored 1-4 (total range 14-56), with higher scores indicating greater anhedonia. A recent study with a non-clinical student sample identified a cut-off score of 23.5 for the nonclinical group (Ho et al., 2024).

The Anhedonia Scale for Adolescents (ASA)

The ASA was created in collaboration with adolescents experiencing symptoms of depression and anhedonia (Watson et al., 2021). Informed by qualitative interviews, the ASA is a multidimensional self-report questionnaire designed to measure anhedonia in adolescents aged 11-18 years. It covers various components of the anhedonia experience relevant to this age group. The scale consists of 14 items rated on a four-point scale (0 = never,1 = sometimes,2 = often,3 = always), with higher scores indicating higher levels of anhedonia.

Total scores range from 0 to 42. The ASA comprises three subscales:  ASA S1-Enjoyment, Excitement, and Emotional Flattening: It has 7 items in the scale and can range from 0 to 21.  ASA S2-Enthusiasm, Connection, and Purpose: has 3 positively worded items, with scores ranging from 0 to 9. Items were reverse scored for analysis so that, consistent with the other ASA subscales and the total score, higher scores indicate greater anhedonia.

 ASA S3-Effort, Motivation, and Drive: It has 4 items in the scale and can range from 0 to 12 (see Supplement for scale details).

In the original validation samples, ASA scores demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with α = .93-.94 for the total score and α values ranging from acceptable to excellent .79 to .92 for the subscales. Test-retest reliability of ASA scores was moderate in the original validation sample (ICC = 0.73).

The scale encompasses both consummatory (enjoyment in the moment) and anticipatory (looking forward to future pleasures) aspects of anhedonia, as well as motivational deficits.

This multidimensional approach provides a more comprehensive assessment of anhedonia in adolescents compared to the SHAPS.

Data analyses

We analysed the data using SPSS 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and AMOS 29.0, with additional CFA analyses conducted in R using the lavaan package (version 0.7-2) (Rosseel, 2012). Participant demographics were summarized using descriptive statistics, and associations between questionnaire measures were examined using Spearman's rank correlation analysis. Multivariate Analysis of Variance (MANOVA) was conducted to assess the effects of clinical diagnosis, medication status, and age group on the three ASA subscale scores (ASA 1, ASA 2, and ASA 3). ASA total scores were analysed separately using factorial ANOVA. The analyses included all main effects, two-way interactions, and the three-way interaction. MANOVA results were followed by univariate ANOVAs. Multivariate effects were evaluated using Pillai's trace, and partial eta-squared (ηp²) was reported for univariate effects.

The analyses included 456 participants with complete information on diagnosis, medication status, and age group. Given the unequal subgroup sizes, interaction effects were additionally examined using heteroscedasticity-consistent standard errors and stratified bootstrap sensitivity analyses (2000 samples).

Group comparisons were made to examine the reliability and validity findings between two age groups, 16-19 (older adolescents) and 20-25 (young adults), and between clinical and non-clinical participants. The classification of clinical versus non-clinical was determined by participants' self-reports on whether they had received a clinical diagnosis of depression.

Normality of the data

We checked the normality of the data using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests to guide the selection of appropriate statistical methods. The majority of the total sample and all subsamples, except for the follow-up subsample of 332 participants, had p-values less than 0.05 on these tests, indicating non normally distributed ASA scores. For variables or subsamples that violated the normality assumption, non-parametric alternatives were employed, or bootstrapping with 2000 samples was applied to ensure robust and reliable results.

Internal Assessments

Internal consistency of the ASA total and its subscale scores was assessed using Cronbach's alpha. Internal consistency values were interpreted as follows: values above 0.70 indicate acceptable reliability, values above 0.80 indicate good reliability, and values above 0.90 indicate excellent reliability.

Item analysis

Corrected item-total correlations were examined to assess the extent to which individual items were associated with the overall scale by calculating Spearman's rank correlation coefficients at both Time 1 and Time 2 between each item and the total ASA score (excluding the item in question). A threshold of 0.40 was used to assess whether each item sufficiently contributed to the overall construct (Vaske et al., 2017).

We also conducted Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) in AMOS to validate the three-factor structure of ASA. We compared the three-factor structure to a one-factor solution to evaluate whether the original three-factor model provided a better representation of the underlying constructs. As an additional robustness check, one-, two-, and three-factor CFA models were compared using robust maximum likelihood estimation (MLR) in R with the lavaan package (version 0.7-2). The alternative two-factor model combined ASA 1 and ASA 3 while retaining ASA 2 as a separate factor. Model fit was assessed using robust fit indices (CFI, TLI, RMSEA), SRMR, and the Akaike and Bayesian information criteria (AIC and BIC). Model-based reliability was estimated using McDonald's omega, from the correlated three-factor CFA model using MLR, computed with semTools (Jorgensen et al., 2022).

In line with the reporting guidelines for CFA (Jackson et al., 2009) we examined both univariate and multivariate normality, along with checking for any outliers. In terms of univariate normality, several items displayed positive skewness (e.g., items 12, 7, 10), while some demonstrated negative kurtosis (e.g., items 13, 4, 7). For multivariate normality, Mardia's multivariate kurtosis was used, yielding a kurtosis value of 41.077 and a C.R. of 33.600, which exceeds the threshold of ±1.96, indicating a breach of multivariate normality (Enomoto et al., 2020). The Mahalanobis distance test for outliers revealed the presence of 31 multivariate outliers (Ghorbani, 2019). A sensitivity analysis, conducted by re-running the CFA without these outliers, showed no effect on the model fit, so the outliers were retained. To assess the robustness of parameter estimates in the presence of non-normality and outliers, bootstrapping with 2000 samples and bias-corrected confidence intervals was applied.

Maximum likelihood estimation was used to estimate parameters, with significance set at p < .05.

To evaluate how well the model fit the data, the chi-square statistic was used alongside the Comparative Fit Index (CFI) (Bentler, 1990), Goodness-of-Fit Index (GFI) (Hair Jr et al., 2010), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) (Tucker & Lewis, 1973), Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA). In accordance with prior literature recommendations (Hair Jr et al., 2010), the criteria employed to establish a good model fit included: (1) a non-significant chi-square test, interpreted cautiously because of its sensitivity to large sample sizes (N = 1,199) and therefore not used as the primary criterion for model fit; (2) CFI, TLI, and GFI values exceeding 0.9; (3) SRMR values below 0.08; and (4) RMSEA values ≤ 0.08 indicating acceptable fit and ≤ 0.05 indicating good fit.

We assessed reliability using Composite Reliability (CR), with values above 0.70 indicating adequate internal consistency and values between 0.60 and 0.70 considered acceptable (Hair Jr et al., 2010). We evaluated convergent validity using Average Variance Extracted (AVE), with a suggested threshold of 0.50 (Hair Jr et al., 2010). To assess discriminant validity, we compared Maximum Shared Variance (MSV) with AVE, where AVE > MSV indicates that the subscale is distinct from other subscales. We also examined Average Shared Variance (ASV), which represents the average squared correlation between a factor and the other factors. An ASV value below AVE was considered a less stringent indicator of discriminant validity than the MSV comparison.

Discussion

The present study extended the validation of the Anhedonia Scale for Adolescents (ASA) from adolescence into young adulthood, examining its psychometric properties in a large sample of individuals aged 16-25 years with varying levels of depressive symptoms and with and without clinical diagnoses. Overall, the findings provide support for the reliability, factor structure, convergent and concurrent validity, and temporal stability of the ASA in this broader age range. The original three-factor structure was supported and provided a better fit to the data than both one-and two-factor alternatives in the additional robust CFA analyses, while reliability estimates were broadly comparable across older adolescents and young adults. The ASA was also strongly associated with established measures of anhedonia and depressive symptoms and demonstrated moderate test-retest reliability over time. Importantly, longitudinal analyses indicated that ASA scores predicted subsequent depressive symptoms, even after accounting for baseline depressive symptoms, providing evidence that the measure may capture clinically meaningful aspects of anhedonia that are relevant to later depression.

Factor structure and internal reliability

The CFA provided support for the original three-factor structure of the ASA in older adolescents and young adults. The three-factor model demonstrated reasonable overall fit and performed better than the alternative one-factor model, with additional robust CFA analyses also showing a modest improvement in fit over the alternative two-factor model, supporting the multidimensional conceptualisation of anhedonia underlying the original ASA (Watson et al., 2021). This is important because the ASA was developed from qualitative accounts of young people's experiences of anhedonia rather than conceptualising anhedonia solely as a reduced capacity to experience pleasure (Watson et al., 2020). The present findings suggest that the dimensions of enjoyment and emotional flattening; enthusiasm, connection and purpose; and effort, motivation and drive remain relevant as individuals transition into young adulthood.

Internal consistency provided further support for the reliability of the ASA in older adolescents and young adults. Reliability was excellent for the total scale and ranged from acceptable to good across the three subscales. This indicates strong internal consistency for the overall ASA. Importantly, internal consistency was broadly comparable across age groups and across participants with and without a clinical diagnosis, suggesting that the ASA performs reliably across the developmental and clinical groups examined.

Consistent with the original validation study, the Enthusiasm, Connection, and Purpose subscale (ASA 2) showed lower internal consistency than the other two subscales. In the original validation, internal consistency for ASA 2 was α = .79 in both independent samples, compared with α = .91-.92 for ASA 1 and α = .83-.84 for ASA 3 (Watson et al., 2021). In the present study, the same relative pattern was observed, although internal consistency for ASA 2 was lower (α = .71). This consistency across studies may indicate that the items assessing enthusiasm, connection, and purpose represent a somewhat broader or more heterogeneous aspect of anhedonia than the other ASA dimensions.

Item-level analyses were broadly supportive of the scale, with all corrected item-total correlations exceeding accepted thresholds. One item warranted attention: Item 5 ("I did not look forward to anything") correlated more strongly with the total ASA score than with its designated Effort, Motivation, and Drive subscale. This mirrors the original development study, in which Item 5 initially loaded on the Enjoyment, Excitement, and Emotional Flattening factor before being assigned to Effort, Motivation, and Drive in the confirmatory analysis (Watson et al., 2021). That this overlap recurs in an independent, older sample suggests the item captures both a loss of anticipatory pleasure and reduced motivation. More broadly, the Enjoyment, Excitement, and Emotional Flattening and Effort, Motivation, and Drive factors were highly correlated (MSV = .92, corresponding to a latent correlation of approximately .96), and discriminant validity between them was not supported. A similar close relationship between these dimensions was observed in a Chinese translation of the ASA (Ye et al., 2025). Similarly, Kuan et al. (2026) found that two-and three-factor solutions fitted comparably in a large Chinese community sample but retained the original three-factor structure on theoretical grounds, and it fitted better in their clinical sample. This is consistent with contemporary neuroscientific accounts of anhedonia, which distinguish subcomponents of reward processing, such as pleasure and motivation, while recognising that these processes are closely interconnected and may be difficult to separate through self-report (Ma et al., 2026).

Although the three-factor model was supported and the distinction between these dimensions is theoretically grounded, the substantial overlap between ASA 1 and ASA 3 warrants caution in interpreting them as fully distinct. Their differentiation may be better examined using complementary measures of reward processing, such as behavioural or neuroimaging indices.

Temporal reliability and convergent validity

The ASA demonstrated adequate temporal stability across the approximately five-month follow-up period. Test-retest reliability was moderate for the total scale (ICC = .69) and across the three subscales (ICCs = .52-.66), indicating that the ASA provides reasonably stable measurement over time. This extends the test-retest reliability reported in the original adolescent validation and provides further evidence that the measure remains reliable into young adulthood. Similar temporal reliability estimates across the two age groups also support its use across the transition from later adolescence into young adulthood.

Convergent validity was supported by significant associations between the ASA and the SHAPS, an established measure of anhedonia. The association was strongest for the total ASA and the Enjoyment, Excitement, and Emotional Flattening subscale, while associations were weaker for Enthusiasm, Connection, and Purpose and Effort, Motivation, and Drive. This pattern is theoretically plausible given that the SHAPS primarily assesses hedonic capacity and pleasurable experiences, whereas the ASA was developed to capture a broader, multidimensional experience of anhedonia that additionally includes motivation, effort, connection and purpose. The measures would therefore be expected to overlap substantially without being interchangeable. Consistent with this interpretation, participants classified as experiencing higher anhedonia according to the SHAPS also had substantially higher ASA scores.

The interaction involving effort, motivation and drive is also noteworthy. Participants with a clinical diagnosis of depression who were taking medication and were aged 16-19 reported particularly high levels of effort-related anhedonia. This may suggest that motivational aspects of anhedonia are particularly pronounced in some younger individuals with more clinically significant presentations. However, these findings were not consistently supported by additional analyses and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Replication in larger clinical samples is required before drawing conclusions about developmental or medication-related differences in effort-related anhedonia.

Predictive validity

An important extension of the original ASA validation was the examination of predictive validity over time. Baseline ASA scores were strongly associated with subsequent ASA scores and significantly predicted depressive symptoms approximately five months later. Moreover, baseline ASA remained a significant predictor of subsequent depressive symptoms when baseline depression was included in the model. This suggests that the ASA may capture information relevant to the subsequent course of depressive symptoms rather than simply reflecting concurrent depression severity.

The comparison with the SHAPS provides additional evidence for the potential utility of the ASA. Cross-sectionally, the ASA explained a substantial proportion of variance in depressive symptoms beyond the SHAPS (32%), whereas the SHAPS added less than 1% beyond the ASA. This broadly corresponds with findings from the original validation study, in which the ASA showed stronger associations with depression severity than existing measures of anhedonia (Watson et al., 2021). One possible explanation is the ASA's broader assessment of anhedonia. By incorporating enjoyment and emotional flattening alongside enthusiasm, connection, purpose, effort, motivation and drive, the ASA may capture aspects of anhedonia that are particularly relevant to depressive symptomatology but are less comprehensively represented by measures focused predominantly on hedonic capacity.

The longitudinal findings also suggest associations between anhedonia and depressive symptoms over time in both directions. Baseline ASA scores predicted subsequent MFQ scores after accounting for baseline MFQ scores, while baseline MFQ scores also predicted subsequent ASA scores after accounting for baseline ASA scores. At the subscale level, Enjoyment, Excitement, and Emotional Flattening and Effort, Motivation, and Drive were associated with subsequent depressive symptoms in the primary regression model, whereas Enthusiasm, Connection, and Purpose did not. Although these findings should not be interpreted as demonstrating causality, they suggest that particular dimensions of anhedonia may be especially relevant to the persistence or development of depressive symptoms.

Further longitudinal research with multiple assessment points will be important for establishing the temporal relationship between specific dimensions of anhedonia and depression.

Strengths and limitations

Several strengths of the present study should be noted. First, the large sample enabled evaluation of the ASA across a broader developmental range than the original validation and provided substantial power for examination of its factor structure. Second, the study evaluated multiple aspects of psychometric performance, including internal consistency, factor structure, test-retest reliability, convergent and concurrent validity, and longitudinal predictive validity.

The inclusion of both older adolescents and young adults, as well as participants with and without clinical diagnoses, also allowed the performance of the ASA to be examined across populations with differing developmental and clinical characteristics. Finally, the longitudinal component extends previous predominantly cross-sectional validation evidence and provides initial evidence that ASA scores are informative about subsequent depressive symptoms. Several limitations should be noted. The sample was predominantly female. Clinical diagnoses were self-reported rather than verified by diagnostic interview and were available for only a subset of participants, with fewer diagnosed participants remaining at follow-up. This limited separate longitudinal analyses in clinical and non-clinical groups, and attrition was selective: follow-up completers had higher baseline depressive and anhedonic symptoms and were more often female, so the longitudinal sample over-represents more symptomatic participants. The SHAPS was not available at both time points for the full longitudinal sample, preventing direct comparison of the predictive performance of the ASA and SHAPS; repeated measurement of both would clarify their relative sensitivity to change. Although the three-factor model was supported, discriminant validity between factors was mixed and ASA-2 performed less well than the other subscales, so further work should examine whether items could be refined while preserving the multidimensional content of the original measure. Finally, although reliability estimates were similar across age groups, further research is needed to establish whether ASA scores can be interpreted equivalently across developmental and clinical groups.

Conclusions

The present findings extend the validation of the ASA beyond the adolescent age range in which it was originally developed and provide evidence for its use in older adolescents and young adults. The original three-factor structure was supported, the total scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency and moderate test-retest reliability, and the ASA showed convergent validity with an established measure of anhedonia and strong concurrent associations with depressive symptoms. Importantly, baseline ASA scores also predicted subsequent depressive symptoms, suggesting that the measure may have value for identifying dimensions of anhedonia relevant to the course of depression. Taken together, these findings support the ASA as a reliable and valid multidimensional measure of anhedonia across the transition from adolescence into young adulthood, while highlighting the need for further investigation of the distinction between its component dimensions and its performance in independently characterised clinical samples.

Relationship with depression and clinical characteristics

The strong association between ASA and MFQ scores provides evidence of concurrent validity and is consistent with the central role of anhedonia in depression. Participants with higher levels of depressive symptoms had substantially higher ASA scores, and participants reporting a clinical diagnosis of depression also showed greater overall anhedonia, particularly in enjoyment/emotional flattening and effort/motivation. These findings indicate that the ASA is sensitive to depression-relevant variation in symptom severity. This is consistent with recent evidence that the Chinese ASA distinguishes adolescents with MDD and subthreshold depression from healthy controls, with ROC-derived cut-offs of 21.5 and 15.5, respectively (Kuan et al., 2026), and that a cut-off of 20.5 identifies adolescents with marked anhedonia (Ye et al., 2025).

At the same time, anhedonia and depression should not be regarded as equivalent constructs.

The strong association between ASA and MFQ scores is expected given that anhedonia is a core feature of depression, but the magnitude of this relationship also warrants consideration.

The ASA was specifically designed to provide a detailed assessment of anhedonic experiences, including motivational and affective components that are not comprehensively assessed by general depression measures. Future work should therefore examine the extent to which ASA scores provide clinically useful information beyond overall depression severity, including their relationships with functioning, prognosis and treatment response.

No overall effect of age group was observed for ASA total or subscale scores. This finding suggests that, within the 16-25-year age range examined here, chronological age alone may not be strongly associated with the severity of anhedonia. In contrast, clinical diagnosis was associated with higher ASA scores. Together with the comparable reliability estimates across age groups, these findings support the applicability of the ASA beyond the adolescent population in which it was originally developed.
