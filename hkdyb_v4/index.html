Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Research has typically examined how self-location in the head or heart is associated with traits and behavior, yet its link to relationality-specifically the felt sense of connectionremains underexplored. This study investigated how dispositional self-location predicts the embodied experience of being relationally linked to others and the world. We initially developed the Dispositional Self-Location Questionnaire (DSLQ), an association-based instrument designed to minimize potential subjective bias in participant reporting of self-location. Using this measure, we categorized a U.S. sample of 218 adults who had reported a strong experience of connection as head-locators or heart-locators and assessed them on self-construal, selfexpansiveness, connectedness, and perceived qualities of connection. Head-locators reported firmer self-boundaries, emphasizing independence, rationality, and decision-making. In contrast, Heart-locators scored significantly higher on connectedness to both self and world, and on metapersonal self-construal, describing their connections as close, warm, caring, and authentic. These findings suggest that self-location functions as a somatic attentional stance that anticipates the experiential boundary between self and other: the head favors separation and autonomy, while the heart favors openness and expanded belonging.

Introduction

Across cultures and generations, poets, mystics, and lovers have reported warm sensations of expansion in the heart area, as if there were a presence radiating out to bond with another, with nature, or with the world itself. Beyond metaphor, these descriptions closely resemble what Yaden et al. (2017) describe as self-transcendent experiences, particularly forms of relational self-transcendence marked by softened self-boundaries, heightened warmth, and a vivid sense of unity with others. Self-transcendent experiences are temporary mental states of "decreased self-salience and increased feelings of connectedness" (Yaden et al., 2017, p. 1).

Because these experiences are frequently reported as centered in the chest, they suggest that selflocation-especially heart-location-may shape relational openness. This aligns with phenomenological accounts suggesting that the felt sense of connection is a bodily experience of relatedness, belonging, and interconnectedness rooted in the body's intertwining with the world (Merleau-Ponty, 2013). Thus, variations in self-location may have direct implications for relational experience. Yet despite its significance in human experience, the phenomenon of felt connection has received limited empirical attention.

The self, embodied and intertwined with the environment, extends beyond conceptual representation (Oyserman, 2001) and is grounded in lived experience (James, 1890). It shapes a first-person perspective-the phenomenal self -as an experiential position from which one perceives the world (Braun et al., 2018), anchored in pre-reflective interactions (Gendlin, 1999).

Along with detecting the surrounding world, the self is modulated by core visceral physiological signals (Damasio & Damasio, 2023). The perception of these signals, labeled interoception, configures self-conceptions and helps stabilize the self over time. Among all organs, the heart, along with the lungs, has been shown to provide substantial visceral input (Monti et al., 2021).

Interoceptive accuracy modulates social and emotional processing and helps establish self-other boundaries, with direct consequences for relational capacities (Von Mohr et al., 2021).

Within subjective experience, some bodily areas are more closely associated with the self than others (Howard & Templeton, 1966). Seih and Lepicovsky (2020) defined it as the organ or bodily site where the sense of "I" is perceived to reside. This definition reflects an embodied, ongoing construction of sense-making (Levin, 2024) that further anchors the first-person perspective introduced above. The concept of self-construal assesses an individual's self-image, reflecting the cognitive and emotional aspects of their identity. Traditionally, individual selfconstrual was categorized as either independent (distinct and unique) or interdependent (interpersonally connected; Singelis, 1994).

Later, a third dimension was added: the metapersonal self-construal (DeCicco & Stroink, 2007), defined as a stable self-understanding in which the self is experienced as profoundly interconnected with all humanity and nature-not merely with close others, but with a wider relational field that includes the non-human world. This construct is distinct from selftranscendence as characterized by Yaden et al. (2017), which refers to temporary mental states of decreased self-salience; the metapersonal self, by contrast, is a dispositional self-construal-a habitual way of understanding the self's relational boundaries. It also differs from universality orientation, which centers on spiritual or mystical identification with the cosmos (Cloninger et al., 1994); the metapersonal self does not require a spiritual framework but instead extends the scope of relational self-construal beyond the interpersonal to include connection to nature and the world. This distinction matters because mainstream social psychology has traditionally defined connection primarily as interpersonal ties (Aron & Aron, 1996), and even when broader connections are acknowledged, they are often minimized or categorized as "spiritual" rather than taken as serious relational phenomena (Baumeister & Leary, 1995).

Phenomenologically, self-location refers to the location within the body-relative space where attention is interoceptively felt to originate, also termed the egocenter (Hartelius et al., 2022). As such, it is distinct from self-concept, which encompasses beliefs and narratives about the self (Gallagher, 2000). Neuroscientifically, self-location refers to where one experiences oneself to be localized in space (Ionta et al., 2011, p. 369). Evidence suggests that changes in self-location within the body correspond to shifts in cortical activation patterns-particularly in the beta and gamma bands (Hartelius et al., 2022)-and in global cognitive states (Dambrun et al., 2023; Hartelius et al., 2025), indicating that its constructed nature does not diminish its functional reality.

Recent proposals suggest that self-regulation of the location from which the felt sense of self attends has direct impact on the global cognitive state-a configuration of the cognitive environment such that psychological capacities and resources are prioritized in a specific manner (Hartelius et al., 2023 (Hartelius et al., , 2025)). Initial evidence supports this view: inducing a specific attentional stance in meditation novices can produce stress and mindfulness outcomes similar to those of experienced practitioners (Hartelius et al., 2025). This finding suggests that shifts in self-location may activate cognitive mechanisms related to those underlying meditative states (Dambrun et al., 2023).

Neurophenomenological studies indicate that the boundaries of the perceived sense of self can soften or dissolve, altering senses of ownership and agency (Ataria et al., 2015; Lindahl et al., 2017; Nave et al., 2021). For example, body scan meditation can shift self-perception from a narrative to a minimal self, fostering self-loss, decentering, and a sense of oneness (Dambrun, 2016; Dambrun et al., 2019). Research shows that self-centeredness generates separateness, whereas dissolving self-boundaries promotes interconnectedness (Ataria et al., 2015; Dambrun, 2023; Trautwein et al., 2016). All studies concur that this weakening or dissolution of boundaries occurs when thoughts quiet, and self-location shifts away from the head (Dambrun, 2017; Nave et al., 2021).

Self-location also predicts psychological functioning, affecting personality, emotions, cognition, task performance (Fetterman & Robinson, 2013), decision-making (Adam et al., 2015), and well-being (Dambrun et al., 2023; Hanley et al., 2021; Irvin et al., 2023); in particular, distribution of the embodied sense of self over a greater area of the body-especially within the torso-is associated with greater psychological well-being than single-pointed selflocation or self-location in the head. Neuroscience identifies the temporo-parietal junction (TPJ) as a key region integrating multisensory information to produce a coherent sense of bodily selflocation within space-distinct from the felt location of the self within the body-and firstperson perspective (Blanke, 2012). Experimental modulation of the TPJ can alter perceived selflocation (Ionta et al., 2011). Individuals can shift their seat of attention toward head, heart, or other areas-each potentially linked to distinct cognitive and relational modes.

Consistent with this, self-location in the heart area is associated with caring, empathy, emotionality, warmth, intuitive thinking, and happiness, whereas head self-location is associated with knowledge, rationality, and individuality (Dambrun, 2023; Fetterman & Robinson, 2013; Pellerin et al., 2022). These associations suggest two relational modes: one oriented toward separation and autonomy, and one toward relational openness and self-transcendence. While not yet demonstrated, heart-based self-location may also relate more closely to cardiac processes, which convey emotional information to the brain via afferent pathways (Armour, 1991; Lacey & Lacey, 1978).

Heartbeat-evoked responses correlate with the sense of self ("I" and "me") in the default mode network, where subjective and emotional states are processed (Azarias et al., 2025; Babo-Rebelo et al., 2016; Molnar-Szakacs & Uddin, 2013), and anecdotal evidence from hearttransplant recipients suggests possible personality changes (Kaba et al., 2005; Liester, 2020; Pearsall et al., 1999; Poole et al., 2009). Preliminary work suggests that cardiac processes may contribute to interpersonal synchrony, with studies documenting physiological interactions between individuals in close proximity (Mandel, 2007; Russek & Schwartz, 1994). Recent work on heart-to-heart synchrony indicates that shared physiological states may facilitate healing (Yount et al., 2021). Although these findings require cautious interpretation, they underscore the plausibility that heart-centered self-location may predict relational experience.

The Present Study

Most self-location research has traditionally examined its impact on the individual as an egoic entity with defined boundaries, identity, and narrative while overlooking its potential relational implications. Yet, if the boundaries of the located self can be softened or expanded, then self-location may shape felt connection or separateness. Additionally, as research has shown, feelings may stem from both rational reasoning and interoceptive perceptions. As a result, a felt sense of connection can be categorized into at least two types: one rooted in cognitive and rational processes, and the other in somatic and interoceptive experiences. To date, no research has identified differences in the qualities of connection between cognitive-rooted and interoceptive-rooted feelings, nor has any investigated these differences among head selflocators and heart self-locators. Because heart self-location is conceptually close to a relational transcendent mode, it seems highly likely that an expansive experience of self-location in the heart area reflects a psychological reality underlying popular notions of a radiant emotional heart. The present study, therefore, examines whether self-location in the heart area is associated with greater connectedness and less separateness, and identifies qualities associated with relationality, offering an initial empirical investigation of the relational implications of heartbased self-location. We propose that self-location functions as a somatic attentional stance that anticipates the experiential boundary between self and other: the head fosters separation and autonomy, while the heart fosters openness and expanded belonging. The present study tests this proposition empirically.

Method

Building on these findings and numerous reports of a recurring sensation of emotional expansion in the heart area across cultures and generations, we designed a cross-sectional, quasiexperimental study using a purposive sample comparing head-locators and heart-locators. The research question asked how individuals with a heart-located self might differ from those with a head-located self in dimensions of self-concept, self-construal, attentional stance, aspects of connectedness, and qualities of experience associated with an emotional connection to others. Scores on self-concept, self-construal, and attentional stance were used as context for examining the felt sense of connection.

To improve reliability and reduce conceptual biases inherent in direct self-location inquiries (such as social desirability bias, or biases related to the values and beliefs associated with these highly symbolic and semantically loaded bodily areas) inherent in asking people directly where individuals feel their "I" resides in their bodies, or in asking them to place a dot on a body map as an analogy, we developed an indirect measurement tool: the Dispositional Self-Location Questionnaire (DSLQ). This association-based approach evaluates links between cognitive or emotional faculties and perceived bodily locations-an assessment previously unavailable.

We hypothesized: • Hypothesis 1: Heart self-locators will report significantly more often self-sensations in the chest area of a visual body map (Attentional Stance) than head self-locators.

• Hypothesis 2: Heart self-locators will score higher on measures of interdependent and metapersonal self-construal, whereas head self-locators will score higher on a measure of independent self-construal.

• Hypothesis 3: Heart self-locators will score higher on the transpersonal subscale of selfexpansiveness (e.g., sense of identity connects to all life through all time and space).

• Hypothesis 4: Heart self-locators will score higher on connectedness to self, others, and world.

• Hypothesis 5: Heart self-locators will rate emotional qualities of connection (warmth, heartfelt) higher than rational qualities (respect, appreciation).

Participants

Participants (N = 218) resided in the United States, aged 25-65, fluent in conversational English, not taking psychotropic substances, not diagnosed with a serious psychiatric or neurological disorder, and reporting having experienced a strong emotional connection with another person (or pet). Participants of any sex, race, educational level, and religious traditions were eligible. Recruitment was done through Qualtrics® and personal social networks. Due to the quotas assigned by Qualtrics, the participants were skewed toward the 25-34 and 55-65 age groups compared to the U.S. population reported by the 2022 United States Census Bureau (U.S. Census Bureau, 2022, Table S0101). The California Institute of Integral Studies Human Research and Review Committee approved the experimental procedures, and participants provided written informed consent.

Materials

Choices of head and heart as focal self-location stem from prior findings indicating individuals commonly associate identity with the head or heart rather than other body parts or external places, at least in a Westernized context and scientific paradigm (Adam et al., 2015; Alsmith & Longo, 2014; Anglin, 2014; Limanowski & Hecht, 2011). Participants were informed that the study investigated the feeling of connection, but not that it also examined differences in head and heart self-location areas.

Selection Questionnaire: The Dispositional Self-Location Questionnaire (DSLQ)

The DSLQ was developed and tested to distinguish between head and heart self-location.

Unlike single-item measures that ask participants to explicitly identify their self-location, the DSLQ mainly uses an indirect, association-based approach. Aside from one direct question, it triangulates the respondent's dispositional location through cognitive and emotional qualities (Sester, 2024). This design minimizes social desirability or conceptual answering (e.g., believing one "should" identify as heart-located) by focusing on felt experience (e.g., "Where in your body do you feel closeness?").

It initially comprised 14 items based on results from relevant studies (Adam et al., 2015; Fetterman & Robinson, 2013), which found associations between cognitive or emotional faculties and head versus heart locations. For each of the items, the maximum score was +1, representing the response expected from a heart-located individual, or -1, representing the response expected from a head-located individual. Other responses were scored 0. For example, item 4, a question with four possible answers, asked: "When you feel close to someone, where in your body do you feel the closeness? Choose one among liver, heart, brain, somewhere else or don't know", for which the score was 0, +1, -1, and 0, respectively.

Validation. Initial reliability analyses indicated excellent internal consistency for the full 14-item scale (Cronbach's α = .91; McDonald's ω = .91). However, among the 14 items, four of them had a two-point scale (1, 2), and mixing response formats is not recommended in scale construction. To ensure conceptual and statistical coherence, we retained the 10 items with uniform three-point response (0, 1, 2; see Appendix A) options and subjected these to Rasch analysis.

Psychometrics. Rasch analysis of the 10-item scale indicated adequate psychometric quality (see Table S1 in Supplemental Material, appended ). All items demonstrated acceptable model-data fit, with Infit and Outfit values well within recommended thresholds. Only Item 7 showed a slightly elevated Infit statistic (1.28), which should be monitored in future studies, but did not exceed critical cutoffs. Item difficulty estimates spanned a broad range (-1.73 to +1.07 logits), ensuring adequate coverage of the latent construct. Point-biserial correlations were consistently high (.70-.85), confirming that each item contributed meaningfully to the total scale score and that redundancy was minimal. The measure thus offers a reliable and valid tool for distinguishing between groups.

Attentional Stance Assessment (Body Map)

Attentional stance was measured using a somatic phenomenology body map (SP Body Maps; Hartelius, 2021), a visual analog scale that allows participants to digitally indicate the felt location of their self on a body chart divided into seven zones (head, chest, right arm, left arm, abdomen, pelvis, and legs). Participants were asked to click in one of these areas to indicate where they felt their sense of self was located at that moment (state), and this click was recorded as a point in the body outline. In this study, identification of self-location in the heart was scored as +1, and self-location in the head was scored as zero.

Self-Construal

Self-construal was measured with (a) the Independent and Interdependent Self-Construal Scale (SCS; Kitayama et al., 2014; Singelis, 1994) and (b) the Metapersonal Self (MPS; DeCicco & Stroink, 2007) scale. The SCS assessed individuals' self-image as either independent (separate and unique) or interdependent (interpersonally connected), using a 7-point Likert scale. The Independent subscale showed satisfactory reliability (α = .80; 15 items), while the Interdependent subscale demonstrated good reliability (α = .86; 15 items). The MPS measured the extent to which individuals identify with an expansive, relationally connected self-construal, also using a 7-point Likert scale (α = .87; 10 items).

Self-Concept

Self-concept was evaluated with the Self-Expansiveness Level Form (SELF) (Friedman, 1983), which quantifies self-concept expansion in time and space using a 5-point Likert scale across three levels: personal (α = .81; 5 items), middle (α = .87; 8 items), and transpersonal (α = .83; 5 items).

Dimensions of Connectedness

Dimensions of connectedness were measured using the Watts Connectedness Scale (WCS) and the Inclusion of Other in the Self (IOS) Scale. The WCS (Watts et al., 2022) assessed felt connectedness using a 0-100 sliding scale across three dimensions: connection to self (α = .80; 6 items), others (α = .71; 6 items), and world (α = .86; 7 items). The IOS (Aron et al., 1992) used seven Venn-like diagrams to measure the degree of perceived overlap between self and other, ranging from simple juxtaposition to complete overlap, in three different relationship contexts: intimate partner, close family, and friends (α = .67; 3 items).

Qualities of Connection

Qualities of Connection were measured using the Qualities of Connection Measure (QCM), a single-item instrument developed specifically, using a 5-point Likert scale to assess the perceived presence of respect, appreciation, warmth, and heartfelt qualities in the experience of connection with others. We a priori categorized respect and appreciation as cognitive and rational tendencies, and warmth and heartfelt feelings as somatic and interoceptive tendencies.

The question was: "When connecting with others, how much do you experience each of these qualities? Cronbach's alpha was .88 with 4 items.

Procedures

We employed a quantitative, cross-sectional, quasi-experimental design. The study used a between-subjects design, stratifying participants into two groups (Head-locators and Heartlocators) based on their scores on the Dispositional Self-Location Questionnaire (DSLQ).

Recruitment was conducted both via Qualtrics® and personal social networks. Participants were informed that the study would examine the nature of a strong feeling of connection with others (as in emotional connection, rather than erotic or sexual touch connection) and investigate what it means to engage with the world from a state of connectedness. No indication was given in the recruitment materials or during the survey process that the study was investigating self-location.

All candidates were directed to the Qualtrics platform for selection. To create a clear boundary and select individuals situated distinctly in one of the two zones, while excluding more ambiguous respondents, we eliminated those scoring between -5 and +5 on the DSLQ. The 105 candidates who scored a minimum of +5 on the DSLQ constituted the primary group (Heart-located), whereas the 105 candidates who scored a minimum of -5 constituted the comparison group (Head-located). The recruitment process was repeated with incremental selections until the primary and comparison groups were matched by gender, age, and level of education. The selected candidates were then invited to complete the study measures. The final Heart group consisted of 115 participants (105 from Qualtrics, 10 from social networks), and the Head group consisted of 103 participants (all from Qualtrics).

Data Analysis

A power analysis using the G*Power app determined the sample size, indicating that two groups of 105 participants each would require an effect size of Cohen's d = 0.5, thereby allowing the use of p-values in hypothesis testing. SPSS v.27 was used for data analyses. The SELF, MPS, SCS, WCS, IOS, and QCM assessments were subjected to Pearson correlations and one-way ANOVA (i.e., t-tests), which allowed us to control for other variables (e.g., age, education) and assess validity. False Discovery Rate (FDR)-adjusted Benjamini-Hochberg p-value at a q < .05 level of false discovery rate-was applied post hoc to control for the elevated risk of type I error rates associated with conducting multiple comparisons. The FDR is reported as q-value. The SP Body Map was analyzed by performing a chi-square test. A post hoc ANOVA test was performed for analysis of the nine qualities of connection included in the DSLQ.

Our analytical approach prioritizes effect sizes and confidence intervals over sole reliance on p-values, reflecting our view that the practical significance of group differences is as important as their statistical significance.

Discussion

The primary aim of this study was to assess whether the somatic location of the selfhead versus heart-predicts the felt sense of connection (Gendlin, 2000; Yaden et al., 2017).

This study robustly supports two distinct somatic phenomenological profiles: head-locators inclined toward strong interpersonal boundaries and a rational focus, and heart-locators with preference for felt connection and emotional warmth. The DSLQ's triangulation with body maps supports the notion that head-location and heart-location are lived experiences, not mere metaphors.

Head-locators did not report any lack of interpersonal connection, scoring similarly to heart-locators on Respect and Appreciation on the QCM. However, their mode of relating relies more on higher-order cognitive processes rather than felt emotions. Lower scores on MPS suggest that head-locators maintain a firmer distinction between self and other, viewing relationships as interactions between separate entities rather than in a field of shared experience.

The validation of the DSLQ through the body map and the post-hoc analysis of the qualities of decision-making and knowing reinforces the validity of this group's unique characterization. The experiential functioning of the self, for head-locators, is likened to that of a control center, prioritizing accuracy and individuality over the diffusion of boundaries.

Heart-locators frequently indicated having the experience of a felt sense of connection, particularly characterized by warmth and heartfelt qualities. The robust correlation with the MPS scale (q < .001) suggests that heart-locators tend toward the metapersonal self-construal: a stable self-understanding in which the self is experienced as profoundly interconnected with all humanity and nature (DeCicco & Stroink, 2007). This is not merely a broader form of interdependence. The interdependent self-construal remains anchored in interpersonal relations-the self as connected to close others. The metapersonal self, by contrast, extends the relational field to include connection to the natural world and to life as a whole, or life itself, without requiring the spiritual or mystical framing that constructs such as universality orientation presuppose (Cloninger et al., 1994). Equally, it should be distinguished from self-transcendence as a temporary experiential state (Yaden et al., 2017): the MPS captures a dispositional way of construing the self, not a transient peak experience. This distinction is theoretically important because mainstream social psychology has predominantly defined connection as interpersonal (Aron & Aron, 1996), and even literature that acknowledges connection beyond the interpersonal tends to marginalize it, broadly labeling such connections "spiritual" or similar (Baumeister & Leary, 1995). The present finding that heart-locators score significantly higher on MPS-while not differing from head-locators on the interdependent subscale-suggests that heart-based selflocation may specifically facilitate this broader, metapersonal mode of relating rather than merely intensifying interpersonal closeness. Therefore, it can be considered that heart-locators do not just experience themselves as socially interdependent as much as deeply interwoven with the fabric of the world. This is consistent with their higher scores on the Watts "Connection to

World" subscale but not necessarily on the IOS "Friends" subscale. Their connection may be understood as a state of being characterized by warmth and authenticity rather than a social accumulation of friends.

The qualitative experience of connection reported by heart-locators appears to be situated in what Gendlin (2018) called the felt sense: a bodily knowing that precedes language. It may be that self-location in the heart area facilitates interoceptive connection with the chest, a region rich in interoceptive afferents associated with emotional safety and bonding, thereby prioritizing access to pre-reflective experiences of relatedness. This is consistent with the view that selflocation is an attentional stance that enhances access to specific cognitive resources: head-location facilitates separation and distinction, while heart-location prioritizes connection through a softening of self-other boundaries.

These results invite a theoretical comparison with neuroscientific accounts of the temporo-parietal junction (TPJ) and default mode network. We advance the following interpretation as a hypothesis for future empirical validation, as no neural data were collected in the present study. As noted by Blanke (2012) and Ionta et al. (2011), the TPJ mediates between "egocentric" (self) and "allocentric" (other) perspectives through multisensory integration.

Simultaneously, as noted by Babo-Rebelo and colleagues (2016), heartbeat-evoked responses that correlate with the sense of self ("I" and "me") modulate cognitive awareness in the default network. The distinct profiles observed in our study suggest that heart-locators are likely prioritizing a different set of psychological resources than head-locators. If the TPJ modulation can shift self-location, and if self-location correlates with connectedness, then the felt sense of connection may represent a complex interweaving of psychological traits and distinct neurophysiological states. The head stance may represent a TPJ-mediated egocentric mode required for analytical tasks, whereas the heart-located state may represent a down-regulation of the boundary-enforcing mechanisms typically associated with the head-located perspective.

Whether these proposed correspondences between self-location profiles and neural mechanisms hold will require targeted neuroimaging or neuromodulation designs in future work.

The word "heart," when referring to lived experience, seems not merely a symbol used to designate some abstract or poetic idea but, for some individuals, is strongly associated with a self-sensation located in the upper torso. Expressions such as "living from the heart" appear to be rather more than a metaphor. The physical organ of the heart appears capable of responding to and participating in these localized sensations (Loescher et al., 2025; Yount et al., 2021), but the neurophysiology of subjectivity is a new research area, and no study has yet demonstrated a clear link. Substantial evidence shows that a heart-located stance is associated with enhanced selfregulation and emotional well-being, as well as increased happiness (Dambrun et al., 2024; Dambrun et al., 2023; Irvin et al., 2023).

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional, correlational design precludes causal inference; it is unclear whether heart-location causes connection, or whether highly connected individuals tend to situate their attention in the heart area. Second, all measures relied on self-report, including the DSLQ, and no behavioral or physiological validation was conducted. Physiological boundary dissolution, for instance, was not measured. The DSLQ's convergent and discriminant validity-particularly against established interoception measures- has not yet been established.

Post hoc analysis showing that even heart-locators most often attribute decision-making to the brain suggests that participants possess a nuanced, non-binary understanding of their own functioning, supporting prior findings on the flexibility of temporary self-location in contextual situations. Third, the sample was entirely American and recruited through purposive sampling of individuals who reported having experienced an intense sense of connection, which may have favored those more oriented toward relationality, even among the subsample of individuals who self-identified as being in the head. Recruiting the heart-located group was notably faster than the head-located group, perhaps because those with a heart-located self may have greater interest in the study of interpersonal connection. Head-located females aged 55-65 required the greatest effort to recruit. These patterns may reflect sample bias. The targeting was appropriate for our exploratory study, but cultural generalizability is therefore limited. Fourth, although participants were deliberately kept unaware that the study examined self-location, reducing demand characteristics, these cannot be fully eliminated in a self-report design. Finally, these findings require replication with new samples drawn from diverse populations (e.g., various cultural and ethnic groups, meditators, neurodivergent individuals), particularly given the U.S. purposive sample, as well as stability reports.

Future Directions

Several directions follow from these limitations. Neurophenomenological measures (e.g., extrapersonal space testing) could assess physiological boundary dissolution, using meditators able to accurately report associated self-location. Longitudinal studies should test the efficacy of these findings over time and across situations to verify the extent of dispositional self-location and context-specific responses, and further studies should investigate the modifiability of these states. Based on the present data, two main adjustments to the DSLQ are anticipated. The option "liver" should be replaced with "gut," which corresponds to distinct somatic intuitions, and certain items reworded to reduce potential semantic associations (e.g., in item #2, the contrasting choice to "interpersonally warm" should become "interpersonally reserved" rather than "interpersonally cold"; Sester, 2024). The focus on head and heart, although empirically grounded and supported by participant responses with multiple choices and/or availability across all body regions, should continue to be challenged. Similarly, the cultural meanings of body organs or regions vary across history and geography, requiring careful examination.

Transcultural validations are needed. On the clinical level, learning to shift the source of attention toward the chest may help restore a sense of connection in individuals who suffer from social anxiety. More broadly, stepping out of the head-and its tendencies toward control-may help broaden the range of adaptive responses and emotional intelligence and open access to meaning by touching what goes beyond the ego and the rational.

Conclusion

This study provides empirical evidence that supports the notion that the located sense of self in the body relates to how individuals engage with the world. The head self-location fosters a sense of individuality, rationality, and boundary-setting as necessity. Somatic self-location centered in the heart area seems likely to foster the warm, expansive felt sense of connection described by poets, mystics, and lovers, a moving transpersonal experience of connection that bridges the gap between self and other.
