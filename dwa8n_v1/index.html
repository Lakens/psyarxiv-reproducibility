How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Objective: Solitary drinking is a low base rate behavior present in some young adults that could be an indicator for the development of later alcohol use problems. The present work will use a combination of observational data, Directed Acyclic Graphs, and latent variable models to test a between-person causal model of the role of solitary drinking in the etiology of alcohol use disorder risk in young adults.

Method: Participants were young adults at baseline (n = 496, aged 18-22, Mage = 20.3, SD = 1.3) recruited from the Pacific Northwest who reported drinking or using cannabis "about once per week" or more over the past three months. Participants completed a baseline assessment and about 32 days of ecological momentary assessments, answering questions about negative emotionality, social withdrawal, urgency, and their alcohol use.

Testing the solitary drinking component of the social-contextual framework for AUD risk among young adults using Directed Acyclic Graphs

Seventy-five percent of adolescents and young adults aged 18-25 in the United States have consumed alcohol (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration [SAMHSA], 2023). However most young adults do not develop drinking problems. For instance, in 2023 about 15% of young adults met criteria for an alcohol use disorder (AUD; SAMHSA, 2023). Given most young adults drink, but only some develop drinking problems, it is important to understand how and why some people develop an AUD, while others do not. Although social influences on drinking have long been a focus of study (Chassin et al., 2016; Sher et al., 2005), more recent research has suggested that the social context of drinking episodes themselves may be a distinct risk factor (Corbin et al., 2020; Creswell, 2021; Creswell et al., 2014 Creswell et al., , 2022;; S. E. King et al., 2025; Skrzynski & Creswell, 2020; Waddell & Acuff, 2025).

Specifically, solitary (i.e., physically alone) and social (i.e., other people present) drinking are argued to be distinct processes (Cooper, 2015, p. 25; Creswell, 2021; Fairbairn & Kang, 2025) with different risks for future alcohol use disorder (Creswell et al., 2014 (Creswell et al., , 2022;; Sayette et al., 2016). Although social drinking is common among young adult drinkers (O'Malley et al., 1998; Sayette et al., 2016), solitary drinking is thought to be more clinically relevant to alcohol use disorder development because it is a low base rate behavior that deviates from social norms (Kouzis & Labouvie, 1992; Skrzynski & Creswell, 2020).

Approximately 14-40% of adolescents and young adults report any solitary drinking, with lower proportions in primarily college samples and higher proportions in primarily clinical samples (Armeli et al., 2016; Creswell et al., 2014; Mason et al., 2020; Skrzynski & Creswell, 2020). However, the odds of reporting any AUD symptoms at age 35 are roughly 35-62% greater among those who report any solitary drinking (i.e., age 18 adjusted OR = 1.35, 95% CI [1.12, 1.62], age 23/24 adjusted OR = 1.60, 95% CI [1.34, 1.90]; Creswell et al., 2022). And in one primarily clinical sample, adolescents who drank alone reported more AUD symptoms than their social-only drinking peers (ηp² = .195; Creswell et al., 2014).

Therefore, solitary drinking is a unique, low base rate behavior in young adults that could be an indicator for the development of later AUD.

Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) for Causal Inference

To date, the literature has primarily contained correlational studies of solitary drinking and its predictors and outcomes. Although causal inference is often not explicitly mentioned as a goal of observational studies in psychology, drawing causal implications are often a goal of programs of research that include observational methods (Grosz et al., 2020). For example, Creswell et al. (2014) found that young adults with a higher percentage of solitary drinking episodes also had higher unpleasant emotion scores (β = 0.15) and an increased likelihood of having an alcohol dependence diagnosis at age 25 (OR = 1.4). Skrzynski and Creswell (2020) synthesized this correlational finding with other, mostly correlational, findings to create a framework with causal hypotheses; that individual differences like negative emotionality or social discomfort might cause more solitary drinking, and that more solitary drinking might cause future AUD symptoms (Creswell et al., 2021). To test these causal hypotheses, Creswell (2021) recommended that "Alcoholadministration paradigms combined with EMA protocols…would help establish the necessary directional and causal relationships presented" (p. 23). While it is true that a laboratory and EMA design could test these hypotheses, using tools recommended for causal inference with observational data is a less resource intensive alternative than a laboratory and EMA design (Grosz et al., 2020; Rohrer, 2018). Indeed, the meta-analysis that spurred Creswell (2021)'s framework synthesized each individual effect separately, meaning the true between-person associations synthesized in Skrzynski and Creswell (2020) may be a different magnitude once relevant confounds are considered in an observational design. Therefore, our goal for this study is to use Directed Acyclic Graphs to test the between-person causal pathways hypothesized by Creswell (2021). We plan to estimate specific, clearly stated causal assumptions derived from Creswell (2021)'s framework. Understanding these causal processes, not just associations, is ultimately necessary for theoretical and applied progress, even in observational studies.

Nonexperimental researchers often try to informally isolate causal effects by selecting appropriate covariates, but the assumptions underlying these attempts at causal inference are rarely documented explicitly. Instead, across much of nonexperimental psychology, including the solitary drinking literature, assumptions remain unarticulated, nomological networks remain unrefined, and original findings are misinterpreted or fail to be built upon (Grosz et al., 2020). Without a principled framework for covariate selection, including variables in a regression model simply because they correlate with outcomes can introduce bias rather than reduce it (Westreich & Greenland, 2013; Wysocki et al., 2022).

One approach to formalizing causal assumptions is the use of directed acyclic graphs (Deffner et al., 2022; Rohrer, 2018 Rohrer, , 2024)), which make causal assumptions explicit and provide principled guidance for analytical decisions.

Directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) are an accessible approach to causal inference due to their graphical nature. They force researchers to make their causal assumptions explicit, often revealing assumptions we may not be aware of otherwise. Finally, they provide us with a logical way to decide which variables to include as covariates in our statistical models (Deffner et al., 2022; McElreath, 2020; Rohrer, 2018 Rohrer, , 2024)). Many comprehensive introductions to DAGs are available elsewhere for the interested reader (Cinelli et al., 2024; Elwert, 2013; McElreath, 2020; Pearl et al., 2016; Rohrer, 2018 Rohrer, , 2024)). In the present study, we used DAGs to illustrate more clearly what we mean by a "confound" and provide a framework to guide our decisions about which confounds we should and should not statistically adjust for in our models if we wish to observe causal effects.

It is important to note that simply creating a DAG with ones' causal assumptions does not mean that the DAG is correct. Instead, most DAGs are wrong or incomplete. They may be missing variables, have arrows misplaced, or contain unobserved confounds that we failed to consider. An incomplete or incorrect causal model is still an improvement from no causal model at all (Deffner et al., 2022; McElreath, 2020; Rohrer, 2024; Smaldino, 2017) because it explicitly states the assumptions under which causal inferences could be justified. Publishing a graph that formalizes these causal assumptions improves the ease at which findings are disseminated, mitigates misinterpretations of findings, and aids cumulative progress because other researchers can more easily refine a causal model when starting from an existing DAG than an ambiguous table of regression coefficients (Grosz et al., 2020; Lundberg et al., 2021; Rohrer, 2018 Rohrer, , 2024;; Westreich & Greenland, 2013).

Figure 1 displays one of our DAGs. We explain how we developed this DAG as a quick tutorial for unfamiliar readers. We started with existing data where trait constructs from solitary drinking theory (i.e., anxiety/depression, social withdrawal, drinking motives) and negative urgency are measured at baseline and drinking episodes are measured subsequently via daily EMA. Since trait measures occurred before we observed drinking episodes, we assume trait scores have some causal effect on drinking episodes due to temporal sequencing (Reichenbach, 1991). So, there are arrows from all four baseline measures into solitary drinking.

We included negative urgency, the tendency to respond impulsively to negative affect (Cyders & Smith, 2007; Whiteside & Lynam, 2009), even though it was not mentioned in a recent solitary drinking conceptual framework (Creswell, 2021) for two reasons. Firstly, negative urgency could be a common cause of both coping motives and solitary drinking.

For example, if solitary drinking behavior represents a coping response to negative affect (Cooper, 2015, p. 25; Creswell, 2021), individuals high in both negative urgency and coping motives may be at particularly elevated risk. This possibility is partially supported by Creswell et al. (2015), who found that constraint (another facet of impulsivity; Carver, 2005; K. M. King et al., 2014; Sharma et al., 2014) and the ability to resist drinking during negative affect both negatively related to the presence of solitary drinking. Secondly, negative urgency is associated with, and could be a facet of the broader construct of, negative emotionality (K. M. King et al., 2014; Sharma et al., 2014). This is why we chose to favor negative urgency over other facets of impulsivity in our DAG.

Since our anxiety/depression measure is a broad indicator of negative emotionality, we assume it has some causal effect on social withdrawal and negative urgency because we think of these constructs as more specific expressions of negative emotionality (i.e., arrows from AnxDep to SocWithdrawal and Urg). These three traits related to negative emotionality then cause drinking to cope motives because we think people who say they experience more negative emotionality on average (anxiety/depression), who say they tend to isolate from others (social withdrawal), or who say then tend to act impulsively when feeling negatively (negative urgency) will recall more memories of drinking to cope while completing the survey. So, there are arrows from anxiety/depression, negative urgency, and social withdrawal into coping motives. Note. AnxDep = Anxiety/Depression score from Achenbach Adult Self-Report; SocWithdrawal = Social Withdrawal from Achenbach Adult Self-Report; Urg = Negative Urgency from UPPS-P; SolDrinks = The proportion of drinking episodes that were solitary from 32 days of EMA.

We think anxiety/depression, social withdrawal, and negative urgency are common causes of coping motives and solitary drinking, so part of the association between coping motives and solitary drinking is confounded. By adjusting for anxiety/depression, social withdrawal, and negative urgency in the model, we close these confounding paths between coping motives and solitary drinking. Assuming this DAG represents the true data generating model in the population, when we include anxiety/depression, social withdrawal, and negative urgency in a regression with drinking to cope motives predicting solitary drinking, we will observe the causal effect of coping motives on solitary drinking.

The Proposed Study

In the proposed study, we will test the between-person causal assumptions implied by the solitary drinking component of the social-contextual framework of alcohol use disorder risk in young adults (Creswell, 2021) using observational data, DAGs, and latent variable models (Kurz, 2023 Ch. 14.1.2; McElreath, 2020, pp. 491-498). Firstly, for aim 1, we will test whether and to what extent individual differences from the model, like negative emotionality, social discomfort, coping motives, and negative urgency cause later solitary drinking. Secondly, for aim 2, we will test whether, to what extent, and for how long solitary drinking causes later alcohol use disorder symptoms. Finally, we will investigate how one's level of solitary drinking causes different AUD symptom trajectories with growth models in aim 3. To answer the questions associated with each of our aims, we created DAGs to make our causal assumptions explicit. All our DAGs can be generated in the R script (https://osf.io/7vh5d/files/t8d5h) and are available as figures in a supplemental document (https://osf.io/7vh5d/files/qupf5). The models we plan to test for each of our estimands (see Lundberg et al., 2021) will be preregistered on OSF (https://osf.io/7vh5d/files/6wkne).

Method

Participants were recruited as part of a larger project on the development of alcohol and cannabis use during adolescence and young adulthood (R01 DA047247). Data collection was approved by the University of Washington IRB (#00006464, "Project SMS").

A detailed account of participant recruitment has been written elsewhere (K. M. King et al., 2025), so we only provide a summary here.

Sample

Participants were young adults at baseline (n = 496, aged 18-22, Mage = 20.3, SD = 1.3). Participants were recruited from King, Pierce, and Snohomish Counties in Washington State from both college and noncollege sources via internet advertisements on social media and non-internet methods like newspaper advertisements and flyers. Recruitment began in August 2020 and was completed in November of 2022. Participants were required to be between the ages of 18 and 22 at study screening, own a smartphone, be fluent in English, and reported drinking or using cannabis "about once per week" or more over the past 3 months. Participants were excluded if they were not fluent in English or if they moved to the United States after age 12.

Procedure

All study procedures were online and approved by the local institutional review board. Eligible participants completed an online survey and a virtual training session on the study procedures with research assistants. Following onboarding, participants were texted links to 5 surveys a day, from Thursday-Sunday, with only a single survey on Monday for 8 weeks (i.e., about 32 days of EMA). Finally, participants completed up to four 45-minute follow-up surveys over the span of 2 years, with one survey every six months starting from their onboarding date. Participants were compensated up to $451 for completing all study tasks. $50 for the onboarding meeting and baseline survey, $40 for the 6-and 12-month follow-up surveys, $50 for the 18-and 24-month follow-up surveys, and $1 per EMA survey (up to $176) with a $5 bonus for completing at least 80% of the EMAs each week (up to $40). Some participants were compensated $5 for providing feedback on the protocol.

EMA Measure

Solitary Drinking Episodes

We define solitary drinking as drinking that occurred while physically alone. At each EMA, participants reported who they were with during the past hour with a "check-all that apply" question. Responses included friend(s), romantic partner(s), family member(s), stranger(s), acquaintance(s), friend(s) of a friend, roommate(s), coworker(s), classmate(s), Other, and, no one; I was alone. Only a response of "no one; I was alone," will be used to indicate solitary drinking context. Each morning from Thursday to Monday, participants were also asked about drinking that occurred the previous day (or during the second assessment of the day if they missed the morning assessment). Participants reported whether they drank that day, how many drinks they had, when they started drinking, and for how many hours they drank. We will use social context data from EMAs that overlap with each drinking episode to characterize whether the drinking episode was social or solitary. Drinking episodes will be characterized as solitary only if participants reported being alone for all EMAs collected during the drinking episode.

Analysis Plan

All analyses will be conducted with various regression models using the brms (Bürkner et al., 2024) package. We will use the dplyr (Wickham et al., 2023) package for data processing. All analyses will be conducted in R (R Core Team, 2025) using R Studio (Posit Team, 2025), a flexible open source data analytic software and user interface. This analysis plan is implemented as code in an accompanying R script (https://osf.io/7vh5d/files/t8d5h). We will test our hypotheses using a Bayesian latent variable modelling approach which we tested on simulated data and an independent pilot dataset.

In aim 1, our goal is to estimate the causal effects of negative emotionality, social discomfort, negative urgency, and coping motives on solitary drinking among young adults guided by Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs). To achieve aim 1, we will estimate latent variables for negative emotionality, social discomfort, negative urgency, and coping motives and use these latent scores in a binomial regression within the same model to predict the proportion of solitary drinking episodes we observed during the 32-day EMA period. In short, we will create Bayesian structural equation models. Then we will observe how coefficients differ across models to update our beliefs about the true DAG in the population. For example, if the effect of coping motives on solitary drinking shrinks when we close backdoor paths by adding negative emotionality, social discomfort, and negative urgency as covariates, this would strengthen our confidence that our DAG resembles the true data generating model in the population. If the association instead grows, this will alert us there may be a confound, collider, or descendant in the true data generating model that our DAG did not account for. See the R script for all model details.

In aim 2, our primary analytic goal is to estimate the causal effect of solitary drinking on future alcohol use disorder symptoms. The count of solitary drinking and total drinking episodes will be used as predictors along with latent psychological constructs and other backdoor variables. Alcohol use disorder symptoms will be the binomial outcome. The number of trials for our binomial outcome will be 11 since that is the total number of possible AUD criterion in the AUDDCSI, and the number of criterion endorsed will be the number of successes. We use a binomial model because the AUDDCSI is a count variable, not a continuous variable. We will update our beliefs about the true DAG in the population by observing how the effect of solitary drinking episodes on alcohol use disorder symptoms changes as we close backdoor paths. We will be attentive to potential unobserved confounds, colliders, or descendants present in the true data generating model that our DAG did not account for. See the R script for all model details.

In aim 3, our goal is to estimate how one's level of solitary drinking causes different AUD symptom trajectories. To achieve this, we will estimate growth curve models testing the effect of solitary drinking on the rate of change of AUD symptoms from baseline over two years with observations every 6-months. We except the effect of solitary drinking episodes on AUD symptom trajectories to decrease as we close backdoor paths. If people who solitary drink more develop increasing AUD symptoms over time, we should see a positive effect where greater solitary drinking leads to an escalation of AUD symptoms up to 2 years later, even after closing backdoor paths. This would strengthen existing evidence supporting solitary drinking as a causal risk factor for future AUD in young adults. We will consider unobserved confounds, colliders, or descendants that might be present in the true data generating model that our DAG did not account for. See the R script for all model details.

We took many precautions to avoid convergence errors, but there might be complications we cannot anticipate. If the Bayesian measurement models fail to converge despite optimization attempts, we will first try the same models in Mplus. If the Mplus models fail to converge, we will use mean scores for the latent constructs within brms binomial regression models instead.

All data processing steps will be available in a supplemental R script after we complete our analyses. Here we highlight some of the important processing decisions.

When estimating latent variables within brms, all items contributing to a factor will be mean centered prior to factor estimation. This allows us to estimate a latent factor centered at 0. Since mean centering the items does not impact the covariance matrix, this will not change our factor loadings or variance estimates. Time in growth models will be coded0 = baseline,1 = 6-months,2 = 12-months,3 = 18-months, and4 = 24-months. We will clearly report any deviations if they occur.

We will not exclude any data. We will check for systematic differences between missing and non-missing cases along demographics, all key predictors, and alcohol use disorder symptoms across the 6-, 12-, 18-, and 24-month follow-up surveys. We know we had about 80% retention over time from baseline to the final 24-month follow-up survey.

We will exclude missing observations when necessary to fit our pre-registered models. Model convergence will be checked via the Rhat statistic, effective sample sizes, and trace plots. Model fit will be checked via posterior predictive checks. Our priors are designed to be weakly informative. See the R script for all model priors.
