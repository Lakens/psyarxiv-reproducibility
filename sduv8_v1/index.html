Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

Rumination, or the tendency to passively and repetitively think about one's distress, is a maladaptive form of emotion regulation that confers risk for psychological disorders including posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Current theories suggest that low emotional clarity may prompt rumination. Yet, the effects of rumination on emotional clarity are less clear, with some research indicating that rumination might foster short-term benefits by increasing emotional clarity, and other research suggesting that rumination may further hamper emotional clarity. The goal of this preregistered study was to test these theoretical, within-subject pathways by applying dynamic structural equation modeling (DSEM) to assess the reciprocal relationships between trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity in daily life. Experience sampling data was collected from 238 trauma-exposed college students five times per day for 14 days (N Surveys = 7918). Bivariate DSEM revealed significant reciprocal effects between trauma-focused rumination and low emotional clarity with equivalent strength of these effects. Both traumafocused rumination and low emotional clarity demonstrated significant autoregressive effects suggesting persistence of each process over time. PTSD symptom severity was associated with higher mean levels of each process and with higher inertia of trauma-focused rumination. Beyond these main findings, several other between-subject correlations emerged that provide preliminary insights into the dynamic relationships between trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity. Findings suggest that trauma-focused rumination and low emotional clarity are reciprocally reinforced in the short-term to keep trauma survivors stuck in their distress. The broader theoretical implications for understanding the dynamic, functional interrelationship between understanding and regulating emotions are discussed.

The Reciprocal Effects of Trauma-Focused Rumination and Emotional Clarity in Daily Life: A Mechanistic Evaluation Using Dynamic Structural Equation Modeling

Rumination, or passively and repetitively thinking about one's distress (Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 2008), is a well-established risk factor for posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD; Moulds et al., 2020; Sbazo et al., 2017). Although research on rumination has largely framed it as an emotion regulation response to depressed mood (Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 2008), other negative experiences -such as traumatic events -can also be targets of rumination (Ehlers & Clark, 2000). When rumination is trauma-focused, the content may closely resemble exaggerated negative trauma-related beliefs and distorted cognitions about the causes and consequences of the trauma that are characteristic of PTSD (Criteria D2 and D3 of the Diagnostic and Statistical

Manual of Mental Disorders-5-TR [DSM-5-TR]; American Psychiatric Association, 2022).

However, rumination is differentiated from negative beliefs by its perseverative process that keeps individuals "stuck" in their negative thinking. Several systematic reviews have found that rumination is associated with PTSD symptoms in cross-sectional research and prospectively predicts increased PTSD symptoms in longitudinal research (Moulds et al., 2020; Sbazo et al., 2017). Experimental research using trauma analogues (Ball & Brewin, 2012; Ehring et al., 2009; Zetsche et al., 2009) and, more recently, a gold-standard personal script-driven imagery paradigm (Wisco et al., 2023) further demonstrates that rumination exacerbates PTSD symptoms. Although these findings point strongly to the adverse effects of trauma-focused rumination, they also raise an important question: why do survivors ruminate about their trauma?

Is Rumination a Response to Low Emotional Clarity?

Although more adaptive forms of rumination and self-reflection exist (e.g., reflective pondering; Treynor et al., 2003), understanding the function of the abstract, self-evaluative form of rumination typically focused on negative content has been a major topic of psychopathology research (Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 2008). We therefore focus on the maladaptive form of ruminative brooding (henceforth rumination) in this study, rather than the adaptive form of reflective pondering (Trenor et al., 2003). Early work on rumination suggested that rumination might function as an understandable but ultimately counterproductive attempt at gaining various kinds of insight. For example, control theory suggests that repeatedly thinking about one's problems or anticipating potential future threats can be helpful in reaching one's goals and successful problem solving (Martin & Tesser, 1996). Though it may seem paradoxical, depressed individuals often hold positive beliefs about rumination, for instance viewing rumination as useful tool for problem solving, coping, and deepening one's understanding about the causes and consequences of their problems (Papageorgiou & Wells, 2001; Watkins & Moulds, 2005), and identifying how one feels (Vine et al., 2014). Following a negative mood induction, individuals low in emotional clarity -defined as the ability to unambiguously identify one's emotionsengage in more ruminative thinking (Salovey et al., 1995). Low emotional clarity also prospectively predicts increased rumination among people intolerant of ambiguity, suggesting that rumination might serve as a response to low emotional clarity to help individuals achieve improved emotional understanding (Vine et al., 2014). These findings collectively offer initial evidence that individuals may ruminate at least in part to resolve unclear emotional experiences.

Understanding how rumination affects emotional clarity is important for a few reasons.

First, because emotions have the potential to provide us with information about environment challenges and responses, individuals who are clear about their emotions should be at a distinct advantage (Keltner & Gross, 1999). Second, low emotional clarity has been conceptualized and identified as a transdiagnostic factor involved in many forms of psychopathology (Vine & Aldao, 2014). Third, individual differences in emotional clarity are related to individual differences in subjective well-being (Lischetzke & Eid, 2017). Finally, although emotional clarity may share features with other constructs capturing emotional experience such as emotional awareness and emotion differentiation (Hoemann et al., 2021), these constructs are conceptually and empirically separable (Boden et al., 2013; Erbas et al., 2014). We focus on emotional clarity because prior research has invoked rumination as a mechanism to deepen one's emotional understanding rather than to attend to one's emotions generally or to impact the complexity of one's emotional experience (Vine et al., 2014).

How Does Rumination Affect Emotional Clarity?

Whereas theoretical and empirical evidence indicate that rumination could serve as a response to low emotional clarity, whether ruminating actually helps resolve low clarity, or perhaps further undermines it, is less well understood. These findings are consistent with those from trait-level research, which reflects individuals' perceptions of average or habitual tendencies to ruminate and experience emotions with clarity. Trait research has found that rumination and emotional clarity are negatively correlated in both non-trauma and traumaexposed samples (Pugach & Wisco, 2023; Salguero et al., 2013; Vine & Aldao, 2014). This finding could reflect an inhibitory process at the state level, such that rumination could impair emotional clarity. For example, cognitive avoidance models of rumination (Borkovec et al., 2004; Liverant et al., 2011) suggest that people may ruminate to avoid more unpleasant emotional experiences (e.g., trauma imagery). Similarly, Ehlers & Clark (2000) argue that rumination about traumatic events prevents emotional processing, due to the focus on "what if" and other big questions rather than the details of the trauma memory itself. Consequently, if rumination serves a cognitive avoidance function, keeping distressing experiences at bay could prevent the necessary emotional confrontation needed to understand one's emotional experience.

A smaller but competing set of findings comes from experimental research suggesting that ruminating might produce an increase in emotional clarity and closely related constructs. For example, Lyubomirsky and Nolen-Hoeksema (1993) compared levels of negative mood and perceived self-insight, or the degree to which people report characterological insight into their understanding of themselves others, and the world, among dysphoric and non-dysphoric individuals following either a rumination or distraction manipulation. Both dysphoric and nondysphoric individuals reported increases in both negative mood and perceived self-insight following rumination, relative to the distraction condition. Similarly, in an unselected sample, Pugach et al. (2025) compared individuals randomized to a distraction, rumination, or mindfulness condition on levels of emotional clarity and perceived self-insight following a negative mood induction. Rumination was found to increase both levels of emotional clarity (measured implicitly using reaction times to emotion ratings) and perceived self-insight relative to the other two conditions, while still interfering with mood repair. These findings suggest that individuals may derive from ruminating a sense of increased understanding about themselves, including emotional clarity, if at some cost, at least in the short-term.

A Dynamic Approach to Investigating Rumination and Emotional Clarity

Taken together, existing streams of evidence suggest that rumination and emotional clarity may demonstrate reciprocal relationships that persist over time, although the precise direction of effects remains unclear. Testing this reciprocal hypothesis dynamically is a critical step in rumination research. As a construct rumination is definitionally conceptualized as a form of mood regulation involving a recursive or perseverative loop that tends to persist once it sets in (Nolen-Hoeksema, 1991). Although early experimental research provides strong evidence for its effects on mood (e.g., Nolen-Hoeksema & Morrow, 1993 , see Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 2008 for a review), its cyclical nature itself has to date only just begun to be documented using repeated measurements in daily life (Blanke et al., 2022; Debra et al., 2025; Hossain et al., 2025).

Moreover, to our knowledge the cyclical dynamics of rumination have never been modeled in tandem with a hypothesized mechanistic reinforcer such as emotional clarity.

Importantly, unpacking dynamic temporal associations with rumination would similarly have implications for understanding the psychological function of emotional clarity itself. For decades now, having a clear understanding of one's emotions has been conceptualized as a precursor or mechanistic contributor to more effective or adaptive emotion regulation in the next moment (Barett et al., 2001; Gratz & Roemer, 2004; Vine & Aldao, 2014). However, because emotional clarity has typically been measured as a trait, evidence for or against its widely presumed functional relationship with emotion regulation has been elusive. Even on a descriptive level, the temporal dynamics of emotional clarity are barely known. A recent experience sampling study shows that emotional clarity is viable to measure dynamically, and moreover that it varies across daily life based on state-level factors such as context (e.g., one's familiarity with a situation; Springstein et al., 2024). By leveraging dynamic methods, it is possible to test the presumed dynamic relationship between momentary emotional clarity and emotion regulation.

The Present Study

The goal of this preregistered study was to examine the reciprocal effects of traumafocused rumination and emotional clarity in a sample of trauma-exposed university students varying in PTSD symptom levels. Investigating trauma-focused rumination in university students is important because research suggests that 66% of matriculating college students report exposure to at least one Criterion A traumatic event (Read et al., 2011). Furthermore, traumaexposed university students provide a cost-effective opportunity to examine the relationship between trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity in a non-clinical sample and a valuable point of comparison for future research studying these processes in clinical samples.

Our methodological approach enabled modeling of the dynamic, time-varying properties of rumination and emotional clarity and their dynamic functional relationships with each other, as well as between-subject differences in these effects. To investigate study hypotheses, we used a 14-day experience sampling method to repeatedly assess levels of trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity in daily life. We analyzed their reciprocal associations using dynamic structural equation modeling (DSEM). This approach is ideal for providing insights into the temporal qualities of psychological processes within people over time for a few reasons. First, prior experience sampling research has found that rumination is highly inert -or tends to persist within people from one moment to the next -reflecting the conceptually perseverative and "sticky" nature of ruminative thinking (Koval et al., 2012). Second, DSEM allows for the inclusion of multiple time series, allowing researchers to simultaneously model multiple outcome variables. This method has generated novel insights into how rumination and related processes (e.g., negative affect) become mutually reinforced over time (Blanke et al., 2022). Finally, DSEM permits evaluation of between-subject differences in these within-subject processes to explore variation in how these processes manifest for different people. For example, ruminative inertia appears to be characteristic of people with heightened depressive symptoms (Bean et al., 2020), indicating that there may be important individual differences in these within-person processes. By assessing trauma-focused rumination, specifically, and examining how dynamic properties of rumination and emotional clarity may each be associated with PTSD symptoms, findings from this study may lend clinical insight into why people ruminate on their trauma despite adverse consequences and inform the identification of strategies to interrupt ruminative cycles. Based on our review of prior research, study aims and hypotheses were as follows:

Aim 1: The first goal was to characterize the momentary, within-subject reciprocal associations between trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity. We hypothesized that significant reciprocal (cross-lagged) associations would emerge such that trauma-exposed individuals would engage in trauma-focused rumination following periods of lower emotional clarity (H1A) and that ruminating about one's trauma would be associated with subsequent levels of emotional clarity (H1B). This latter association was hypothesized nondirectionally given evidence that rumination might either improve or impair levels of emotional clarity. The comparative strength of these reciprocal associations was considered exploratory (H1C).

Aim 2: The second goal was to examine the within-subject inertia of each process (trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity), or whether and how much these processes persist for individuals over time. We hypothesized that trauma-exposed individuals would experience significant positive autoregressive (lagged) effects for both trauma-focused rumination (H2A) and emotional clarity (H2B). The comparative strength of these autoregressive pathways was considered exploratory (H2C).

Aim 3: Finally, to fully characterize the dynamic relationships between trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity, we examined between-subject correlations among these within-subject features (i.e., the cross-lagged and lagged effects of trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity) and with PTSD symptom severity. Probing these associations may allow for novel preliminary insights into the theoretical underpinnings of trauma-focused rumination and its role in PTSD. These relationships were considered fully exploratory. Taken together, findings are expected to inform why trauma-exposed individuals engage in rumination despite its deleterious effects on PTSD and interventions that could help interrupt this process.

Method Participants

Participants were 238 students from a large public university in the southeastern United States. One participant was unable to complete the EMA portion of the study due to technical issues with their smartphone. The average age of the final sample of N = 237 was 19.79 (SD = 3.61). Most individuals in the sample were White (n = 96, 40.5%) or Black/African American (n = 60, 25.3%) and female (n = 173, 73.3%). Full demographic details are reported in Table 1.

Participants were eligible for the study if they were at least 18 years of age, able to read and write in English, had access to a "smartphone," and reported exposure to at least one potentially traumatic lifetime event on the Life Events Checklist for DSM-5 (APA, 2022). Ninety participants (38.0%) scored ≥ 33 on the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5; Weathers, Litz, et al., 2013) indicating a probable PTSD diagnosis (Bovin et al., 2016).

Measures

Trauma Exposure. The Life Events Checklist for DSM-5 (LEC-5; Weathers, Blake, et al., 2013) is a 17-item self-rating checklist assessing for exposure to potentially traumatic events (e.g., sexual or physical assault, transportation accident, combat). Participants indicated if they experienced each event and, if so, how they were exposed (e.g., directly experienced, witnessed, learned about the event). Participants who reported multiple events were asked to nominate an index ("worst") event. Participants then answered a series of follow-up questions about the index event such as a brief description of the event; time since the event occurred; and whether the event involved life threat, serious injury/death, or sexual violence. Follow-up questions were used to determine whether the index event met the DSM-5 definition of "Criterion A" trauma exposure, that is, an event involving exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence.

PTSD Symptoms.

The PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5; Weathers, Litz, et al., 2013) is a 20-item self-report measure assessing PTSD symptoms as defined by DSM-5. Participants rated each item on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (Not at all) to 4 (Extremely) based on how much each symptom has bothered them in the past month. For each item, symptom ratings were anchored to the index event identified on the LEC-5. Internal consistency for the PCL-5 was excellent (α = .93).

Momentary Trauma-Focused Rumination.

Participants rated three items at each observation assessing the process and content of momentary trauma-focused rumination (i.e., "I felt weighed down by my thoughts of the event," "I got stuck on certain thoughts of the event and couldn't move on," and "The same thoughts about the event kept going through my head again and again"). Items were rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Not at all) to 7 (Very much). Items were averaged to create an overall index of momentary trauma-focused rumination. Items were selected from psychometric work evaluating the assessment of repetitive negative thought in experience sampling research (Rosenkranz et al., 2020). For all experience sampling questions, participants were instructed to keep in mind the event that they indicated was the "worst event" on the LEC-5 to ensure that our measure of rumination was anchored to the same event used to assess PTSD symptoms and clearly assessed trauma-focused rumination rather than other forms of ruminative thinking. Multilevel reliability values for the traumafocused rumination variable were ω B = .85 and ω W = .98.

Momentary Lack of Emotional Clarity.

Participants rated two items assessing momentary lack of emotional clarity at each observation (i.e., "I am confused about how I feel"

and "I have no idea how I am feeling") on the same 7-point Likert scale used above. These two items were averaged to create an overall index of momentary low emotional clarity, with higher numerical values reflecting greater lack of clarity. Items were selected based on research that has developed and validated a state-based measure of emotion dysregulation (Lavender et al., 2017).

Multilevel reliability values for the low emotional clarity variable were ω B = .73 and ω W = .98.

Procedures

Respondents were recruited from a Psychology Subject Pool advertising a study about emotions in daily life. Participants attended a 90-minute baseline session where they completed informed consent, the LEC-5, and the PCL-5. Experimenters trained in the Criterion A trauma assessment reviewed the index trauma reported by each participant to determine if the event met DSM-5 Criterion A for PTSD. If the self-nominated index event did not meet Criterion A, the experimenter reviewed the other events endorsed on the LEC-5 to determine if any other events met Criterion A. If another event met for Criterion A, this latter event was selected as the index event, which allowed us to minimize false negatives in identifying trauma exposed individuals (Bardeen & Benfer, 2019; Nester et al., 2024). If a participant reported potentially traumatic events on the LEC-5, but none met Criterion A according to follow-up questioning, they selected the most stressful event from the LEC-5 to focus on as their index event. One-hundred eightyseven participants (78.9%) reported an index event that met Criterion A (e.g., physical or sexual assault, life-threatening motor vehicle accident or natural disaster, violent or accidental death of a loved one), whereas 50 (21.1%) reported an extremely stressful event that did not meet Criterion A (e.g., exposure to a motor vehicle accident or natural disaster determined not to be life threatening, sudden death of a loved one due to natural causes). Because definitions of Criterion A have shifted across different DSM versions, and because all participants identified an index event recognized as a potentially traumatic event on the LEC-5, we elected to retain all participants in our analyses. Participants then completed a battery of self-report questionnaires administered on a tablet via Qualtrics.

Following completion of the questionnaires, experimenters assisted participants in downloading "Expiwell" -a smartphone-based experience sampling survey application.

Participants were led through an example survey and were provided with an opportunity to ask questions. Fourteen days of consecutive experience sampling began in the following day, with five surveys administered at pseudorandom intervals every 3 hours beginning at ~9 AM and ending at ~10 PM. A notification was sent during the first 45 minutes of each block. Participants had 15 minutes to respond to each survey and received a reminder if they had not responded within five minutes. Participants could complete up to 70 total experience sampling surveys.

Each survey took about five minutes to complete. Following the experience sampling period, participants returned to the lab once to complete a second lab session, which consisted of two emotion-focused tasks not examined here. Participants were awarded with course credits through the psychology participant pool based on their completion of the first and second lab sessions and the number of surveys completed across the experience sampling period.

Data Analysis Plan

To test for the bidirectional effects of trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity, we modeled a two-level bivariate DSEM, which integrates a structural equation modeling framework with time series analysis. DSEM combines the strengths of each method -namely, the simultaneous modeling of multiple outcome variables, modeling of within-subject residuals and covariances among the residuals as random effects within the same model, and the ability to handle unequal spacing between measurement occasions. This final point is important in experience sampling research as equal assessment intervals are an assumption of models analyzing multilevel data but are typically the exception rather than the rule.

Trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity were modeled as two separate time series and treated as correlated outcomes. Each was modeled as an observed (rather than latent) variable given by the average of the individual items comprising each scale. The temporal aspects of both processes were captured using within-subject autoregressive (lagged) effects, with significant autoregressive effects indicating inertia over time. We also captured the reciprocal within-subject effects of each process on the other using cross-lagged effects to test whether lack of clarity predicts engagement in rumination at the next time point and whether rumination predicts short-term increases or decreases in clarity (see Figure 1). Data were decomposed into a within-subject and a between-subject component. The between-subject component reflects stable person-level (i.e., trait) means and the within-subject component reflects deviations from those means. DSEM models analyzed in Mplus offer standardized results, meaning that the relative strength of cross-lagged effects can be compared (e.g., the effects of rumination on changes in clarity and vice versa). The standardization of these effects is within-subject such that values are standardized based on each participant's person-specific standard deviation. DSEM also permits for random variances of all parameters, their covariances, and within-person standardized correlation coefficients. In other words, in addition to random intercepts, DSEM estimates random effects for each of the pathways of interest -the autoregressive (inertia) effects for rumination and clarity, and each of the cross-lagged pathways between rumination and clarity. This allows for investigation of the magnitude of random effects and correlations between these random effects at the between-subject level (e.g., the correlation between the random effect for ruminative inertia and the random effect for the cross-lagged path from rumination to clarity would indicate whether individuals who ruminate more persistently over time also tend to report lower clarity after rumination). Such exploratory associations of between-subject effects of trauma-focused rumination, emotional clarity, and PTSD severity were examined for all pathways. Finally, DSEM allows for inclusion of random residual (co)variances.

DSEM exists in a Bayesian framework with parameter estimation following a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) procedure. We modeled a bivariate DSEM with two time series (trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity). Parameters were estimated using the fbiter option in Mplus and gradually increasing the number of iterations. Nonconvergence was checked using a combination of Potential Scale Reduction, trace plots, and autocorrelation plots of the parameter estimates. The trace plots of the two chains should be overlapping without spikes or other irregularities. A small autocorrelation between the iterations of each chain is preferred to obtain relatively independent draws from the posterior distribution of the parameters. This is most typically achieved through a process called thinning, where uses only every kth iteration of the estimation. In the final model, we used two MCMC chains with 20,000 iterations and thinning of 100. Default diffuse priors were used, meaning that results were data-driven and not driven by previous assumptions.

We used the tinterval option in Mplus to deal with unequal spacing between intervals and overnight lags. Intervals were set to 3 hours as five surveys were completed within a 12-hour time frame. Within-subject residuals were allowed to vary across individuals. To evaluate whether effects were significantly different from zero, 95% credibility intervals (95% CIs) were used. Credibility intervals that did not contain zero were considered statistically significant.

Within-subject standardized results were used to ascertain the magnitude of reciprocal and autoregressive effects (H1A, H1B, H2A, and H2B). Parameter comparison tests were performed to determine whether the reciprocal and autoregressive effects of each process were significantly different from one another (exploratory H1C and H2C). For exploratory Aim 3, we examined the correlations between the random effects for all parameters. As a robustness check, we examined this model in both full sample and in the subsample of individuals who endorsed a traumatic event that unambiguously met the DSM-5 Criterion A definition of PTSD.

Results

Descriptive Statistics

Descriptive statistics from the experience sampling procedure were examined prior to testing study hypotheses. Participants completed 7848 total surveys (M = 33.54 surveys per person). The compliance rate was 47.9%. The intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) indicated that 48% and 65% of the total variance in trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity, respectively, was between-subject. Average endorsement of trauma-focused rumination across the experience sampling period was M = 1.41 (SD = 1.01) and average endorsement of lack of emotional clarity was M = 1.83 (SD = 1.48). Finally, the within-and between-subject correlations between trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity were each significant at r = .42 and r = .54 (both ps <.001), respectively, indicating a positive significant association between traumafocused rumination and a lack of emotional clarity at the within-and between-subject levels. The correlations between negative affectfoot_0 and trauma-focused rumination, and negative affect and lack of emotional clarity, were all moderate-to-high and significant at the within-subjects (negative affect and trauma-focused rumination r = .62, p < .001; negative affect and lack of emotional clarity r = .49, p < .001) and between-subjects levels (negative affect and traumafocused rumination r = .78, p < .001; negative affect and lack of emotional clarity r = .60, p < .001).

Given the lower experience sampling compliance rate, we evaluated patterns of data missingness by examining whether demographic, trauma/PTSD, and affect variables were associated with compliance rates. Results showed that compliance rate was not significantly associated with age (p = .954), gender (p = .392), race/ethnicity (p = .352), or Criterion A trauma status (p = .866). However, lower compliance was significantly associated with higher PTSD symptoms (r = -.15, p = .023), greater trauma-focused rumination (r = -.18, p = .005), higher lack of emotional clarity (r = -.17, p = .010), and greater negative affect (r = -.13, p = .046).

Model Convergence and Explained Variance

We began by modeling random effects for the within-subject trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity variables, including their intercepts, auto-regressives, cross-lags, and residual variances and covariance effects, consistent with recommendations by methodologists and approaches used in prior affective science research (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2020; Blanke et al., 2021; Houssain et al., 2025). Estimation of between-subject correlations for the residual variances and covariance produced problems for model convergence. Thus, we removed these estimates from the between-subjects portion of the model and instead prioritized estimation of correlations between the hypothesized random effects of interest. Examination of PSR values for all parameters and trace plots suggested satisfactory model convergence for this bivariate DSEMfoot_2 . Overall, the average amount of explained within-subject variance for trauma-focused rumination was 0.308 (95% CI [0.28, 0.33]). The average amount of explained within-subject variance for emotional clarity was 0.286 (95% CI [0.27, 0.31]). The overlapping CIs suggest that the explained within-subject variance was similar across constructs. The full DSEM is shown in Figure 2.

Reciprocal (Cross-Lagged) Effects

Consistent with Hypothesis 1, we found significant reciprocal effects supporting the within-subject pathways from emotional clarity to trauma-focused rumination and from traumafocused rumination to emotional clarity. Unstandardized and standardized estimates of fixed and random effects are shown in Table 2. Non-zero crossing 95% CIs suggest these effects are statistically significant. In other words, higher momentary trauma-focused rumination was predicted by lower-than-usual emotional clarity (i.e., a higher lack of clarity) at the prior observation, consistent with Hypothesis 1A. Lower momentary emotional clarity (i.e., a higher lack of clarity) was predicted by higher-than-usual trauma-focused rumination at the prior observation, consistent with Hypothesis 1B. The parameter comparison test of the standardized reciprocal effects of these pathways, reflecting Hypothesis 1C, was not statistically significant (Wald test = 1.34, p = .247), suggesting that the null hypothesis that these effects are equivalent could not be rejected.

Autoregressive (Lagged) Effects

Consistent with Hypothesis 2, we found significant autoregressive effects for traumafocused rumination (H2A) and emotional clarity (H2B). These effects were positive for both parameters, indicating that at the within-subject level, higher trauma-focused rumination and lower emotional clarity (i.e., higher lack of clarity) at the prior observation predicted persistence of these respective states at the next observation. The parameter comparison test of these standardized autoregressive effects, reflecting Hypothesis 2C, was not significant (Wald test = 0.21, p = .651), suggesting that we were unable to reject the null hypothesis that these effects are equivalent.

Correlations Between Random Effects and PTSD Symptoms

We pursued our final, exploratory aim by examining correlations between random effects at the between-subject level to gain insight into how these dynamic, within-subject properties of trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity are associated with one another and with PTSD symptoms (see Table 3). Higher average momentary trauma-focused rumination (i.e., the intercept of rumination) was strongly correlated with lower momentary emotional clarity (i.e., a higher lack of clarity intercept) and with higher PTSD symptoms, suggesting that individuals who ruminated about their trauma were, on average, more likely to experience lower emotional clarity and higher PTSD symptoms. Average momentary trauma-focused rumination was not associated with either reciprocal effects between rumination and clarity or with inertia of either construct. By contrast, lower average momentary emotional clarity (higher lack of clarity) was associated with greater inertia of trauma-focused rumination but a weaker cross-lagged pathway from lower clarity to rumination, indicating that individuals who generally experience a lack of emotional clarity are more likely to get stuck ruminating about their trauma but are less likely to ruminate following moments of lower clarity.

Inertia of trauma-focused rumination was positively associated with the pathway from trauma-focused rumination to emotional clarity but negatively associated with the pathway from emotional clarity to trauma-focused rumination. These findings suggest that the tendency to get stuck in a ruminative cycle is associated with lower emotional clarity following moments of higher rumination and with lower levels of trauma-focused rumination following moments when emotional clarity is lower. By contrast, inertia of low emotional clarity was positively associated with the pathway between low emotional clarity and trauma-focused rumination but negatively associated with the reciprocal pathway between trauma-focused rumination and low emotional clarity. These results suggest that individuals who find themselves stuck in low levels of emotional clarity over time may be more likely to turn to rumination following moments when clarity is lacking, and they experience a potential short-term benefit characterized by reductions in a lack of clarity following trauma-focused rumination.

Finally, we found mixed evidence that PTSD symptoms are associated with dynamics of trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity. Individuals reporting higher PTSD symptoms were more likely to experience higher average levels of trauma-focused rumination and greater inertia of trauma-focused rumination. Individuals with higher PTSD symptoms also evidenced lower average emotional clarity, but not inertia of emotional clarity. PTSD symptoms were not associated with reciprocal pathways between trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity.

Non-Pre-Registered Sensitivity Analysis

We conducted three follow-up sensitivity analyses to probe robustness of results. First, we sought examine whether model results held after adjusting for time since trauma (in months) and negative affect by modeling these variables as time-invariant covariates (i.e., as cross-level predictors) of trauma-focused rumination and lack of emotional clarity dynamics (i.e., the inertia and cross-lagged pathways). Fixed within-subject effects and between-subject random effect correlations are shown in Supplement Table 1, and standardized cross-level associations between time since trauma and the rumination and clarity dynamics are shown in Supplemental Table 2.

All model parameters replicated in terms of direction and magnitude of effects. All relationships between model parameters were consistent with those reported in the main analysis. Time since trauma was only significantly related to the cross-lagged effect of lack of emotional clarity on trauma-focused rumination such that individuals whose index event was more distal were more likely to ruminate on their trauma following moments of low clarity. However, given the size of this small effect and the non-preregistered nature of these analyses, we caution interpretation of this result. The DSEM examining negative affect as a covariate produced a non-positive-definite covariate matrix, and we were ultimately unable to include negative affect as a covariate.

Second, we estimated another DSEM among the subsample of individuals who reported an index event that met the strict definition of Criterion A (N = 185). Fixed within-subject effects and between-subject random effect correlations are shown in Supplement Table 3. All model parameters demonstrated the same direction of effects and similar magnitudes of effects compared to those observed in the full sample. Associations between model parameters were largely consistent with one exception -the association between PTSD symptoms and traumafocused rumination was no longer significant.

Finally, given the potential for bias based on evaluation of patterns of missingness, we reestimated the DSEM among the subsample of participants who evidenced higher rates of compliance as defined by a compliance score ≥ 1 SD above the mean (N = 51, M = 73%; Bolger & Laurenceau, 2013). Fixed within-subject effects and between-subject random effect correlations are shown in Supplement Table 4. The direction, magnitude, and significance of all fixed within-subject trauma-focused rumination and lack of emotional clarity effects replicated those found in the full sample, as did the direction and magnitude of correlations among these dynamics. However, several of the correlations among these effects were no longer significant in this sensitivity analysis (e.g., the association between trauma-focused rumination intercept and inertia, the association between the clarity intercept and clarity-to-rumination cross-lag).

Similarly, associations between PTSD symptoms are the rumination and clarity dynamics were no longer significant. We suspect this is likely due to reduced power at the between-subject level.

Discussion

This daily life study examined the dynamic, short-term relationships between traumafocused rumination and emotional clarity among trauma-exposed individuals varying in their PTSD symptom levels. We used bivariate DSEM to address why individuals get caught in potentially harmful cycles of trauma-focused rumination (Moulds et al., 2020; Sbazo et al., 2017). By modeling reciprocal (cross-lagged) and autoregressive (lagged) effects and exploring individual differences among these processes, results from this study offer insights into the theoretical underpinnings of trauma-focused rumination. Results suggest small but significant reciprocal effects between trauma-focused rumination and low emotional clarity in daily life. At the within-subject level, our findings point to an intensifying cascade whereby higher-than-usual rumination is associated with subsequent impairment in emotional clarity, which in turn is associated with heightened rumination. By contrast, findings at the between-subject levelreflecting individual differences in these within-subject processes -provided some evidence that ruminating might bring about relief for individuals trapped in cycles of low emotional clarity, at least on the short-term timescales of hours. Individuals with more inert levels of low emotional clarity ruminated more following moments of low clarity and were less likely to experience low clarity after rumination. Consequently, this multilevel investigation offers novel insights into how trauma-focused rumination may be reinforced over time.

Our findings aid ongoing research focused on clarifying mechanistic understanding of rumination. Consistent with prior work, we found that rumination functions as a response to low emotional clarity (Pugach et al., 2020; Salovey et al., 1995; Vine et al., 2014). To the extent that impaired emotional clarity reflects an uncomfortable affective state characterized by heightened distress (e.g., Thompson et al., 2015), results are consistent with response styles theory (Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 2008), which suggests that rumination serves as a response to negative affect that paradoxically maintains and exacerbates negative affect and depressed mood, intensifies self-focused attention, impairs effective problem solving, and increases uncertainty about one's emotional state. From another lens, our findings are consistent with control theory (Martin & Tesser, 1996), which suggests that rumination functions as a response to unsatisfactory goal progress. To the extent that emotional clarity confers information about self-relevant goals (e.g., what goal is threatened, the discrepancy between one's current and desired state, and appropriate responses to resolve this discrepancy), low emotional clarity could trigger rumination as a counterproductive attempt at problem solving and meaning making. Thus, response styles theory and control theory offer useful complementary frameworks for understanding how rumination can represent maladaptive responses to a lack of emotional clarity.

Perhaps more broadly, findings underscore the need to approach emotional clarity as a dynamic rather than static construct. Here, we found that low clarity not only serves as a temporal risk factor for heightened rumination, but that states of low emotional clarity persist for people over time. Several theoretical models invoke emotional clarity as a trait that shapes emotion regulation, with some theories even viewing emotional clarity as a subdomain of emotion regulation (Gratz & Roemer, 2004) or -in more extreme cases -as ontologically indistinguishable from emotion regulation (Gross & Barrett, 2011). Several studies suggest that low emotional clarity is a predictor of maladaptive emotion regulation, which in turn confers risk for psychopathology (Boden & Thompson, 2015; Vine & Aldao, 2014). Experience sampling data similarly show that low momentary emotional clarity is associated with subsequent internalizing symptoms via less successful momentary emotion regulation at both the within-and between-subject levels (Park & Naragon-Gainey, 2019). These findings support the idea that emotional clarity may function as an affective determinant of emotion regulation with downstream consequences for mental health symptoms. We extend prior work by examining autoregressive effects of low emotional clarity, or inertia. Our findings suggest that getting "stuck" in cycles of low clarity may pose risk for periods of worse emotion regulation and, in turn, psychopathology. Despite this, individuals notably report some moments when they are clearer about their emotions than others (Springstein et al., 2024). Capitalizing on moments of higher clarity may provide a means to interrupt harmful cycles wherein low clarity could cascade into problematic forms of emotion regulation, such as rumination.

But does ruminating about one's trauma help or harm emotional clarity? Our results suggest that the answer to this question may be more nuanced than previously believed.

At the within-subject level, trauma-focused rumination appears to impair emotional clarity such that when one is ruminating more than usual, their emotional clarity worsens in the following hours. This finding is consistent with those from previous observational studies suggesting that at the trait level, rumination is associated with worse emotional clarity (Pugach & Wisco, 2023; Salguero et al., 2013; Vine & Aldao, 2014). We found no support at this level for the idea that ruminating on one's trauma offers short-term benefit for emotional clarity. This runs counter to experimental research that has found short-term benefits to emotional clarity following a rumination induction (Lyubomirsky & Nolen-Hoeksema, 1993; Pugach et al., 2025). These experimental studies have used indirect measures of emotional clarity such as perceived selfinsight and response times [RTs] to emotion ratings, however, suggesting that rumination could produce discrepant short-term effects on divergent measures of emotional clarity. Additional research incorporating multiple measures of emotional clarity could help resolve these inconsistencies before firm conclusions are drawn about the short-term consequences of rumination.

Several between-subject associations were observed between the dynamic features of trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity that help advance theoretical understanding of the mechanisms underlying rumination. Individuals whose low emotional clarity was more inert over time were more likely to ruminate following moments of low emotional clarity, perhaps in an attempt to increase clarity. And these individuals were also less likely to report low clarity following trauma-focused rumination. In other words, these individuals also appear to experience at least some temporary benefit from ruminating. Findings highlight a preliminary explanation for how these within-subject dynamics might crystallize to become reinforced over time. The experience sampling approach and DSEM framework used here broadly underscore the importance of considering these affective processes on multiple timescales. Although these correlations provide novel insights into the maladaptive dynamic features of trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity, these between-subject correlations were exploratory, and results need to be replicated. Nevertheless, findings suggest that the dynamic properties of trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity are intertwined and may lead to new developments in our understanding of affective risk.

Clinical Implications

Findings from this study also provide novel insights into the mechanistic role of traumafocused rumination in PTSD. The symptom exacerbation mechanism of rumination, first applied to depression, has been extended to and received support from studies of PTSD (Ehring et al., 2009; Wisco et al., 2023). For example, cognitive theory of PTSD suggests that trauma-focused rumination might strengthen harmful appraisals, preclude the development of a coherent trauma narrative, and fuel the continued experiencing of negative affect and intrusive memories of the trauma (Ehlers & Clark, 2000). PTSD symptom severity was associated with higher average momentary trauma-focused rumination and lower average momentary emotional clarity, replicating prior findings using trait self-report (Pugach & Wisco, 2023). PTSD symptom severity was also associated, to a lesser extent, with trauma-focused ruminative inertia. By contrast, PTSD symptom severity was not significantly associated with inertia of low emotional clarity. Findings suggest that although trauma-exposed individuals are likely to get "stuck" in negative cycles of trauma-focused rumination and low emotional clarity, targeting harmful cycles of trauma-focused rumination may be important for individuals with PTSD symptoms. Findings also extend prior research demonstrating that ruminative inertia is heightened among people with major depression (Bean et al., 2020). Together, the tendency to get "stuck" in unhelpful cycles of rumination may be a problematic feature of rumination associated with psychological disorders.

Providing individuals with active strategies in treatment to help disengage from rumination may help interrupt rumination once it begins, such as is done in Cognitive Therapy for PTSD (CT-PTSD; Ehlers & Wild, 2020). Interestingly, CT-PTSD also helps patients target cognitive themes of guilt, shame, and anger -often the content of ruminative thinking -suggesting that helping individuals clarify the basis of their emotional experience might provide a means to reduce potential targets for rumination.

Limitations and Future Directions

Findings must be considered in light of conceptual and methodological limitations. Our conceptualization of trauma-focused rumination focused on the broader, process-related aspects of repetitive negative thinking (e.g., uncontrollability, repetitiveness, perseveration) espoused by leading theories of rumination (Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 2008). Our decision to prioritize a brief, psychometrically validated measure of rumination for daily life research (Rosenkranz et al., 2020) might have led to other important aspects of rumination such as abstract thinking being missed (Watkins, 2008). Likewise, because of the process-related and trauma-focused measure of rumination used here, content-related aspects of how rumination is often conceptualized and measured including its negative/sad valence; its focus on themes of personal problems, unattained goals, self-worth, meaning, and emotions; and its distinction from that of reflective pondering were not included (Treynor et al., 2003). Consequently, our measure may have missed certain aspects of rumination that could be relevant for emotional clarity. Most individuals in this sample also endorsed multiple types of traumatic events. Although our rumination measure ensures that rumination is tightly linked to the same index event used to assess PTSD symptoms, this measure could also underestimate the frequency or intensity of trauma-focused rumination because instances of ruminating about non-index traumatic events were missed.

The sample also evidenced unique characteristics that have implications for interpretation of results. The relatively low average levels of momentary trauma-focused rumination in daily life suggest that trauma-focused rumination may have been less problematic in this sample of trauma-exposed university students compared to what might be observed a clinical sample. The experience sampling compliance rate was also relatively low compared to other studies daily life investigations using student samples. This might have been affected in part by the compensation method (i.e., course credit based on survey completion), and future work should consider alternative methods (e.g., monetary incentives; Wrzus & Neubauer, 2023). Nevertheless, lower compliance rates were associated with greater PTSD symptoms, trauma-focused rumination, and lack of emotional clarity, suggesting potential for bias. Although reanalysis of a subsample of participants who evidenced higher compliance replicated results found in the full sample, results from this study should be replicated before drawing firm mechanistic conclusions about traumafocused rumination and its association with PTSD.

In considering our findings, we cannot rule out that the autoregressive effect observed for trauma-focused rumination was partly due to potential reactivity effects associated with its repeated assessment on the experience sampling protocol. We similarly cannot rule out the role of within-subject covariates relevant to trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity (e.g., negative affect) influencing the associations observed here. Because no covariates (within-or between-subject) were used in our main analyses, residual DSEM (R-DSEM) did not offer us a modeling advantage; however, future research, research involving within-subject covariates should consider using R-DSEM, which separates contemporaneous time-varying covariate effects from autoregressive effects (Asarouhov et al., 2018). Finally, although findings from this observational study shed light on the temporal effects between two time series, and our hypothesis is mechanistic, these study design cannot support causal links between traumafocused rumination and emotional clarity. More rigorous, within-subject designs are needed to address causality, which could be achieved through experimental designs or pseudo-experimental daily life designs that manipulate the experience sampling time series at randomly selected occasions.

Several noteworthy future directions also emerged from this study. Findings showed a deleterious cascade whereby reciprocal relationships between trauma-focused rumination and low emotional clarity are mutually reinforced to keep individuals stuck in their distress, at least in the short-term. Yet, these within-subject relationships manifested differently at the betweensubject level, where individuals prone to more inert cycles of low clarity were not only more likely to ruminate in response to low clarity, but experienced greater clarity after ruminating. These findings highlight how future research could integrate these "fast-moving" dynamics with "slower moving," developmental approaches to understand how short-term dynamics shape long-term affective functioning (Hollenstein et al., 2013). The dynamics of trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity also evidenced significant random effects, indicating betweensubject variation in these within-subject processes. Future work would benefit from exploring candidate moderators of these effects to identify individual differences in these processes.

Finally, future research could integrate multiple measures of emotional clarity (e.g., subjective self-reported clarity and implicit clarity measured via RTs). Together, these efforts could help resolve prior inconsistencies in the literature demonstrating divergent effects of rumination on emotional clarity and clarify the mechanistic role of rumination in psychopathology.

Conclusions

This study used DSEM to improve theoretical understanding of the relationship between trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity among trauma-exposed individuals. Results suggest that trauma-focused rumination may serve as both a response to low emotional clarity and as a trigger for further impairments in emotional clarity, underscoring a potentially harmful downward spiral. Trauma-focused rumination and impaired emotional clarity each demonstrated inertia over time, suggesting that individuals may get easily "stuck" in these psychological states. Furthermore, higher inertia levels of each process were associated with distinct reciprocal effects between trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity, which underscores potentially unique dynamic properties of these PTSD correlates. PTSD symptom severity was not associated with the reciprocal effects between trauma-focused rumination and emotional clarity but was related to higher average momentary levels of each process and higher inertia of trauma-focused rumination. The findings from this study inform how trauma-focused rumination becomes reinforced over short timescales and offer novel insights into how trauma-focused rumination might contribute to PTSD symptom maintenance.
