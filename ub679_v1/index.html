Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Research on restorative environments has long examined the psychological benefits of contact with nature. Findings from this work are often interpreted as showing that nature itself causes restoration. In this article, we provide both a critical assessment of this causal claim, and propose an alternative "bundle" approach. In the first part, we assess whether nature satisfies two requirements for a scientifically useful causal category: coherence and mechanistic relevance. We argue that it remains unclear whether environments classified as natural share a coherent cluster of properties that supports generalization across instances, and whether those properties -such as relative untouchedness or the presence of vegetation and water -are directly mechanistically relevant to restorative outcomes. This lack of clarity has implications for stimulus selection, causal explanation, generalization, and theory development. In the second part, we propose a bundle approach that shifts the units of explanation from the elusive notion of nature to configurations of potentially interacting environmental components that cut across conventionally defined natural and built settings.

These components may include low-level perceptual properties, spatial and spatiotemporal relations, affordances, meanings, and social conditions. Integrated with a goal-discrepancy account of restoration, this approach holds that environmental components become restorative insofar as they help resolve an individual's active situation-goal discrepancies.

The bundle approach thus offers an analytical framework for developing more precise, testable, and generalizable explanations of restorative experiences across both natural and built environments.

1. Introduction

One of the central aims of environmental psychology is to understand how physical environments shape human psychological states. For decades, this project has been organized around a foundational contrast between natural and built environments (Davies et al., 2025; Huynh et al., 2026), generating a substantial empirical literature showing that contact with nature can yield psychological benefits (Collado et al., 2017; Hartig et al., 2014). These benefits may arise through several routes, including indirect pathways involving physical activity, social interaction, or improved environmental quality (Markevych et al., 2017). An important strand of this literature, however, focuses on how the direct perception or experience of natural environments can promote psychophysiological and cognitive recovery -commonly referred to as "restorative" (nature) experiences. Reported restorative outcomes include (reductions in) perceived and physiological stress (Steininger et al., 2026; Ulrich et al., 1991; Van den Berg et al., 2026), improvements in affect (McMahan & Estes, 2015) and cognitive performance (for reviews, see Bell et al., 2025; Ohly et al., 2016; Stevenson et al., 2018), as well as broader benefits for mental health (Bratman et al., 2019). In the present article, we use the restoration literature as a case study through which to examine a broader causal framing: the treatment of nature as a coherent environmental category that can itself be said to cause beneficial psychological outcomes.

Two theories have been highly influential in research on restorative environments (for other accounts, see Joye et al., 2024): Stress Reduction Theory (SRT) and Attention Restoration Theory (ART). SRT, proposed by Roger Ulrich (1983; Ulrich et al., 1991), holds that humans are biologically prepared to respond to unthreatening natural elements, such as vegetation and water, with positive affect and reduced stress because these elements historically signaled safety and resources. ART, developed by Rachel and Stephen Kaplan (Kaplan, 1995; Kaplan & Kaplan, 1989), instead locates a natural environment's restorative potential in its capacity to replenish depleted cognitive resources, particularly directed attention. The theory assumes that sustained demands on directed attention, such as those arising from work or family obligations, can deplete this capacity. Natural environments may help restore this attentional resource by eliciting soft fascination (Kaplan, 1995), a form of effortless attentional engagement that allows directed attention to rest and recover (Basu et al., 2019).

Importantly, across both restoration theories and the broader empirical literature, the field of restoration research has commonly treated nature as a coherent causal category capable of producing restorative effects. For example, Berman and colleagues (2008) found that participants who walked through a park subsequently performed better on the Digit Span Backwards task than those who walked through a downtown urban district. On the basis of this finding, the authors concluded that they had "shown that simple and brief interactions with nature can produce marked increases in cognitive control" (p. 1211, italics added). Such attribution of causal power to nature is widespread throughout the restoration literature. From article titles and research summaries to statistical path models, nature is routinely represented as a factor that improves attention and affect, or reduces stress (Table 1).

2. What would it take for nature to function as a causal category?

The treatment of nature as a causal category is not something that is confined to the theoretical narrative of restoration -as illustrated by the article titles in Table 1 -but it seems integral to the field's typical experimental paradigm. In a prototypical restoration study, participants first undergo a manipulation intended to induce stress or cognitive fatigue (Stevenson et al., 2018). They are then assigned to either a natural or a built condition, presented through direct, in situ exposure, such as a forest or urban walk, or through mediated representations of these environments, such as photographs, slideshows, or videos. Stress, affect, cognitive performance, or related outcomes are then reassessed. Any subsequent observed differences between conditions on these measures are then interpreted as effects of the environmental manipulation. Because this manipulation is thought of as operationalizing the contrast between natural and built environments, the resulting causal inference is expressed in the same categorical terms: exposure to natural rather than built environments causes restoration. The same logic extends to meta-analyses, in which heterogeneous environments and stimuli are aggregated into broad natural and built categories (Bell et al., 2025; Ohly et al., 2016; Stevenson et al., 2018). By synthesizing findings at this categorical level, such analyses further reinforce the impression that the observed restorative effects are attributable to nature as a category.

Importantly, the claim that nature causes restoration need not be understood as implying that nature or naturalness is an irreducible property inhering in objects or environments that directly produces restorative effects. More plausibly, nature functions as a summary label for a diverse family of stimuli and settings -such as forests, parks, coastlines, gardens, birdsong, or indoor plants -that are assumed to share certain beneficial properties.

On this interpretation, the category itself is not causally efficacious; rather, it is taken to track a coherent cluster of (environmental) properties that contribute to restoration. In principle, this is a legitimate way for category-level concepts to function in causal explanation.

Throughout this article, we use the phrase nature causes restoration as shorthand for this category-level causal framing.

2.1. Coherence and mechanistic relevance

For nature to function as a valid summary causal category, two general requirements must be met. The first is coherence: exemplars considered as nature must share a reasonably stable pattern of properties that provides a basis for grouping them together (as nature) and helps distinguish them, perhaps imperfectly, from non-natural environments or stimuli. The second is mechanistic relevance: at least some of the shared properties supporting this grouping must bear a theoretically plausible connection to outcomes relevant to restoration.

Such properties might be causally linked to the putative mediators of restoration, such as positive affect (in SRT) or soft fascination (in ART), and/or directly to restoration-relevant dependent measures, such as indicators of stress (in SRT) or cognitive performance (in ART).

We can illustrate how these two requirements for a causal category jointly support a category-level causal claim with an instructive example: the claim that doing endurance sports can improve mental health. Much like nature, endurance sports can take many forms.

Distance running, cross-country skiing, rowing, and road cycling are all endurance activities.

Of course, there are important differences between these activities, but they do share a defining physiological characteristic, namely sustained bodily exertion relying (predominantly) on aerobic energy metabolism.

Although this definition does not yield perfectly sharp boundaries, it does give the category coherence by identifying a shared basis for grouping otherwise diverse activities.

Importantly, it also distinguishes endurance sports from predominantly anaerobic activities, such as track sprinting and powerlifting, and from sports involving little sustained cardiorespiratory exertion, such as archery. Crucially, the shared reliance on aerobic energy metabolism that supports the category's coherence also offers a plausible basis for mechanistic relevance. After all, aerobic exertion can be connected to neuroendocrine, inflammatory, sleep-related, and affective processes implicated in mental health. Because the same property both unifies the category and may help produce its mental health effects, the category-level causal claim that endurance sports can improve mental health remains scientifically informative, even though the activities it encompasses are in many ways quite different.

2.2. Is nature coherent?

How, then, does nature fare with respect to the first requirement for a valid causal category, coherence? In the theoretical narrative of restoration research, highly diverse environments and stimuli are typically treated as instances of nature, ranging -for instancefrom forests (Bodin & Hartig, 2003) and urban parks (Wang et al., 2016) to coastlines (Laumann et al., 2003), gardens (Ottosson & Grahn, 2005), birdsong (Ma & Shu, 2018; Ratcliffe, 2021), and indoor plants (Evensen et al., 2015). This grouping recurs in empirical practice: meta-analyses aggregate different environments and stimuli under the category of nature, and some individual experiments even expose participants to different instances of nature within a single experimental condition (e.g., Chow & Lau, 2015: "forests, lakes, gardens, and mountains"; Delgado & Zamarron, 2019: "rivers, forests, beaches, cliffs, lakes, fields and seas"). At first glance, these practices give the impression that the shared properties justifying the grouping of these diverse instances as nature are well understood.

Definitional practice, however, suggests that this coherence is assumed, but not established. Many restoration studies leave nature entirely undefined (Heft, 2024; Joye & De Block, 2026), while studies that do attempt a definition frequently rely on extensional definitions of nature, listing examples such as forests, parks, gardens, beaches, or mountains (White et al., 2023). Such lists indicate which environments researchers include under nature but, unlike intensional definitions, they do not specify which properties justify grouping the listed examples together or provide criteria for classifying further cases. These definitional issues are also acknowledged within the restoration literature itself (Bratman et al., 2012; Wohlwill, 1983).

This is not to say that more explicit attempts at defining nature intensionally are entirely absent. One prominent approach defines nature in terms of its relative freedom from human intervention, or its apparent "untouchedness" (Hartig et al., 2014). Although this criterion appears to offer a sharper and more principled basis for distinguishing nature from non-nature, it too faces difficulties. One issue is that untouchedness is difficult to reconcile with empirical practice. A substantial part of the evidence commonly interpreted as showing the restorative effects of nature comes from studies based on encounters with urban parks (Berman et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2016), gardens (Ottosson & Grahn, 2005), and other environments substantially shaped by human activity (Honold et al., 2016).

Moreover, untouchedness does not seem to satisfy the requirement of coherence.

Untouchedness may group environments as diverse as rainforests, wetlands, deserts, and pristine mountain landscapes according to their historical relationship to human intervention, but this relationship may tell us very little about the properties of the environments themselves. Indeed, an untouched meadow may be virtually indistinguishable in its observable properties from one restored through human intervention, despite the two falling on different sides of the untouchedness criterion. Conversely, a desert and a rainforest may differ dramatically in their observable properties despite both qualifying as untouched. Thus, untouchedness may separate environments that are highly similar while grouping together environments that are highly dissimilar. In other words, untouchedness characterizes an environment's history of human intervention without establishing that environments so classified share a coherent pattern of properties relevant to restoration. 2.3. Is nature mechanistically relevant?

Given these problems with the coherence of the nature concept, one straightforward solution would be to stipulatively define nature in terms of environments dominated by vegetation, water, or both (Taylor & Hochuli, 2017). Unlike untouchedness, such a conception would offer a more substantive basis for coherence, both because it classifies environments according to their own features -principally vegetation and water -and because these features can be straightforwardly specified and quantitatively measured.

Vegetation greenness and cover can, for example, be estimated using the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), while the presence and spatial extent of open water can be mapped using the normalized difference water index (NDWI; McFeeters, 1996). The resulting criterion would more clearly distinguish natural from non-natural environments, although it would exclude otherwise uncontroversial cases of nature, such as desert landscapes with little vegetation or surface water.

Yet even if this sharper definition of nature satisfied the requirement of coherence, it would not by itself establish the second requirement, mechanistic relevance. To be sure, we do not doubt that environments containing vegetation and water may support psychological well-being indirectly, for example by encouraging physical activity, facilitating social interaction, or providing distance from daily stressors (Markevych et al., 2017). These affordances, however, are neither unique to nor defining of green and blue spaces. More fundamentally, classifying an environment as green or blue space does not explain why the presence of vegetation or water should, in itself, make that environment restorative.

Establishing mechanistic relevance requires a theoretically plausible account of how experiencing vegetation or bodies of water could elicit proposed drivers of restoration, such as positive affect or soft fascination, or produce restorative outcomes, such as reduced stress or attentional recoveryfoot_0.

2.4. The evolutionary bridge

One influential strategy for supplying this theoretical bridge is to appeal to human evolutionary history. On this view, just as the human body is adapted to function within a specific range of atmospheric conditions, the human mind is presumed to be adapted to the ecological conditions that characterized our species' ancestral heritage (Kellert & Wilson, 1993; Ulrich, 1993; Wilson, 1984). These settings were relatively free from human modification and had vegetation and water as recurrent features (Ulrich, 1983 (Ulrich, , 1993)). As Wohlwill (1983) noted, "The evolutionary heritage of the individual may well underlie man's consistent preference for stimuli taken from the natural environment" (p. 35). Natural settings are thus treated as psychologically congenial. In these environments humans are, in an evolutionary sense, "at home", and for that reason they are expected to promote restoration.

While this view attempts to build the required causal connection to restoration into the characterization of nature itself, it is faced with the problem that neither relative untouchedness nor the presence of vegetation and water made ancestral environments restorative in any unqualified sense. Those environments were equally characterized by predators, food scarcity, harsh weather, disease, and hazardous terrain (Volk, 2023). If restoration followed simply from an evolutionary attunement to such settings, we should expect a mixed profile of responses, including vigilance, fear, and avoidance rather than restoration specifically. The evolutionary account, which already rests on speculative assumptions about ancestral psychology (Joye & De Block, 2026), can therefore predict restoration only by distinguishing benign from threatening nature. But if the conditions used to make this distinction -such as environmental features indicative of safety -are themselves conducive to restoration, it must be shown that naturalness makes an explanatory contribution beyond those conditions.

2.5. A sanitized nature prototype?

Importantly, the exclusion of threatening and aversive environments reveals a fundamental problem with category-level claims about nature's restorativeness. Although statements such as nature causes restoration appear to concern natural environments as a general category, restoration research does not sample from the full range of settings ordinarily classified as natural. Threatening, hazardous, and otherwise aversive natural environments are largely absent from the literature (Gatersleben & Andrews, 2013; Huynh et al., 2025a). What is typically studied instead is a selectively benign subset of what is considered as nature: environments characterized by being relatively safe, pleasant, and nonthreatening, and usually encountered under controlled, low-risk conditions. Bratman and colleagues (2012, p. 120) make this selectivity unusually explicit, noting that they "… developed a definition of nature that is applicable to all of the environments considered within these studies [on restoration]." So, the category nature is drawn around the evidence rather than the evidence sampled from the broader category.

In other words, the stimuli used in the nature conditions in restoration research reflect, at least in part, a researcher-, field-level or culture-specific prototype of what counts as nature (Hartig et al., 2014). Of course, studying environments that match this prototype can yield valuable insights into how people perceive and respond to settings they regard as natural, pleasant, or safe. Yet it also introduces theoretical and methodological problems. Prototypes of nature may vary across individuals, cultures, activities, and contexts (Joye & De Block, 2026; Zelenski et al., 2026), and thus lack the stability required of a well-specified causal intervention. Furthermore, if the operative prototype is already infused with associations ofsay -pleasantness, findings that prototype-consistent settings have psychological benefits may establish that these settings are beneficial without actually showing that their naturalness produces those benefits. To the extent that pleasantness already forms part of the operative prototype, research on nature's psychological benefits risks becoming research on the positive effects of environments already conceived of in positive terms (Hicks et al., 2020).

3. Consequences of treating nature as an underspecified causal category

In restoration research, nature has come to function as a convenient summary label for a highly heterogeneous class of environments. Yet it remains unclear which properties these environments share that would justify grouping, for example, woodlands, wetlands, rivers, deserts, and alpine landscapes into a single causal category, and it is equally unclear how these shared properties could plausibly causally connect to the restorative effects attributed to them. This underspecification is not merely a philosophical concern. As we aim to show in the following sections, it creates direct downstream problems for stimulus selection, the interpretation and generalization of empirical findings, and theory development.

3.1. Selecting nature stimuli

A first consequence of the absence of clear and stable criteria for what counts as nature is that the category provides little guidance for experimental stimulus selectionfoot_2 . If it is unclear what nature is, researchers may fall back on intuition, disciplinary conventions, personal preferences, or expectations about which environments should be restorative when deciding which stimuli count as natural. Experiments may then test not nature as such, but an idealized prototype of nature: pleasant, safe, and visually manageable. Of course, researchers sometimes attempt to move beyond intuition by matching natural and built conditions on potentially relevant dimensions (Steininger et al., 2025). However, this does not rule out the possibility that the natural stimuli to be matched were intuitively selected, nor does it establish that those stimuli are appropriate representatives of the broader category to which the findings are meant to apply.

3.2. Interpreting restorative nature effects

Beyond potential selection bias, uncertainty about which properties the category nature tracks also limits the interpretation of experimental effects: one can conclude that the nature condition produced an effect, but not identify which specific properties or combinations of properties were doing the causal work. This problem becomes especially acute in meta-analyses, where effect estimates from studies using highly heterogeneous instances of nature are pooled together to provide broad summary effects (e.g., Bell et al., 2025; Ohly et al., 2016; Stevenson et al., 2018). The result is a "nature-in, nature-out" loop: studies enter the analysis under the broad label of nature, and the resulting pooled effect simply reproduces that same black-boxed category without isolating the actual causal drivers (Russo & Williamson, 2007).

At the level of individual experiments, manipulating nature constitutes a "fat-handed" intervention: it alters a package of variables simultaneously (e.g., vegetation density, acoustic profiles, air quality, perceived social density, spatial openness) making it difficult to pinpoint which specific property or combination of properties actually drives any observed effect. This problem is compounded at the level of the broader literature. What is manipulated across studies is not one single, broadly standardized fat-handed intervention repeated from one experiment to the next, but a collection of different fat-handed interventions. A green wall (Bernardo et al., 2021), a virtual beach scene (Blum et al., 2019), a green roof (Lee et al., 2015), an urban green walk (Roe et al., 2020), and office plants (Evensen et al., 2015) may all be categorized as nature, despite representing markedly distinct property "cocktails" that may engage substantially different causal pathways. By compressing this property heterogeneity, both within and across studies, into the broad label nature, the field artificially simplifies the explanans. This creates the impression of the possibility of an elegant, singlevariable explanation where the underlying causal structure may in fact consist of multiple properties and pathways.

3.3. Generalizing restorative nature effects

The lack of an established common causal basis among natural exemplars also creates a structural problem for generalization, or, in philosophical terms, projectibility (Boyd, 1999).

Findings can be projected from one instance of a category to another insofar as those instances share causally relevant properties. Endurance sports is again instructive: trail running and cross-country skiing are both fundamentally endurance activities. If we know that trail running improves mental health through aerobic exertion, this shared property gives us a solid basis for expecting the health effect to extend to cross-country skiing as well.

In restoration research, however, no comparable basis has been established for projecting findings across the diverse stimuli grouped under the broad label of nature.

Accordingly, a finding that viewing footage of a lush forest reduces stress cannot automatically be projected onto walking through a tree-lined urban park, let alone encountering a potted plant in an office. Without establishing which specific properties relevant to restoration are shared across these different instances, we have little basis for generalizing the effect from one to another. At best, such studies establish that a specific environmental package produced a given outcome under a particular set of conditions. However, shared membership in the category nature does not, by itself, provide a basis for expecting an effect observed in one instance of nature to recur in another.

3.4. Theorizing about restorative nature effects

Finally, treating nature as a single causal category risks a form of theoretical lock-in.

Once a heterogeneous range of environments and stimuli is collapsed into a single variable and the resulting outcomes are framed as general "nature effects", explanations may be sought at the same level of abstraction. Evolutionary ideas invoked in restoration research, including biophilia-based arguments (Ulrich, 1993; Wilson, 1984), fit this structure well.

They can make broad nature effects seem theoretically coherent despite substantial heterogeneity among the environments grouped under that label. Such accounts can, in turn, reinforce the original categorical framing, making nature itself appear to be the primary causal unit. Subsequent research may then continue to test and explain category-level nature effects rather than investigate which specific environmental properties produce which psychological outcomes. In this way, theoretical lock-in can preserve the explanansexplanandum structure that our preceding analysis has identified as problematic.

3.5. Intermediate conclusion: from reifying nature to environmental properties

We have tried to show that the category-level causal claim that nature causes restoration is difficult to sustain. One might reasonably object that our critique overlooks strands of restoration research that move beyond nature as the explanans and already investigate more specific environmental properties, ranging from low-level visual features (Celikors & Wells, 2022; Menzel & Reese, 2022; Valtchanov & Ellard, 2015; Van den Berg et al., 2016), visual complexity (Gao et al., 2026) to higher-order structure (e.g., prospectrefuge structure: Appleton, 1975; Gatersleben & Andrews, 2013; Tabrizian et al., 2018). We are aware of this work and agree that it is important. Indeed, it points in precisely the direction we think the field should move: if specific properties do the restorative work, then research should be organized primarily around those properties. A higher-order organization around the broad dichotomy between natural and built environments could still be justified if the properties responsible for restoration were shown to recur systematically across the diverse environments classified as natural. But, as we have argued, this is precisely what has not yet been established.

4. Outlining a bundle approach of restorative experiences

If nature cannot provide a sufficiently stable causal or projectible foundation for restoration research, the field might instead shift to a lower level of analysis by focusing on more specific environmental categories -for example, parks, forests, meadows, or waterways.

Recent attempts to differentiate among types of nature-based interventions can be understood as moving in this direction (Saeedy Robat et al., 2026). Still, such categories do not really resolve the underlying problems. First, instances of these narrower categories do not necessarily share restoration-relevant properties. A pocket park along a busy motorway and a large, secluded park are both parks, but they may differ substantially in, say, noise exposure and vegetation, and opportunities for solitude -features that may independently or jointly influence restoration. Second, membership in these categories does not contribute to mechanistic relevance. The fact that a setting qualifies as a park does not explain why it should be restorativefoot_3 . The alternative we propose is a bundle approach. On this view, environments should not primarily be treated as instances of broad ontological categories such as nature or built, but as bundles of properties that may or may not be relevant to restoration. A useful parallel to this shift is found in health behaviour change research. There, interventions were initially described under broad labels, such as smoking-cessation, lifestyle-change, or healthpromotion programs. However, such labels turned out to be too coarse to explain why particular interventions worked. Accordingly, researchers have increasingly sought to specify their active ingredients in terms of behaviour change techniques, such as self-monitoring, goal setting, prompts, feedback, action planning, and social support. Moreover, the question was no longer just what worked, but also for whom, under what conditions, and in which combinations (Peters et al., 2015). We contend that restoration research could benefit from a similar reframing.

4.1. Beyond nature versus built: environments as bundles of components

What are the components through which environments may shape experience? An arbitrary inventory or an inventory based on intuition would probably reproduce, at a finer scale, the same problems that the bundle approach intends to address. Hence, it is important that the inventory of components is based on how an environment is actually experienced.

Environmental experience can be understood as unfolding across multiple, interrelated scales, from moment-to-moment sensory processes to sensorimotor engagement with spatial configurations and broader situated experience (Varela, 1999; Djebbara et al., 2024). Based on work in scene perception, spatial research, and ecological approaches to perception and action (Castelhano & Krzyś, 2020; Chong & Proctor, 2020; Epstein & Baker, 2019; Groen et al., 2017), we group environmental components into three related levels: sensory-perceptual properties, spatial and spatiotemporal organization, and agent-relative properties (Table 2).

These levels progress from properties largely describable from sensory input, through relations among environmental elements, to relations between the organized environment and a situated agent. These levels form the framework of our approach and should not be understood as separate layers of reality, or as stages that perception passes through one after another. In recent literature on visual and scene perception, researchers use similar distinctions between low-level sensory characteristics and higher-order spatial or configurational properties of scenes, while ecological work on perception emphasizes properties defined at the level of the relation between an environment and a situated agent (Epstein & Baker, 2019; Groen et al., 2017; Castelhano & Krzyś, 2020; Chong & Proctor, 2020). It almost goes without saying that the properties at different levels are often closely interrelated and jointly shape environmental experience.

At the first level are sensory-perceptual properties. These are characteristics that can be specified largely from the structure of sensory input. Within the visual domain, these include luminance, contrast, color, spatial-frequency and edge structure, curvature, fractal structure, and image-level complexity. Research suggests that such properties contribute to perceived naturalness, environmental preference, and restorative qualities, although they are unlikely to account for these responses independently of higher-order environmental information (Berman et al., 2014; Celikors & Wells, 2022; Ibarra et al., 2017; Menzel & Reese, 2022; Valtchanov & Ellard, 2015). Specific sensory properties such as color and light characteristics have also been associated with wayfinding and cognitive performance (Grant et al., 2021; Kalantari et al., 2022). Fractal structure provides one well-developed example, having been associated with landscape preference as well as perceptual and physiological responses (Hagerhall et al., 2004 (Hagerhall et al., , 2008 (Hagerhall et al., , 2015;; Taylor et al., 2011). Similar work on architectural form indicates that properties such as curvature can influence evaluations of built environments (Vartanian et al., 2013).

The second level concerns spatial and spatiotemporal organization. This level is about relations among environmental elements, surfaces, and boundaries, and how these relations unfold across successive views. Components at this level include openness and enclosure, depth and scale, visibility and occlusion, prospect and refuge, symmetry, repetition and variation, rhythm, spatial complexity, and sequence or transition. Such configurational properties have been linked to environmental preference and emotional experience (Akcelik et al., 2024; Dosen & Ostwald, 2016; Sander et al., 2024), while symmetry and asymmetry have also been associated with differences in cognitive processing (Sztuka & Kühn, 2025).

Spatial organization further shapes navigation, spatial representation, and memory (Griesbauer et al., 2021; He et al., 2019; Peer & Epstein, 2021). Importantly, many of these properties can be quantified quite directly. Openness and visibility, for instance, can be measured with isovists (Weitkamp et al., 2014). This level also captures the temporal organization of environmental experience: during locomotion, spatial information unfolds through sequences, boundaries, and transitions, which can influence attention, spatial representation, and memory (Djebbara et al., 2025; Huynh et al., 2025b; Lamont & Bilkey, 2026; Reisner et al., 2025).

The third level comprises agent-relative properties. These characteristics cannot be fully specified from sensory or spatial structure alone because they depend on relations between environmental conditions and an agent's capacities, goals, experiences, or social situation. This relational understanding is central to ecological approaches to affordances, in which possibilities for action emerge through the relation between environmental conditions and the agent (Gibson, 1977 (Gibson, , 1979;; Rietveld & Kiverstein, 2014; Withagen et al., 2012 Withagen et al., , 2017)). Such affordances can include opportunities for movement, exploration, retreat, or social interaction, and have increasingly been examined in relation to architectural and navigational experience (Bonner & Epstein, 2017; Djebbara et al., 2019 Djebbara et al., , 2021;; Jelić et al., 2016). Agent-relative properties may also include appraisals such as perceived safety (Lis et al., 2023; Van Rijswijk & Haans, 2018), as well as meanings shaped by personal experience or cultural learning (Ellard, 2026). At this level, nature itself may remain psychologically relevant as a learned representation that carries particular meanings and expectations, even though it does not constitute a coherent physical causal category.

Crucially, components at all three levels cut across the conventional nature-built distinction, and across other intuitive distinctions such as green or blue spaces. This means that environments within the same category may differ substantially in their component bundles, while environments from different categories may share similar components.

4.2. Agents as bundles of goal states and discrepancies

Reframing environments as bundles of clearly definable and operationalizable components does not, by itself, explain why an environment with those properties is restorative. Consider the example of landscape openness, defined as "the amount of space perceivable to the viewer" (Weitkamp et al., 2014 , p. 140, based on Kaplan et al., 1989), which can be objectively quantified using isovist analysis (Weitkamp et al., 2014). Much like nature, openness is not relevant to stress or attentional fatigue in the abstract. Rather, openness becomes relevant when an individual is in a state that openness can plausibly help resolve. For instance, this can be the case when they feel spatially confined, or restricted in movement. Within a bundle approach, therefore, mechanistic explanation requires specifying not only the potentially relevant environmental component, but also the particular aspect of the individual's antecedent state to which that component may respond.

This requirement exposes a key limitation in how the individual is typically specified within traditional restoration theories. In SRT, the individual's relevant antecedent state is generally characterized as stress (e.g., Ulrich et al., 1991), whereas in ART it is characterized as attentional fatigue (e.g., Kaplan, 1995). These descriptions are not incorrect, but they tend to treat the particular situations that gave rise to those states as theoretically secondary. For example, watching videos of workplace accidents (Ulrich et al., 1991) and performing a public-speaking task (Greenwood & Gatersleben, 2016; Jiang et al., 2014) may both be classified as stressors within SRT, despite involving markedly different situations. The former confronts the individual with a situation of physical threat and bodily harm, whereas the latter involves social evaluation and performance pressure. This finer-grained situational structure highlights which environmental components could be mechanistically relevant. Just as nature must be unpacked into specific components to support causally precise explanations, manipulations of broad states such as stress and attentional fatigue must be specified in terms of the situational and goal-related conditions that created the need for restoration in the first place.

4.3. Goal-discrepancy account

The goal-discrepancy account of restoration (Joye et al., 2024) can provide precisely this kind of fine-grained specification. Rather than treating stress or attentional fatigue as unitary antecedent states, it shifts the unit of analysis to the situation-goal discrepancies underlying them (henceforth, "discrepancy" or "discrepancies"), asking which specific discrepancy between the agent's perceived current situation and a particular goal gives rise to these antecedent states. Returning to the examples above, watching workplace-accident videos may be stressful because their aversive content conflicts with a goal to experience pleasant or non-aversive stimulation, whereas a public-speaking task may create discrepancies with the goals for competence or social safety (Joye et al., 2024). Stress and attentional fatigue thus remain observable or experienced states, but the relevant unit of analysis lies at a lower level, at the level of the discrepancy that produces them.

Within this framework, one of the ways in which environments can become restorative is by instantiating components that can help reduce discrepancies associated with specific goals thwarted in the antecedent situation (Joye et al., 2024). If watching workplaceaccident videos conflicts with the goal to experience pleasant or non-aversive stimulation, environments rich in perceptually pleasant components (e.g., patterns of color and luminance) may directly help reduce that discrepancy. If public speaking thwarts the goals of competence and social safety, then environments that afford opportunities for mastery (e.g., successfully navigating a forest trail), or escape from social-evaluative demands (e.g., low social presence) may prove restorative because they allow the individual to regain a sense of competence and social safety. In both cases, the relevant environmental components are not restorative in the abstract; they become restorative precisely because they enable the individual to fulfill goals that were thwarted in the antecedent situation.

An important nuance is that not all components of an environmental bundle need contribute to positive experience through discrepancy reduction. Some may elicit positive responses more directly, through relatively direct perceptual pathways or through learned meanings and associations. For example, perceptual properties such as fractal structure, symmetry or contrast may elicit positive aesthetic or affective responses (Hagerhall et al., 2004; Jacobsen & Höfel, 2002; Joye et al., 2016; Reber et al., 2004) because of efficient sensory processing (Reber et al., 2004). Similarly, settings such as one's home, a favorite café, or a familiar holiday destination may acquire positive value through positive personal experience and cultural learning. Encountering these components or settings may therefore be positive even in the absence of an active situation-goal discrepancy. Such benefits are consistent with the notion of "instoration" (e.g., Prugger & Kühn, 2026): positive outcomes arising from environmental exposure without requiring a prior restorative need.

4.4. Towards experimentally testing the bundle approach

Unavoidably, these considerations raise the question of how the bundle approach can be tested experimentally. As outlined in the Introduction, restoration research has largely relied on a characteristic experimental paradigm consisting of two key elements: a manipulation of participants' psychological state (i.e., stress, attentional fatigue) and a comparison between broad environmental categories. The bundle approach reconceptualizes both by requiring greater specificity on both the environmental and person side.

On the environmental side, the bundle approach changes the target of experimental manipulation. Rather than comparing broad environmental categories (i.e., nature versus built), it focuses on varying specific environmental components while holding others constant. Fractal patterning, for example, could be varied within an otherwise identical urban setting, or levels of openness, enclosure, or refuge could be systematically manipulated while other environmental characteristics remain unchanged. Although studies have already examined individual environmental components in this way (e.g., Menzel & Reese, 2022), such analyses still seem to serve the larger project of explaining nature effects, with nature remaining the primary unit of interest. The bundle approach instead treats component configurations as the primary units of explanation and asks when and for whom they promote restoration across both natural and built settings.

On the person side, the bundle approach requires psychological specificity. Rather than inducing a generic antecedent state such as stress or attentional fatigue, studies could focus on manipulating specific discrepancies, such as autonomy frustration, competence threat, social overload, or perceived lack of safety. Person-side manipulations are, of course, not unique to the bundle approach. In "mainstream" restoration research, however, they primarily serve to create a state in which restoration can occur. The particular psychological content of the manipulation is therefore often secondary to the fact that participants become stressed or depleted, even though a single manipulation may simultaneously involve several qualitatively distinct situations, such as social evaluation, cognitive challenge, physical discomfort, or loss of control (e.g., Steininger et al., 2026).

Under the bundle approach, by contrast, the specific psychological content of the depletion manipulation is relevant. As restoration depends on the fit between a person's discrepancies and the components of the environment, different discrepancies will be restored by different environmental bundles. A competence threat, for example, should be more effectively reduced by a bundle with components affording mastery and control, whereas understimulation should be more effectively addressed by one affording novelty. Each bundle should therefore be especially effective when matched to the discrepancy it is functionally suited to address, and less effective when paired with an alternative discrepancy.

This basic approach lends itself to several empirical extensions. First, once a candidate component has been shown to affect restorative outcomes, its effects can be examined parametrically rather than categorically. This would allow researchers to test doseresponse relationships and determine whether effects of the component are linear, nonlinear, or characterized by thresholds or diminishing returns. Second, multiple environmental components can be manipulated simultaneously in factorial designs to determine whether their effects are additive, offset one another, or interact synergistically, such that their combination produces a stronger effect than expected from their individual contributions.

Third, the generalizability of particular components can be tested by implementing them across different environmental settings. If, for example, a given component produces comparable effects in a forest and a built setting, this would support the claim that restoration depends on the component itself rather than on the broader environmental category in which it occurs.

Ultimately, these methodological changes shift restoration research from asking whether broad environmental categories produce restorative effects to asking which environmental components produce which benefits, for whom, and under which antecedent psychological conditions. The bundle approach thus shifts explanation from category membership to specific component-discrepancy relations.

5. Discussion

For decades, restoration research, both in its theoretical narrative and experimental practice, has been organized around the distinction between natural and built environments.

As we have argued, this framing is beset by problems of definition and coherence, as well as by difficulties in establishing mechanistic relevance. These problems, in turn, may create practical difficulties for stimulus selection, experimental manipulation, interpretation, and generalization of results. We have therefore proposed a shift from nature as a broad environmental category to bundles of potentially interacting environmental components. By linking these components to the goal-discrepancy account of restoration (Joye et al., 2024), we argue that restorative experiences can occur when environmental components help resolve an active situation-goal discrepancy.

5.1. Reconsidering causal explanation, generalization, and heterogeneity

Crucially, a bundle approach aims to address the conceptual and methodological obstacles associated with treating the category nature itself as a causal variable (see Section 3). It shifts the unit of analysis from nature as a broad environmental category to environmental components that can be adequately defined and operationalized, and whose relevance to restorative outcomes can be investigated. This implies a move away from undifferentiated fat-handed manipulations, in which environments remain black-boxed "cocktails" summarized under the label nature, toward experiments in which environmental components are explicitly specified and their effects investigated, both separately and in combination.

Under a bundle framework, generalization can also become more firmly grounded. If a particular environment produces a psychological benefit because, for example, its spatial properties provide refuge and opportunities for withdrawal following social overload, there is a principled theoretical basis for expecting other environments containing those same causally relevant components to produce similar effects under comparable psychological conditions. Generalization thus proceeds from shared causally relevant components rather than from shared membership in an intuitive, broad category such as nature. Obviously, this does not guarantee identical effects across settings: even a correctly identified component may not work the same way everywhere, because other components in the bundle, or characteristics of the person, can change its effect.

A bundle approach also fundamentally changes how heterogeneous findings (Huynh et al., 2025a) are interpreted. Given that traditional nature manipulations typically alter multiple properties, that the composition of these property bundles may differ across studies, and that the relevance of particular properties depends on the antecedent psychological state of the individual, inconsistent empirical findings should hardly be surprising (Bell et al., 2025; Ohly et al., 2016; Stevenson et al., 2018). A computerized rural drive (Cassarino et al., 2019), virtual beach scenery (Blum et al., 2019), and potted plants (Evensen et al., 2015) do not constitute interchangeable manipulations simply because they share the nominal label nature (Bell et al., 2025). Likewise, we should not expect that the same environmental bundle will result in identical responses in individuals with distinct discrepancies. In that sense, empirical heterogeneity is not necessarily noise surrounding a universal nature effect, but may itself be theoretically informative, because it might reflect differences in the environmental components instantiated across studies, the discrepancies participants bring to the encounter, and the fit between the two.

5.2. Should we eliminate nature altogether?

Our argument, and the bundle approach developed from it, may raise an important concern: by unpacking nature into specific components, are we not effectively eliminating the category nature altogether, and is this not difficult to reconcile with the observation that nature clearly carries profound psychological significance for many people (Zelenski et al., 2026)? Not necessarily. What is abandoned on this view is not the category nature itself, but its status as an ontologically unified causal category. The category can, however, remain causally relevant in a different sense: as a psychological representation that carries particular associations.

Through repeated personal experience and social learning individuals may acquire psychological representations of nature that may involve associations with calm, purity, beauty, leisure, or freedom from daily demands (Vining et al., 2008; Zelenski et al., 2026).

Once acquired, such representations can become causally relevant components of a broader environmental bundle. Perceiving a setting as natural may activate associated expectations, meanings, and appraisals that shape attention, affect, and behavior (Egner et al., 2020), thereby contributing to discrepancy reduction or to positive experience without restorative need. Consistent with this idea, experimental research shows that the psychological effects of identical ambiguous stimuli can differ depending on whether they are construed as originating from nature or from an industrial source (Haga et al., 2016; Koivisto et al., 2022).

Ultimately, this suggests that nature need not constitute a coherent causal kind in the physical world to function as a psychologically consequential representation in the mind.

Note that this reconceptualization also carries implications for individual-difference constructs such as connectedness to nature (Mayer et al., 2009), nature relatedness (Nisbet et al., 2009) or inclusion of nature in self (Schultz, 2001). Such scales arguably assess a psychological relation, not to an independently specified environmental category, but to whatever respondents themselves construe as nature. This entails that observed scores may reflect not only differences in the strength of a person's connection, but also differences in the object of that connection. This does not render such constructs meaningless, but it complicates their interpretation and raises questions regarding construct comparability across respondents and studies.

5.3. Will explanations not become too complex?

Another concern is whether, in a bundle approach, explanation risks becoming intractably complex once we acknowledge that antecedent, to-be-restored states may reflect many different discrepancies and that environments may instantiate many different components. While the bundle approach indeed introduces additional complexity, this need not preclude systematic explanation. Although the situations giving rise to stress or cognitive fatigue can be highly diverse, the goals thwarted in these situations may map onto a considerably smaller set of recurring motivational domains (e.g., Grouzet et al., 2005). A thunderstorm and social exclusion are very different at the surface level, yet they may both frustrate recurring safety goals. The explanatory task is therefore not to construct a unique account for every episode of stress or cognitive fatigue, but to independently identify the recurring discrepancies that underlie it.

Regularities can likewise be found on the environmental side. Once a particular goal discrepancy has been identified, it naturally narrows the range of environmental components plausibly relevant to its resolution. When stress arises from working with a bossy colleague, for example, environmental opportunities for choice, control, or self-directed action become theoretically relevant in a way that many other features of the environment do not. Following social overload, by contrast, low social density, physical refuge, or opportunities for withdrawal may become especially relevant. The active discrepancy thus functions as an explanatory filter that directs attention toward a potentially relevant subset of environmental components.

Explanation thus shifts from a single broad pathway in which generic states of stress or cognitive fatigue are followed by nature as the restorative solution, to a multi-pathway account in which different discrepancies may be addressed by different environmental components. Such discrepancy-property mappings may generalize across very different physical settings, allowing explanation at a level below broad categories such as nature but well above individual cases. To be sure, further complexity arises because environmental components are embedded within multi-component bundles, in which some discrepancyrelevant components may reinforce, compensate for, or counteract the effects of others.

Mapping such interaction effects is methodologically demanding, but these interactions are themselves open to systematic investigation.

5.4. Making (more) place for diversity

A bundle approach also fundamentally transforms the theoretical status of individual variation and diversity. Both SRT and ART rely, albeit to varying degrees, on evolutionary premises regarding human-environment fit, positing that humans retain an evolved, speciestypical attunement to unthreatening natural settings (Kaplan, 1987; Ulrich, 1993). These frameworks create an expectation of broadly similar responses to nature. Individual or cultural variation is then seen as something of a theoretical puzzle, and such "deviations" are often accommodated through ancillary assumptions -for example, that cultural learning may override evolved responses to nature (Balling & Falk, 1982) or that these responses require sufficient environmental input to develop (Ulrich, 1993).

In contrast, a bundle approach is not constrained by the assumption of a universal response to a particular environmental category. Instead, restorative potential attaches to the specific environmental components capable of addressing an active discrepancy, and those properties need not be tied to any particular type of environment. What matters is whether the environmental components provide a functional match for the discrepancy at hand for that specific individual. Individual and situational variation will therefore be the rule, and not the awkward deviation from a general nature effect.

5.5. The theoretical status of a bundle approach

Finally, what, then, is the theoretical status of the bundle approach? To be clear, the bundle approach should not, by itself, be understood as a substantive theory of restoration.

Treating environments as configurations of components is first and foremost an analytical move. Driven by the causal issues associated with the label nature (see Section 2), the bundle approach replaces that broad category with a finer-grained specification of the environmental components that may shape psychological outcomes. The theoretical substance lies in linking this component-based analysis to the goal-discrepancy account of restoration (Joye et al., 2024). On this account, restoration is explained through the reduction of situation-goal discrepancies, and environmental components become causally relevant insofar as they help resolve those discrepancies.

A bundle approach also entails theoretical pluralism. Traditional restoration theories such as ART and SRT are relatively self-contained, nature-centered accounts developed foremost to explain the effects of natural settings. A component-based discrepancy account takes a different approach: different discrepancy-component relations may draw on different psychological theories to explain the mechanisms through which they operate. For example, perceptual-fluency (Joye et al., 2016; Reber et al., 2004) or predictive-processing (Van de

Cruys & Wagemans, 2011) models may help explain why particular low-level visual features generate hedonic value and may thereby satisfy hedonic goals. Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan, 2000) may clarify how environmental affordances can support autonomy or competence (Yang et al., 2022), and privacy-regulation theories may explain why opportunities for refuge or withdrawal become beneficial under conditions of social overload (Evans et al., 2000). The bundle approach thus provides an architecture for specifying which theoretical mechanisms are relevant to particular discrepancy-component relations, rather than seeking a single overarching mechanism of restoration anchored in the slippery concept of nature.
