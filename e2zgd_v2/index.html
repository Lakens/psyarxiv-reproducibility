Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Full text

Abstract

How can knowledge systems preserve competence and identity while the organization supporting them changes? This theoretical article develops a processual framework for investigating persistence through selective transformation. It distinguishes a relational field from four interacting processual dimensions: continuity, discontinuity, selective reorganization, and emergence, while treating recursive epistemic niche construction as a longitudinal feedback mechanism rather than a fifth dimension. The framework brings together four requirements for explanation: an antecedent configuration, a specified organizational change, a capacity treated as persisting, and evidence concerning subsequent transmission. This architecture separates continuity of content, function, and identity and links conceptual change within individuals to the reproduction of distributed practices without assuming identical mechanisms across levels. A worked experimental proposal tests whether opportunities for reorganization preserve competence when inherited procedures no longer fit changed task demands. Religion provides a substantive case in which memory, ritual, epistemic authority, and cultural transmission can be investigated together; mythos, theos, and logos are treated as historical-processual heuristics rather than as universal or obligatory stages. Eight conditional propositions connect the framework to cognitive psychology, cultural evolution, and historical psychology. The account distinguishes descriptive persistence from epistemic justification and specifies conditions under which faithful reproduction should outperform transformation. Its explanatory value depends on comparative prediction, intervention, and prospective failure conditions rather than on treating every instance of change as evidence of emergence.

[div-01]

The issue becomes especially important when psychological research concerns phenomena that persist while their surrounding conditions change. A concept may continue to shape thought after being explicitly rejected. A religious tradition may retain a recognizable identity while modifying its interpretations and practices. A collective may preserve competence even as members are replaced and responsibilities reorganized. In each case, persistence cannot be explained simply by asking whether the original components remain unchanged.

This leads to the central question of the article: under what conditions does transformation contribute to the persistence of a knowledge system? The framework developed here distinguishes continuity of content, continuity of function, and continuity of identity. These forms of continuity need not coincide. Preserving the wording of a practice, for example, may fail to preserve the competence that the practice once supported, whereas reorganizing the practice may allow that competence to persist under changed conditions.

Here, knowledge system is used descriptively to refer to an organized configuration of claims, skills, practices, artifacts, and institutions through which people acquire, evaluate, use, and transmit what they take to be knowledge. The term does not imply that every transmitted claim is true or epistemically justified. Explaining how information or belief persists is therefore distinct from evaluating its epistemic standing.

Several theoretical traditions already address important parts of this problem.

Psychological pluralism draws attention to the heterogeneous constitution of psychological phenomena (Teo, 2010). Distributed cognition examines how individual, social, and material contributions become jointly effective through their organization (Hutchins, 1995). Cultural evolution investigates the retention and transformation of socially acquired information (Boyd & Richerson, 1985). Historical psychology, in turn, examines psychological variation across time and the traces of earlier cognition preserved in texts, artifacts, and other historical evidence (Muthukrishna et al., 2021).

These traditions illuminate different parts of the explanatory problem, but none requires exactly the same evidential sequence proposed here. The framework is therefore not intended to replace theories of distributed cognition, cultural evolution, conceptual change, or historical psychology. Its proposed contribution is architectural. A claim that a system persists through transformation should specify, together, (a) the antecedent configuration, (b) the relations or organization that changed, (c) the capacity or identity criterion treated as persisting, and (d) whether the resulting arrangement remains available for subsequent transmission. Keeping these elements distinct allows continuity of content, function, and identity to diverge rather than collapsing them into a single notion of survival.

The framework represents this architecture through a transversal relational field and four interacting processual dimensions: continuity, discontinuity, selective reorganization, and emergence. These are not proposed as obligatory historical stages. Their explanatory value depends on whether they support discriminating comparisons with plausible alternatives, including faithful reproduction, gradual change, replacement, and failed coordination.

The sections that follow first clarify the theoretical and methodological approach and its historical foundations. The article then develops the processual framework, applies it to religious transmission and epistemic recoding, and derives eight conditional research propositions. A worked experimental proposal illustrates how persistence through transformation could be investigated without treating either survival or change, by itself, as sufficient evidence for the other.

Theoretical and Methodological Approach

This article develops a conceptual synthesis rather than reporting an empirical study or a systematic literature review. Its sources were selected for their relevance to the explanatory problem: how cognitive and cultural systems retain identifiable capacities while their components and relations change. The analysis brings together historical and philosophical work on psychology, accounts of distributed cognition, cultural-evolutionary theory, and research on religious transmission and conceptual change.

The analytical procedure has three components. First, it distinguishes concepts that can otherwise become conflated: analytical separation and ontological independence; persistence and invariance; information transmission and epistemic justification; and organization-dependent performance and ontological emergence. Second, it examines how these distinctions apply across individual, collective, and institutional settings. Third, it translates the resulting framework into conditional propositions with identifiable comparison conditions and outcomes that would count against them.

Comparisons across levels are treated as heuristic unless the relevant mechanism is specified. An analogy between biological maintenance and cultural persistence does not demonstrate identical causal organization. Likewise, an experimental finding about an individual's conceptual interference cannot establish a historical claim about institutional transformation. Each application requires an explicit unit of analysis, temporal scale, outcome, and account of transmission or interaction.

The literature considered is selective rather than exhaustive. No comprehensive database search, predefined screening protocol, or quantitative evidence synthesis is claimed. The proposed experiments have not been conducted, and no results are reported. Their purpose is to clarify the commitments of the framework and the evidence needed to evaluate particular applications.

Accordingly, the framework is assessed here through conceptual differentiation, consistency across applications, and the specification of possible empirical contrasts. Its explanatory adequacy remains an open question for subsequent research.

Theoretical Foundations Process and Pluralism in the History of Psychology

A processual perspective treats some forms of stability as achievements of ongoing activity rather than as the absence of change. Psychology has historical resources for developing this perspective, but its history should not be reconstructed as a uniform transition from substance to process. Competing conceptions of science, mind, and explanation have coexisted. Abib (2009) examines Wundt and James to illuminate the plurality of psychology's scientific projects and its implications for historiography. In this account, the history of psychology includes philosophical traditions, cultural practices, and ideas, rather than beginning exclusively with the institutional establishment of experimental psychology. James's (1890) discussion of the stream of thought provides a primary example of attention to continuity and change within psychological life.

Wundt's principle of creative resultants, discussed here through Abib's historical analysis, also directs attention to the organization of psychological combinations. Its historical significance should be distinguished from the claim that it anticipates every contemporary theory of emergence. Historical continuity between questions does not imply theoretical identity between their answers. Teo (2010) relates psychological pluralism to the different ontological levels of psychological phenomena, including their cultural and historical constitution. Muthukrishna et al. (2021) develop a complementary case for investigating cognition and behavior across time.

These projects intersect but are not interchangeable. The history of psychology examines, among other things, the construction of disciplinary objects and methods; historical psychology investigates psychological variation through historical evidence.

Organization and Distributed Cognition

Accounts of cognition differ over the status of representations, the boundaries of cognitive systems, and the role of organism-environment relations. Clark and Chalmers (1998) argue that some cognitive processes can extend beyond the individual under appropriate conditions. Read and Szokolszky (2020) examine ecological and enactive approaches while emphasizing substantive disagreements between them. Their positions should not be combined merely because both attend to context. Favela and Raja (2026) discuss the continuing relevance of dynamical systems approaches to cognitive science, including temporal organization and interactions across brain, body, and environment. This work motivates the investigation of dynamics; it does not independently validate the present framework. A dynamical claim becomes informative when it identifies the variables involved, their coupling, and the consequences for prediction or intervention. Hutchins (1995) demonstrates how navigation can be investigated through coordinated activity involving people, instruments, and representations. Finkel and Lamm (2025) argue that collective knowledge adds explanatory resources to accounts centered on individual copying. Miton and Jackson (2026) further examine how technological complexity creates demands for distributing cognition while imposing coordination costs. These contributions support distinguishing information possessed by individuals from the organization that makes their contributions usable.

Persistence and Cultural Transformation

Cultural-evolutionary theory analyzes how socially acquired information changes and persists across populations (Boyd & Richerson, 1985). A central debate concerns whether stability and directional change are explained primarily by differential selection among cultural variants or by systematic transformation during reconstruction and transmission. Mesoudi (2021) argues that cultural selection and biased transformation are neither mutually exclusive nor a simple dichotomy, whereas Acerbi et al. (2021) formally show that convergent transformation can generate population-level stability even without high-fidelity copying or selection. Cultural Attraction Theory therefore matters here because it treats transformation as a potentially stabilizing process rather than as transmission error alone.

The present framework does not adjudicate this debate in advance. Selective reorganization is an architectural description of what changes in a knowledge system, not a claim that cultural selection is always the mechanism. A reorganization may arise through differential retention of already available variants, cognitively biased or convergent transformation, deliberate revision, institutional filtering, or combinations of these processes. The additional question posed here is whether the resulting reorganization preserves specified content, function, or identity and remains available for subsequent transmission.

In philosophy of biology, Nicholson and Dupré (2018) develop processual approaches to persistence and individuality. Anderson (1972) emphasizes the explanatory significance of higher-level organization. These works provide resources for asking how stability depends on activity and relations. They do not demonstrate that biological maintenance, conceptual development, and institutional reproduction have the same mechanisms.

A processual account of knowledge must consequently explain both retention and change while preserving distinctions between the domains under investigation. The proposed framework adopts this requirement as a constraint on its use.

The Processual Framework

Persistence Through Transformation

The central proposition is that some knowledge systems preserve continuity partly through selective transformation. Languages can remain recognizable while their vocabulary and use change; disciplines can retain institutional continuity while revising concepts and methods; religious traditions can reproduce identities while reinterpreting practices and authorities. These are candidate cases for analysis rather than demonstrations of a universal law.

Persistence requires a specified criterion. A community may preserve a name while abandoning a practice. A skill may survive after an institution disappears. An institution may endure while losing competence. Research must therefore identify whether continuity concerns semantic content, performance, social identity, authority, or organizational structure.

The claim that persistence occurs through transformation is causal only when changing an arrangement contributes to maintaining a specified capacity. The co-occurrence of change and survival is insufficient. Researchers must identify the relevant transformation and compare its consequences with plausible alternatives, including faithful reproduction or a different form of reorganization.

The framework distinguishes a relational field from four dimensions: continuity, discontinuity, selective reorganization, and emergence. These dimensions can overlap and operate at different temporal scales. Reorganization can precede a recognized discontinuity, and an emergent practice can stabilize one part of a system while destabilizing another.

Transformation can also produce fragmentation, loss, or extinction.

The Relational Field

Relation is not a fifth stage. The relational field denotes the task-relevant organization of dependencies among participants, representations, artifacts, practices, and institutions. It can include access to external representations, communication channels, divisions of expertise, record keeping, and rules for recognizing authority.

The concept does not imply that every contextual factor belongs within a cognitive system. Its boundaries depend on the explanatory question. Ramstead et al. (2016), for example, examine cultural affordances through embodied agents, shared expectations, and socially organized attention. Their analysis directs attention to how opportunities for action are structured within cultural settings.

Operationally, a relational field might be represented by a communication network, an artifact-access arrangement, or a sequence of role-dependent transformations. These are alternatives appropriate to different tasks. When participants have equivalent information, manipulating who can communicate with whom can test whether organization affects performance.

More distribution is not necessarily better. Specialization without reliable integration can undermine collective competence. An explanation must account for both the advantages of complementary contributions and the costs of coordinating them (Miton & Jackson, 2026).

Continuity

Continuity concerns the relation between antecedent and subsequent configurations.

Individuals encounter languages, classifications, tools, and practices they did not invent. Yet inheritance does not imply exact reproduction.

Researchers can measure retained propositions, recurring social roles, stable task performance, or persistent semantic relationships. These indicators should remain distinct.

Stability in a label can conceal changes in meaning, while altered vocabulary can express a continuing practical discrimination.

The framework therefore treats continuity as an outcome to be specified rather than a property assumed whenever a tradition or institution retains its name. Identifying what persists is the first step toward explaining how it persists.

Discontinuity

Discontinuity denotes a change substantial enough to alter a specified organization or pattern of functioning. Kuhn's (1962) account of scientific revolutions offers an influential treatment of such transitions, without implying that every conceptual change is revolutionary.

A discontinuity must be distinguished from an observer's decision to divide a continuous record into periods. Changes in semantic relations, accepted procedures, or transmission patterns can provide evidence, but each requires an appropriate baseline. Sparse historical sampling can make gradual change appear abrupt. Conversely, aggregate continuity may conceal local ruptures.

The relevant question is whether the transition changes the organization responsible for a particular capacity, not simply whether a later state differs from an earlier one.

Selective Reorganization

Selective reorganization occurs when inherited elements are differentially retained, abandoned, translated, recombined, or assigned new functions. The adjective selective does not by itself denote cultural selection. Empirically, cultural selection concerns differential uptake or retention among available variants, whereas biased transformation concerns directional modification during reconstruction or reproduction (Mesoudi, 2021). Because these dynamics can generate similar population-level patterns, studies that seek mechanism identification should measure variant generation, modification, uptake, and retention separately.

Reorganization should not be presumed adaptive or truth-enhancing. An altered practice can become easier to transmit while becoming less accurate. An institution can preserve its authority by suppressing useful alternatives.

The ecological metaphor of recycling remains a limited analogy. Cultural elements are interpreted and reconstructed rather than conserved like material constituents in an accounting system. Demonstrating conceptual inheritance requires historical evidence; the recurrence of an older word is not sufficient.

At the individual level, Shtulman and Valcarcel (2012) show that scientific understanding can coexist with earlier intuitions that interfere with speeded judgments. This finding motivates investigation of coexistence within conceptual change. It does not establish that every revised belief survives or that an institutional history can be inferred directly from an individual task.

Emergence

Emergence refers here to a system-level capacity that depends on organization and is not adequately characterized by an additive description of isolated components. It is a commitment to investigating organization, not an assertion that mechanistic explanation is impossible.

Collective performance provides a candidate case because coordination can make partial contributions jointly effective (Hutchins, 1995). However, a statistical interaction is not sufficient evidence of strong ontological emergence. Differences in information, motivation, and task exposure must be considered.

A flexible nonlinear model can also improve fit simply by containing more parameters.

Evidence for an organization-sensitive explanation therefore requires predictive evaluation, appropriate complexity controls, or intervention on the proposed relations.

Recursive Epistemic Niche Construction as Longitudinal Feedback

Recursive epistemic niche construction is not a fifth processual dimension. It names the longitudinal feedback through which outcomes of one configuration alter the material, social, conceptual, and institutional conditions under which later knowledge practices occur. Recent niche-based accounts conceptualize research environments as multidimensional, relational, historically dynamic configurations that both enable and constrain inquiry (Trappes & Leonelli, 2025), while work on epistemic niche construction emphasizes how practices and values can reshape later conditions of knowledge production (De Benedetto & Luchetti, 2025). At a broader scale, adaptive knowledge-ecology accounts connect cumulative culture, symbolic scaffolding, and epistemic niche construction to long-run social persistence (d 'Errico, 2026). Bentley and O'Brien (2012) provide an earlier example focused on transitions in information storage and transmission.

Schematically, a later knowledge configuration depends on inherited claims and skills, relational organization, institutions, technologies, and available evidence. Practices emerging from this configuration can modify these conditions for subsequent participants, thereby linking one cycle of organization and transmission to the next. Applications to Cognition, Religion, and Cultural Knowledge Mythos, Theos, and Logos as Historical-Processual Heuristics

Mythos, theos, and logos are used here both as analytical heuristics and as markers of possible historical-processual tendencies. They do not represent universal or obligatory stages of cultural development, nor do they imply a necessary sequence in which myth is replaced by religion and religion, in turn, by science. Historical scholarship has challenged such simple replacement models, showing that mythic and rational forms can remain intertwined and that changes in their relationship are better understood in terms of differentiation, reinterpretation, and transformation (Fowler, 2011; Dege, 2023; Liu, 2026).

Mythos refers primarily to narrative and symbolic organization, through which meaning, memory, identity, and practical orientation can be transmitted. Theos is introduced here as a heuristic label for orientations toward sacred, divine, ancestral, or transcendent realities and for the practices, authorities, and institutions organized around them. Logos refers to more explicit forms of argument, categorization, inference, conceptual differentiation, and justification. These terms therefore identify predominant modes of organizing and transmitting knowledge rather than mutually exclusive forms of thought.

The historical dimension of the heuristic concerns changes in the relative organization of these modes. Under particular social, material, and institutional conditions, narrative-symbolic resources may become incorporated into more explicitly religious systems, while religious traditions may develop increasingly formalized practices of interpretation, argument, and justification. Such developments need not eliminate earlier forms. Mythos may persist within theos or logos, just as religious forms of reasoning may remain active within highly rationalized social settings. Historical work on the relation between myth and reason similarly suggests that transformation need not involve straightforward replacement: myth may change form, be reinterpreted, or become incorporated into new intellectual configurations (Fowler, 2011; Dege, 2023). Comparative work also indicates that the differentiation of mythic and rational discourse can follow different historical trajectories rather than a single developmental path (Liu, 2026).

From the perspective developed in this article, such trajectories are better described as selective reorganization than as progression through fixed stages. Narrative can organize scientific communication without replacing evidential testing, just as religious argument can involve sophisticated inference without making all of its premises empirically established. The cognitive science of religion examines how religious representations draw on ordinary cognitive capacities and cultural transmission (Boyer, 2001), while cultural-evolutionary approaches investigate how practices, institutions, and group processes shape their persistence and spread (McNamara, 2024; Norenzayan et al., 2016). Shared cognitive resources therefore do not imply equivalent methods or standards of justification.

The heuristic also has clear limits. Its Greek-derived vocabulary should not be treated as a universal taxonomy of human knowledge. Theos, in particular, cannot adequately encompass religious traditions that are not organized around gods or person-like supernatural agents, and the meanings associated with mythos and logos are themselves historically situated. Locally meaningful categories should replace these terms whenever they obscure rather than clarify the organization of a particular historical or cultural case. The processual question is how these modes persist, interact, become reorganized, or lose prominence over time; the epistemological question is how particular claims are warranted. Keeping these questions distinct allows historical transformation to be investigated without turning mythos-theos-logos into either a hierarchy of rationality or a universal theory of cultural development.

Implications for Measuring Religiosity and Spirituality

Religiosity/spirituality (R/S) is treated as a provisional umbrella rather than an assumed unitary latent trait. Belief, ritual participation, identity, experience, authority, and social relationships can be assessed separately and then examined for their organization within a population.

Whether these dimensions form a higher-order factor, a network, or partly independent processes is an empirical question. Attendance can index participation without exhausting religious cognition. Affiliation can identify membership without measuring experiential commitment. This approach does not require abandoning conventional scales. It requires matching measures to explanatory targets and testing the assumptions linking them. Cross-cultural comparisons additionally require evidence that translated items and response categories refer to sufficiently comparable phenomena.

Indigenous Knowledge and Epistemic Recoding

Indigenous knowledge provides a consequential case for examining how practices are translated across institutions. Spiers et al. (2024) document diverse historical and contemporary literatures on Indigenous psilocybin mushroom practices. Their annotated bibliography supports attention to plurality and context; it is neither a clinical efficacy study nor evidence for one uninterrupted tradition shared by all Indigenous communities.

Epistemic recoding denotes changes in the relevant object, method, evidence, and authority when a practice enters another setting. A biomedical investigation might isolate a compound and measurable outcome, whereas a community practice may organize the relevant event through relationships, interpretation, and ceremonial activity. These accounts can address different questions: contextual relevance does not validate every metaphysical interpretation, and therapeutic claims require independent evidence rather than inference from cultural continuity.

Research can compare community accounts, ethnographic records, and scientific publications to examine what is retained, omitted, translated, or reassigned. Such comparisons should not portray Indigenous knowledge as a uniform precursor awaiting scientific confirmation; relevant communities are contemporary participants in knowledge production.

Interpretive collaboration, attention to language, and appropriate control over culturally restricted material are therefore methodological requirements rather than optional additions.

Historical pathways should be documented rather than fitted to a presumed sequence of marginalization, extraction, and recognition. The analytic target is the transformation of objects, methods, evidential standards, and authority relations across settings, not a predetermined narrative of epistemic succession.

Conceptual Change, Mind Attribution, and Authority

Conceptual change can be studied through learning tasks, speeded judgments, and repeated assessments of explanatory relationships. The central question is whether earlier structures disappear, remain available in particular contexts, or acquire new functions. Retention of a label should be distinguished from retention of an inference.

Historical analysis can investigate terms such as soul, mind, consciousness, and cognition without arranging them in a presumed succession. Their meanings overlap and diverge across languages, institutions, and genres. Semantic modeling should therefore be paired with historically grounded interpretation (Muthukrishna et al., 2021).

Mind-attribution research provides another application. Comparisons involving stones, plants, fungi, animals, humans, artificial agents, and supernatural agents concern human judgments about these entities. They do not establish actual consciousness or place the entities on an evolutionary ladder. Gray et al. (2007) distinguish agency and experience as dimensions of mind perception, motivating separate investigation of these judgments.

Finally, source-attribution experiments can hold a proposition constant while varying whether it is attributed to a scientist, religious specialist, Indigenous knowledge holder, public institution, or artificial system. Source effects alone do not demonstrate irrationality: deference may track relevant expertise. Stronger designs independently vary source identity, demonstrated accuracy, and domain relevance.

Research Propositions and Empirical Agenda

The following propositions are conditional hypotheses rather than established findings.

Each application requires a specified outcome, meaningful effect threshold, adequate precision, and competing explanations. Failure of a prediction challenges the corresponding application; evidence from one application cannot establish universal validity.

Where cultural mechanisms are at issue, the empirical agenda should distinguish change produced by differential selection among extant variants from change produced by biased transformation during reconstruction. At minimum, studies should record which variants are generated, how participants alter inherited material, which variants are preferentially adopted or retained, and what survives into the next transmission episode (Mesoudi, 2021; Acerbi et al., 2021). A mechanism-focused extension can distinguish selection from transformation by varying whether participants choose among fixed candidate procedures, modify an inherited procedure, or can do both. Differential survival of pre-existing variants would support a selection component; systematic directional alteration during reconstruction, especially when candidate frequencies are controlled, would support biased or convergent transformation. Because both processes may operate together, the aim is to estimate their relative contribution rather than force a binary classification.

Eight Testable Propositions

Subsequent participants learn from the resulting records and procedures. Outcomes include fidelity to earlier descriptions, current classification accuracy, and the accuracy achieved by these subsequent learners. The design permits a procedure to preserve wording while losing competence, or to change its representations while maintaining a usable discrimination.

Better prediction by the second model would support the relevance of historical and relational variables in this task. It would not establish that existing learning theories cannot explain the result. The purpose is to turn the framework into a discriminating comparison, linking relational organization, adaptation, and transmission within one design.

Discussion

Theoretical Contribution

The framework addresses persistence of specified epistemic capacities under changes in their supporting organization. Its principal distinction is between continuity of content, continuity of function, and continuity of identity. These outcomes can vary independently and should not be collapsed into a single measure of cultural survival.

Boundary Conditions

Faithful reproduction can outperform transformation when relevant demands remain stable. Reorganization can fail when indispensable information is lost, and distributed arrangements can deteriorate when coordination costs exceed their benefits. When a prespecified capacity falls below its meaningful threshold, the outcome should be classified as degradation, fragmentation, functional loss, or dissolution rather than persistence. Content, function, and identity can also diverge: identity may persist while function collapses, or function may be preserved after identity changes. These are substantive limits rather than outcomes to be redescribed as successful transformation.

The framework also preserves differences between explanatory levels. Biological regulation is not equivalent to propositional knowledge. Cultural transmission is not identical to genetic inheritance. Collective competence does not establish a collective conscious subject.

Similarly, statistical interaction, system-level description, and ontological emergence must remain distinct. An organization-sensitive explanation may be mechanistic and compatible with localized processes. The selection of explanatory units should follow the phenomenon rather than an advance commitment to locating cognition at the widest possible scale.

Persistence and Epistemic Justification

A belief can spread because it is memorable, authoritative, useful, or enforced, regardless of its truth. Conversely, a well-supported claim can fail to spread. A descriptive account of transmission cannot replace an evaluation of evidence, justification, and error correction.

This distinction is especially important for the religious and Indigenous cases.

Recognizing that a tradition preserves practical knowledge does not validate every proposition within it. Equally, disagreement with particular propositions does not demonstrate that the surrounding system contains no competence or reliable information.

The relevant task is to identify which claims and practices are being evaluated, by what criteria, and for which purposes. The processual framework investigates their organization and transformation while leaving their specific epistemic standing open to appropriate evaluation.

Limitations and Future Development

Relational language becomes uninformative when every outcome is redescribed as emergence or every alteration as reorganization. Applications should therefore define retained content, changed relations, failure, and novelty before interpreting the evidence.

The eight propositions also do not uniquely distinguish this framework from every neighboring account. Future work should derive domain-specific models and compare them with established learning, transmission, and organizational explanations. The framework gains support when its distinctions improve predictive or explanatory performance, not merely when observations can be expressed in its vocabulary. The decisive distinction is between continuity of content, function, and identity.

Transformation supports persistence only when it contributes to maintaining a specified capacity.

Following retained content through changed organization and subsequent transmission provides a common research problem across cognitive and cultural settings, while leaving the mechanisms and explanatory adequacy open to investigation.

To test cumulative rather than single-step persistence, the procedure can be extended across successive learner generations. Relevant outcomes include functional performance at each generation, best-so-far performance, backward slippage from previously achieved performance, retention of beneficial modifications, procedural complexity, and the performance of naïve learners exposed to the transmitted record. Transmission-chain research treats cumulative culture as repeated modification plus social transmission, but methodological analyses caution that simple performance increases alone do not establish a ratchet effect (Caldwell & Millen, 2008; Miton & Charbonneau, 2018). Evidence for ratcheting would therefore require retention and retransmission of advantageous modifications across generations rather than merely withingeneration practice effects.

Monotonic improvement, however, is not a general prediction of the framework. Under changing demands, successful persistence may consist of maintaining competence despite contextual change. Increased complexity without preserved or improved function would not count as evidence that transformation supported persistence.

The conditional prediction is that opportunities for reorganization will better sustain performance when a learned procedure no longer fits the distribution of useful cues. Effects attributable only to additional practice, motivation, or information access would not identify the proposed mechanism. An improvement confined to current participants would support local adaptation, but not the stronger claim about renewed transmission.

An information-based model predicts outcomes from available cues and measured individual knowledge. A history-sensitive model additionally represents learned procedures and their interaction with opportunities for revision. Their predictions should be compared on heldout groups with model complexity controlled.

Historical dependence does not entail historical inevitability. The same inherited resources can support different developments. An archive is simultaneously a resource for later investigation and the product of earlier decisions about what to preserve, classify, or exclude.

The relevant traditions already contain resources for studying this problem. Culturalevolutionary theory examines retention and change, distributed cognition examines organized performance, conceptual-change research investigates the influence of earlier representations, and historical psychology examines temporal variation. The proposed contribution is their coordination within a common explanatory sequence: antecedent configuration, organizational change, specified persistence criterion, and subsequent transmission. This sequence keeps continuity of content, function, and identity analytically separable while requiring explicit alternatives against which a transformation claim can be compared.Relative to debates between selection-centered and cultural-attraction accounts, the framework is mechanistically pluralist. High-fidelity copying, differential selection, and biased or convergent transformation are treated as candidate processes whose contributions must be identified empirically; none is built into the definition of persistence through transformation. The framework's distinct claim concerns whether organizational change contributes to preserving specified capacities across subsequent transmission. This is not a claim that persistence through change is itself newly discovered. Nor does the framework require competing theories to be characterized as static or exclusively individualistic. Its value depends on whether jointly specifying historical resources, current organization, preserved capacity, and later transmission improves explanation or prediction in a particular setting.The stronger causal proposition requires showing that transformation contributes to maintaining a capacity. Mere survival alongside change is insufficient. Experiments can manipulate opportunities for reorganization while matching information and resources. Historical analyses require evidence of mechanisms and plausible alternatives rather than retrospective resemblance alone.

Historical work faces additional limits, including selective preservation, changing genres, uncertain temporal resolution, and unequal representation of communities. Computational analysis should complement close interpretation rather than replace it. Cross-cultural experimental work likewise requires attention to whether measures identify comparable constructs. Finally, the article is a selective theoretical synthesis. Its applications illustrate a research program rather than exhaust the relevant literature. Further development should prioritize a small number of well-specified comparisons, allowing successes and failures to refine the framework's scope. Conclusion Knowledge systems can remain recognizable while their components, relations, and environments change. The proposed framework makes this problem explicit by distinguishing a relational field from continuity, discontinuity, selective reorganization, and emergence. Its central proposition is conditional: some systems persist partly because transformation renews the practices through which their capacities are reproduced. For cognitive psychology, this directs attention to conceptual persistence, mind attribution, and epistemic trust. For the cognitive science of religion, it connects individual cognition with memory, ritual, authority, and transmission. For cultural evolution and the history of psychology, it examines how inherited resources and changing institutions shape subsequent inquiry.
