To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

p=0.33 The tests for subgroup differences were nonsignificant (pandemic analysis: p=0.33; end decade analysis: p=0.77).
p=0.77 The tests for subgroup differences were nonsignificant (pandemic analysis: p=0.33; end decade analysis: p=0.77).

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Abstract

Background: The treatment prevalence of mental health problems among children and adolescents has risen significantly in recent decades. It is unclear whether the increase in treatment prevalence is caused by changes in epidemiological prevalence. Method: A systematic review and meta-analysis of repeated cross-sectional studies involving children and adolescents up to the age of 18 was conducted. The review included studies from around the world that were carried out using identical study designs at least twice in the same region. Results: 58 publications were included, with data collected between 1973 and 2023. The overall odds ratio for changes in prevalence was 1.38 (95% confidence interval 1.26-1.52). This corresponds to a risk ratio of 1.32. The odds ratio for females was 1.47 (95% CI: 1.28-1.70), and the odds ratio for males was 1.25 (95% CI: 1.09-1.43). The greatest increase in prevalence was observed in the pandemic decade of 2020. Discussion: The overall result corresponds to a 3.7% increase in prevalence over the entire 50year period. This can be interpreted as a slight increase. Conclusion: The rise in psychiatric treatment for children and adolescents is not primarily due to an increase in epidemiological prevalence.

Introduction

The mental health of children and adolescents has received considerable attention in recent years, both in the scientific community and in public debate. The Lancet Psychiatry Commission on Youth Mental Health recently identified a global mental health crisis in children and adolescents, due to rising treatment rates and other deteriorating issues such increasing loneliness (McGorry et al., 2024). The issue of media consumption in this context has been of great public concern (Haidt, 2024), suggesting that the youth mental health crisis started with the widespread utilization of smartphones in the 2010s which is now approaching universality. Other factors considered relevant to the perceived crisis include climate breakdown, loneliness, body dissatisfaction, childhood maltreatment, extremist political attitudes, dysfunctional parenting, and the decline in freedom to engage in independent activities such as playing outside supervised areas. Additionally, both in research and in health policy, the conviction has prevailed that the COVID-19 pandemic has had the most substantial negative psychosocial consequences for this age group with its impact extending for several years beyond the initial peaks of the crisis (Panchal et al., 2023).

An associated trend was the significantly increasing rates of treatment for young people with mental disorders. Even before the pandemic, there was a surge in young people seeking psychological treatment and being prescribed psychotropic drugs (Collishaw, 2015; Steinhausen, 2015). The increase in treatments for autism and attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder has attracted particular attention. In various countries, this development has even led to political reactions regarding research into the causes or the issue of overdiagnosis (Pearson, 2025). However, it is unclear whether the increase in therapeutic treatments has anything to do with the 'true' prevalence (Richter et al., 2026). Treatment prevalence studies have the disadvantage that the characteristics of the care system can be decisive (for example, access to treatment or treatment capacities) and, therefore, do not accurately reflect the underlying prevalence of mental health problems.

Before we describe further epidemiological aspects, we must clarify a terminological issue. In the remainder of this article, we will use the term 'mental health problems' to describe the characteristic we are investigating. We use this term for two reasons. Firstly, the concept of 'mental illness' remains controversial among service users and, secondly, as explained in detail below, we include studies that have used symptoms or syndromes such as 'behavioural problems' or 'distress'. Such characteristics also do not meet the criteria of the DSM or ICD classification systems, so that the term 'disorder' cannot be applied here. 'Mental health problems' can be seen as an umbrella concept that encompasses all variants (Richter & Dixon, 2023).

The question of whether the prevalence of mental health problems have increased in children and adolescents has long been the subject of epidemiological research (Rutter & Smith, 1995).

Given the well-established epidemiological observation that 50 per cent of all first manifestations of mental disorders have occurred by the age of 19 or 20 (McGrath et al., 2023), the question of increasing mental health problems is of interest to the entire field of mental health care, beyond children and adolescent care. Two earlier global reviews (without metaanalyses) from the mid-2010s concluded that the prevalence of mental health problems of children and adolescents had hardly changed at all over the previous decades, with only internalizing symptoms in young women showing such an increase (Bor et al., 2014; Collishaw, 2015). However, in addition, numerous behaviours associated with substance use, abuse and dependence that are classified as risky have declined significantly in recent decades (Grucza et al., 2016; Twenge, 2023).

Besides treatment prevalence, the predominant methodological approaches in this context include (1) longitudinal cohort studies and (2) repeated cross-sectional studies. The longitudinal design not only reflects changes in prevalence over time but also reflects the ageing process of the study participants. In view of the biological and social changes in adolescence, longitudinal study designs are therefore subject to considerable uncertainty. From an epidemiological perspective, repeated cross-sectional studies are best suited to investigate prevalence changes that are solely attributable to the time factor (Richter et al., 2019).

To the best of our knowledge, there has been no meta-analysis that is based exclusively on repeated cross-sectional studies on changes in the prevalence of mental health problems in children and underage adolescents. Existing meta-analyses pool studies with different designs (Ludwig-Walz et al., 2022 , 2023) or use the year of data collection to compare point prevalence rates among different publications (Deng et al., 2023; Shorey et al., 2022). Similarly, another meta-analysis used the timing of data collection during the COVID-19 pandemic to address temporal changes of point prevalence rates (Racine et al., 2021). However, these approaches are less effective from an epidemiological perspective, as it cannot be ensured that the pooled studies have the same research designs.

We are only aware of three meta-studies that address similar issues in some way, but each of these have methodological issues which we seek to improve upon. One of the meta-studies analysed psychosomatic complaints surveyed in repeated cross-sectional studies between 1982 and 2013 (Potrebny et al., 2017). The meta-analysis yielded a pooled significant odds ratio of 1.04, which was interpreted by the authors as a minor increase. However, these psychosomatic diagnoses are not part of the core mental health construct and cannot meaningfully be applied beyond its specific focus.

The two other meta-studies known to us used a similar analysis method to the study presented here to a certain extent worked with data from the Global Burden of Disease project. The first of these studies focused on Europe exclusively and examined, among other things, changes in prevalence between 1990 and 2019 (Castelpietra et al., 2022). This study found, for example, increasing rates of anxiety disorders, but decreasing rates of depressive disorders and alcoholrelated disorders. Unfortunately, no overall prevalence rates were reported. The second study had a global focus (Yang et al., 2024). Based on the same age range up to 24 years, the global meta-regression analyses showed a significant increase in the global prevalence of mental disorders between 1990 and 2019, but these changes were primarily demographically driven. The estimated age-standardized prevalence showed virtually no change. However, in many cases, the Global Burden of Disease studies do not work with collected data but are based on metaregressions of numerous contextual factors, which are then used to estimate prevalence rates (Murray, 2022). In this respect, there is much uncertainty regarding the reported changes in prevalence.

There is a clear need to gather prevalence rates from general population groups to inform professionals and warrant plans and priorities for policy makers. Estimating changes in the extent of children and adolescents' mental health problems will further support the current need in health care provision and future improvement in service planning. It will also inform an important topic in contemporary public debate. Although previous studies have reported prevalence of mental health problems in children and adolescents, to our knowledge there has been no attempt to consolidate these prevalence rates globally using repeated cross-sectional studies.

Methods

The aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to examine global prevalence rates over time in mental health problems amongst children and adolescents (4-18 years) in studies using repeated cross-sectional population surveys with specific reference to changes since the onset of COVID-19. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines have been followed. The study is registered with Prospero (CRD42019121701). We followed the study registration with minor changes related to subgroup analyses. Ethical approval was not required for the study.

Eligibility Criteria

Inclusion criteria were applied to publications reporting repeated cross-sectional studies with at least two time points on any kind of mental problem in children and adolescents (4-18years).

When a study reported multiple survey waves, the first and last observation point were included.

We used a range of methods to define mental health problems. We included formal diagnoses based on DSM as well as those based on validated self-report symptom and distress scales. We included studies reporting in the following languages: English, French, German, and Dutch. No time restrictions or geographical restrictions were applied. Exclusion criteria comprised: longitudinal and cohort study designs which assessed the same population at both time points; treatment prevalence studies (including consumption of medication or prescriptions); studies in which the diagnosis was not core to the mental health construct, for example substance use/misuse, psychosomatic symptoms or personality traits; studies on suicides, suicidality or self-harm.

Information Sources

The search was performed on the following databases: Medline (Ovid), Psychinfo and Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature. The search was conducted across all databases in May 2023 and updated in March 2025.

Search strategy

The primary search process was conducted by AD and DR (see appendix for search strategy). The filter limited to ages (birth-childhood, adolescents-18 years) was selected.

Selection process

The study selection process was conducted by all authors who were paired to screen a selection of publications in batches. Duplicates were identified and removed electronically and then manually. Screening comprised reading titles and abstracts at stage 1, then full texts of selected studies at stage 2. Disagreement on inclusion was resolved by consulting the lead author (DR).

When multiple studies reported different analyses of the same dataset over the same time period, the most representative study was selected (e.g., the study with the longest timeframe) and all the other studies were excluded.

Risk of study bias assessment

To assess the risk of bias, a quality appraisal was carried out for all included studies. As the current instruments were not suitable for our purposes of appraising repeated cross-sectional population studies specifically, we adapted an instrument by Munn et al (Munn et al., 2015).

Higher scores were given to publications that had: national representativeness, clearly stated age range, random sampling, sample size greater than 500 per time point, description of subjects and settings, response rate of at least 80% or at least 50% with explicit justification, utilisation of a DSM or ICD related tool or validated instrument, identical procedures at both time points and a statistical analysis which was sufficiently described. The same pairs of reviewers conducted quality appraisal independently then discussed within their pairs the final quality score. The risk of bias assessment is provided in the appendix.

Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted by DR and 5% of papers were cross-checked by AD. We included the following for data extraction; authors, publication year, country, world region, first data point year, second/last datapoint year, source of response (child, parent, teacher), mental health problem (depression, anxiety etc.), type of assessment (clinical diagnosis, symptoms, distress) and instrument used, number of cases per time point, youngest/oldest age, decade of first/last sample, sample size per time point, data collection during COVID-19 and quality appraisal score.

When studies reported percentages only and the sample size, we calculated and extracted the raw counts from these variables as required for effect size calculation by the statistical software.

Analytical Strategy

The analytical strategy followed the procedure of a previous study on prevalence changes of mental disorders in adults (Richter et al., 2019). We utilised a methodology which is usually applied to intervention studies that compare intervention and control conditions with odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CI hereafter) as the effect size metric. The first time point constituted a baseline or control assessment and was used as a control condition and the second or last time point was comparable to a post-intervention assessment to find out whether changes have occurred. In other words, we considered 'time' as a form of intervention.

Current taxonomy research on mental disorders postulates a higher order of spectra (HiTOP) or even a general psychopathology beyond DSM/ICD classifications (Kotov et al., 2021). For this reason, we pooled all effect sizes to identify a general trend regarding the prevalence of mental health problems independent of the underlying construct. Most studies reported their results separately for each gender, but some reported results aggregated across genders. Therefore, we conducted three main analyses: total (encompassing both genders), females, and males. Due to this circumstance, primary observations in the gendered analyses do not add up to the total observations. For sub-group analyses over time, we conducted an analysis of all studies and then compared pre-pandemic studies (both timepoints prior to 2020) with studies that had at least one timepoint in or after 2020. We also conducted an analysis separately for each decade based on the year of the second timepoint (1990s, 2000s, 2010s, 2020s). The aim of this latter analysis was to determine whether certain decades showed a particular increase. Finally, we performed a meta-regression with the aim of exploring moderators of between-study heterogeneity. Peters' regression test was used to check for publication bias (Peters et al., 2006).

We used R (version 4.5.3) package 'meta' (version 8.2-1) (Schwarzer et al., 2015) and package 'metafor' (version 4.8-0) (Viechtbauer, 2010) with random effects models for the statistical analyses.

We also used the R package 'effectsize' (version 1.0.1) to convert odds ratio into risk ratio (Ben-Shachar et al., 2020). This is due to the general recommendation to report risk ratios instead of odds ratios, as they are easier to understand (Grant, 2014; van Rhee & Suurmond, 2015). Effect sizes such as the odds ratio or risk ratio are often not interpreted appropriately and tend to cause confusion due to their statistical and verbal characteristics (odds vs. risk) (Grant, 2014; Voas & Watt, 2025). A higher or lower risk between two groups is easier to understand than a higher or lower odds.

Results

Study Characteristics

Our search identified 4829 publications from databases. We assessed 213 publications in full text, and 58 studies were selected for inclusion (see Flow Chart, Appendix). Of these 46 studies were conducted entirely prior to the COVID-19 pandemic and 12 had at least one timepoint during the COVID-19 period. Altogether, 540,006 primary observations were included for the first time point and 520,672 primary observations for the second and last time points in the total analysis.

We identified several characteristics from the studies. 36 samples were from Europe, with a further 6 samples from Asia, 5 from Oceania, 3 from South America and 8 from North America.

Of the 58 studies 40 were based on instruments in which the child or adolescent self-reported on their condition. The rest of the studies were based on reports by others (carers or teachers) or a combination of sources (carers, teachers and children/adolescents). Carers and teachers were the main source in samples involving very young children. 31 studies reported data separately for females and 31 for males. The overall time-range for baseline estimates was 50 years, ranging from 1973 to 2023. The average duration between the first and the last year within each study that reported total data was 11.7 years (median: 10 years). The baseline sample sizes were very heterogeneous and ranged from 279 to 242,734. In terms of mental health problems, we found 36 studies covering general and behavioural problems, 15 covering depression, 9 covering general mental health issues, 4 covering eating disorders, 2 covering anxiety and 1 insomnia. Risk of bias analyses indicated that the quality of the studies ranged from moderate to good. 37 of the 58 included studies scored 6 or more out of a possible 9 points.

Main Analysis

The main analysis (Table 1) revealed a total odds ratio of 1.38 (95%-confidence interval: 1.26-1.52, I² = 99%, τ² = 0.13, p = 0.000), see also Forest Plot (Appendix). The publications with the female data showed an odds ratio of 1.47 (95%-CI: 1.21-1.65), while the publications with the male data showed an odds ratio of 1.25 (95%-CI: 1.06-1.47). We found a high heterogeneity in the pooled effect sizes of the total sample, the female sample, and the male sample (more than 90%). The overall odds ratio of 1.38 corresponds to a risk ratio of 1.32.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

The first subgroup analysis compared studies with the final endpoint during the pandemic with entirely pre-pandemic studies showed that the latter had an odds ratio of 1.35 (95%-CI 1.32-1.51;

Table 2) while the pandemic related studies had an odds ratio of 1.51 (95%-CI: 1.25-1.82). The second subgroup analysis by endpoint decade showed that the calculated odds ratios for the decades prior to the 2020s were relatively similar, while the pandemic decade of the 2020s had a relatively higher odds ratio (Table 3). However, only two included studies were conducted in the 1990s yielding a highly imprecise estimate. The tests for subgroup differences were nonsignificant (pandemic analysis: p=0.33; end decade analysis: p=0.77).

The third subgroup analysis covered different mental health problems (Table 4). This analysis revealed a significant difference between the subgroups (p=0.04), with depression standing out in particular. Whilst publications reporting a general mental health problem had an odds ratio of 1.29 (95%-CI: 1.14-1.47), those reporting depression had an odds ratio of 1.58 (95%-CI: 1.37-1.83).

To test the robustness of the analyses, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by excluding the samples assessed using DSM instruments (k=7) from the analysis. This analysis yielded an odds ratio of 1.36 (95% CI: 1.23-1.51).

The meta-regression with moderators world region, mental health problem group, end decade and range of years between first and last data collection yielded no significant model and no significant moderator (Table 5). The test for publication bias was not significant (t = -0.43, p = 0.6682), indicating no funnel plot asymmetry (see Funnel Plot, Appendix).

Limitations

First, as quite often with global epidemiological analyses, the publications used in our data set are based overwhelmingly on samples in the Global North. Hence, firm conclusions on other world regions cannot be made due to a shortage of studies. Second, the coverage of mental illhealth problems is unevenly distributed with depression and behavioural problems as the most surveyed conditions in the original studies. Third, as our analytical approach is focussed on the bigger picture of prevalence change for mental health problems of children and adolescents over time, we cannot rule out that there are temporal fluctuations in terms of prevalence changes between the two time points that we used in our analysis.

Discussion

This systematic review employed a meta-analysis to examine global prevalence rate changes of mental health problems amongst children and young adolescents based on 58 studies from the period of 1973-2023. The meta-analysis yielded an odds ratio of 1.38. The relevant question here is how this effect size should be interpreted. Is an OR of 1.38 or an OR of 1.51 from studies that had their endpoint during the pandemic a lot or a little?

When interpreting a change in prevalence, it is helpful to first know the baseline, or base rate.

According to a recent meta-analysis of studies conducted between 2015 and 2020, the prevalence of mental disorders among children and adolescents in Europe is approximately 15.5 percent (Sacco et al., 2024). Since our meta-analysis suggests an increase over several decades that date further back, we hypothetically assume a base rate of 12 percent.

At a base rate of 12 percent our estimated odds ratio of 1.38 corresponds to a risk ratio of 1.32. This means that children and adolescents currently have a 32 percent higher risk of developing a mental disorder compared to previous times. Translated into prevalence, this would currently be 15.7 percent (a baseline prevalence of 12 percent increased by 32 percent). Overall, the prevalence of mental problems of children and adolescents would thus have increased by 3.7 percent. Given the time range of 50 years, we regard this as a minor increase.

When we compare our overall results with those of other meta-studies, we see similar trends and conclusions. Our assessment that the mental health problems of children and adolescents have only increased slightly, especially in the pre-pandemic phase, is consistent with the metaanalysis of self-report psychosomatic complaints with studies between 1980 and 2013 (Potrebny et al., 2017). Our findings can also be interpreted in a similar way to the results of the Global Burden of Disease study (Yang et al., 2024), which reported some fluctuations in prevalence but no increase in prevalence in the age-adjusted analysis. Using a different meta-analytical methodology based on proportions, another research group concluded that depressive disorders in children under the age of 13 did not increase between 2000 and 2019 (Spoelma et al., 2023). A meta-analysis of longitudinal cohort studies, with data collected before and during the COVID-19 pandemic, found a slight but statistically significant increase in general mental health problems and depressive symptoms among children and adolescents, but not in anxietyrelated symptoms (Sun et al., 2026). The estimates from this study were significantly lower than those from an earlier meta-analysis of cohort studies (Miao et al., 2023). Whilst the estimates were numerically higher, the authors cautioned against directly translating these into clinical relevance.

Another meta-analysis of longitudinal studies found standardized differences of 0.1 for anxiety symptoms and 0.26 for depressive symptoms (Madigan et al., 2023). A value of 0.2 is commonly regarded as the threshold for small increases (Hopkins et al., 2009).

Our other findings are also consistent with the general research literature. We found a significantly greater change in prevalence in studies that reached their endpoint during the pandemic. This corresponds to the research literature in psychiatric epidemiology for this age group (Samji et al., 2022; Wolf & Schmitz, 2024).

However, our findings do not support the hypothesis that the 2010s were associated with a particular rise in prevalence, as suggested by Haidt (Haidt, 2024). According to our analysis, the influence of digital tools and social media would be less profound in terms of mental health prevalence changes than is generally assumed. Furthermore, the higher burden we found among females compared to males is consistent with the research literature (Herrmann et al., 2024). Although this literature does not report any significant differences between the genders overall, females show a higher burden in the area of internalising disorders, while externalising disorders dominate among males. However, the primary studies included in our meta-analysis predominantly measured internalising constructs.

Although the pooled estimates suggest an overall small increase, heterogeneity was high, likely reflecting real differences across regions, periods, measures, informants, ages, and conditions.

The pooled effect should be read as an average trend, not a uniform change, and deviations are expected across contexts or between conditions. However, the meta-regression was unable to identify any significant moderator. Neither a specific mental health problem, nor a particular region of the world, nor a specific decade, nor the number of years between the first and last data collection proved to be predictive of heterogeneity.

Conclusion

The mental health of children and adolescent is a significant concern with profound implications for service demands in mental health care. To our knowledge this is the first meta-analysis that calculates pooled prevalence rates of mental health problems from studies in this population which consistently use the most robust design.

The trend we found of only a slight increase in mental health problems does not correspond with the high demand for psychiatric or psychological treatment in this age group. We therefore conclude that only a small part of the increase in demand is based on changes in the epidemiological prevalence. Similar to adults, other socio-cultural factors appear to play a role here as well, driving demand for psychiatric services (Richter et al., 2026).
