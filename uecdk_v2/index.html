Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Transitions in Teachers' Technology Utilization: Evidence from a Large-Scale, Longitudinal, and International Panel Study 1. Introduction

The integration of digital technologies into teaching and learning has become a central priority for educational systems worldwide (European Commission, 2021 , 2024; Law et al., 2018; OECD, 2021), placing teachers at the forefront of efforts to translate this transformation into classroom practice. A substantial body of research shows that teachers play a crucial role in developing students' digital skills (Siddiq et al., 2016; Tondeur et al., 2017). However, the literature does not provide a clear understanding of which teaching practices are most conducive to fostering students' digital competence development. One reason for this uncertainty may be that teachers vary widely in the degree to which they engage in digital teaching practices. Two teachers rarely employ technology in the same way, and this heterogeneity in enacted teaching practices remains largely unobserved in large-scale research.

Previous studies have often focused on observable characteristics such as gender, experience, or education level but have paid less attention to unobserved patterns that reflect how teachers combine different forms of technology use in their classrooms (Hatlevik & Christophersen, 2013; Konstantinidou & Scherer, 2022; Scherer et al., 2015). Capturing this heterogeneity matters practically as well as conceptually: professional development and policy interventions that target a single average 'level' of technology use risk mismatching teachers whose practices combine differently (e.g., high pedagogical emphasis on ICT paired with limited classroom enactment, versus consistent but narrow tool use), and person-centered approaches are explicitly designed to recover such qualitatively distinct combinations rather than assume a single underlying continuum (Morin & Marsh, 2015; Nylund-Gibson & Choi, 2018).

Two established frameworks offer contrasting accounts of where such heterogeneity resides: in what teachers know or in what teachers do. The Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge model (TPACK; Mishra & Koehler, 2006) frames technology integration primarily as a question of teacher knowledge, representing an intersection between technological, pedagogical, and content expertise. The Substitution-Augmentation-Modification-Redefinition model (SAMR; Puentedura, 2012), by contrast, frames it behaviorally, as a continuum of enacted practice that ranges from technology as a substitute for existing tools to technology that redefines what is pedagogically possible. The present study adopts the latter, behavioral perspective: rather than inferring technology integration from what teachers know, we examine what teachers actually do in the classroom including their patterns of practice.

From this perspective, engaging in digital teaching practices can be viewed as an individual behavioral choice, influenced but not determined by contextual factors such as school resources, collaboration, or professional development (Ertmer & Ottenbreit-Leftwich, 2010; Opfer & Pedder, 2011; Scherer & Teo, 2019). Teachers operate within institutional environments that can either support or constrain their capacity to innovate with technology, yet their actual classroom behavior reflects individual agency, preferences, and self-efficacy. Understanding how these individual and contextual elements jointly shape technology use therefore calls for methods that can recover latent forms of diversity in practice directly from data, rather than assuming a single, uniform pattern of adoption across teachers; the present study takes up this challenge.

The present study addresses this need by examining the latent transitions in teachers' technology utilization using large-scale longitudinal data from the International Computer and Information Literacy Study (ICILS) Teacher Panel 2018-2020. Specifically, it investigates (a) the existence of unobserved profiles of ICT use that reflect distinct combinations of teaching practices and emphasis on students' digital skill development, (b) teachers' transitions between these profiles over time, and (c) the individual and contextual factors associated with profile membership and change. Using latent transition analysis across three educational systems (i.e., Denmark, Finland, and Uruguay) that represent distinct contexts of digital education policy and practice, this study contributes to understanding how teachers' technology integration evolves and how individual and contextual factors shape its development. Throughout this article, we use technology utilization as our primary term for the empirical construct measured here, that is, the observed frequency and pedagogical emphasis of teachers' ICT-related classroom practices. We reserve technology integration for the broader theoretical process, of which utilization, as we measure it, is one behavioral manifestation. Related terms used in the literature we cite, such as digital teaching practices or ICT use, are treated as synonyms for technology utilization unless otherwise specified.

2. Theoretical Background

>Theoretical Background

2.1 Digital Literacy and Teachers' Technology Integration

The concept of digital literacy provides a useful lens for understanding the pedagogical and behavioral dimensions of technology integration. Digital literacy is understood as the ability to use technology to investigate, manage information, create content, communicate, collaborate, and solve problems (Fraillon & Rožman, 2025). Within education, this construct extends beyond technical proficiency to include the capacity to engage critically, creatively, and responsibly with digital tools. Teachers therefore play an important role in designing learning experiences that integrate technology for inquiry, collaboration, and creation rather than information delivery alone (Ertmer & Ottenbreit-Leftwich, 2010; Tondeur et al., 2017; Siddiq et al., 2016).

In recent years, several frameworks have conceptualized how digital literacy and technology integration intersect within classroom teaching. The ICILS assessment framework distinguishes between computer and information literacy (CIL) and computational thinking (CT), reflecting two complementary aspects of digital competence. Similar ideas underpin international frameworks such as UNESCO's ICT Competency Framework for Teachers (Version 3; UNESCO, 2018) and the European Commission's DigCompEdu (Redecker, 2017), which emphasize that effective teaching for digital competence combines technical, cognitive, and pedagogical dimensions.

Empirical research, including analyses of ICILS data, consistently highlights wide variation in teachers' technology integration practices both within and across education systems (Hatlevik & Hatlevik, 2018; Konstantinidou & Scherer, 2022). Teachers differ not only in how frequently they use ICT but also in the purposes and pedagogical orientations that guide this use, ranging from direct instruction and information transmission to inquiry-based, collaborative, and creative approaches. These differences are shaped largely by teacher-level factors such as selfefficacy, motivation, and collaboration, as well as by opportunities for professional learning and school-level support (Ertmer & Ottenbreit-Leftwich, 2010; Scherer & Teo, 2019). However, most studies have employed cross-sectional designs, capturing technology integration at a single point in time. While such designs reveal important associations between teacher characteristics and ICT use, they provide only a static snapshot of teachers' digital practices.

A small but growing body of research has examined how teachers' technology-related beliefs and practices change over time. Using a two-wave panel of Chinese K-12 teachers, Lai et al. (2023) found that the associations linking technology-related beliefs, pedagogical practices, and barriers to technology use shifted within a single academic year, with value beliefs becoming less predictive and TPACK-related self-efficacy becoming more predictive of use. Ottenbreit-Leftwich et al. (2018) traced how beginning teachers' technology-related knowledge, beliefs, and practices co-evolved across their early careers. Francom (2020) used a three-year time-series design to show that first-order barriers (e.g., access to tools) diminished over time, whereas second-order barriers (e.g., beliefs, training, and support) remained comparatively persistent. This is a pattern directly relevant to the first-and second-order barrier distinction (Ertmer, 1999) that also informs the present study's covariate selection. The COVID-19 disruption has since motivated several shorter-term longitudinal studies: Paetsch et al. (2023) linked early-career teachers' pre-pandemic self-efficacy and ICT literacy to subsequent changes in technology use following school closures. Wohlfart and Wagner (2025) traced a cyclical pattern of technology acceptance showing rapid initial adaptation followed by reflection and either sustained integration or reassessment across repeated interviews with secondary teachers through the pandemic transition. Despite this progress, existing longitudinal research remains variablecentered, typically tracing single beliefs, barriers, or practices in isolation, and has largely been confined to single national contexts. The present study extends this literature by adopting a person-centered, cross-national approach that captures qualitatively distinct configurations of practice rather than change in individual variables across three educational systems. Because our panel spans this same disruption without a non-pandemic comparison period, we treat the COVID-19 context throughout as an important but uncontrolled condition shaping teachers' technology use, rather than as a quasi-experimental manipulation.

2.2 Conceptual Framework for Teachers' Technology Integration

The integration of digital technologies in teaching is not only a technical process but also a pedagogical and behavioral one, shaped by teachers' underlying instructional approaches and their perceptions of technology. Two broad pedagogical orientations underpin classroom practice: direct instruction and inquiry-based instruction (Reigeluth & Carr-Chellman, 2009).

These orientations describe how teaching is structured, but they do not capture how deeply technology transforms it. The SAMR model (Puentedura, 2012) addresses this second dimension, describing how teachers' technology integration can progress through four developmental levels: Substitution, Augmentation, Modification, and Redefinition. At the lower levels, technology serves to enhance existing instructional methods, whereas at the higher levels it transforms teaching by enabling new forms of learning and interaction.

Beyond these pedagogical dimensions, the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM; Davis, 1989) explains why teachers adopt and sustain technology use. TAM posits that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use shape individuals' attitudes toward technology, which in turn influence behavioral intention and actual use. Extensions of the model highlight additional determinants, including self-efficacy, collaboration, organizational support, and professional development (Teo, 2011; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). These constructs align closely with the covariates examined in this study-teachers' self-efficacy, perceived resources, engagement in professional learning, and views on technology-and provide a theoretical explanation for the factors associated with profile membership and transitions.

Taken together, these frameworks inform three aspects of the present study: First, they shape how we operationalize teachers' technology utilization. The distinction between general classroom use, teaching practices, digital-competence emphasis, and specific tool use in the ICILS conceptual framework (Fraillon et al., 2019) structures the eight profile indicators used to identify latent profiles, and the SAMR's substitution-to-redefinition continuum anticipates a lowto-high integration gradient along which these profiles may later be interpreted. Second, they justify our choice of covariates. Specifically, the TAM's emphasis on perceived usefulness, ease of use, and their antecedents, including self-efficacy, collaboration, professional development, and organizational support (Teo, 2011; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000), motivates the individual and contextual predictors. Third, Ertmer's (1999) distinction between first-order (resource-related) barriers and second-order (belief-and practice-related) barriers organizes these covariates and generates two concrete predictions: (a) Some teachers with adequate access or a high pedagogical emphasis on ICT should nonetheless show comparatively limited classroom implementation; (b) Because second-order barriers are rooted in beliefs and organizational conditions rather than resources, profile membership should change comparatively slowly once established.

2.3 The Present Study

Building on the theoretical foundations outlined above, the present study investigates how teachers' technology utilization manifests and evolves over time across educational systems, drawing data from the large-scale, cross-national ICILS 2018-2020 Teacher Panel Study. While previous research has provided valuable insights into the factors associated with technology integration, most evidence remains cross-sectional, offering only a static view of teachers' ICT practices, and ignores unobserved teacher profiles as a source of heterogeneity. The ICILS panel data make it possible to overcome these limitations by examining changes in the same teachers' digital practices over time. To capture such changes, we adopted a person-centered approach to identify and track unobserved patterns of teachers' ICT utilization and possible transitions between them, using latent transition analyses (LTA). Person-centered approaches have similarly proven valuable for capturing heterogeneous configurations of technology-related practice among other groups of educators (e.g., Scherer et al., 2021). 3. Method

>Method

3.1 Sample

The present study utilized the longitudinal data from the ICILS 2018-2020 Teacher Panel

Study, conducted by the International Association for the Evaluation of Educational

Achievement (IEA). The target population consisted of all teachers who were teaching eighthgrade students (or the nationally equivalent students) in regular school subjects during the ICILS 2018 cycle and who remained in the profession in 2020.

Three countries participated in this panel study: Denmark (DNK), Finland (FIN), and Uruguay (URY). Following a stratified, two-stage cluster sampling design, schools were first selected with a probability proportional to size, followed by a random selection of approximately 15-20 teachers per school. In the present study, we applied a strict inclusion criterion, that is, we selected only those teachers who participated in both measurement occasions (i.e., late 2018 and late 2020/early 2021). This resulted in a final longitudinal sample of more than 2,000 teachers (𝑛 !" # = 445, 𝑛 $%" = 1,246, 𝑛 &'( = 467).

Across the three countries, the teacher samples showed a slight female predominance, consistent with national teacher statistics (about 67.5%). The age distribution was primarily midcareer, with the majority of respondents aged between 35 and 55 years (M = 43.9, SD = 10.4, Mdn = 45 years). In 2018, more than 60% of the participants reported over five years of experience using ICT for instructional purposes. Teachers in this sample taught a broad range of subjects, including Mathematics and Sciences, Languages and Arts, and Human Sciences.

3.2 Measures

Technology utilization for teaching and learning was operationalized as a multidimensional construct encompassing the frequency and emphasis of teachers' digital practices across pedagogical, creative, and technical domains. Rather than treating utilization as a single frequency measure, we captured its configurational nature-recognizing that teachers differ not only in how often they use technology for instruction but in which combinations of practices they enact (cf. Ertmer & Ottenbreit-Leftwich, 2010).

Eight scale scores derived from the ICILS 2018 and 2020 teacher questionnaires served as latent profile indicators (see Table 1). These scales were selected to represent the full range of teacher ICT engagement captured in the ICILS conceptual framework (Fraillon et al., 2020b), spanning general classroom use, teaching practices, emphasis on digital and computational skills, and the use of specific tools. We constructed these scale scores via confirmatory factor analysis with alignment optimization (see 3.3.1) across the three participating countries and the two measurement occasions, ensuring comparability of the profile indicators. Notably, the items measuring the respective scales were the same for ICILS 2018 and 2020. Internal consistencies ranged from McDonald's 𝜔 = .60 to .96 across countries and over time (see Table 1).

Conceptually, USEUTIL is distinct from the other seven indicators, as it captures teachers' use of general-purpose utility software (e.g., word processing, presentation tools) for teaching. This reflects baseline digital-tool fluency, whereas CLASACT, ICTPRAC, ICTEMP, and CODEMP capture more pedagogically specific classroom practices, content-creation tool use, and codingrelated emphasis.

To examine predictors of profile membership and profile transitions (RQ3), we included covariates from three domains: (a) Individual characteristics comprised demographic and professional background variables (e.g., age, gender, years of teaching experience); (b) Psychological antecedents included teachers' ICT self-efficacy and their views on the outcomes of ICT use for teaching and learning; and (c) Contextual and institutional factors captured perceptions of the school environment, including the availability of ICT resources, frequency of ICT-related professional collaboration, and participation in ICT-focused professional development. The selection of these covariates was guided by the second-order barrier framework (Ertmer, 1999), which distinguishes between external, resource-related constraints (first-order barriers) and internal, belief-based orientations (second-order barriers) as drivers of technology integration. Supplementary Material S1 contains a detailed list of these variables, including their internal consistency estimates and correlations.

3.3 Statistical Approaches

To address our research questions, we took four main steps: First, we conducted alignment confirmatory factor analyses (ACFA) to derive comparable factor scores for the eight profile indicators across countries and waves (Section 3.3.1). Second, we used these factor scores in time-and country-specific latent profile analyses (LPA) to identify the number and nature of teachers' ICT utilization profiles on each occasion (Section 3.3.2). Third, we linked the retained profile solutions across waves using latent transition analysis (LTA), testing a series of increasingly constrained specifications. These specifications included basic LTA, randomintercept LTA (RI-LTA), longitudinal profile-similarity models, and, where applicable, a moverstayer extension (Section 3.3.3). Fourth, we introduced teacher-level covariates into the retained LTA models via the R3STEP approach to examine predictors of profile membership and transitions (Section 3.3.4). Throughout, we accounted for the complex ICILS sampling design and conducted a series of sensitivity analyses (Section 3.3.5).

To ultimately identify teachers' latent profiles of technology utilization (RQ1), examine possible transitions over time (RQ2), and quantify how covariates may be associated with profile membership, we chose a mixture modeling framework for our statistical analyses (see Figure 1).

In this framework, unobserved heterogeneity in the teacher data is modeled by a categorical latent variable that allows researchers to classify individuals into distinct latent profiles based on a set of observed profile indicators (Morin & Marsh, 2015; Nylund-Gibson & Choi, 2018).

Latent transition analysis extends this idea by additionally modeling the probabilities of transitioning between these latent profiles across measurement occasions, thereby capturing changes in teachers' profile membership over time.

3.3.1 Step 1: Measurement Models

As a first step, we estimated the measurement models underlying the constructs that later served as profile indicators. Specifically, we identified the constructs, the underlying scales, and the corresponding item indicators from the ICILS 2018 technical report (Fraillon et al., 2020a) and then specified the respective measurement models with reflective latent variables and a categorical treatment item responses. One key challenge with these models was that the data set was comprised of teacher data from multiple countries over time. To ensure that the measurement models were as comparable as possible, we conducted confirmatory factor analysis with alignment optimization (ACFA), using countries × time points as groups. In the present study, we estimated the ACFA models using the Weighted Least Squares with Mean and Variance adjustment (WLSMV) estimator with theta parameterization and treating item responses categorically. We then evaluated the fit of these models and considered conventional guidelines for an acceptable model fit (Comparative Fit Index [CFI] ≥ .95, Tucker-Lewis Index [TLI] ≥ .95, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation [RMSEA] ≤ .08, Standardized Root Mean Square Residual [SRMR] ≤ .08; e.g., Hu & Bentler, 1999; Marsh et al., 2004). Once wellfitting measurement models were established, we extracted their factor scores and used them as observed profile indicators in further analyses (Marsh et al., 2009; Morin & Marsh, 2015). We preferred factor scores to a simultaneous estimation of the measurement and mixture models for three reasons. First, a joint model combining the eight alignment models, which were estimated with categorical items across country-by-wave groups, with a random-intercept latent transition model would have been computationally demanding and prone to non-convergence, particularly given the complex sampling design and the many random starts required for mixture models. For this reason, fully latent mixture models are often impossible to estimate in practice (Morin et al., 2016). Second, the factor scores carry the approximately invariant metric established by the alignment optimization, which keeps the profile indicators comparable across countries and waves (Morin et al., 2016). A joint mixture model could only have reproduced this metric through additional constraints. Third, although factor scores do not control for measurement error in the way latent variables do, they give more weight to items with stronger factor loadings and thus provide a partial control for measurement error, which makes them preferable to scale scores (Morin et al., 2016). Factor scores saved from preliminary, invariance-tested measurement models have also been used as indicators in previous latent transition analyses (e.g., Kam et al., 2016). The cost of this choice is that the measurement error of the factor scores is not propagated into the mixture models (see Section 5.6). We conducted these analyses using the statistical software Mplus 8.9 (Muthén & Muthén, 1998 -2017), using WLSMV estimation with pairwise present correlations to handle missing data.

3.3.2 Step 2: Time-Specific Latent Profile Analysis (LPA)

In a second step, we conducted latent profile analyses for each country and time point to examine the heterogeneity among teachers' technology utilization and identify the number of latent profiles (RQ1). To identify the latent profiles, we estimated several LPA models and varied the potential numbers of profiles. We then compared the LPA models with respect to their information criteria (i.e., Akaike's Information Criterion [AIC], Bayesian Information Criterion [BIC]), the sizes of the latent profiles, the respective entropy (i.e., classification accuracy), and whether the profiles were interpretable (Masyn, 2013; Tein et al., 2013). Finally, we examined the approximate correct model probabilities (cmPk) and Bayes Factors (BFs) to identify the best candidate model among several models (Masyn, 2013). We weighed these criteria as follows.

First, a solution was considered admissible only if its best log-likelihood was replicated and its smallest profile comprised at least 5% of the sample under both the complex and the standard specification (see Section 3.3.5), because non-replicated solutions may reflect local maxima and very small profiles are often unstable or spurious (Masyn, 2013; Nylund-Gibson & Choi, 2018).

Second, among the admissible solutions, we preferred the solution with the lowest BIC, which performs well in class enumeration (Nylund et al., 2007; Tein et al., 2013). Third, we examined whether the profiles of this solution were substantively distinct and interpretable. We did not retain additional profiles favored by the information criteria if they failed the admissibility criteria, because information criteria often continue to decrease in large samples and may then favor spurious profiles (Marsh et al., 2009). If no solution with three or more profiles was admissible under one of the two specifications, we based the decision on the other specification, the profile structure at the other time point, and the profile sizes in the retained RI-LTA (this applied to Denmark at T1; see Section 4.2.1). We specified the LPA models with freely estimated means and variances across profiles (Nylund et al., 2007).

In cases where the same number of profiles existed within a country over time, we further tested for profile invariance over time, following Morin et al.'s (2016) profile similarity testing procedure that estimates several multiple-group LPA models with different invariance constraints on means, variances, and profile distributions. In a next step, we selected the bestfitting LPA invariance model. In cases where the number of profiles differed over time, we did not perform any additional invariance testing of profiles. We estimated all LPA models using the statistical software Mplus 8.9 (Muthén & Muthén, 1998 -2017), using the robust maximumlikelihood estimator (MLR) with the full information maximum-likelihood procedure to handle missing data.

3.3.3 Step 3: Latent Transition Analysis (LTA) and Profile Similarity Over Time

In a third step, we modeled the latent transitions between the profiles over time (RQ2).

Specifically, we conducted a series of latent transition analyses with the number of profiles at each wave fixed to the solutions retained in step 2. The first model was a basic LTA linking the latent profiles for 2018 and 2020, in which the later profile variable was regressed on the earlier one to obtain transition probability matrices that summarize stability and change in profile membership over time. The second model was a random-intercept LTA (RI-LTA; Muthén & Asparouhov, 2022), which adds a person-specific random intercept factor measured by all indicators across time. This factor represents each teacher's time-invariant, trait-like tendency to score higher or lower on all profile indicators and thus captures stable between-person differences. The latent profiles at each wave therefore describe time-specific configurations of the indicators beyond these stable differences. In our specification, the random intercept had a mean of zero and a variance of one in all profiles, and its loadings were held equal across waves.

Hence, it was not related to profile membership. The resultant profile-specific indicator intercepts describe the expected indicator values of a teacher with an average random intercept and, at the same time, the model-implied profile means. Differences between profiles thus reflect differences in the time-specific level and shape of teachers' ICT utilization, net of each teacher's stable general tendency, and transitions reflect within-person changes in these time-specific configurations.

When the same number of profiles emerged at each wave, we also tested longitudinal profile similarity following Morin's and Litalien's (2017) sequence of configural, structural, dispersion, and distributional similarity models by imposing equality constraints on profile-specific means, variances, and, finally, profile proportions over time, and comparing nested models. Where applicable, we also tested a mover-stayer extension (Nylund-Gibson et al., 2023).

Among these competing specifications (basic LTA, RI-LTA, longitudinal-similarity constrained LTA, and, where applicable, mover-stayer LTA), we selected the final model primarily using information criteria, likelihood-ratio tests, and substantive interpretability. We estimated all LTA models using the statistical software Mplus 8.9 (Muthén & Muthén, 1998 -2017), using the robust maximum-likelihood estimator (MLR) with the full information maximum-likelihood procedure to handle missing data. Supplementary Material S2 shows the respective Mplus code.

3.3.4 Step 4: Introduction of Covariates

Having identified the LTA working models within the three countries, we added the covariates to the models to examine the extent to which they are associated with individuals' profile membership (RQ3). We chose the R3STEP approach to estimate the covariate effects.

This approach maintains the integrity of the initial latent profile and transition estimation when incorporating the predictor variables (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2014; Nylund-Gibson & Choi, 2018). Specifically, three models were estimated: Model 1 predicted T1 profile membership from covariates; Model 2 extended this to T2 membership; and Model 3 added covariatetransition interactions. All models used 2018 covariate scores, given their relative stability over time.

3.3.5 Complex Sampling Survey Design and Sensitivity Checks

Given the complex sampling design of ICILS 2018 and the panel design of the follow-up study in 2020 (Fraillon et al., 2020a; Strietholt et al., 2021), we incorporated the recommended weights and strata in all analyses. Specifically, we used the final teacher weight TOTWGTT from the ICILS Teacher Panel data file as a sampling weight. In this file, TOTWGTT is defined only for panel teachers, is identical for both waves, and includes the adjustment for panel participation. We used the jackknife zone JKZONET as a stratification variable and the jackknife replicate indicator JKREPT as a clustering variable within zones (recoded for Mplus as TJKREP = JKREPT + 1), together with the TYPE = COMPLEX specification in the software Mplus. With this specification, Mplus estimates standard errors by Taylor-series linearization, which approximates the jackknife repeated replication design. The jackknife replicate weights themselves were not used, because re-estimating each mixture model across 75 replicates was computationally infeasible. Supplementary Material S2 shows the corresponding Mplus syntax.

We performed country-specific analyses using the SUBPOPULATION option to obtain standard errors that were in line with the complex sampling design.

To assess robustness, we estimated LPA models under three alternative covariance structures (Type 1: freely estimated means, variances, and covariances; Type 2: freely estimated means and variances; Type 3: equal variances across profiles) and re-ran all analyses under a standard specification that ignored the complex sampling design, comparing results with the complex-design solution. Results are reported in Supplementary Materials S3-S4.

To evaluate whether restricting the analytic sample to panel completers introduced attrition bias, we compared panel completers with teachers who participated only at baseline (2018) on the ten teacher-level covariates used in the R3STEP models, separately by country, using teachers' original ICILS 2018 cross-sectional weight and design-based standard errors.

Because our target population is explicitly restricted to teachers who remained in the profession in 2020, we excluded baseline-only teachers explicitly coded as retired or as having permanently left teaching (n = 151) from the comparison group, so as not to conflate genuine non-response with structural ineligibility. This tracking information was complete for Finland but substantially incomplete for Denmark and, especially, Uruguay (see Supplementary Material S7).

To examine whether the retained profile solutions depended on the two indicators identified as psychometrically weakest (i.e., CODEMP due to the weakest fit of the measurement model and UTOOLC due to the lowest alignment invariance), we re-estimated the retained LPA solution for each country and wave, excluding these two indicators and holding the number of profiles constant. We then compared the classification agreement with the original eightindicator solution.

To further assess whether the two profile indicators with the most modest internalconsistency reliability, UTOOLI (McDonald's ω = .60-.77) and UTOOLL (ω = .64-.84), also contributed the least to distinguishing profiles, we computed each indicator's discriminating power (η², the between-profile share of its variance) directly from the retained models' classspecific means, variances, and class proportions. To test whether these indicators also affected the estimated transition probabilities, and not only single-wave profile separation, we reestimated each country's full retained RI-LTA model excluding UTOOLL and, separately, excluding UTOOLI. We compared the resulting model-implied transition matrices with the retained model's own matrix after aligning classes across runs.

4.1 Descriptive Statistics, Correlations, and Measurement Models

The eight profile indicators were drawn from their respective measurement models as factor scores (see Section 3.3.1). For the mixture analyses, we standardized each indicator within each country's panel sample and separately for each wave (unweighted), such that each score had a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one within each country and wave. The profile indicators therefore describe teachers' standing relative to the other panel teachers of their country at the same wave rather than their absolute level of utilization on a metric common to both waves or to all countries. Consequently, a general increase or decrease in a country's mean between 2018 and 2020 is not reflected in the profiles, and profile transitions describe changes in teachers' relative standing within their country. The respective alignment CFA models underlying the profile indicators showed acceptable to good fit for the majority of scales (see Table 2). CFI values ranged from .961 to .999, TLI from .944 to .994, and SRMR from .001 to .064 across the eight scales. RMSEA values ranged from .000 to .137 and exceeded the guideline of .08 for three scales: CODEMP (.137), ICTEMP (.097), and, marginally, CLASACT (.081).

Three scales merit particular attention: CODEMP showed the weakest fit profile (CFI = .961, TLI = .944, RMSEA = .137), indicating that the cross-national and longitudinal comparability of the coding emphasis scale is more limited than for the remaining indicators. Nonetheless, we retained this measurement model, given the theoretical importance of CODEMP as a distinct dimension of ICT use. ICTEMP also exceeded the RMSEA guideline (.097), although its incremental fit indices and SRMR met the guidelines (CFI = .982, TLI = .973, SRMR = .038). This misfit should be considered when interpreting profiles that are distinguished by ICT emphasis, including the emphasis-practice gap profiles. UTOOLC showed a low average invariance index (AvgInvariance = .227), suggesting substantial variation in parameters across groups and/or over time, which should be borne in mind when interpreting profiles that could be distinguished primarily by this indicator. Finally, two three-item scales (i.e., UTOOLI, UTOOLL) were exactly identified, precluding traditional fit evaluation-their adequacy is supported by near-zero alignment function values and satisfactory internal consistencies (McDonald's ω = .60-.84; see Table 1). Supplementary Material S1 provides separate correlation matrices for all three country samples. Across the three countries, the correlations among the eight profile indicators were uniformly positive. In Denmark and Uruguay, the three tool-use indicators (i.e., UTOOLC, UTOOLI, UTOOLL) were correlated more strongly among themselves than with the instructional indicators (i.e., CLASACT, ICTPRAC, ICTEMP), suggesting that tool-specific ICT practices form a partially distinct cluster of profile indicators; in Finland, both sets of correlations were of similar size. In five of the six country-by-wave matrices, UTOOLC or UTOOLL showed the lowest average correlation with the remaining indicators (CODEMP in Uruguay 2018). Correlations between the profile indicators and background variables, such as teachers' age and gender were small throughout |𝑟| (|r| ≤ .24). The stability of profile indicators over time-indicated by the 2018-2020 correlation for each indicator-was moderate and ranged from r = .41-.66 in Denmark (M[r] = .49), r = .42-.63 in Finland (M[r] = .54), and r = .36-.56 in Uruguay (M[r] = .46), indicating that teachers' ICT practices show meaningful but incomplete stability across the two-year panel window.

4.2 Latent Transition Analyses

4.2.1 Profile Enumeration

Following the model selection procedure described in section 3.3.2, we estimated LPA models with up to six profiles for each country and time point under both the complex-design and standard specification without complex design information, and retained solutions that were admissible under both specifications (i.e., a replicated best log-likelihood and a smallest profile of at least 5% of the sample; for the one exception, Denmark at T1, see below), weighing the information criteria against profile sizes, classification quality, and interpretability. Supplementary Material S3 reports the fit statistics across the full range of solutions under both specifications. Table 3 reports the final model fit for the retained solutions.

Denmark. Four profiles were retained at both time points (T1 and T2), yielding a 4→4 transition structure. At T1, the BIC was minimized at four profiles under the standard analysis.

At T2, the evidence was decisive (BF > 2,000,000, cmPk = .854). Although the five-profile solution produced a lower BIC at T1 under the complex specification (ΔBIC = 73), its best loglikelihood was not replicated, it was not supported under the standard specification, and its T2 counterpart contained only n = 11 teachers in its smallest profile, rendering it unsuitable for transition modelling. The retained solution showed good entropies (.842 at T1, .819 at T2) and converged across both specifications. At T1, however, no solution with three or more profiles met the profile-size criterion under the complex specification (smallest profiles: n = 5 and n = 6 for three and four profiles; the five-profile solution was not replicated). We therefore based the T1 decision on the standard specification, under which the four-profile solution had the lowest BIC, a replicated best log-likelihood, and an adequate smallest profile (n = 31, 7.0%), on the four-profile structure at T2, and on the retained RI-LTA, in which all T1 profiles comprised 18-41% of teachers. The symmetric 4→4 structure also allowed us to track teachers across four, potentially consistent categories over time.

Finland. Three profiles were retained at T1 and T2, yielding a 3→3 transition structure.

The BIC favored four profiles at T1 under both specifications (standard: BIC = 24,546.2 vs. 24,700.6, ΔBIC = 154; complex: BIC = 24,495.7 vs. 24,663.7, ΔBIC = 168) and at T2 under the complex specification (BIC = 24,444.6 vs. 24,760.4). At T2 under the standard specification, by contrast, the cmPk for three profiles was 1.0, and the Bayes Factor decisively rejected a fourth profile. We nevertheless retained three profiles for four reasons. First, the four-profile solutions were not reproducible across specifications. The best log-likelihood was not replicated for the complex four-profile solution at T1 or for the standard four-profile solution at T2, and the additional profile differed in content between the specifications: At T1, it separated a belowaverage group from the average profile under the standard specification (n = 209; average posterior classification probability = .785) but a small group (5.3% of teachers) from the high-engagement profile under the complex specification; at T2, it contained only n = 2 teachers under the standard specification. Second, the three-profile solution was reproduced under both specifications on both occasions, with replicated best log-likelihoods and nearly identical profile means (differences ≤ 0.08 SD). Third, under the standard specification at T1, the three-profile solution separated the profiles more clearly than the four-profile solution (entropy = .818 vs.

.800; minimum average posterior classification probability = .912 vs. .785) and had larger profiles (smallest profile: n = 258 vs. n = 188). Fourth, the three profiles ("disengaged", "constrained integrators", "holistic integrators") were substantively distinct, whereas the additional profiles at T1 represented graded variations of adjacent profiles rather than qualitatively different utilization patterns. The retained 3→3 RI-LTA supported this choice: its best log-likelihood was replicated, its classification quality was high (entropy = .885; average posterior classification probabilities = .88-.99), and its smallest profile comprised 18.5% of teachers. A further, secondary consideration was that the 3→3 structure enabled formal longitudinal profile similarity testing.

Uruguay. Four profiles at T1, and three profiles at T2 were retained, yielding a 4→3 transition structure. At T1, the evidence for four profiles was overwhelming (cmPk = .996, BF = 231), and the five-profile alternative had cmPk = .004 and 𝑛 )*+ = 16, but its best log-likelihood was not replicated under the complex specification, and it did not converge under the standard specification. At T2, the BIC favored five profiles under both specifications and, under the complex specification, also favored four over three profiles (BIC = 7,707.7 vs. 7,748.4).

However, neither alternative was admissible under both specifications: The standard four-profile solution did not replicate its best log-likelihood and its smallest class contained only n = 12 teachers, and the complex five-profile solution did not replicate its best log-likelihood and its smallest class contained only n = 9 teachers. The three-profile solution was stable across both specifications, with adequate entropy (.814 and .827 under the complex and standard specifications, respectively) and minimum class size (𝑛 )*+ = 93). The 4→3 transition reflects a de-differentiation of the Uruguayan ICT utilization landscape over the panel period. Given the differing number of profiles over time, longitudinal profile similarity testing was not applicable.

4.2.2 Profile Similarity and LTA Model Selection

Similarity of latent profiles over time.

To assess the longitudinal measurement invariance in the profile structure for the Danish and Finnish teacher data, we estimated LPA models assuming configural, structural, dispersion, and distributional similarity over time. Fit statistics for all models under the complex and standard specifications are reported in Supplementary Material S4.

Similarity testing faced two challenges: First, the configural model did not converge for either country under either specification. Model comparisons therefore proceeded from the structural model. Second, for Denmark, both specifications favored the distributional similarity model, but only marginally over the dispersion model (complex: BIC = 17,147 vs. 17,154, ΔBIC = 7; standard: BIC = 17,214 vs. 17,220, ΔBIC = 6). To determine the similarity constraints within the longitudinal models, we conducted additional direct comparisons of LTA and RI-LTA models with structural and dispersion similarity constraints under both specifications (see Supplementary Material S4). Across all comparisons, the RI-LTA with dispersion similarity constraints consistently produced the lowest BIC by a substantial margin (complex: BIC = 16,299 vs. 17,536 for RI-LTA dispersion vs. structural; standard: BIC = 16,473 vs. 17,254) and was therefore selected as the final solution for Denmark. For Finland, the distributional model likewise produced a marginally lower BIC than the dispersion model under both specifications (complex: BIC = 44,834 vs. 44,840; standard: BIC = 45,076 vs. 45,082; ΔBIC = 6 in both cases).

In both countries, we nevertheless retained the dispersion model. Distributional similarity additionally constrains the profile proportions to be equal at T1 and T2 and would thus have ruled out, a priori, net changes in profile prevalence between 2018 and 2020, which are part of the change examined in RQ2. Moreover, in an LTA, this constraint cannot be imposed as a simple equality constraint but requires nonlinear constraints on the transition parameters (Morin & Litalien, 2017). Given the small BIC advantage of the distributional model (ΔBIC = 6-7), we therefore favored the less restrictive dispersion model. These results indicated that for both Denmark and Finland, the latent profiles maintained the same mean structure and within-profile variances across T1 and T2, while profile proportions were free to differ. Hence, the profiles represented the same latent teacher types at both measurement points, while permitting meaningful shifts in profile membership over time.

LTA vs. RI-LTA. Across all three countries, the RI-LTA consistently outperformed the standard LTA in AIC and BIC (see Table 3), confirming that including a time-invariant random intercept meaningfully improved model fit. The Mover-Stayer extension, estimated for Denmark and Finland, did not improve fit in either country-the log-likelihood was identical to the standard RI-LTA, with higher AIC and BIC. We therefore retained the RI-LTA as the final model for all three countries, incorporating the dispersion similarity constraints for Denmark and Finland. All three retained RI-LTA models terminated normally. With 10,000 random starts and 500 final-stage optimizations, the best log-likelihood was replicated (reached by 361, 197, and 7 of the converged final-stage optimizations for Denmark, Finland, and Uruguay, respectively), and no estimation warnings remained; the only warnings concerned the input data (e.g., missing design information for cases outside the analyzed subpopulations). The convergence problems reported above therefore concerned non-retained specifications only. The lower entropy of the Mover-Stayer variant (.752 for Denmark, .789 for Finland; Table 3) reflected this non-retained alternative specification and should not be read as characterizing the classification quality of our reported (RI-LTA) results: Denmark's retained model achieved an entropy of .827, comparable to Finland's (.885) and Uruguay's (.837) retained model entropies. Average posterior classification probabilities were likewise adequate for all retained models (diagonal range across profiles and waves: Denmark = .85-.96, Finland = .88-.99, Uruguay = .86-.99), exceeding the conventional .70 threshold (Masyn, 2013) for every profile.

4.2.3 Profile Descriptions (RQ1)

Across all three countries, the latent profiles formed a gradient from low to high ICT utilization, reflecting distinct combinations of classroom activity, teaching practices, digital competence emphasis, and tool use. Figures 2 3 4display these profiles, and Table 4 summarizes them for each country and time point. Both show the profile-specific intercepts and profile proportions of the retained RI-LTA models, that is, the expected indicator values for a teacher with an average random intercept, which equal the model-implied profile means (see Section 3.3.3). Because the indicators were standardized within each country and wave, these values express a profile's standing relative to the country's panel sample at the respective wave (see Section 4.1). Differences between profiles on UTOOLC in Denmark and Finland and on UTOOLL in Denmark and Uruguay are practically not adjusted for stable between-person differences, because the random-intercept loadings of these indicators were close to zero (unstandardized loadings ≤ .04; absolute standardized loadings ≤ .07, except for UTOOLL in the Danish "functional integrators" profile, whose within-profile variance on this indicator was close to zero: .35). Similar labels describing these profiles across countries (e.g., "comprehensive integrators" appears in Denmark and Uruguay) denote conceptual similarity rather than empirical equivalence-the respective indicator values may differ across countries and over time and should therefore be interpreted within their national context. Likewise, labels such as "disengaged," "aspirational non-users," and "constrained integrators" are shorthand for each profile's indicator pattern relative to the country's panel sample at the respective wave. Because these profiles scored below the national average rather than at an absolute minimum, the labels do not imply that their teachers did not use ICT at all ("non-users") or rejected it ("disengaged"), nor that every teacher assigned to a profile shares the same beliefs or reasons for their practice.

Two profiles of particular theoretical interest emerged from this typology-the "constrained integrators" in Finland (see Figure 3) and the "aspirational non-users" in Uruguay at T1 (see Figure 4a). Both profiles combined above-average pedagogical emphasis on ICT (in Uruguay, also on coding) with comparatively limited classroom practice: in Finland, mainly well below-average content creation tool use; in Uruguay, below-average classroom activities and teaching practices. This emphasis-practice gap did not persist as a distinct profile in Uruguay at T2 (see Figure 4b). In section 5.4, we consider several possible, non-exclusive explanations for this cross-national difference. In Finland, the "disengaged" profile was below average on all indicators and was distinguished most strongly by its low emphasis on ICT capabilities and coding: ICTEMP was more than, and CODEMP nearly, one standard deviation below the Finnish mean (-1.07 and -0.96). Within Finland, this profile was therefore characterized by a markedly below-average emphasis on ICT and coding in class, not only by limited tool use.

Because the indicators were standardized within each country, this profile cannot be compared directly with the structurally analogous "minimally engaged" profiles in Denmark and Uruguay.

Figures 5-7 display the transitions between latent profiles for the three countries, including the profile proportions and transition probabilities. Complete numeric transitionprobability matrices for the retained models are reported in the Supplementary Material S8.

Denmark. The overall profile structure was moderately stable over time, with stability highest for "functional integrators" (75.1%) and "minimally engaged" (48.5%), and lower for "selective tool adopters" (36.3%) and "comprehensive integrators" (39.7%). The most pronounced shift in profile composition was the growth of the "functional integrators" profile from 21.9% at T1 to 41.1% at T2. This growth was driven by inflows from all other profiles. Notably, 48.0% "comprehensive integrators" at T1 transitioned to "functional integrators" at T2, while only 39.7% remained stable. Among the "minimally engaged", 26.1% transitioned to "selective tool adopters" and 21.0% to "functional integrators". The "selective tool adopters" showed the lowest stability, with 38.3% transitioning to "functional integrators".

Finland. The Finnish profile structure showed high overall stability. Specifically, stability was high for "holistic integrators" (74.9%) and "disengaged" (72.7%), and somewhat lower for "constrained integrators" (61.5%). Profile proportions remained largely unchanged over time ("disengaged": 20.0% → 18.5%; "constrained integrators": 36.9% → 33.6%; "holistic integrators": 43.1% → 47.9%). The largest off-diagonal transition was from "constrained integrators" to "holistic integrators" (33.7%). Uruguay. Given the 4→3 structure, within-profile stability cannot be assessed for the latent profiles as for the Danish and Finnish data. The "comprehensive integrators" at T1 showed the highest over-time consistency, with 86.6% remaining in the "comprehensive integrators" profile at T2. The "minimally engaged" profile at T1 split predominantly into the "tool-avoiders" (49.7%) and "emerging integrators" (37.9%) at T2. The "tool-oriented integrators" at T1 transitioned mostly into the profile of "emerging integrators" (54.3%), with 32.4% moving to "comprehensive integrators". 4.2.5 Associations with Teacher Characteristics (RQ3)

The associations with teacher characteristics are fully reported in Supplementary Material S5, separately for each country and across the three RI-LTA models with the characteristics as covariates. All estimates reported below are from Model 3, which includes the covariatetransition interactions (Section 3.3.4). For profile membership effects, results were highly consistent across all three models, providing evidence of robustness. For transition effects, Model 3 estimates were in several cases unstable due to small profile sizes. We did not replace these estimates with those of Model 2, which predicts T2 membership without covariatetransition interactions; instead, we report transition effects for Finland only and describe the instability for Denmark and Uruguay below. In all models, profile membership was predicted relative to the highest-engagement profile as the reference category (i.e., "comprehensive integrators" for Denmark and Uruguay; "holistic integrators" for Finland). All predictors were measured at T1.

Profile membership at T1. In Denmark, collaborative ICT use among teachers was the only predictor consistently differentiating the "comprehensive integrators" profile from the other three profiles. Specifically, lower collaboration was associated with higher odds of belonging to the "minimally engaged" (OR = 0.27, SE = 0.07, p < .001), "selective tool adopters" (OR = 0.49, SE = 0.16, p = .033), and "functional integrators" profiles (OR = 0.50, SE = 0.13, p = .007).

Female teachers had substantially higher odds of belonging to the "minimally engaged" (OR = 4.30, SE = 1.58, p < .001) and "selective tool adopters" (OR = 6.80, SE = 3.34, p < .001) profiles relative to "comprehensive integrators". In Finland, five predictors significantly differentiated the "disengaged" from the "holistic integrators" profile: lower ICT self-efficacy (OR = 0.38, SE = 0.07, p < .001), lower collaborative ICT use (OR = 0.67, SE = 0.08, p = .001), less positive views on ICT (OR = 0.68, SE = 0.10, p = .009), fewer years of teaching experience (OR = 0.54, SE = 0.08, p < .001), and female gender (OR = 1.98, SE = 0.53, p = .011). For the "constrained integrators" relative to "holistic integrators", significant predictors were lower ICT self-efficacy (OR = 0.47, SE = 0.08, p < .001), lower professional development participation in structured ICT training (OR = 0.79, SE = 0.08, p = .014), younger age (OR = 0.97, SE = 0.008, p < .001), more years of teaching experience (OR = 1.32, SE = 0.18, p = .039), and female gender (OR = 2.36, SE = 0.45, p < .001). In Uruguay, significant predictors were identified only for the "minimally engaged" profile: lower ICT self-efficacy (OR = 0.26, SE = 0.09, p < .001), lower collaborative ICT use (OR = 0.60, SE = 0.11, p = .007), lower structured professional development participation (OR = 0.43, SE = 0.10, p < .001), and fewer years of experience (OR = 0.57, SE = 0.15, p = .030) were all associated with higher odds of membership in the "minimally engaged" profile. No other predictors were associated with profile membership concerning the "aspirational non-users" or "tool-oriented integrators" for the Uruguayan data.

Profile transitions between T1 and T2.

The predictability of profile transitions by teacher characteristics differed substantially across countries. For Denmark, several covariates showed associations with transitions out of the lowest-engagement profile, but the corresponding estimates were unstable across model specifications and associated with large standard errors. In fact, no transition effects can be reported with confidence for Denmark. For Uruguay, no covariates reliably predicted transitions between the T1 and T2 profiles across any of the model specifications. Moreover, the estimation of transition effects was further complicated by the small size of T1 profiles, particularly that of the "aspirational non-users". For Finland, there was partial evidence that covariates predict transitions, though estimates varied in stability across model specifications. Specifically, lower ICT self-efficacy was associated with higher odds of remaining among the "constrained integrators" rather than moving to the "holistic integrators" (OR = 0.55, SE = 0.13, p = .018) and of moving from the "holistic integrators" to the "constrained integrators" (OR = 0.57, SE = 0.13, p = .019). Among teachers in the "holistic integrators" profile at T1, more negative views on ICT (OR = 1.49, SE = 0.22, p = .021) and female gender (OR = 2.46, SE = 0.76, p = .001) were also associated with higher odds of moving to the "constrained integrators" profile rather than remaining "holistic integrators" at T2, and less positive views on ICT (OR = 0.37, SE = 0.14, p = .009) were associated with higher odds of moving to the "disengaged" profile.

Overall, lower collaborative ICT use was associated with lower-engagement profile membership in all three countries and lower ICT self-efficacy in Finland and Uruguay, while the predictability of transitions was limited.

4.2.6 Sensitivity Analyses

As noted earlier (Section 3.3.5), we examined the sensitivity of our analyses to the LPA model types, the model estimation under the standard and complex specifications, the exclusion of psychometrically weaker profile indicators, panel attrition, and the reliability of individual profile indicators.

LPA model type. Profile enumeration was examined under three model types differing in their constraints on the within-profile covariance structure: Type 1 allowed freely estimated means, variances, and covariances among profile indicators; Type 2 allowed freely estimated means and variances with no covariances; and Type 3 constrained variances to be equal across profiles while freely estimating means and fixing covariances to zero. Across all three countries, Type 2 models consistently provided the best balance of fit and parsimony and were therefore retained as the basis for profile enumeration. Full model output for all three types is available in the openly available OSF folder at <Link blinded for peer review>.

Standard versus complex model specification. All LPA, profile similarity, and LTA models were estimated under both the complex (i.e., accounting for the stratified cluster sampling design) and standard specification (i.e., without design weights). The two specifications showed broad agreement in profile enumeration, model selection, and final LTA solutions across countries. Differences were observed in the estimation of transition effects for certain small profiles, where the standard specification occasionally produced more stable estimates. These discrepancies were addressed directly in the model selection procedure by requiring a solution to be admissible under both specifications before retaining it (Section 3.3.2); the only exception was Denmark at T1, where no complex-design solution with three or more profiles met the profile-size criterion (Section 4.2.1). Overall, these analyses supported the robustness of profile enumeration and model selection to the choice of specification.

Indicator exclusion and model convergence. The profile solutions were robust to excluding CODEMP and UTOOLC in Denmark (2020) and both Uruguay waves (89-90% classification agreement, κ = .83-.87), less robust in Denmark (2018; 57% agreement, κ = .37), and could not be estimated for Finland at either wave when both indicators were removed simultaneously (the models did not converge). A follow-up analysis dropping CODEMP and UTOOLC individually showed that this non-convergence in Finland reflected two distinct mechanisms. In 2018, neither indicator alone was sufficient to destabilize the model (both singleindicator models converged, κ ≈ .91), indicating a joint-removal effect. In 2020, UTOOLC alone was sufficient to reproduce the non-convergence, pointing to a more specific reliance on this indicator for that wave and country. These results indicated that the eight profile indicators did not carry equal weight in determining classification across countries and waves. Because removing indicators changes the construct being modeled, these results do not necessarily invalidate the retained eight-indicator solutions, and they are consistent with the country-specific measurement structures established through the alignment CFA (Section 3.3.1). However, they also show that the classification of individual teachers depended on specific indicators, particularly in Denmark (2018) and Finland. Full model-fit statistics, classification-agreement indices, and convergence statuses for all sensitivity models are reported in the Supplementary Material S6.

Panel attrition.

Of the 30 country-by-covariate comparisons between panel completers and eligible baseline-only teachers (ICILS 2018), seven were statistically significant (|d| = .16-.32, p < .05). Denmark showed no significant differences. Finland's panel completers were significantly older and more experienced than eligible non-completers, and somewhat lower in ICT self-efficacy. These differences were undetectable before excluding the retired/departed teachers from the comparison. Uruguay's completers scored higher on ICT self-efficacy, collaboration, and professional development. Full results, including the unrestricted comparison, are reported in the Supplementary Material S7.

Indicator discriminating power. Reliability and discriminating power were dissociated for the two profile indicators with the most modest internal consistency, UTOOLI (McDonald's ω = .60-.77) and UTOOLL (ω = .64-.84). More specifically, UTOOLI was consistently among the strongest discriminators across the six country-wave models, whereas UTOOLL was consistently the weakest or near-weakest, comparable to or below CODEMP and UTOOLC. Full results are reported in the Supplementary Material S8.

Indicator exclusion and transition estimates.

Excluding UTOOLI changed the estimated transition probabilities only trivially in all three countries (average absolute cell difference ≤ .024) and introduced no new estimation problems. Excluding UTOOLL produced only a trivial change in Finland (.006) but a substantively meaningful one in Denmark (average absolute difference = .067, maximum single-cell shift = .187) and Uruguay (average absolute difference = .122, maximum shift = .328), and in both of the latter two countries introduced genuine estimation difficulties not present in the retained model (i.e., one or more multinomial logit parameters fixed to avoid singularity of the information matrix; for Uruguay, additionally a non-positive-definite residual covariance matrix for ICTPRAC). UTOOLL's modest reliability was therefore associated with reduced stability of transition estimates, as well as weaker profile separation, in two of the three countries studied. Individual transition probabilities in Denmark and Uruguay should therefore be interpreted as sensitive to the indicator set. Full results are reported in the Supplementary Material S8.

5. Discussion

>Discussion

5.1 Summary of the Main Findings

Taking a person-centered approach, this study generated evidence on the heterogeneity in teachers' technology utilization, identifying (a) latent profiles at two measurement occasions, (b) transitions between them, and (c) predictors of profile membership and transitions.

RQ1-Profile structure.

Across all three countries, teachers clustered into distinct profiles ranging from "minimally engaged" to "comprehensive integrators". The number and structure of profiles differed by country (4 profiles in Denmark, 3 in Finland, 4→3 in Uruguay).

While this may partly reflect genuine cross-national variation in ICT utilization landscapes, it could equally stem from sampling variation or measurement non-invariance across countries (see Section 4.2.6); the country-specific profile solutions are therefore best interpreted as distinct national typologies rather than directly comparable latent classes. Notably, in Finland and Uruguay, one group in each country existed at T1 that combined above-average pedagogical emphasis on ICT with comparatively limited classroom practice ("constrained integrators" in Finland and "aspirational non-users" in Uruguay), a pattern consistent with second-order barriers to technology integration, although our data do not identify the barriers responsible for it.

RQ2-Transitions.

Profile structures were moderately to highly stable over time.

Finland showed the greatest stability, with the highest average proportion of teachers remaining in their profile and largely unchanged profile sizes; that the profiles had the same shape on both occasions in Denmark and Finland follows from the dispersion similarity constraints. Denmark showed a gravitational pull toward the middle (e.g., "functional integrators" grew from 22% to 41%), with both upward movement from lower profiles and notable downward transition from the highest-engagement group. Uruguay showed de-differentiation from four to three profiles, with the emphasis-practice gap group dissolving-mostly into the highest-engagement T2 profile-and a new "tool-avoiders" group emerging as distinctly low-engaging. Because the indicators were standardized within each country and wave, this stability refers to teachers' standing relative to their national panel sample; it does not exclude a general change in ICT utilization between 2018 and 2020.

RQ3-Predictors.

Lower collaborative ICT use was associated with lower-engagement profile membership in all three countries, and lower ICT self-efficacy in Finland and Uruguay.

Female teachers were more likely to belong to lower-engagement profiles in Denmark and Finland. Lower participation in structured professional development was associated with lowerengagement membership in Finland and Uruguay. Transitions were largely unpredictable by covariates with partial exceptions in Finland, where lower self-efficacy was associated with remaining among the "constrained integrators", and lower self-efficacy, more negative or less positive views on ICT, and female gender with downward transitions of the "holistic integrators".

5.2 ICT utilization as a multidimensional and context-dependent phenomenon

A central contribution of the present study is the demonstration that teachers' ICT utilization is inherently multidimensional and heterogeneous, both within and across educational systems. Across all three countries, teachers did not cluster into simple "low," "medium," and "high" ICT users. Instead, the identified profiles reflected qualitatively distinct configurations of classroom practices, pedagogical emphases, and digital tool use. This empirically derived gradient, ranging from minimally engaged practice to frequent and broad ICT use across all indicators, broadly mirrors the substitution-to-redefinition continuum proposed by SAMR (Puentedura, 2012). This convergence provides conceptual support to the profile structures identified here. However, our indicators capture the self-reported frequency and breadth of ICT use and emphasis, not the transformative quality of technology use that defines SAMR's higher levels, and SAMR was developed to describe within-lesson technology use, not cross-sectional teacher typologies. This parallel should therefore be read as suggestive rather than confirmatory.

The dimensions distinguishing profiles varied substantially across countries. Because the indicators were standardized within each country, these comparisons concern which dimensions differentiated teachers within each country, not differences in absolute levels of utilization between countries. In Denmark, content creation tools differentiated lower-engagement profiles; in Finland the ICT-emphasis dimensions separated the lowest group from the others; and in Uruguay, learning tool use and coding emphasis created distinctive intermediate patterns. These findings suggest that teachers' ICT utilization cannot be adequately conceptualized as a single continuum of technology use but rather reflects multiple dimensions of practice that combine differently across teachers and educational systems.

These cross-national differences may partly reflect variation in national digital education policies, institutional support structures, curriculum priorities, and the differential ways educational systems responded to the COVID-19 disruption during the 2018-2020 period.

Denmark emphasized nationally coordinated ICT integration and more recent initiatives in technological literacy, Finland adopted a decentralized and cross-curricular approach to digital competence development, whereas Uruguay's Plan Ceibal strongly emphasized digital inclusion, infrastructure expansion, and equitable access to technology in schools (Fraillon et al., 2020b).

These findings align with prior research emphasizing that teachers' technology practices are shaped not only by individual competencies or attitudes, but also by broader institutional and cultural environments, including school norms, leadership practices, and shared visions for technology use (Ertmer & Ottenbreit-Leftwich, 2010).

5.3 Stability and selective adaptation

A second major finding concerns the substantial longitudinal stability of teachers' ICT utilization profiles. Across all three countries, teachers tended to remain within the same profile type across the two-year panel period. Finland showed the strongest stability, with the highest average proportion of teachers remaining in their profile and largely unchanged profile proportions across measurement occasions. Denmark showed moderate movement toward the middle-engagement profile, while Uruguay exhibited the greatest degree of structural change through a de-differentiation from four to three profiles. Nevertheless, the dominant pattern across countries remained one of continuity rather than transformation. This stability concerns teachers' time-specific profile configurations relative to the other teachers in their country at each wave, net of the stable between-person differences captured by the random intercept. Because the indicators were standardized within each wave, the profiles would not register a general shift in ICT utilization that affected all teachers of a country similarly, such as an overall increase in technology use during the pandemic. The sensitivity analyses further qualify this finding (Section 4.2.6). The number and broad structure of the profiles were consistent across the complex and standard specifications, and the classification of teachers was largely unchanged after excluding CODEMP and UTOOLC in Denmark (2020) and Uruguay (κ = .83-.87). In contrast, the classification of individual teachers in Denmark in 2018 (κ = .37 after excluding CODEMP and UTOOLC) and individual transition probabilities in Denmark and Uruguay (maximum cell shifts of .187 and .328 after excluding UTOOLL) were more sensitive to the indicator set. Statements about specific movements between profiles, such as the growth of the Danish "functional integrators", should therefore be read with this sensitivity in mind. This degree of stability is theoretically and educationally important, though it should be interpreted cautiously. The 2018-2020 period represented a significant disruption to educational systems, forcing teachers worldwide to engage with digital technologies under unprecedented conditions. The persistence of teachers' broader ICT utilization profiles within this window is consistent with 'dynamic conservatism', whereby schools accommodate external pressures for technological change while largely preserving existing organizational and pedagogical structures (Cuban & Jandrić, 2015). However, the non-experimental design of the ICILS teacher panel cannot establish this pandemic-specific interpretation directly. As an alternative explanation, practice stability may be normative over any two-year period, independent of the pandemic. If profile stability does prove generally resistant to disruption, this would suggest that short-term interventions or isolated professional development initiatives have limited capacity to alter teachers' ICT utilization trajectories. We present this as a hypothesis for future experimental research rather than as a conclusion supported by the present data.

5.4 The emphasis-practice gap and barriers to enactment

One of the most theoretically important findings of the present study was the emergence of profiles characterized by high pedagogical emphasis on ICT (and, in Uruguay, on coding) alongside comparatively low levels of classroom technology use. It is important to note that our profile indicators-ICTEMP and CODEMP-capture teachers' pedagogical emphasis on ICT and coding capabilities rather than direct psychological attitudes such as perceived usefulness or enjoyment (see Section 5.6); we therefore describe this pattern as an emphasis-practice gap rather than an attitude-practice paradox. The Finnish "constrained integrators" and the Uruguayan "aspirational non-users" both reflected this gap, suggesting that a strong pedagogical emphasis on ICT does not necessarily translate into enacted classroom practice. In Finland, however, this gap was relative to the profile's own practice rather than to other teachers' emphasis: the "constrained integrators" emphasized ICT capabilities above the national average (ICTEMP = +0.24) but slightly less than the "holistic integrators", whereas their content creation tool use was well below average (UTOOLC = -0.76 vs. +0.90 for the "holistic integrators").

This finding is broadly consistent with Ertmer's (1999) distinction between first-and secondorder barriers to technology integration, though our data speak more directly to a gap between pedagogical intention and enactment than to psychological attitudes per se. Our data establish this configuration but do not identify the barriers responsible for it. Whereas first-order barriers involve external constraints such as infrastructure and access, second-order barriers relate to teachers' beliefs, confidence, pedagogical orientations, and professional conditions that shape whether technology is meaningfully enacted in practice. More broadly, this gap between pedagogical emphasis and enacted classroom practice echoes evidence that teachers' beliefs and their actual technology practices do not always align (Ertmer et al., 2012; Petko, 2012).

The comparison between Finland and Uruguay is particularly informative, though we interpret it cautiously. In Uruguay, the "aspirational non-users" profile dissolved by T2, with many teachers transitioning into the "comprehensive integrators" profile during a period that coincided with pandemic-related digital teaching demands and continued national investment through Plan Ceibal; this coincidence is compatible with, but not directly tested by, our data. In Finland, by contrast, the "constrained integrators" profile remained comparatively stable across both occasions. At least two further, non-exclusive explanations deserve consideration: national policy differences in how the two systems supported ICT enactment during this period, and the fact that Uruguay's overall profile structure also changed from four to three profiles between waves, which may itself have contributed to the dissolution of this specific group rather than reflecting an underlying substantive shift. A further possibility concerns panel attrition: Our analyses were restricted to teachers who participated in both waves, and Uruguayan panel completers reported higher ICT self-efficacy, collaborative ICT use, and professional development participation than eligible baseline-only teachers (Section 4.2.6; Supplementary Material S7). If teachers who struggled to translate their pedagogical emphasis on ICT into classroom practice were more likely to leave the profession or the panel during the pandemic, the retained sample may over-represent those "aspirational non-users" who went on to enact this emphasis. This would be consistent with the large share of these teachers who moved into the "comprehensive integrators" profile (55.2%). Because profile membership could not be estimated at T2 for teachers who left the panel, and because tracking information was incomplete for Uruguay, this possibility cannot be tested with the present data. Our design does not allow us to adjudicate between these explanations, and we therefore treat the emphasis-practice gap's differential persistence as an open question rather than a settled finding.

Educationally, these findings suggest that a strong pedagogical emphasis on ICT alone may not be sufficient to support sustained classroom technology integration. Even teachers who prioritize digital technologies in their pedagogical goals may continue to encounter practical, pedagogical, or organizational barriers that constrain enactment in classroom practice.

5.5 Predictors of profile membership and mobility

The role of ICT self-efficacy and collaborative ICT use in shaping teachers' technology utilization is already well-established (Ertmer & Ottenbreit-Leftwich, 2010; Konstantinidou & Scherer, 2022; Wang et al., 2004). The contribution of the present findings lies less in confirming that these factors matter, but more in showing how they operate within qualitatively differentiated profiles. Self-efficacy and collaboration were not simply related to a single, continuous level of technology use. Instead, they were related to profile membership-that is, which specific combination of practices, emphases, and tool use a teacher displayed. This pattern held for collaboration in all three educational systems and for self-efficacy in Finland and Uruguay, which analyses of single dimensions of technology use would not show directly.

Equally important was the role of collaborative ICT use among teachers. Across countries, teachers who reported lower levels of collaboration around ICT practices were significantly more likely to belong to lower-engagement profiles. Unlike self-efficacy, collaborative ICT use reflects not only individual competence but also the broader professional culture within schools. This finding is consistent with earlier international evidence showing that collegial collaboration is positively associated with teachers' technology-related teaching practices (Konstantinidou & Scherer, 2022). The findings further suggest that collaborative professional cultures may be related not only to isolated ICT practices but also to broader configurations of teachers' technology utilization. Collaborative ICT use did not, however, distinguish the emphasis-practice gap profiles from the highest-engagement profiles, so it does not explain these particular configurations.

Gender also emerged as a meaningful predictor of profile membership, particularly in Denmark and Finland, where female teachers were substantially more likely to belong to lowerengagement profiles. Importantly, the dimensions on which these differences emerged most strongly-coding emphasis and content creation tool use-align closely with broader policy priorities surrounding computational thinking and advanced digital competence (Fraillon et al., 2020b; Redecker, 2017). These findings are consistent with prior literature on gender differences in technology-related confidence and perceived ease of use (Scherer & Teo, 2019; Teo et al., 2015), while also suggesting the possibility of unequal student exposure to advanced digital learning, which represents a relationship warranting direct empirical investigation.

Transitions between profiles were considerably harder to predict than profile membership. This finding suggests that the factors associated with teachers' initial placement within ICT utilization profiles may differ from those shaping profile transitions over time. One possible interpretation is that profile membership is more closely related to relatively stable individual characteristics, whereas transitions depend more on contextual conditions not captured here. However, the weaker prediction of transitions may also reflect small transition-specific cells, the resulting imprecise estimates, and the fact that all covariates were measured at baseline only, so that changes in, for instance, self-efficacy or collaboration between 2018 and 2020 could not predict transitions. We therefore treat the contextual interpretation as a hypothesis. This interpretation aligns with broader perspectives conceptualizing technology integration and teacher professional learning as contextually embedded processes shaped by institutional conditions, collegial culture, and organizational support (Ertmer & Ottenbreit-Leftwich, 2010; Opfer & Pedder, 2011). If confirmed, this asymmetry would be a substantive contribution of the person-centered longitudinal design adopted here: a single-wave, person-centered analysis can show that qualitatively distinct configurations of practice exist, and variable-centered longitudinal models can separate predictors of initial levels from predictors of change in single dimensions of technology use; the person-centered longitudinal design adds the possibility of relating predictors to membership in, and movement between, qualitatively distinct configurations of practice.

5.6 Limitations and Future Directions

The present study has several limitations: First, the small number of countries that participated in the ICILS 2018-2020 teacher panel study limits, on the one hand, the degree of generalizability of the technology utilization profiles. Whether these profiles replicate in other systems, and whether additional profile types exist that the three-country sample does not reveal, remain open questions. An extended multi-country panel study could address both replicability and heterogeneity while also linking profile structures to system-level characteristics such as national digitalization policies or teacher education programs.

Second, the measures in ICILS 2018 and 2020 focused on technology in general, despite some specification of broad categories. Specific technologies, such as artificial intelligence or virtual environments, had not been considered. Follow-up ICILS cycles, however, have included or will be including some of them, yet with cross-sectional designs (Fraillon & Rožman, 2025; IEA, 2026). These datasets will provide opportunities to examine more specific technology utilization profiles and their possible impacts in education.

Third, using alignment-based factor scores as observed profile indicators does not propagate their measurement error into the LPA and LTA steps. This limitation is particularly relevant for CODEMP and UTOOLC, where fit and invariance were weakest (see Table 2), and may affect the precision of profile solutions in which these indicators are primary discriminators.

Fourth, restricting the sample to panel completers could introduce attrition bias.

Comparisons with eligible baseline-only teachers showed no significant differences in Denmark, while completers were older, more experienced, and somewhat lower in ICT self-efficacy in Finland, and scored higher on self-efficacy, collaboration, and professional development in Uruguay. Tracking-status information needed to identify ineligible teachers was complete for Finland but substantially incomplete for Denmark and Uruguay, so some residual, unidentifiable ineligibility may remain in those two countries' comparison groups. Attrition therefore was not entirely random: the analytic sample may over-represent older and more experienced teachers in Finland and teachers with higher ICT self-efficacy, collaboration, and professional development participation in Uruguay, which may modestly limit generalizability.

Fifth, our profile indicators relied on teacher self-reports of ICT use, which may be subject to social desirability or recall bias and may not fully correspond to observed classroom practice. Future work could benefit from complementing self-report with classroom observation or log-file data.

Sixth, our analyses were based on only two measurement occasions (2018 and 2020).

While this design allows us to model transitions in teachers' technology utilization profiles, it does not permit examination of longer-term trajectories, non-linear patterns of change, or the stability of profiles beyond this two-year window. Seventh, the "emphasis-practice gap" profiles (the Finnish "constrained integrators" and the Uruguayan "aspirational non-users") were identified using ICTEMP and CODEMP, which capture teachers' pedagogical emphasis on ICT and coding capabilities rather than direct psychological measures of attitudes (e.g., perceived usefulness, enjoyment). ICILS did not include such direct attitudinal measures, so this interpretation could not be validated post-hoc; this distinction should be kept in mind when relating our findings to attitude-based frameworks, such as the TAM.

Eighth, the eight profile indicators did not contribute equally to profile identification. In Finland, the profile structure could not be estimated due to non-convergence when CODEMP and UTOOLC were excluded jointly. For the 2020 assessment, the exclusion of UTOOLC alone was sufficient to reproduce this non-convergence (see Section 4.2.6). The Finnish typology therefore relied heavily on the joint presence of these specific indicators. This may limit the application of the profile typology in future studies that use a reduced or different set of indicators of teachers' technology utilization. It also underlines the value of retaining coding emphasis and content-creation tool use when profiling teachers' technology utilization.

Similarly, excluding UTOOLL changed individual transition probabilities in Denmark and Uruguay appreciably (maximum cell shifts of .187 and .328), so that estimates of mobility between specific profiles in these countries depend on the indicator set and should be interpreted with caution.

Ninth, profile enumeration required judgment where the information criteria and the other selection criteria disagreed. Most notably, the BIC favored four profiles in Finland at T1 under both specifications (ΔBIC = 154 and 168). We retained three profiles because the four-profile solutions were not reproducible across specifications (see Section 4.2.1), but a finer differentiation of the Finnish profiles at T1 cannot be ruled out. Similar discrepancies between the BIC and the other criteria occurred for Denmark at T1 and Uruguay at T2. Replications with larger or independent samples could clarify whether additional profiles exist.

6. Conclusions and Implications

This study provides large-scale and longitudinal evidence on the typological structure and temporal dynamics of teachers' ICT utilization using data from three countries across two measurement occasions. By taking a person-centered longitudinal approach, the study extends existing research on teachers' ICT utilization by demonstrating that teachers' technology-related practices differ qualitatively in how digital technologies are used in classroom practice. Taken together, the study demonstrates the value of person-centered longitudinal approaches for understanding the complexity and developmental dynamics of digital teaching practices across educational systems.

The findings of the present study suggest that professional development initiatives should move beyond generic, one-size-fits-all models of ICT training. Because teachers' ICT utilization differs qualitatively, professional support may need to be differentiated to match teachers' specific profiles and practices-with foundational access support for minimally engaged teachers, support in translating pedagogical emphasis into classroom practice for teachers who combine a high pedagogical emphasis on ICT with comparatively limited classroom implementation, and pedagogical deepening for functional integrators. Whether such profilespecific support is more effective than generic training remains to be tested. Separately from these profile-specific needs, the association between collaborative ICT use and profile membership in all three countries suggests that fostering collaboration around ICT may support teachers' technology utilization more broadly, although our design cannot establish such an effect.

The results also suggest that professional development related to advanced digital competencies, including coding and content creation, should be designed with greater attention to potential gender disparities in teachers' engagement and confidence. Schools and policymakers should therefore ensure that opportunities to develop advanced ICT-related pedagogical skills are equally accessible and supportive across teacher groups.

Finally, the findings indicate that digital education policies should recognize that technology integration is shaped by local curricular priorities, institutional conditions, professional cultures, and broader national approaches to digitalization. Policies and implementation strategies may therefore be more effective when adapted to the specific contextual realities of individual educational systems and schools.
