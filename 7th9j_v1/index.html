Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

However, the module missed 42% of all true statements (false negative rate).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

We try to exclude figure and table notes like “* p < .05”, but may not succeed at excluding all false positives.

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Full text

Abstract

Agreement attraction in comprehension often manifests itself as a speed-up in the of processing ungrammatical sentences where a verb carries the wrong number feature when a noun of the verb-matching number-the attractor-intervenes. The effect has been claimed to be insensitive to increased linear distance between the attractor and the agreement target. Recent studies on Czech found weak attraction effects attested only with case syncretic attractors. In two self-paced reading experiments with Czech comprehenders, we tested whether including material between the attractor and the verb could modulate attraction depending on whether the material was reinforcing the attractor's features or not. In Experiment 1, we aimed to increase attraction strength by modifying the attractor with a relative clause, giving it the subject feature and reinforcing its plurality. In Experiment 2, we matched the increased distance employed in Experiment 1 with feature-inert material to see if attraction was maintained. Our Bayes factor analysis provided us with evidence of a small attraction effect in the both experiments that did not differ meaningfully between the two. We therefore show that Czech exhibits online number attraction but of a tiny effect size. Our results further replicate earlier findings that distance does not affect agreement attraction in comprehension, as our effect sizes were comparable between the two experiments as well with previous work on Czech. Finally, they show that modifying attractors with relative clauses does not increase the strength of attraction speed-ups.

1 Introduction

Agreement attraction speech errors such as the following often occur in production (Bock & Miller, 1991; Bock & Cutting, 1992): (1) *The editor of the history books were competent.

Sentence (1) is ungrammatical, since the verb were ought to be the was due to the agreement controller subject head the editor being singular. Speakers are more likely to produce a number agreement error in using a plural-marked verb when there is a plural distractor noun (known as the attractor), in this case intervening between the subject head and the verb compared to cases where the noun is singular (Bock & Miller, 1991; Bock et al., 2001; Vigliocco et al., 1995; Hartsuiker et al., 2003). Moreover, agreement attraction occurs in comprehension as well. Wagers et al. (2009) exposed their English speaking participants to ungrammatical sentences such as (1) and observed that their RTs were sped-up when the attractor (e.g., books) was plural compared to when it was singular. Comprehenders also often come under the illusion that attraction sentences such as (1) are grammatical even though they are not (Wagers et al., 2009; Hammerly et al., 2019; Royer, 2021).

Agreement attraction in comprehension has been replicated both in English (Tanner et al., 2014; Parker & An, 2018; Cunnings & Sturt, 2018) and in a myriad of other languages (Lago et al., 2015; Lago & Felser, 2018; Lago et al., 2021; Avetisyan et al., 2020; Tucker et al., 2015 Tucker et al., , 2021;; Franck et al., 2015; Franck & Wagers, 2020; Bhatia & Dillon, 2022; Slioussar, 2018; Bleotu & Dillon, 2022; Ivanova-Sullivan et al., 2024; Sturt & Kwon, 2024; Türk & Logačev, 2024), making it arguably one of the most thoroughly cross-linguistically validated effect in language processing. Jäger et al. (2020) conducted a large controlled replication and found that attraction can be reliably observed in English, while a Bayesian meta-analysis estimated the size of the effect to be centred at -22 ms (Jäger et al., 2017). Researchers have also found the effect with gender agreement (Slioussar & Malko, 2016; Paspali & Marinis, 2020; Acuña-Fariña et al., 2014; Slioussar et al., 2022) and honorifics (Kwon & Sturt, 2024) as well as in adjective-noun (Gonzalez Alonso et al., 2021) or clitic agreement (Ivanova-Sullivan et al., 2024). This has prompted claims that attraction is universally present in all languages with agreement, for example by Lago et al. (2015), who see the effect as arising via domain-general mechanisms within the cue-based retrieval approach (Parker et al., 2017; Lewis & Vasishth, 2005; Engelmann et al., 2019). Overall, the phenomenon has received many theoretical explanations next to cue-based retrieval, such as feature percolation accounts (e.g., Eberhard et al., 2005), self-organising sentence processing (Smith et al., 2018), and a recent theory based on the SOB-Complex span working memory model (Keshev et al., 2025).

However, recent studies on one language have shown the limits of the universality of the effect. Chromý, Lacina and colleagues (Chromý, Lacina, & Dotlačil, 2023; Chromý, Brand, et al., 2023; Chromý et al., 2025; Lacina & Chromý, 2022; Lacina & Dotlačil, 2024; Lacina et al., 2025) conducted a series of attraction studies on Czech comprehension (and in one experiment on Slovak). Overall, they found very little evidence for the presence of the effect. Most of the experiments, analysed with Bayesian models, showed evidence in favour of no attraction effects either in number or gender agreement. In their research, attraction effects surfaced only under case syncretism. This is when a single word form corresponds to more than one combination of features such as number, gender or case (Caha, 2019). Only when the attractor noun had the same form as the nominative was a very small attraction effect seen in reading (-12ms in Chromý, Lacina, & Dotlačil, 2023) and in grammaticality illusions (4% increase in acceptability in Lacina & Dotlačil, 2024). Similar results regarding the crucial role of case syncretism in Slavic attraction were reported by Badecker & Kuminiak (2007) and Slioussar (2018) foot_0 . However, even under the case syncretism, the effect of agreement attraction was extremely weak and hard to detect: Bayes factor analysis showed no evidence against attraction but it also showed no clear evidence for attraction, even under very narrow priors.

Nonetheless, it appears that if there is any attraction in Czech, it only occurs when there is confusion as to what the subject is. This could be accommodated within the retrieval-as-repair strategy as originally proposed by Wagers et al. (2009). The idea is that retrieval processes only kick in when an anomaly is detected. If confusion about subjecthood is the key to attraction effects in the language, it is plausible that should the attractor appear even more like a possible subject of the matrix clause, attraction effects would increase in strength.

This is the empirical question of the current study. Within standard retrospective attraction set-ups, the attractor is placed as an unmodified noun within the NP that contains the subject head. We hypothesise that modifying the attractor noun with a subject relative clause (RC) strengthens the attraction speed-up. The reason for this is that the attractor could be represented as the subject of its RC, appearing even more subject-like. Additionally, Czech relative pronouns as well as verbs within relative clauses also exhibit number agreement. This means that when a particular noun is relativised on, its number feature is repeated with agreement markers several times in the course of processing. When encountered by comprehenders, this could additionally reinforce the plurality of attractors in memory.

Using RC modification introduces the subject feature to the attractor, reinforces the plurality of the attractor yet also simultaneously increases the distance between the attractor noun and the verb. The results of Parker & Phillips (2016) suggested that distance alone does not turn off attraction effects in English. They contrasted two potentially attraction-inducing structures: (2) *According to the janitor, the key to the cabinets probably were destroyed by the fire.

(3) *The key to the cabinets, according to the janitor, probably were destroyed by the fire.

In (3), the parenthetical intervenes between the attractor and the verb, whereas in (2), it precedes the subject and the attractor. Parker & Phillips (2016) found attraction with both (2) and (3). However, it remains unclear whether Czech comprehenders, who show very weak effects already, would also prove invulnerable to increased distance.

Taking this together then, we believe there is a reason to test whether in Czech, attraction effects can be either strengthened or weakened by the inclusion of further material between the attractor and the verb. We predict that including material reinforcing the number and subject features of the attractor would strengthen the effect. According to earlier results on English, simply increasing the distance with feature-irrelevant material should not diminish the effect.

2 Experiments 1 and 2

We ran two web-based self-paced reading experiments with large samples of native speakers of Czech. In Experiment 1, we tested whether by making the attractor the subject of its RC, we could strengthen the previously identified small attraction effect. In Experiment 2, we included feature-inert material (a series of of adverbs) and asked if it could diminish or turn off the effect.

2.1 Data availability

All of our anonymised data as well as the analysis scripts are available here: OSF project repository.

2.2 Method

2.2.1 Materials

We implemented a 2 × 2 factorial within-subject within-item design in both experiments. We manipulated verb and attractor number within our items. As our starting point, we took the items from Experiment 4 reported in Chromý, Lacina, & Dotlačil (2023). These sentences consisted of a subject NP, which included the subject head in the singular, the preposition pro ('for'), the attractor noun, which was either in the singular or the plural depending on the condition, an adverb, the future tense auxiliary either in the singular or plural, again dependent on the condition, an infinitive verb followed by the rest of the sentence, whose structure was varied. What we added to these items was a relative clause modifying the attractor noun that was placed between the attractor and the adverb preceding the auxiliary. The relative clause included the word která/které ('which.SG'/'which.PL'), which varied according to the number of the attractor. This was followed by an adverb and a verb in the present tense, which was conjugated according to the number of the attractor. See an example item below: Experiment 2 replaced the added RC with a series of adverbs. This was done in order to replicate the same distance (measured in words) that was created in Experiment 1 by means of inserting the RC. Adverbs were used due to them not containing any features relevant for agreement such as number or gender. See an example below: Altogether, there were 32 critical items and 64 filler items. Each sentence was followed by a yes-no comprehension question that was targeting pieces of information within it.

2.2.2 Participants

For Experiment 1, 322 participants were recruited from the pool of students at Charles University, Prague, who received course credit. We excluded 19 non-native Czech speakers and nine participants who self-reported dyslexia. The average age was 22 years (sd = 3.8). 19 participants were removed because they self-identified as non-native speakers, another 9 participants were removed because they self-identified as dyslexics, and 14 participants were removed because their reading time fell below 100 ms per word for at least 10% of the words and/or they had more than 20% of mistakes in their answers to comprehension questions. The median of correct answers to the comprehension questions was 95% (before the exclusion). Experiment 2 saw 209 participants from the same pool. The average age was 23 years (sd = 4.5). We excluded 8 self-identified non-native speakers and 7 self-identified dyslexics. 15 participants were removed using the same exclusion criteria as in Experiment 1. The median of correct answers to the comprehension questions was 94% (before the exclusion).

2.2.3 Procedure

The experiments, distributed via a link, were hosted on PCIbex (Zehr & Schwarz, 2018) and employed the moving window word-by-word self-paced reading paradigm. The participants first read a consent form and filled in their demographic and linguistic background. Next, they were exposed to several practice sentences. Each item was followed by a comprehension question that was to be answered by clicking on 'yes' or 'no'. There was no feedback or a time-out. The items were distributed based on the Latin Square design. Each participant was exposed to eight instances of each condition.

2.2.4 Analysis

We ran Bayesian hierarchical models with log-normal likelihood predicting reading times in the critical post-verbal region. We used the package brms (Bürkner, 2017) in R (R Core Team, 2026). The model included verb number (i.e. grammaticality), attractor number, and their interaction as fixed effects. We included the full random effects structure containing both intercepts and slopes for the two main effects and their interaction. We used regularising priors, which were set in the following way: • Intercept: Normal(6,1) • Fixed effects: Normal(0,0.5) • Random effects: Normal+(0,0.5) (half-normal truncated at 0), with LKJ distribution η = 2 for correlation matrices (Lewandowski et al., 2009) • Residual standard deviation: Normal+(0, 1) (half-normal truncated at 0).

We ran 3,000 iterations using 4 chains with the first half of samples discarded as warm-up. All R values were below 1.02, indicating convergence.

We also ran a Bayes factor analysis to quantify evidence for the full model, including the interaction term as a fixed effect, compared to the almost identical model, which only lacks the interaction term as a fixed effect. The model was run for 20,000 samples, 2,000 warm-up, Bayes factor was calculated using the Savage-Dickey ratio method (see Schad et al. (2023) for introduction). We selected two priors for the Bayes Factor analysis: Normal(-0.027, 0.009) and Normal(-0.0135, 0.0045). We use negative priors since facilitatory interference of the attractor in ungrammatical sentences, vs. the lack thereof in grammatical sentences, should show as a negative interaction. The values are based on the estimates of the agreement attraction effects provided in the metaanalysis of attraction experiments in Jäger et al. (2017); Schad et al. (2023). These priors, with means rounded to the second decimal point, were also used by Chromý, Lacina, & Dotlačil (2023) in their Bayes factor analysis. In that work, none of their four experiments revealed clear evidence in favour of the model with the interaction term with these priors. There, the results generally favoured the model without the interaction term, and only in the experiment with syncretic forms was there anecdotal evidence, with BF10 above 1 but smaller than 3, for the interaction term model).

2.3 Results

2.3.1 Experiment 1

Let us turn to the results of Experiment 1 first. In Figure 1, the reader may consult mean RTs per region for each condition. Left and right graphs in Figure 2 show the plot of the posteriors of our parameters of interest in the critical region (verb) and the post-critical region (verb + 1).

We focus on the post-verb region in our discussion. This region shows a substantial ungrammaticality effect, manifested as a slow-down in the conditions with the plural auxiliary. As for the main effect of the number of the attractor, the 95% Credible Intervals included zero. Most importantly for us, the 95% Credible Intervals of the posterior estimate for the interaction term did not include zero (estimate=-0.008, 95% CrI: [-0.014, -0.002]).

We now turn to the Bayes factor analysis. For the prior Normal(-0.027, 0.009), the Bayes factor BF10 , measuring the evidence in favour of the model with the interaction, was 1.5. For the prior Normal(-0.0135, 0.0045), we saw BF10 of 13.0. Recall that values lower than 1 indicate evidence in favour of the model without the interaction, values higher than 1 indicate evidence in favour of the model with the interaction. Values in the range 1/3-1/10 are sometimes labelled as moderate evidence for the null model, values in the range of 3-10 are sometimes labelled as moderate evidence for the alternative model (Lee & Wagenmakers, 2014) and values higher than 10/lower than 1/10 are labelled as strong evidence for the alternative or the null model, respectively. In sum, there is borderline moderate-to-strong evidence for the model with the interaction term, but only in case of a very restricted prior. This supports the summary of the posterior distributions, which shows that the 95% credible interval of the interaction term, while excluding zero, does not range lower than -0.014, albeit with the caveat that the posterior values of the interaction term are closer to zero in Czech than in most other languages (see also Chromý, Lacina, & Dotlačil (2023)).

2.3.2 Experiment 2

The mean and SE summaries of the RT data collected in Experiment 2 may be consulted in Figure 3. Left and right graphs in Figure 4 depict the posterior estimates of our Bayesian models for the critical (verb) and post-critical (verb + 1) regions respectively.

In the post-verb region we see a clear ungrammaticality effect, manifested as a slow-down in the conditions with the plural auxiliary. Just as in Experiment 1, we also see an effect of interaction. The 95% Credible Intervals of the posterior estimate for the interaction term is on the borderline of including zero (estimate=-0.006, 95% CrI: [-0.013, 0.000]).

We now turn to the Bayes factor analysis. For the prior Normal(-0.027, 0.009), the Bayes factor BF10 , measuring the evidence in favour of the model with the interaction, was 0.2. For the prior Normal(-0.0135, 0.0045), we saw the BF10 as 1.6. That is, we see that the prior assuming a larger effect shows borderline moderate evidence in favour of the model without the interaction term. For a narrower prior, the evidence switches in favour of the model with the interaction but since the value stays below 3, it is indecisive. In Experiment 1, the BF10 value for the narrow prior was slightly above 10, providing moderate-to-strong evidence for the model with the interaction term.

2.4 Joint analysis

In order to evaluate whether the strength of the attraction effect observed in Experiments 1 and 2 differs, we conducted a joint analysis of both datasets. The model had the same structure but included an extra fixed effect, Experiment (contrast-coded such that the second experiment is coded as +1, the first experiment as -1), and the interaction of Experiment with Verb Number, with Attractor Number, and the three-way interaction. The model included the maximal random structure for subjects and items random effects. The prior and sampling were the same as in Experiment 1 and 2.

The posterior distributions are summarised in Figure 5. We see a clear negative effect of the verb number × attractor number interaction, i.e., the signal of agreement attraction (estimate=-0.007, 95% CrI: [-0.012, -0.0021]). At the same time, there is no interaction of agreement attraction with the experiment (verb number × attractor number × experiment, estimate=-0.0002, 95% CrI: [-0.005, 0.004]).

Finally, to investigate the size of evidence for the verb number × attractor number interaction in the joint dataset, we computed a Bayes factor analysis. The models were identical to the ones used for Experiment 1 and 2, but they have one additional factor, experiment. The models reveal evidence for agreement attraction in the joint dataset: For the prior Normal(-0.027, 0.009), we saw BF10 of 5.5. For the prior Normal(-0.0135, 0.0045), we saw BF10 of 45.8. In sum, the joint dataset provides moderate evidence for a slightly broad prior, and strong evidence for agreement attraction when the prior is kept narrow, i.e., narrower than what one would expect based on the previous research on agreement attraction.

3 Discussion

Does making attractors more subject-like by modifying them with relative clauses (RCs) increase the strength of facilitatory speed-ups in attraction configurations? In our Experiments 1 and 2, we attempted to expand the research on agreement attraction in comprehension by conducting a selfpaced reading study with native speakers of Czech in which we tested whether number attraction effect size could be modulated by (a) RC modification and (b) simple distance.

In Experiment 1, we modified the attractor with a subject RC. This achieved two effects, it increased the distance between the attractor and the verb and crucially, it gave the attractor the feature of being the subject of the RC. We hypothesised that assigning the attractor an additional feature that would make it appear more subject-like, i.e., being made the subject of an RC, would strengthen the agreement attraction speed-up in ungrammatical sentences in cases of mismatching number between the verbal auxiliary and the (matrix-)subject head. This was based on the idea first advocated by Wagers et al. (2009), who put forward that attraction only occurs when there is sufficient confusion as to what the subject is. In a recent study on gender attraction in Czech and Slovak by Lacina et al. (2025), this was suggested as one of the ways to explain the general lack of effects and their surfacing only under the condition of case syncretism with the nominative, that is when the attractor had the form that the subject takes in the language. In Experiment 2, we replaced this additional intervening relative clause with feature-inert adverbial material of the same length measured in words to test whether (a) distance diminishes or turns off the effect and (b) any potential boosting effect of the relative clause in Experiment 1 was in fact masked by the contravening diminishing effect of increased distance.

In Experiment 1 (RCs), our Bayesian analysis showed a speed-up in reading times in the post-verbal region. What this means is that in Experiment 1, number agreement attraction in ungrammatical sentences exhibited as facilitatory interference was present. The Bayes factor analysis showed that we had evidence only in favour of a small effect rather than the effect size standardly reported in the literature (Jäger et al., 2017). Experiment 2, on the other hand, did not provide the same evidence in favour of attraction with even the narrow prior not allowing us to draw any strong conclusions. However, when we combine the two datasets, we see clearly that there is evidence for attraction, strong for a small effect and moderate even for the standardly sized effect. The size of the effect does not meaningfully differ between the two experiments based on the joint analysis results.

In sum, we see that Czech shows number agreement attraction effects in reading but that the effect is substantially smaller compared to other languages studied. The current research, therefore, joins the acceptability judgement results of Lacina & Dotlačil (2024), which reported tiny rates of grammaticality illusions in Czech with the same sentences with case-syncretic attractors that we used here, albeit additionally modified with RCs or distance. When we compare our results with the other study that used the current stimuli, Chromý, Lacina, & Dotlačil (2023), we see that adding the RC or increasing the distance does not meaningfully strengthen the effect. These researchers found only anecdotal evidence in favour of attraction in the experiment using syncrectism. We strengthen that evidence, but not the size of the effect. Most likely due to our larger sample size, we can now have more credence in the attraction effect observed and in its likely size. When we take all of this together, we find that in general, the attraction effect in Czech is essentially invariant between the three configurations, i.e. no increased distance, RCs or adverbs inserted-it appears to be not influenced at all by additional intervening material, be it feature-relevant or feature-inert.

Therefore, our hypothesis derived from the idea that increasing the degree to which the attractor could be misconstrued as the subject would increase the strength of the attraction effect turned out to be incompatible with the current data. This could be taken as going against the retrievalas-repair approach (Wagers et al., 2009). However, the data could still be compatible with it-it could be claimed that subject-confusion could function in a binary way, that is if it crosses a certain threshold, retrieval is initiated as a means of repairing the ungrammatical structure. At this point, the fact that the attractor is modified by an RC making it its subject is no longer relevant, as it does not influence the plural feature.

The current results ought to be discussed in relation to those of Parker & Phillips (2016), which tested both number agreement attraction and NPI illusions in English. As mentioned in section 1, they found that adding more material between the attractor and the agreeing verb had no effect on attraction, while it turned the NPI illusion effect off. Our current results support the attraction invariance finding and, arguably, improve on the design of Parker & Phillips (2016). In their study, they used parentheticals such as according to the janitor, which included a noun marked for number (janitor ), which could have played a role in the process of establishing agreement. On the other hand, our Experiment 2 used feature-inert adverbs to increase the distance between the attractor and the verb. The pattern of results, however, remains the same-the attraction effect surfaced, yet was no stronger or weaker compared to when this intervening adverbial material was not inserted. What our study additionally contributes is the datum that there is no decrease or turning-off of the agreement attraction effect in reading times, since Parker & Phillips (2016) only used speeded judgements to target attraction. This further supports the idea that number agreement attraction effects in comprehension are driven by retrieval interference, as increased distance can only lead to more memory decay for both the target of agreement as well as the attractor, but it cannot, being feature-inert in the current study, play the role of a further interference actor.

4 Conclusion

In the current study, we investigated the phenomenon of number agreement attraction in the comprehension of Czech sentences. Based on previous research by Chromý, Brand, et al. (2023); Chromý, Lacina, & Dotlačil (2023); Lacina & Chromý (2022), we attempted to strengthen the facilitatory interference effect found in ungrammatical sentences by means of giving the attractor the subject feature in Experiment 1. This was achieved by modifying the attractor with a subject relative clause. In Experiment 2, we matched this introduced distance by replacing the relative clause with a series of adverbs. What we found was that there was no difference in the strength of the attraction speed-up either between Experiment 1 and 2 or between our current data and those reported for Experiment 4 in Chromý, Lacina, & Dotlačil (2023), which used the same sentences without the added linguistic material intervening between the attractor and the verb. Overall, our results are in line with the previous results of Parker & Phillips (2016), who found that attraction effects remain invariant under distance manipulations. We expand on these findings by showing that the invariance is also observed in self-paced reading and does not depend on the additional material being a paranthetical. Admittedly, our results do not provide any new theoretical insights into the attraction effect. They do, however, provide additional empirical data and clarify the "Czech attraction anomaly". Drawing on our current results and earlier studies, we can close with stating that number agreement attraction in Czech exists but that it is extremely small and only occurs when there is case syncretism.
