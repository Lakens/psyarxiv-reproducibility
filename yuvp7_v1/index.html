How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

A hallmark of the mature human memory system is the ability to extract commonalities across experiences while preserving details that distinguish individual events. Although research 20 suggests that memory generalization emerges earlier than memory specificity, their developmental trajectories and interrelations remain poorly understood. Here, we examined the development of generalization and multiple aspects of memory specificity in children aged 4 to 8 years, assessed at two timepoints one year apart (NTP1 = 170; NTP2 = 119). We found that generalization improved more rapidly than memory specificity during early childhood. 25 Moreover, longitudinal changes in generalization and specificity were unrelated, suggesting distinct developmental trajectories. These findings reveal an early developmental advantage for generalization over specificity, potentially reflecting an adaptive prioritization of extracting environmental regularities over retaining unique details of individual experiences.

Main Text:

Human memory must balance two competing demands: extracting regularities across experiences to facilitate generalization in similar but new situations versus preserving the details that keep individual experiences distinct (1, 2). These complementary capacities, generalization and specificity, respectively, are fundamental to adaptive cognition (3) (4) (5). But how do they emerge in typically developing children? 35 Prominent theoretical perspectives propose that specific memories constitute the foundation from which generalized knowledge emerges. Computational models suggest that generalizations arise through operations over specific episodic representations (6) or that they develop gradually from individual memories via consolidation (7, 8). In line with these models, several theoretical frameworks addressing the ontogenetic development of human memory endorse a "generalization-from-specificity" account. This view emphasizes that abstract knowledge structures are built upon initially discrete and instance-bound experiences (3, 9).

However, a growing body of developmental research challenges the generalization-fromspecificity account. Evidence from studies on memory development indicates an early emergence 5 of generalized semantic knowledge derived from regularity extraction, such as word learning (10), category acquisition (11), and routine-event script knowledge (12). Moreover, this knowledge is more robust in infancy and early childhood than memory for specific details, including object-context associations (13) (14) (15) and detailed item representations (16, 17). Contrary to adults (18, 19), recent findings suggest that generalization performance in children 10 depends less on detailed and context-specific episodic memory (20, 21). These results make a case for a "generalization-before-specificity" account.

At the intersection of functional and computational taxonomies of memory, an alternative, process-based framework proposes that memory development reflects a hippocampally mediated rebalancing between different representational modes (1, 2). Specifically, early childhood is 15 initially characterized by a dominance of generalized representations, consistent with pattern categorization processes. Subsequent development increasingly supports more specific and differentiated memory representations, associated with pattern separation and completion (1, 16). These processes are thought to coexist across development, becoming more flexibly and adaptively deployed with age. Empirical support for this framework has accumulated over recent 20 years (4, 5, 20), with studies investigating the behavioral readouts of component processes: pattern categorization via generalization (22) (23) (24), pattern separation via mnemonic discrimination (5, 16, 25), and pattern completion via cued retrieval (26, 27). The available evidence suggests that at least pattern separation and completion follow partially independent developmental trajectories (28). Together, this process-based approach argues that age-related 25 improvements do not reflect a unitary increase in memory capacity. Rather, they portray an increasing equilibration of the computations necessary for multiple mnemonic demands.

A key prediction of this generalization-before-specificity account has not yet been directly tested. The existing literature so far relied near-exclusively on cross-sectional investigations (29). Such approaches are inherently limited in their ability to characterize developmental dynamics 30 within individuals (30, 31) and, crucially, cannot determine whether generalization and specificity differ in rates of change across development. As a result, this prediction of the generalization-before-specificity account, that generalization should develop earlier and more rapidly than specificity, remained largely untested. Addressing this question requires longitudinal data that track multiple memory processes within the same children over time, an approach that 35 is methodologically demanding and, therefore, rare (29).

Using a cohort-sequential longitudinal design, we followed children over a one-year period spanning early to middle childhood. Within a single task, we assessed generalization alongside multiple forms of memory specificity, including context binding as well as memory for conceptual and perceptual details (20, 21). This approach enabled a direct comparison of how 40 generalization and different facets of memory specificity evolve over development.

Consistent with the generalization-before-specificity account (1), we show that generalization exhibits earlier and more rapid developmental gains, whereas improvements in memory specificity are more gradual and independent of one another. This temporal dissociation provides the first longitudinal evidence that generalization and specificity differ in their developmental 45 timing. These findings demonstrate that asynchrony of change in component processes is a hallmark of human memory development, offering an empirical foundation for a process-level account (7, 32, 33) of mnemonic maturation (1, 29).

Dissociable developmental changes in generalization versus specificity

Many different tasks exist that probe generalization abilities (for reviews see (34, 35)). However, it remains unclear whether these tasks capture a common underlying ability across development.

Recent findings indicate that different generalization measures are weakly related to one another, at least cross-sectionally (22). A similar pattern has been reported for other component 5 processes: pattern separation and pattern completion appear largely dissociable in childhood (4, 16, 28). Hence, a critical open question remains: are developmental changes in pattern categorization, separation, and completion coordinated within individuals? Neurocomputational models postulate that these processes rely on partially distinct hippocampal subfields (6, 7, 36, 37). These hippocampal subfields exhibit different age-related profiles (38, 39), predicting that 10 their developmental changes may be at least partially independent. To test this, we examined correlations among longitudinal changes in generalization, context binding, and conceptual and perceptual specificity, controlling for age (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Generalization enables the formation of portable knowledge by allowing us to move beyond our direct experiences. Especially early in development, learning environmental regularities may take precedence over preserving detailed representations of individual episodes. Here, we tested whether memory generalization and multiple forms of memory specificity follow differential 5 within-person developmental trajectories across childhood. We find that as children transition from preschool to school-aged years, generalization shows stronger and steeper longitudinal gains than memory specificity. Thus, when children encounter experiences that contain both common structure and episode-specific information, their ability to derive generalities develops more rapidly than their ability to retrieve or retain specific details. The present study provides the 10 first direct longitudinal evidence for a developmental generalization-before-specificity account (1), documenting an uneven maturation of component processes of memory.

These findings advance the theoretical understanding of memory development beyond prior work in two respects. First, by showing that generalization and specificity differ in rates of developmental change, the data adjudicate between the generalization-before-specificity and 15 generalization-from-specificity accounts in a way that cross-sectional age comparisons cannot. Second, the dissociable trajectories of generalization and different aspects of memory specificity extend the process-based framework (1) from a largely descriptive taxonomy of memory operations into a set of testable predictions about the relative timing of their developments.

This temporal dissociation also provides a process-level perspective on broader developmental 20 changes in memory. Childhood is characterized by substantial gains in both episodic and semantic memory (42) (43) (44). Viewing memory development through the component processes lens (1) potentially offers a mechanistic explanation as to why semantic memory is more robust than episodic memory in the first few years of life (45). Generalization hinges upon on the detection and extraction of commonalities across related experiences and may thereby contribute to the 25 early growth of semantic memory, which is similarly stripped from the original encoding context. In contrast, pattern separation and pattern completion support the retention and retrieval of distinctive information about individual events and may contribute to the more protracted development of episodic memory. Generalization and specificity should not be directly equated with semantic and episodic memory, respectively. Nevertheless, the present findings suggest that 30 the earlier emergence of semantic knowledge may partly reflect the earlier developmental strengthening of processes that support regularity extraction. On the other hand, the slower maturation of detailed episodic memory may reflect a later and more gradual development of processes supporting event specificity.

How do regularity detection and generalization function so effectively in children? This general 35 question of human reasoning dates back to Plato's theory of induction and has been reconsidered from both probabilistic computational and connectionist perspectives over the past few decades (46) (47) (48) (49) (50). It is worth noting that artificial intelligence systems, in particular large language models, require significantly more data than humans, and especially children, to generalize to unseen instances (51). From the probabilistic perspective, inference relies on Bayesian learning 40 that is constrained by a hypothesis space of possible common structures such as hierarchical trees or low-dimensional spaces that explain the available data based on a given set of features (46). This top-down account would enable children to quickly extrapolate their knowledge by identifying the best fitting structure to their limited data (47). In contrast, connectionist accounts provide a mechanism-first alternative (50). Under this view, structural forms are not selected 45 from a pre-specified hypothesis space but emerge over time through domain-general neocortical learning over property covariations (52). These gradually built neocortical representations preshape the inputs sent to the hippocampus, additionally providing a basis for generalization prior to specific episodic encoding (53). Once established, they can support rapid assimilation of new, concept-consistent information (54, 55).

It is important to emphasize that the present findings do not imply specific memories are absent 5 or irrelevant in early childhood (3). Rather, they indicate that generalization and specificity are differentially prioritized across early development. Even infants can encode specific details, albeit rudimentarily (56) (57) (58), and children as young as 21 months of age are able to form context-dependent what-where associations (13, 59). In the present study, conceptual specificity and context binding aids generalization success, consistent with the notion that particular forms 10 of specificity may support generalization under some conditions (22, 60). This pattern suggests that children increasingly integrate general and specific representations as memory systems mature (61). Different forms of generalization differentially draw on specific representations, and this reliance varies across development (62, 63), enabling increasingly efficient joint use of general and specific representations. A path towards reconciliation is to clarify that 15 generalization-before-specificity describes the relative timing across development, whereas generalization-from-specificity characterizes the task-dependent use of specific information to support particular forms of inference that differs across development.

The present findings also speak to the question of whether generalization and specificity reflect independent processes or are coupled through shared representations. Consistent with 20 neurocomputational models that ascribe pattern categorization, separation, and completion to partially non-overlapping hippocampal subfields (37, 64), we find that their longitudinal changes are largely unrelated. This suggests that the key mnemonic computations develop simultaneously, but along separable trajectories across childhood. At the same time, gains in generalization are selectively associated with gains in conceptual specificity and converge across 25 tasks. The selective coupling indicates that some forms of generalization may draw on shared representational capacities, such as the ability to retain the semantic features of experiences, and may develop along similar trajectories, even if inter-individual performance levels are not correlated.

The fact that we did not find an association between individual differences in change for memory 30 of contextual and perceptual details has important theoretical implications. Within the traditional functional taxonomy of memory, both abilities are considered defining features of episodic memory, reflecting the retrieval of an episode's spatiotemporal context along with their perceptual details (65). Adding to prior cross-sectional studies that did not find behavioral associations across individuals (4, 16, 28), our longitudinal analyses show that these two abilities 35 follow dissociable developmental trajectories when tracked within individuals over time. This aligns with computational models proposing that pattern completion and pattern separation are distinct neural computations and make complementary contribution to remembering specific episodes. Accordingly, treating episodic memory as a single construct may obscure meaningful developmental differences among individual processes that support it. A computational 40 perspective helps explain how a seemingly unitary function can emerge from multiple underlying computations that develop in parallel.

A plausible neurodevelopmental basis for this pattern lies in the continued maturation of hippocampal circuitry across childhood and adolescence (5, 23, 66), and the hippocampus' role in both general and specific memory from early ontogeny (56, 67). Its subfields are thought to 45 make partially distinct contributions to pattern categorization, separation, and completion, and their developmental profiles are not uniform. In particular, earlier maturation of CA1 relative to dentate gyrus and CA3 (38, 39) provides a potential mechanism for the earlier emergence of regularity extraction relative to highly differentiated episodic representations. Computational work that places CA1 central to statistical learning and generalization (36, 37) is consistent with the early ability to detect regularities in infancy. However, the present behavioral findings cannot 5 establish this mechanism directly. Longitudinal studies combining component process measures with high-resolution hippocampal imaging are needed to test whether differential subfield maturation drives the asynchronous developmental trajectories observed here.

Future research should also directly test the practical implications of these findings for early education. Given that young children are biased toward regularity extraction before they reliably 10 encode episode-specific details, instructional approaches emphasizing the shared structure across experiences should promote early learning. For example, grouping related examples together or explicitly highlighting the commonalities in a set of experiences, may be particularly wellmatched to how young children's memory systems naturally operate, compared to approaches that rely heavily on remembering one-off details. As memory specificity processes mature later 15 and more gradually, curricula may also need to scaffold detail-oriented learning more explicitly in early childhood than in middle childhood. We encourage future work to examine this interplay in applied educational settings.

In conclusion, we find that central component processes of memory differ in their developmental timing across early and middle childhood. Generalization develops earlier and more rapidly and 20 is coupled with changes in conceptual specificity, whereas memory specificity improves more gradually and its different measures do not follow a common trajectory. These findings support a process-based account of mnemonic maturation in which development reflects not a unitary increase in memory capacity, but asynchronous changes in the computations required to extract regularities and preserve event-specific information (1). Such differential developmental pacing 25 may be adaptive: prioritizing the formation of portable general knowledge templates about the environment guides adaptive behaviors based on limited experience early in life (51), while laterdeveloping specificity supports more flexible retention of contexts, exceptions, and individual events.

Collection Game Task

The materials and design of this task were adapted from previous studies (20, 21) and are also described in detail in (68). A schematic overview of the task can be found in Fig. 1B.

Materials.

Cartoon images of 20 characters, 80 contexts, and 180 black-and-white, line-drawn objects were selected from the Google Images search engine. Unpopular and androgynous 5 characters were used to reduce the probability of children having pre-existing semantic knowledge about the characters. Twenty categories of semantically congruent objects and contexts were chosen based on their probable familiarity to young children (e.g., musical, cooking, and medical objects). Each character was arbitrarily assigned to a category (e.g., the character named 'Luntik' was be assigned to the category music). Each character was placed in 10 four different contexts to create four encoding trial images for that character. All four contexts images paired with a given character were semantically congruent with the character's assigned category (e.g., Luntik was placed on four perceptually distinct performance stages). The 180 objects were chosen such that there were nine distinct objects for every category (e.g., the musical instrument category consisted of a guitar, a piano, a drum, a trumpet, etc.). Every line-15 drawn object was manually painted with three distinct colors using Photoshop, resulting in a total set of 720 object images (180 objects x 4 color versions). An additional two characters, eight backgrounds, and 18 objects were selected from Google Images for use in the training phase and as an example trial. These additional stimuli were semantically unrelated to those used for the study and test phases of the experiment. 20

Procedure. The experiment comprised two testing sessions: an immediate session conducted in the late afternoon or early evening on Day 1 and a delayed session conducted on the morning of Day 2, however we only report results from the immediate session here. The immediate session consisted of two encoding-test blocks using non-overlapping stimulus sets. Each block included character familiarization, an encoding phase, and a test phase. The test phase comprised four 25 tasks assessing generalization, context binding, item conceptual specificity, and item perceptual specificity. The immediate session lasted approximately 45 minutes.

(1) Training and Task Screening. Prior to the encoding phase of the first block, children received training that illustrated the encoding and test procedure using two characters and categories that were not used in the main task. The experimenter introduced both characters, presented a total of 30 8 encoding trials (4 per character, shown in an interleaved order) and subsequently children performed the test phase in the same manner as in the real experiment, only that children received corrective feedback during the training in each task condition. This was done to ensure that children understand the task. Importantly, on the generalization trials, children were given instructions to select the new item that the character would most likely add to their collection and 35 were also reminded of the category of objects the respective character had collected in the encoding trials. The two generalization trials were then used to assess children's understanding of a collection of items and that items of the same category belong together. Children who selected the correct item in at least one of the two trials of the generalization task were considered to have understood the task. We excluded participants that failed on this task 40 screening at both timepoints or at the first if they did not come back for a second timepoint (n = 0).

(2) Character Introduction. Children were then told that they would be introduced to some other new friends. They were presented with images of each character sequentially and in a fixed order. On each trial, the experimenter verbally introduced the character by name (e.g., "This is Luntik"). There were 10 characters per block.

(3) Encoding. At encoding, children were told that each friend was making a collection of their favorite things, and that each friend would visit different places to find items for their collection.

Children were instructed to pay attention to the kinds of things that each friend liked. In each 5 block, the encoding phase consisted of 40 trials, each comprising an image of a character in a context presented on the left side of the screen and an object presented on the right side of the screen (5 s, 0.5 s ITI). Every character appeared in four encoding trials, each time in a different context and paired with a different object. Critically, the context and objects paired with a given character were semantically related. For instance, Luntik -a character assigned to the "music" 10 category -was seen on perceptually different performance stages and collected categoryconsistence objects such as a drum, a guitar, a horn, and an accordion. One randomized fixed order of the encoding trials was created for all participants, with the only restriction being that the same character did not appear on consecutive trials.

(4) Test. In the immediate session, the self-paced 3AFC test phase immediately followed the 15 encoding phase of each block. There were four task conditions, administered in a fixed order across participants: (a) generalization, (b) context binding, (c) item conceptual specificity, (d) item perceptual specificity. There were 10 test trials per task condition in each block. Each task condition had 20 trials administered in a fixed randomized order across participants.

(a) Generalization. Every test trial showed a character at the top of the screen and three objects 20 at the bottom of the screen. Children were asked to choose one object that this friend would add to their collection. All three objects were novel items that had not appeared in the encoding phase. One object was the target -the correct item that belonged to the category assigned to that character. The other two objects were lures -objects that belonged to different semantic categories assigned to two other characters. Target selection indicates that participants 25 successfully made a novel inference based on the related episodes associated with a given character.

(b) Context Binding. Every test trial showed an image of a character in one of that character's four encoding contexts at the top of the screen and three objects at the bottom. Children were asked to choose the object previously found by the friend in that exact context. All three objects 30 were seen with the character at encoding, but only one (i.e., target) was the item that appeared in that context. The other two objects were lures -objects that would have been seen with the character, but that were paired with that character in different contexts. Correct target selection would indicate that participants remembered the specific object-context co-occurrence.

(c/d) The Item Conceptual Specificity and Item Perceptual Specificity.

Both subtasks were 35 linked, such that the item perceptual specificity trial immediately followed the item conceptual specificity trial for each character. Every item conceptual specificity test trial started with a character at the top of the screen and three line-drawn objects at the bottom of the screen.

Children were asked which of the three objects the character had found for their collection. All three objects belonged to the same category assigned to the character (e.g., musical instruments 40 for the trial of Luntik). One object was the target -the correct item that had appeared at encoding. The other two objects were lures -objects that belonged to the semantic category assigned to the character but had never appeared at encoding. In the presence of conceptually similar lures, target selection indicated that participants remembered the objects' identities with high specificity. Critically, all three objects were presented as color-stripped line-drawn versions, 45 because participants' memories for the perceptual details of the objects were tested subsequently. After target selection, a red square appeared around the choice, and the experimenter provided feedback on whether children had selected correctly. The item perceptual specificity trial then immediately followed, in which the same character was shown at the top of the screen with three objects below: one perceptually identical to the encoded object (target), and two perceptually 5 similar exemplars differing in color only. Correct responses indicated memory for detailed perceptual attributes.

Vacation Game Task

The materials and design of this task were adapted from two previous studies that examined associative inference (23, 24). This task was implemented using an identical experimental 10 procedure as two prior studies in different samples (22, 40). A schematic overview of the task is shown in fig. S2.

Materials.

The stimuli comprised 30 cartoon images of animals (e.g., goat) and 15 cartoon images of scenes (e.g., beach) sourced from flaticon (https://www.flaticon.com/). A fixed set of 15 ABC triads was created, each triad consisting of two animals (designated A and C) and one 15 scene (B). From these triads, 30 unique animal-scene pairs (AB and BC) were constructed.

Procedure. The task consisted of three encoding-direct associative memory test cycles. After the three cycles, participants completed an associative inference test, followed by a procedural understanding check. A single randomized order of study and test items was administered across all participants. Total task duration was approximately 30 minutes. 20 (1) Encoding. Children were told they would meet groups of animal friends, each planning a vacation, and would see each animal paired with its vacation destination. The experimenter emphasized attentive viewing of each animal's selected destination. Before the first encoding block, children completed two familiarization trials using an animal and a scene not included in the test set. Each encoding block comprised 30 animal-scene (AB and BC) pairs, with each trial 25 presenting the scene on the right and the animal on the left side of the screen (3 s per trial, 0.5 s inter-trial interval). Randomized orderings of AB and BC pairs were generated for each encoding block, with the constraints that (i) AB and BC pairs from the same triad did not appear consecutively, and (ii) each triad's AB pair preceded its BC pair. Children were not informed of the relationship between AB and BC pairs, nor that inference questions would follow, at any 30 point prior to the associative inference test.

(2) Test. Two test phases were administered: an associative memory test and an associative inference test.

(a) Associative Memory Test. Immediately after each encoding block, children completed a selfpaced three-alternative forced choice (3AFC) memory test on the 30 directly learned (AB and 35 BC) pairs. Trial order was fixed across participants. Before the associative memory test began, the experimenter told the participant that they would now always see one of the animal friends on the top of the screen and that they should pick the place that this friend wanted to go on their next vacation from the three options on the bottom of the screen. On each trial, an animal cue appeared at the top of the screen with three scene options below. The target was the correct 40 paired scene, while the other two were scene lures from other triads. Responses were indicated by pointing, and the experimenter entered answers via key press ('1', '2', or '3', corresponding to left, middle, or right). No corrective feedback was provided.

(b) Associative Inference Test. Upon completion of the three encode-test cycles, children were tested on their ability to infer the relationship between indirectly linked animal pairs (A and C), which had both been paired with the same scene (B). Each triad contributed one test item, yielding 15 inference trials. On each trial, animal C of a triad appeared as the cue at the top of the screen, with three animal options (all A animals from different triads) at the bottom. Children 5 were instructed to choose the animal that wanted to visit the same place as the cue animal. The target was the A animal from the same triad; the other two were lures. No corrective feedback was provided.

(3) Procedural Understanding Screening. To ensure comprehension of the task, a procedural understanding check followed the associative inference phase. Children were told that there were 10 a few more friends who were late. They were then shown three trials with a novel AB and BC pair from one triad as well as one single AB pair of a different triad. After that, all were presented simultaneously on the screen and children were asked to select which of two animals (one target, one lure) would choose the same vacation place as the cue animal. Correct responses on these trials indicated that children have understood the task instruction that animals who went 15 the same place would go on vacation together, without the memory demand. We additionally excluded participants that failed on this procedural understanding screening at both timepoints or at the first if they did not come back for a second timepoint (n = 3: one four-year-old, one fiveyear-old, and one six-year-old).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R (Version 4.

2.1; (69)) using RStudio (Version 1.3.1073; (70)).

Longitudinal Trajectories.

To examine the longitudinal trajectories of memory generalization and specificity, we fit a linear mixed-effects model using the lmer function from the lme4 25 package with the bobyqa optimizer (71). The dependent variables were the aggregated accuracy across all trials of a given condition in the Collection Game Task (Generalization, Context Binding, Item Conceptual Specificity, Item Perceptual Specificity). We included timepoint (coded as 0 and 1 for TP1 and TP2, respectively), baseline age (ageTP1, in months, centred and scaled) as numerical predictors as well as task condition as categorical predictor and all their 30 interactions in the model. Additionally, we allowed for random intercepts as well as random slopes for timepoint per subject. Degrees of freedom were approximated using the Satterthwaite method. We used the generalization task as reference group. Significant effects were determined by investigating the respective type III sum-of-squares (SS) analysis of variance (ANOVA)-table using the stats::anova function. Follow-up contrast comparisons between parameter estimates 35 were conducted using the emmeans package (72).

Retest effects.

To test whether children showed retest effects at TP2, we performed two-sample ttests for each subtask and each age range separately. To do this, we compared the accuracy between children of the same age that came in for their first time (e.g. 5-year-olds at TP1) to those who came in for the second time but were the same age (e.g. 5-year-olds at TP2, i.e. 4-40 year-olds at TP1). We corrected for multiple comparisons in each subtask by the number of tests, in the current case 4, using the Bonferroni-Holm method (73).

Change-Change Associations.

To test whether within-child changes in same construct were coupled between tasks, we examined change-change associations by calculating the TP1 and TP2 accuracy differences in each task. We calculated correlations for the entire sample, controlling for age. The change-change correlations of interest were (i) between the different task conditions within the collection game, (ii) between generalization performance in the Collection Game and associative inference performance in the Vacation Game -both were measures of generalization, and (iii) between context binding in Collection Game and direct associative 5 memory in Vacation Game -both were measures of pattern completion. For the direct associative memory in Vacation Game, we used accuracy from the first test block, as this mirrors the one-shot learning in context binding from the Collection Game.

Contingency Analyses.

In order to investigate whether generalization success is affected by accuracy in any of the specificity task conditions, generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) 10 were estimated using the glmer function from the lme4 package, with a logit link function and the bobyqa optimizer (71). We additionally excluded those participants that were at ceiling in the generalization or conceptual specificity task condition. participants that were at ceiling on either the conceptual specificity or the generalization task condition The dependent variable was the trial-level accuracy in generalization, and the trial-level accuracy in each of specificity tasks 15 were included as dichotomous independent variables (correct / incorrect) separately, as well as their interaction with age and timepoint. We implemented a two-step approach to determine significant effects. In step 1, we ran type III Wald chi-square tests to identify interactions on the highest level (three-way interaction) on the full model including all predictors and interactions between the individual specificity task conditions, ageTP1, and timepoint. Since there were no 20 significant three-way or two-way interactions, we subsequently ran type II Wald chi-square tests to identify significant main effects. In step 2, we conducted individual log-liklihood ratio tests (LRTs) with the significant and trending predictors from the type II Wald chi-square tests where we iteratively removed each significant predictor from the model and investigated whether each removal significantly reduced the model fit. Additionally, to confirm previous cross-sectional 25 results that correct conceptual specificity increases success in the perceptual specificity condition, we took the same approach as above by fitting a GLMM predicting trial-wise perceptual specificity success by accuracy in the conceptual specificity condition. Here, we additionally excluded those participants that were at ceiling in the conceptual specificity task condition. We used ageTP1and timepoint together with conceptual specificity accuracy 30 (dichotomous: correct / incorrect) and their interactions as predictors and followed the same steps as described above.

Materials & Methods

The data analyzed in the present report were collected as part of a larger longitudinal study examining the co-development of memory component processes, intra-hippocampal subregions, and sleep-dependent consolidation. A comprehensive description of the full study protocol is 5 provided elsewhere (68). Here, we summarize the general design features relevant to the two timepoints analyzed in the current paper, along with the procedures for the Collection Game and Vacation Game Task.

Participants and Longitudinal Study Design

The initial sample at the first measurement time point (TP1) included 180 children aged 4 (mean 10 age: 53.9 ± 3.25 months, n = 38; 19 females), 5 (mean age: 66.3 ± 3.18 months, n = 34; 14 females), 6 (mean age: 78.7 ± 3.92 months, n = 39; 17 females), 7 (mean age: 89.6 ± 3.51 months, n = 35; 18 females), and 8 (mean age: 102.3 ± 3.43 months, n = 34; 16 females).

Data from a total of 8 children were excluded from both timepoints: four children were retrospectively excluded due to a diagnosis of ADHD at a later time than their initial participation and four 15 children only completed the first of five experimental sessions at their initial measurement time point.

For the Collection Game, data from two additional children at TP 1 were missing due to experimenter error, resulting in 170 children from TP1 included. For the Vacation Game, data from two additional children at TP 1 were missing due to early termination by the participant, resulting in 170 children from TP1 included. Except for 8-year-olds at TP1, all children were 20 invited to return for a second assessment (TP2) that was scheduled approximately 12 months (± 3 weeks) later (see Fig. 1A for a schematic on the longitudinal design and for a display of time between TP1 and TP2 for each participant). Of the initial 4-to 7-year-olds included at TP1, 120 children returned for TP2, from which 119 completed the Collection Game Task and 118 completed the Vacation Game Task.
