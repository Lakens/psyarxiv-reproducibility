Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Depression-Anxiety Comorbidity Across Sex During COVID-19: An Evaluation of Partial Invariance with Invariance Partial Pruning

Although depression and anxiety are distinct diagnostic categories, they often do not present as isolated conditions; instead, they co-occur so frequently that comorbidity, defined here as the presence of more than one diagnosable disorder in the same individual, is a central clinical presentation rather than an exception in clinical populations (Cheng et al., 2025; Lamers et al., 2011; Li et al., 2025b; Zhang et al., 2024). In the Netherlands Study of Depression and Anxiety (NESDA), among patients diagnosed with depression, 75% were diagnosed with anxiety at least once in their lifetime, and 67% were currently suffering from an anxiety disorder; among patients diagnosed with anxiety, 63% were suffering from a current depressive disorder, and 81% were diagnosed with a depressive disorder at least once (Lamers et al., 2011). The comorbidity of anxiety and depression has been a major contributor to more persistent or treatment-resistant symptom presentations and a challenge to intervention, linked to greater need for professional care, worse clinical outcomes, more disruption to daily functioning, and elevated suicide risk (Beard et al., 2016; Kaiser et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024).

The study of comorbidity and psychology in general was heavily influenced by latent-variable models, such as factor and item-response theory models (Borsboom, 2008; Cramer et al., 2010). This perspective defines and studies mental disorders as unobservable latent common causes that lead to a series of observable features (i.e., symptoms). The observed correlations among features arise from the latent common cause. These correlations are considered trivial and are expected to disappear once the common cause is controlled for (Pearl, 2009). Remaining correlations among residuals 1 are considered to be violations of the local independence assumption (Kline & Little, 2023). Treating symptoms themselves or targeting their relations has no impact on the latent common causes, as the symptoms are the effects rather than the cause of mental disorders. observed correlations among sneezing, headache, and coughing are meaningless, and treating these symptoms themselves does not cure a cold, as the cold itself is a latent common cause (i.e., a virus or bacterial infection). Curing the cold requires targeting the common cause of the symptoms: the virus or the infection. Once the common cause is removed, the symptoms will disappear naturally. Applying such a perspective to psychopathology is challenging: despite extensive research, attempts to identify shared pathogenic mechanisms have not yet produced a clear or consistent answer for most mental disorders (Borsboom et al., 2019). Furthermore, symptoms and their correlations are meaningful. For example, sleep disturbance can lead to lethargy, which can impair concentration. The latent-variable perspective is also limited in its understanding of comorbidity: observed symptom relations are explained by unobserved constructs (i.e., latent variables). Comorbidity arises solely from relations among latent constructs, providing limited insights for identifying concrete, symptom-level intervention points.

Recent advances in psychopathology understand comorbidity through a network perspective. Instead of considering mental disorders to be unobservable latent factors, it defines mental disorders as systems of observable symptoms (i.e., nodes) that causally interact with each other (the relations are defined as edges). The inter-symptom interactions sustain mental disorders, and interventions should be based on understanding the connections between observed symptoms, as well as identifying the central symptoms that explain most other symptoms. Consequently, the network perspective posits that comorbidity emerges when symptoms from one disorder causally activate and sustain symptoms from another, with cross-disorder "bridge" symptoms transmitting symptom activation across diagnostic categories (Borsboom, 2017; Borsboom & Cramer, 2013; Cramer et al., 2010; Robinaugh et al., 2020). Therefore, understanding comorbidity involves identifying the bridging symptoms that connect the symptoms of one disorder to those of another. Due to its focus on observable features and the interactions among them, the network analysis of depression and anxiety symptoms can offer more direct insights into the onset of and the intervention for depression-anxiety comorbidity, compared to the latent variable perspective that focuses on unobservable latent variables and their relations (Borsboom, 2017; Borsboom & Cramer, 2013; Cramer et al., 2010).

Motivated by the aforementioned rationale, prior research has examined the network structure of depression and anxiety symptoms using Gaussian graphical models (GGMs) applied to cross-sectional data (Beard et al., 2016; Kaiser et al., 2021; Li et al., 2025b) and cross-lagged panel network models based on two-wave panel data (Zhang et al., 2024). These studies identified essential bridge symptoms linking depression and anxiety, including psychomotor and concentration problems, restlessness (Beard et al., 2016; Kaiser et al., 2021), nervousness, guilt, (Li et al., 2025b), mood and physical retardation, somatic complaints, hypochondriasis, and diurnal variation (Zhang et al., 2024). Yet, GGMs offer a poor estimate of causal relations (Ryan et al., 2022), and they do not reveal within-person dynamic effects (Epskamp et al., 2018). Two-wave panel data do not allow models to separate within-and between-person variance. Consequently, for cross-lagged panel network models, the estimation of within-person effects can be biased by between-person effects, leading to spurious within-person relations (Hamaker, 2023; Hamaker et al., 2015).

The panel graphical vector autoregression (panelGVAR) model, by contrast, can estimate fixed effects of within-person dynamical relations between depression and anxiety symptoms while separating within-and between-person variances (Epskamp, 2020). We therefore aim to use this model to study the dynamics of depression and anxiety symptoms over time.

In addition, existing evidence suggests both robust and nuanced sex differences in depression, anxiety, and their comorbidity, which may have implications for how comorbidity manifests and should be treated. Large cohort studies such as the Netherlands Study of Depression and Anxiety have reported that women with major depressive disorder showed higher levels of comorbid anxiety and atypical features than men, suggesting potentially different underlying mechanisms and course of illness across sex groups (Schuch et al., 2014). Likewise, reviews of anxiety disorders consistently find that women are about twice as likely as men to meet criteria for an anxiety disorder across the lifespan, and that comorbidity with depression is common and often more burdensome among women (McLean et al., 2011). Taken together, these findings indicate that depression-anxiety comorbidity is not uniform across biological sexes.

These considerations further motivated a modeling strategy that can explicitly compare network structures across sexes. Hence, to estimate the dynamical comorbidity networks, while considering the structural differences across males and females (Du et al., 2026), we adopted a novel approach developed under the panelGVAR framework termed the invariance partial pruning (IVPP) approach (Du et al., 2026). IVPP allows multi-group estimation of the panelGVAR model while revealing group-specific variances and the invariance of edges across groups. The model outputs a unique network structure for each group, with some edges constrained to be equal across groups and others freely estimated within each group, based on data fit. While designed for network comparison, IVPP is also suitable for evidence synthesis that evaluates what connections are shared across groups and what are unique to some groups. To this end, it is well suited to investigating the dynamical relations among depression, anxiety, and transdiagnostic mechanisms to explain comorbidity, while evaluating the cross-group stability of the discovered network structures.

In this exploratory study, we seek to map the within-person temporal network linking depression and anxiety symptoms and to explore whether these dynamical networks differ between males and females. In the networks, we also added mechanism variables that can be targeted and intervened on to inform intervention. To do so, we will (1) fit panelGVAR models to recover within-person lagged effects among symptoms while separating within-and between-person variance, and (2) use the IVPP multi-group estimation approach to determine which edges are shared across sexes, which are group-specific, and to quantify the degree of invariance.

Methods

Participants and data

The data is from the Mental Health and Adherence Project (MAP-19), a large-scale longitudinal survey of the Norwegian adult population during COVID-19. As in previous studies (Hoffart, Skjerdingstad, Freichel, et al., 2023; Hoffart et al., 2025), we used the fifth through eighth measurements, which were approximately equidistant, to satisfy the assumption of equidistant measures in panelGVAR (Epskamp, 2020). We implemented the measurements from May 8 to May 25, 2021 (W5), July 4 to August 1, 2021 (W6), October 24 to November 12, 2021 (W7), and January 2 to January 14, 2022 (W8). Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Regional Committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics (reference number 125510).

Respondents were adults (age ≥ 18 years) residing in Norway during the measurement waves (Ebrahimi et al., 2021). We recruited most participants (70%) using a Facebook Business algorithm, which was expected to reach 3.6 million of Norway's 4.2 million adults. To reach the remaining 600,000 target population, we used national, regional, and local information platforms (e.g., television, radio, and newspapers). All participants received the same online survey, regardless of the recruitment method. We also contacted the same participants and asked them to participate in all measurement waves. Data quality was assessed through an attention check question (Please enter the answer option "A little" to show that you are following this survey). After excluding participants who failed the attention check, 10,061 cases remained at the first measurement wave (W1; March 2020). To match the known demographic characteristics of the Norwegian adult population (e.g., a similar sex ratio and distribution of education levels), we extracted a stratified, representative sample of 4,361 adults from the total sample. After excluding participants who did not participate in any of the selected waves or failed to provide information on their biological sex, 1,235 data remained (582 female, 653 male, and M age = 39.0). In this sample, 1,060 responded at W5, 915 responded at W6, 1,006 responded at W7, and 867 responded at W8 (Hoffart, Skjerdingstad, Freichel, et al., 2023).

We analyzed data with full information maximum likelihood (FIML; Schafer and Graham, 2002) to handle missingness, which assumes that the data are missing at randomfoot_0.

Variable selection

To maintain reasonable estimation accuracy for parameter-laden network models, we kept the number of nodes at 10, the same maximum used in the simulation study that validated IVPP (Du et al., 2026). In the present study, we selected three depression symptoms and three anxiety symptoms to capture core depression and generalized anxiety outcomes. In addition, to facilitate intervention and explain the mechanisms of comorbidity, we selected three transdiagnostic mechanism variables. Outcomes are defined as specific reactions common to the relevant phenomenology, while mechanisms are observable, actionable processes that psychological treatment can target (Hoffart & Johnson, 2020). The depression symptoms selected were anhedonia, depressed mood, and sleep disturbance from the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9; Over the last 2 weeks, how often have you been bothered by any of the following problems [0 = Not at all,1 = Several days,2 = More than half the days,3 = Nearly every day]; Kroenke et al., 2001).

These three symptoms correspond to the two gateway criteria for major depressive disorder and a key somatic symptom (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). They are also central nodes of the PHQ-9 according to previous dynamic network analysis (Hoffart, Skjerdingstad, Freichel, et al., 2023; O'Driscoll et al., 2022). For anxiety, we selected Nervous, Restless, and Fear from the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7; Over the last 2 weeks, how often have you been bothered by any of the following problems [0 = Not at all,1 = Several days,2 = More than half the days,3 = Nearly every day]; Spitzer et al., 2006). They tap the somatic tension and threat-related hyper-arousal components that characterize generalized anxiety and are robustly associated with depression in comorbid samples (Hoffart et al., 2025; Kaiser et al., 2021; O'Driscoll et al., 2022). For mechanisms, we selected uncontrollable worry (beliefs about the uncontrollability of worry) from GAD-7 and worry and rumination and threat monitoring from the Cognitive-Attentional Syndrome-1 scale (CAS-1; In the past week, how much have you engaged in the following [0 = none of the time,8 = all of the time]; Nordahl and Wells, 2019). They are the three transdiagnostic mechanisms identified by the theory of self-regulatory executive function (S-REF). Specifically, worry, rumination, and threat monitoring are cognitive processes termed CAS, and the uncontrollability of worry is a negative belief about the controllability of CAS. Taken together, these outcome and mechanism nodes align with network-based accounts in which overlapping symptoms and shared cognitive-attentional processes act as "bridges" between depression and anxiety clusters (Groen et al., 2020). Statistical Methods

We used the IVPP approach to estimate panelGVAR models while accounting for partial invariance in network structures between males and females (Du et al., 2026). The method comprises three steps to determine group invariance. The first step is a global invariance test on both the temporal and the contemporaneous networks (Hoekstra et al., 2024), where the algorithm compares a fully constrained model (M 0 ) in which both the temporal (B B B) and the contemporaneous networks (Ω Ω Ω) are constrained to be equal across groups with a free model (M 1 ) in which neither network is constrained.

If the free model (M 1 ) fits better than the fully constrained model (M 0 ), the procedure proceeds to the second step, which is to determine the global invariance of the temporal networks and the contemporaneous networks separately. The procedure can either start from M 0 or M 1 : To determine the global invariance of the temporal networks, we can either add an equality constraint on the temporal networks to M 0 to check whether the equality constraint lowered model fit, or remove the equality constraint on the temporal networks from M 1 to check whether the removal of the equality constraint improved model fit. M 1 . A global invariance test for the contemporaneous networks can be M 2 vs. M 0 or M 3 vs. M 1 . We based the comparison on sparse networks, as they showed greater power to detect true differences while maintaining false-positive rates similar to tests based on saturated networks in simulation (Du et al., 2026). Model comparisons can be based on AIC, BIC, or the likelihood ratio test (LRT). When these criteria conflicted on global invariance, we based our inference on AIC, as in the simulation study that validates IVPP; BIC showed a high risk of insufficient power, and LRT showed high type I error rates. AIC showed the best power-error balance (Du et al., 2026).

The end-product of IVPP is a temporal and a contemporaneous network for each group, with some edges constrained to be equal and others freely estimated across groups.

Using the networks, we further computed the in-and out-strength centrality of each node in temporal networks and the strength centrality in contemporaneous networks.

In-strength centrality of a node is defined as the sum of all incoming edge weights, that is, the total lagged influence the node receives from all other nodes in the network (Borsboom et al., 2021). Out-strength centrality is defined as the sum of all outgoing edge weights, reflecting the total lagged influence a node exerts on all other nodes. Strength centrality, computed in undirected contemporaneous networks, is the sum of all edge weights incident to a node, capturing a node's overall connectivity regardless of direction. In addition, we computed bridge centrality indices to quantify nodes' role in connecting depression, anxiety, and mechanisms (Jones et al., 2019). Bridge in-strength is the sum of incoming edge weights from nodes in a different community, and bridge out-strength is the sum of outgoing edge weights to nodes in a different community. Bridge strength is the undirected analog, defined as the sum of all edge weights connecting a node to nodes outside its own community. We further broke down the bridge centrality measures by community pairs.

For example, the bridge out-strength of a depression symptom to anxiety indicates how much this depression symptom contributes to the transition from depression to anxiety.

Together, these indices identify nodes that may facilitate the spread of activation across symptom clusters, which is of particular clinical relevance in transdiagnostic network models.

Discussion

In this longitudinal study, we examined within-person temporal and contemporaneous networks linking depression symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and transdiagnostic mechanisms during the COVID-19 pandemic, and compared these structures across biological sex using the IVPP approach. Across four waves of data, fear ("feeling afraid as if something awful might happen") emerged as the primary temporal predictor of subsequent depressive mood and anhedonia, as well as of nervousness and restlessness. Worry and rumination predicted later sleep disturbance. In the contemporaneous networks, nervousness and fear were tightly connected with depressive mood. Worry and rumination were more strongly associated with depression, whereas beliefs about the uncontrollability of worry and threat monitoring were more strongly associated with anxiety. Overall, temporal and contemporaneous network structures were largely invariant across males and females, with only minimal, potentially spurious sex differences detected.

Bridge centrality analysis by community pairs in the temporal networks revealed the central role of fear in predicting depression in the next measurement (highest bridge out-strength to depression), through anhedonia and depressive mood (highest bridge in-strength from anxiety). Furthermore, worry and rumination were the primary transdiagnostic mechanisms that predicted sleep disturbance in the next measurement occasion. The contemporaneous networks showed bridges between communities within the same measurement occasion. In the contemporaneous networks, anxiety and depression were connected through nervousness, fear, and depressive mood. For mechanism variables, worry and rumination were more connected to depression, and beliefs about the uncontrollability of worry and threat monitoring were more connected to anxiety.

These results indicated that the main driver for depression-anxiety comorbidity could be anxiety, and depression could be a downstream disorder following persistent anxiety. The channeling symptom was the fear that awful events might happen, which further led to anhedonia and depressive mood. The fear was also a main predictor of other anxiety symptoms in the temporal network. Fear and depressive mood were also the central channels between anxiety and depression in the contemporaneous networks. However, the connection between nervous and depressive mood was stronger in the contemporaneous networks in the present study, which agreed with the findings of Beard et al. (2016) based on GGMs, where they also discovered the connection between nervous and depressive mood as a channel between depression and anxiety. As transdiagnostic mechanisms, beliefs about the uncontrollability of worry and threat-monitoring were more connected to anxiety symptoms in the contemporaneous networks compared to worry and rumination. However, beliefs about the uncontrollability of worry and worry and rumination were more connected to depression symptoms than threat monitoring. This central role of fear aligns with findings from Hoffart et al. (2025), based on daily diary data, and Hoffart, Burger, et al.

(2023), based on panel data. This node also had the highest out-strength centrality in the network. Moreover, (Hoffart, Burger, et al., 2023) replicated the strong connection between fear and threat monitoring in contemporaneous networks. Furthermore, the contemporaneous connection between worry and rumination and depressive mood discovered in (Hoffart, Skjerdingstad, Freichel, et al., 2023) was replicated.

Overall, our within-person temporal analyses suggest that anxiety-related processes-particularly fear (the sense that awful events might happen)-often precede increases in depression symptoms, consistent with anxiety acting as a potential driver of short-term depression risk. This interpretation aligns with a substantial body of longitudinal evidence. In NESDA, anxiety preceded depression in 57% of comorbid cases, compared to only 18% in which depression came first (Lamers et al., 2011). A narrative synthesis of 76 NESDA-based papers further confirmed that depression-anxiety comorbidity was most frequently preceded by an anxiety disorder (ter Meulen et al., 2021).

A daily diary study of individuals with generalized anxiety disorder found that anxious mood robustly predicted depressed mood at subsequent time points-peaking at a two-day lag-whereas depressed mood did not consistently predict later anxious mood (Starr & Davila, 2012).

This asymmetry suggests that the temporal precedence of anxiety over depression may be an essential motor driving depression-anxiety comorbidity. The temporal and contemporaneous findings suggest that fear and worry-related processes may function as dynamic "gateways" from anxiety to depression, while also providing an explanation for the dense co-occurrence of anxiety and depression symptoms at any given time point.

Promising symptom-level targets for intervention, therefore, include specific mechanisms assessed here: worry, rumination, and negative meta-cognitive beliefs (Wells, 2011). The largely identical temporal and contemporaneous patterns across males and females suggest that these mechanisms operate similarly across sexes in this sample.

Regarding sex invariance in network structures, previous research also yielded inconsistent results. For example, Li et al. (2025a) found no sex differences in network connectivity, node centrality, or edges in a GGM analysis of depression-anxiety comorbidity involving 22,868 adolescents from 27 schools in 8 cities in China. The study of (Jin et al., 2022) also reported no sex difference in a network analysis of depression, anxiety, and quality of life based on GGM of 4, 931 clinicians in the public hospitals of China during the late stage of COVID-19. However, there were also studies indicating sex differences in depression-anxiety networks. For example, in a network study on the relation between depression, anxiety, and health-promoting lifestyles with 2,420 adults in China, Zheng et al. (2023) discovered that restlessness and low energy had the highest strength centrality in the female network, whereas suicidal ideation and restlessness were the most central in the male network. These studies were all based on GGM, and the comparisons used the network comparison test (NCT; van Borkulo et al., 2023). One possible explanation for the sex invariance was that the present study focused on within-person effects, whereas GGM merged within-and between-person variance. The other reason might be the conservatism of partial pruning, which relies on BIC (Du et al., 2026). This might be the case for comparing temporal networks. In this test, global AIC-based tests suggested group invariance, yet only the fear self-loop showed a sex difference. The other sex differences might not be estimated because partial pruning is conservative when the true temporal effects were weak, given the long temporal distance between the measurement periods. The null sex difference in the contemporaneous networks might be more credible given that both AIC and BIC suggested invariance.

These findings must also be understood in light of the COVID-19 context in which the data were collected. The pandemic introduced a combination of chronic, largely uncontrollable stressors-health threats, social restrictions, economic uncertainty, and disruptions to daily routines-that may have selectively amplified fear, worry, and threat monitoring (Hoffart et al., 2022; Magnúsdóttir et al., 2022). This could partly explain why fear emerged as such a prominent temporal driver and why beliefs about the uncontrollability of worry and threat-focused attention occupied central positions in the contemporaneous networks. At the same time, pandemic-related restrictions and lifestyle changes (for example, altered work patterns, reduced social contact, and changes in physical activity and sleep schedules) may have strengthened links between worry, rumination, and sleep disturbance. Thus, the observed network structure may reflect, in part, how depression-anxiety comorbidity is organized under sustained external threat and uncertainty. Although the basic mechanisms we identified (fear, perseverative negative thinking, and threat monitoring) are likely relevant beyond the pandemic (Hoffart & Johnson, 2020), the strength of specific edges-and the prominence of certain nodes-may differ in less extreme or more stable environmental conditions.

Comparing network structure across groups is crucial for both theory and practice.

Multi-group analyses reveal whether the dynamical relations that sustain psychopathology are universal or vary systematically across subpopulations, information that cannot be recovered from pooled analyses alone. Clinically, identifying edges that are shared across groups points to transdiagnostic targets suitable for broad interventions, whereas group-specific connections highlight the need for tailored strategies (for example, sex-sensitive assessment or intervention components). Finally, testing for invariance helps reconcile inconsistent findings in the literature by distinguishing true heterogeneity from sampling variation or methodological differences (e.g., cross-sectional GGMs vs within-person panel models). For these reasons, multi-group network comparison is not merely an auxiliary analysis but a central step toward translating symptom-level network insights into equitable and effective clinical practice.

Limitations

When interpreting the results of the present study, one should bear in mind that VAR-based network models only represent temporal predictive relations, namely, Granger causality (Epskamp et al., 2018). One should not take predictive relations to be causal effects. Furthermore, the measurements in this study were spaced far apart (i.e., 1.5 -2 months), so the true temporal effects were weak and difficult to detect. Therefore, the results based on temporal networks may not generalize well to other samples. The temporal effects estimated in the study were also weak. In addition, the mechanism variables and anxiety symptoms are relatively stable features, whereas depression is episodic (American Psychiatric Association, 2022; Wells, 2011). This might attenuate the temporal variance of mechanism and anxiety variables, leading to weaker temporal links from and towards these variables. Interpret the sex difference in contemporaneous networks with caution, as both AIC and BIC indicated invariance in the global test. In this case, the discovered sex differences were likely false positives (Du et al., 2026). Additionally, the data were positively skewed, with most respondents scoring low on the symptoms and transdiagnostic mechanism items. Furthermore, the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scales are ordinal rather than continuous (Isvoranu & Epskamp, 2021). However, there are currently no estimation methods for handling ordinal data in panelGVAR models. This can be a promising area to pursue in the future.

In this study, missing values were handled using full-information maximum likelihood (FIML), which in general performs better than list-wise deletion, pairwise deletion, and mean substitution and yields essentially the same results as multiple imputation (MI) and complete-data maximum likelihood across a wide range of sample sizes, missingness rates, and degrees of model misfit (Schafer & Graham, 2002). However, FIML accuracy relies on the assumption that the data are missing-at-random (MAR).

MAR implies that, conditional on observed variables (e.g., prior-wave symptom levels and other covariates), the probability of missingness does not depend on the unobserved values themselves (Mazza & Enders, 2013). This assumption may be violated if participants with more severe depression symptoms did not complete questionnaires during the assessment period, even after accounting for their observed symptom history. In this case, the measurement is missing not at random (MNAR), which would underestimate symptom severity, leading to lower-than-expected scores and lower variances, and underestimating the correlational effects in this study. Another possibility is that respondents participated only when they felt terrible and missed measurements when they felt better, because they felt it was unnecessary to join. In this case, the sample's symptom severity would be overestimated. However, if a person's missingness can be explained by the measurements he/she has already attended to, the data are closer to MAR, thereby enhancing the credibility of the present study. The more missingness is explained by scores the person did not attend to, the closer it is to MNAR, thereby threatening the credibility of the present study.

Regarding the recruitment process, we aimed to reach the entire Norwegian population through a combination of the Facebook algorithm (3.6 out of 4.2 million Norwegian adults), television, radio, and newspapers (remaining 600,000). However, the generalizability of the present sample is subject to self-selection bias (Kaźmierczak et al., 2023), which occurs when individuals volunteer for a study, leading to a non-representative sample that is often skewed toward extreme opinions or specific demographic traits (often more females and younger participants). In addition, to ensure accurate estimation, we selected a limited number of nodes that did not cover all depression and anxiety symptoms.

Results may be different when additional nodes are added to the networks. Furthermore, all data are based on self-report questionnaire data and are not clinically validated. As such, the presence of depression or anxiety symptoms should not be interpreted as depression illness or GAD on a diagnostic level.

Future directions

Future research can build on this work in several ways. First, studies employing denser time-series designs, such as daily diary or ecological momentary assessment, are needed to capture more fine-grained temporal dynamics. Such designs would enable examination of shorter-lag processes (hours or days rather than weeks or months), potentially revealing additional pathways by which anxiety and depression influence each other. Second, experimental and intervention studies could test the causal status of the mechanisms highlighted here using RCT designs. Third, applying multi-group panelGVAR and IVPP to other group comparisons (for example, clinical vs. community samples, individuals with vs. without trauma history, or different age cohorts) could clarify the extent to which the network structure of depression-anxiety comorbidity is universal versus context-specific. Such work would also help identify which edges can be treated as broadly transdiagnostic targets and which require group-specific adaptation.

Conclusion

Anxiety symptoms predicted depression symptoms in the temporal comorbidity analysis. Fear and mechanisms like worry, rumination, and beliefs about the uncontrollability of worry emerged as central drivers linking anxiety to depression symptoms contemporaneously. These mechanisms formed a transdiagnostic core that connected anxiety and depression. Network structures were largely invariant across males and females, suggesting that key dynamic mechanisms are shared across sexes. These findings support network-based accounts in which comorbidity is sustained by specific symptom and mechanism interactions, and they highlight fear and perseverative negative thinking as promising targets for transdiagnostic intervention aimed at reducing the burden of co-occurring depression and anxiety.
