Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Educational impact and implications statement

Students' self-directed use of generative AI (GenAI) tools for schoolwork is increasing rapidly, and this study found that students with lower achievement at the beginning of the school year reported using GenAI more often at the end of the year, whereas earlier GenAI use did not predict later achievement. Considering both types of use together, students who used GenAI more often to get tasks done reported investing less effort by the end of the school year in four of six subjects, whereas those who used it more often to learn tended to report more effort. Self-Directed Generative AI Use and Academic Development Among Secondary School Students: A Large-Scale, One-Year Longitudinal Study

The widespread availability of generative AI (GenAI) tools, such as ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini, is transforming how students learn and how teachers prepare and teach in schools and classrooms (Chiu, 2024; Giannakos et al., 2025). To date, research has primarily focused on pedagogically designed AI tools that scale established learning interventions and are implemented in structured, often closely supervised settings (e.g., An et al., 2026; Glüsing et al., 2026; Liang & Wu, 2024; Lin & Zhang, 2024; Meyer et al., 2024; Rahman & Watanobe, 2023; Wu et al., 2026; Y. Xu et al., 2024; Yan et al., 2025). Studies focusing on students' self-directed use of GenAI tools, which typically occurs without predefined instructional designs or systematic pedagogical guidance, are emerging (e.g., Granström & Oppi, 2025; Hugerth & Hugerth, 2026). Yet, substantially less is known about how students' self-directed use of GenAI tools affects achievement, motivation, and effort over time (Bastani et al., 2025; Gerlich, 2025; Kosmyna et al., 2025; J. Liu et al., 2026; Schorr et al., 2026). Likewise, research on the antecedents of self-directed GenAI use in educational contexts is only beginning to emerge (Granić, 2025; Nguyen et al., 2026). This gap is particularly striking given the intense societal debates surrounding potential effects of selfdirected GenAI use and its growing prevalence among students (e.g., Baek et al., 2024; Darvishi et al., 2024; Kasneci et al., 2023; New York City Mayor's Office, 2026; Panadero & Broadbent, 2025; Yan et al., 2024).

Prior research suggests that it is not GenAI use per se, but rather the purpose for which and the way in which GenAI is used that is critical to understanding its relation to achievement, motivation, and effort (e.g., Y. Chen et al., 2026; Giannakos et al., 2025; Han et al., 2025; Panadero & Broadbent, 2025; Weidlich et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2024). For instance, theoretical work has argued that GenAI systems may differentially support or undermine learning depending on whether they increase or reduce cognitive engagement (Acemoglu et al., 2026). Related to this, decades of educational psychology research have shown that motivation-an important educational outcome (Jansen et al., 2022) and predictor of later academic achievement and choices (Eccles & Wigfield, 2002 , 2020)-is closely linked to active engagement and effort during learning processes. Thus, integrating theoretical and empirical insights from research on motivation and GenAI in education suggests that students' patterns of self-directed GenAI use may both shape and be shaped by students' effort, motivation, and academic achievement over time (e.g., Panadero & Broadbent, 2025).

To date, large-scale longitudinal studies examining reciprocal associations between selfdirected GenAI use and students' academic achievement, motivation, and effort in naturalistic school settings remain scarce, leaving these associations poorly understood.

To address this gap, we conducted a secondary analysis of longitudinal data from the TOSCAneo study, comprising 4,062 students from 136 secondary schools in Baden-Württemberg, Germany, surveyed at the beginning and end of the 2023/2024 school year (Schnaufer et al., 2026a (Schnaufer et al., , 2026b)). Our study design has three key features: First, we measured achievement with standardized tests rather than self-reported grades. Second, we captured students' self-directed GenAI use, rather than assigning or experimentally inducing it. Third, we assessed achievement, self-concept, value beliefs, and effort at both time points in six subjects, which allowed us to estimate associations with GenAI use in both directions. With this design, we move beyond asking whether GenAI use helps or harms students on average. Instead, we examine which forms of self-directed use relate to students' achievement, motivation, and effort, and which students turn to GenAI in the first place. This offers a level of specificity that is currently lacking in the debate about the effects of students' self-directed GenAI use on their academic development.

Students' Use of Generative AI Tools and Academic Outcomes Prior GenAI Use and Future Academic Achievement

Theories of learning emphasize that knowledge acquisition depends on learners' active cognitive engagement with tasks and learning materials, including processes such as elaboration, explanation, and the integration of new information with prior knowledge (e.g., Chi & Wylie, 2014; Fiorella & Mayer, 2015; Freeman et al., 2014). These engagement processes may be altered when GenAI is used for a task (e.g., Cho, 2026). Through active cognitive engagement, learners are more likely to develop coherent, transferable, and wellstructured knowledge representations. Conversely, when cognitive engagement is reduced, opportunities for meaningful learning and skill development may become limited. Prior educational research suggests that students can approach academic tasks with different goals and intentions, which influence how they engage with learning activities and regulate their effort (Ames, 1992; Elliott & Dweck, 1988). Drawing on these perspectives, the effects of students' self-directed GenAI use may differ depending on whether GenAI is primarily used to facilitate task completion or to support understanding and learning.

When GenAI is used to complete academic tasks efficiently, students may offload substantial parts of cognitive work to the system, thereby reducing active cognitive engagement with the learning material (Fan et al., 2025; Stadler et al., 2024). GenAI may make this form of task completion particularly accessible because academic outputs can increasingly be produced with comparatively little effort or active processing. In such cases, GenAI use may reduce opportunities for practice, elaboration, and knowledge construction.

In contrast, GenAI can also be used in ways that support active learning processes. For example, students may use GenAI for learning-oriented purposes, such as obtaining explanations, clarifying concepts, generating examples, or supporting exam preparation. In these cases, GenAI use may promote rather than reduce cognitive engagement by supporting elaboration and deeper processing of learning materials (J. Lee et al., 2025; Xiao et al., 2026).

From this perspective, the educational consequences of GenAI use may depend less on whether students use GenAI and more on the purpose for which GenAI is used during academic activities. Forms of use that are focused on task completion may contribute to reduced motivation and effort and, potentially, to deskilling over time (Stadler et al., 2024), whereas learning-oriented use may support effort and skill development (Xiao et al., 2026).

Prior GenAI Use and Future Academic Motivation and Effort

Student GenAI use might also play a pivotal role in developing academic motivation and effort. According to (situated) expectancy-value theories (e.g., Atkinson, 1957; Eccles & Wigfield, 2020), students' expectancy and value beliefs represent central motivational drivers of academic behavior. Expectancy beliefs reflect students' perceptions of their competence and likelihood of success, whereas value beliefs capture the perceived importance, usefulness, or intrinsic interest of a task (e.g., Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). Together, these beliefs shape students' willingness to invest effort, persist in challenging activities, and actively engage in learning processes (e.g., Eccles & Wigfield, 2000). Academic effort can therefore be understood as a behavioral manifestation or indicator of students' underlying motivation. Sustained effort, in turn, is considered an important prerequisite for long-term skill development and academic achievement. Therefore, in line with prior studies (e.g., Gaspard et al., 2016), we consider self-concept and value beliefs as theory-based aspects of academic motivation and effort as a behavioral consequence of motivation.

Different forms of GenAI use may have distinct implications for students' academic motivation and willingness to invest effort in future learning activities. Forms of GenAI use in which GenAI is primarily used to complete tasks efficiently or replace cognitive work may reduce the perceived necessity and value of exerting effort during learning activities (Fan et al., 2026). If students repeatedly experience that academic tasks can be completed successfully with minimal cognitive engagement, effort itself may become perceived as less important or less worthwhile (e.g., Jansen et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2024). In the same vein, students might feel they will not be able to perform as well as the AI, potentially undermining learners' subject-specific self-concept (Jansen et al., 2025). Over time, this may reduce students' willingness to engage actively, persist in cognitively demanding tasks, and exert effort (H.-P. Lee et al., 2025).

In contrast, when GenAI is used to support rather than replace cognitive processes, it may foster learners' perceptions of competence while still requiring active effort and engagement. For example, GenAI-supported explanations, feedback, or scaffolding may help students successfully engage with challenging tasks without bypassing the underlying learning processes (Fütterer et al., 2026). In line with expectancy-value frameworks (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020), such experiences of competence and successful engagement may support motivation and sustained effort in future learning activities.

Emerging empirical evidence supports the assumption that GenAI use may have motivational consequences; however, the direction of effects seems mostly negative. This is in line with hypotheses laid out by Fan et al. (2026). For example, G. Liu et al. (2026) found that although GenAI assistance improved short-term task performance, participants subsequently performed worse without GenAI support and were more likely to give up on challenging tasks, indicative of reduced motivation and effort. Mei et al. (2025) found in a university context that learners were able to improve their text quality when using GenAI in a creative writing task but reported it was less enjoyable and valuable. These findings suggest that certain forms of GenAI use may negatively affect not only performance but also motivation, task persistence, and willingness to exert effort during learning activities (see also H.-P. Lee et al., 2025). Overall, while evidence is emerging, longitudinal studies of selfdirected GenAI use in K-12 contexts that distinguish between different ways of using GenAI and consider academic outcomes are still missing in the context of self-directed AI use (see Fütterer et al., 2026, for studies on GenAI-driven interventions).

Prior Academic Achievement and Future GenAI Use

Students' prior achievement might also influence how they engage with GenAI because productive use requires students to formulate appropriate queries, evaluate generated outputs, and integrate them with their existing knowledge. These processes depend on prior knowledge and subject-specific competencies (Alexander, 1992; Dochy et al., 1999), and the related capacity to regulate one's own learning (e.g., Zimmerman, 2000). Given the positive association between academic achievement and self-regulated learning (Dent & Koenka, 2016), higher-achieving students may therefore be better positioned to evaluate whether and how GenAI use serves their longer-term learning goals and to use GenAI in ways that effectively support their own learning processes (X. Xu et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024). For instance, they may be more likely to recognize when using GenAI to generate an answer would replace learning-relevant cognitive activity and instead use it to obtain explanations, clarify concepts, or support their own problem-solving. In contrast, lower-achieving students may face a double disadvantage: greater need for immediate assistance with academic tasks while simultaneously having fewer cognitive and self-regulatory resources to use GenAI in ways that support longer-term learning. Prior achievement may therefore shape not only students' need for GenAI assistance, but also their capacity to use such assistance strategically, potentially contributing to greater task-completion use among lower-achieving students and greater learning-oriented use among higher-achieving students.

Prior Academic Motivation and Effort and Future GenAI Use

Students' academic motivation and effort may also shape how they use GenAI during academic tasks. According to expectancy-value theory, students' decisions about whether and how strongly to engage in academic activities depend on their expectations of success and the value they attach to these activities (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). These motivational beliefs may therefore also influence whether students use GenAI primarily as a means of completing academic tasks or as a resource for learning (e.g., Pan et al., 2026).

Situated expectancy-value theory (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020) implies that students with lower academic self-concept in a subject would be particularly likely to use GenAI to complete academic tasks when they perceive themselves as less capable of successfully completing them independently. In such situations, GenAI provides an accessible way to obtain solutions or produce academic outputs while reducing the demands placed on the learner. Similarly, students who perceive little value in an academic task may have fewer reasons to invest substantial cognitive effort in completing it themselves (Fan et al., 2026).

Using GenAI to facilitate task completion may therefore be particularly attractive when students consider an activity uninteresting, unimportant, or of little utility. Finally, students who generally tend to invest less effort in academic activities may be more inclined to use GenAI in ways that minimize the effort required for task completion. Thus, situated expectancy-value theory (e.g., Eccles & Wigfield, 2020) would predict greater taskcompletion GenAI use among students with lower subject-specific self-concept, value beliefs, and tendency to exert academic effort in a subject. Conversely, stronger academic motivation may be associated with greater learningoriented GenAI use. Students who value learning activities and are willing to invest effort may be more likely to use GenAI in ways that support their own learning processes (e.g., Wang et al., 2026), for example, by requesting explanations, generating examples, or preparing for exams or tests. Students with stronger academic self-concepts tend to show more adaptive engagement with academic tasks, including greater persistence and willingness to invest effort in learning (Eccles & Wigfield, 2002; Marsh & Craven, 2006). They may therefore be more likely to approach GenAI as an additional learning resource rather than as a substitute for their own cognitive activity. From this perspective, students' motivation may shape not only whether they engage with GenAI, but also the purposes for which they use it.

These relations may contribute to reciprocal dynamics between GenAI use and academic motivation and effort. If lower academic motivation increases task-completion GenAI use, and task-completion GenAI use subsequently reduces students' willingness to invest effort in academic activities, initial motivational differences may be reinforced over time. Likewise, learning-oriented GenAI use and stronger academic motivation may mutually reinforce one another. Examining these reciprocal relationships is thus central to improving our understanding of how different forms of GenAI use and key academic variables, such as achievement, motivation, and effort, co-develop over time.

The Present Study

As outlined above, empirical research on students' self-directed use of GenAI and its reciprocal relations with academic achievement, motivation, and effort remains limited, particularly in naturalistic school contexts. Much of the existing evidence relies on selfreported achievement outcomes (e.g., Gerlich, 2025; J. Lee et al., 2025), focuses on adult learners (e.g., H.-P. Lee et al., 2025; Lira et al., 2025; Prather et al., 2024), or does not differentiate between qualitatively different forms of GenAI use (e.g., Bastani et al., 2025).

Moreover, prior work has largely relied on short-term experimental paradigms rather than examining students' self-directed GenAI use in authentic classroom contexts using sufficiently large longitudinal samples (Kosmyna et al., 2025; G. Liu et al., 2026).

Understanding how students use GenAI in the absence of system-led prompting or instructor guidance is particularly important because these tools are continuously available to learners and are likely to be used under authentic, self-directed conditions. The present study addresses this gap by examining secondary school students' self-directed GenAI use across an entire school year, considering a rich set of academic outcomes (i.e., achievement, self-concept, value beliefs, and effort) in six subjects. Importantly, we distinguish between taskcompletion and learning-oriented forms of GenAI use as qualitatively different patterns of engagement that may differentially shape skill development, motivation, and effort. Specifically, we address the following research question: How do secondary school students' task-completion and learning-oriented uses of GenAI tools relate reciprocally to their academic achievement, self-concept, value beliefs, and effort over the course of a school year across six subjects?

Addressing this question has important implications not only for understanding what predicts self-directed GenAI use and how this form of GenAI use is related to subsequent achievement, motivation, and effort, but also for the development of interventions and guidance that distinguish between forms of GenAI use that support versus undermine learning-oriented cognitive and motivational processes. Based on the theoretical considerations outlined above, we hypothesized that learning-oriented GenAI use would be positively associated with future academic achievement, motivation, and effort, whereas taskcompletion GenAI use would be negatively associated with these outcomes over time.

Similarly, we hypothesized that students' academic motivation (i.e., subject-specific selfconcept and value beliefs), academic effort as a behavioral manifestation of motivation, and academic achievement would predict subsequent GenAI use. Specifically, students with higher academic motivation, greater prior effort, and higher academic achievement were expected to engage more frequently in learning-oriented GenAI use and less frequently in task-completion GenAI use than students with lower motivation, effort, and achievement.

Measures

GenAI Tool Use for Academic Purposes

Students' GenAI tool use for academic purposes was assessed with seven items describing different academic activities: "solving tasks during class", "preparing for exams or tests", "doing homework", "preparing presentations and essays", "better understanding of class material", "further learning", and "translating texts". Students rated how often they engaged in each activity on a 5-point response scale scored 0 (never), 1 (less than once a month), 2 (at least once a month but not every week), 3 (at least once a week but not every school day), and 4 (every school day). We grouped the items into a task-completion factor and a learning-oriented factor of self-directed GenAI use. The task-completion GenAI factor consisted of four items capturing task-completion use (i.e., using GenAI for "translating texts", "doing homework", "preparing presentations and essays", and "solving tasks during class"), whereas the learning-oriented GenAI factor consisted of three items capturing learning-oriented use (i.e., using GenAI for "better understanding of class material", "further learning", and "preparing for exams or tests"; see Supplement S2: Mplus Code A). Weighted likelihood estimate (WLE; Warm, 1989) reliabilities for task-completion and learningoriented GenAI use were .63 and .67 at T1 and .69 and .75 at T2, respectively.

Student Academic Achievement

In TOSCAneo, student academic achievement was measured to assess skill development across the school year (Schnaufer et al., 2026a (Schnaufer et al., , 2026b)). We provide details on the standardized achievement tests further below. In all cases, we used WLEs from a unidimensional two-parameter logistic (2PL) item response theory (IRT) model (for polytomous or dichotomous items) as in Schnaufer et al. (2026a Schnaufer et al. ( , 2026b)). If items had an average solution rate ≤ 5% or ≥ 95% or a negative item discrimination, they were dropped from the test to avoid convergence issues. All tests but the English test were developed by the Institute for Educational Quality Improvement (IQB) to assess the educational achievement of students at the end of lower secondary school for the IQB Trends in Student Achievement study. To account for the somewhat higher age of the students in TOSCAneo compared with those in the IQB Trends in Student Achievement study, a selection with relatively higher item difficulties was used in both assessment waves. Additional information on the tests and example items can be found here: http://www.iqb.hu-berlin.de/en/schule/sekundarstufe-i/bildungstrend/2024 and in Sachse et al. (2025).

Mathematics Achievement.

Mathematics achievement was assessed using items from the IQB mathematics test, which covers five key content areas: Number, Measurement, Space and Shape, Functional Relationships, and Data and Chance. For the TOSCAneo study, some of the items from the IQB were slightly modified and a few items were newly developed by experts in mathematics education to better align with the most recent curricular requirements. The final test scores relied on 38 (complex) multiple-choice items. The WLE reliability amounted to .77 at T1 and T2 (Schnaufer et al., 2026a (Schnaufer et al., , 2026b)). Importantly, in all models, achievement outcomes were modeled using a single-indicator approach to account for measurement error (e.g., Hoyle, 2012;

see Statistical Analyses section for further details).

Science Achievement. Science achievement was assessed separately for each subject-biology, chemistry, and physics-using three distinct sets of test items, each targeting subject-specific content knowledge (Sachse et al., 2025). Owing to the study's multi-matrix design, each student completed between 33 and 44 items covering at least two domains. Estimated WLE reliabilities of the tests at T1 were .55 in biology, .59 in chemistry, and .58 in physics. At T2, they were .61 in biology, .64 in chemistry, and .63 in physics (Schnaufer et al., 2026a (Schnaufer et al., , 2026b)).

English Achievement. English achievement was assessed using a C-test developed and piloted by the Foreign Language Center at the University of Tübingen, consisting of four authentic texts on different topics drawn from various newspapers. The texts covered the following topics: proteins, airport security, climate change, and Bitcoin. In each of the Ctests, the second part of every second word was deleted (25 gaps per text), and test-takers were asked to restore the missing parts (e.g., "A rec[ ] study fo[ ] that" needed to be solved as A rec[ent] study fo[und] that). Correctly filled gaps were coded as 1 (correct), and all others as 0 (incorrect). The WLE reliability of the test was high at T1 (.87) and T2 (.87) (Schnaufer et al., 2026a (Schnaufer et al., , 2026b)).

German Achievement.

Students' achievement in German was assessed using a C-test from the IQB (Sachse et al., 2025). These tests were developed similarly to the C-test in English (see above) and consisted of 120 items administered across four texts. The WLE reliability of the test was high at T1 (.90) and T2 (.89) (Schnaufer et al., 2026a (Schnaufer et al., , 2026b)).

Self-Concept

Students' subject-specific self-concepts in mathematics, chemistry, biology, physics, English, and German were measured using items from Gaspard et al. (2016) that are based on the Self-Description Questionnaire III (Marsh & O'Neill, 1984). On four items (e.g., "I am good at [subject]"; " [Subject] is easy for me"), respondents were asked to rate their agreement on a 4-point scale ranging from 1 (does not apply at all) to 4 (completely applies).

Items were administered separately for each of the six domains. The scales showed high internal consistency across both waves and across all domains (.87 ≤ α ≤ .94).

Value Beliefs

Subject-specific value beliefs were assessed separately for each subject using four items from Gaspard et al. (2016). These items were developed based on Eccles (1983) and taken from a larger value belief questionnaire (Gaspard et al., 2015). The items focused on three different value aspects: intrinsic value, utility value, and attainment value. All items required responses on a 4-point rating scale from 1 (does not apply at all) to 4 (completely applies). The scales showed high internal consistency across both waves and across all domains (.84 ≤ α ≤ .87).

Effort

Subject-specific effort was assessed separately for each subject using four items from Gaspard et al. (2016) that were adapted based on a questionnaire used in Trautwein et al. (2009). All items were answered on a 4-point scale from 1 (does not apply at all) to 4 (completely applies). Example items include "I really work hard in [subject]" or "I do my best in [subject]." The scales showed high internal consistency across both waves and across all subjects (.87 ≤ α ≤ .92).

Statistical Analyses

We first compiled the data using the statistical software R Version 4.6.1 (R Core Team, 2026) and transferred the data to Mplus Version 9 (Muthén & Muthén, 2017) using the R package MplusAutomation (Hallquist & Wiley, 2018). The analysis followed two major steps: First, we examined the different items related to GenAI tool use for academic purposes and the changes between T1 and T2. This was done by estimating means of the latent response variable (y*) in an IRT framework for each of the seven GenAI items at T1 and T2 and comparing these means using the delta method implemented in the model constraint options in Mplus (Ver Hoef, 2012), cluster-robust standard errors, and survey weights (see Supplement S2: Mplus Code B). In addition, we estimated latent change score models for task-completion GenAI use (T) and learning-oriented GenAI use (L), with the T1 latent variance fixed to 1 so that the average latent change could be interpreted in SD units of T1 (see Supplement S2: Mplus Code C).

Second, considering the reciprocal associations between GenAI use and academic outcomes, we specified a cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) that included the six latent variables of interest at T1 (i.e., task-completion GenAI use, learning-oriented GenAI use, academic achievement, self-concept, value beliefs, and effort) and the same set of latent variables at T2 (see Supplement S2: Mplus Code D for exemplary code). In line with prior research, we tested for metric measurement invariance (e.g., Bardach et al., 2023). As can be seen in Supplement S3, the changes in fit indices between the models with and without longitudinal constraints were small and did not exceed the cutoffs recommended by Chen (2007) and Cheung and Rensvold (2002), who considered a decrease in CFI > .01 and an increase in RMSEA > .015 as indicating a substantial decrement in model fit. Based on these results, the assumption of metric invariance across time appears justified.

Overall, we specified six CLPMs, one for each of the six subjects (i.e., mathematics, chemistry, biology, physics, German, and English). For a conceptual illustration of the specified CLPMs, see Figure S4A in Supplement S4. In all cases, to control for measurement error, we either specified the latent variables using the respective indicators (i.e., for GenAI use, self-concept, value beliefs, and effort) or applied a single-indicator approach used in prior research (achievement; e.g., Hoyle, 2012; Hübner et al., 2022). The single-indicator approach accounts for measurement error by fixing the indicator's residual variance to the value of the weighted mean of the squared standard error of the WLE, allowing the latent variable to represent only the reliable portion of the observed score. Following standard practice in longitudinal structural equation modeling (SEM) with repeated measures, we allowed the residuals of matching items to correlate across measurement occasions (i.e., correlated uniquenesses) to avoid biased parameter estimates due to item-specific variance shared across time (e.g., Marsh et al., 2014 Marsh et al., , 2022)). Notably, to appropriately account for the categorical nature of the GenAI indicators (i.e., within an IRT framework), we used the recommended weighted least squares mean-and variance-adjusted (WLSMV) estimator in Mplus (Muthén & Muthén, 2017), which estimates thresholds and models the associations among categorical indicators using polychoric correlations while providing robust standard errors and a mean-and variance-adjusted chi-square test statistic.

Cluster Structure and Survey Weights

In the TOSCAneo study, students are nested within classes and schools, and it has been shown that students in the same class or school tend to be more similar to one another than to students from other classes or schools. When specifying the different models, we therefore estimated cluster-robust standard errors at the school level, as recent research suggests this approach adequately accounts for nested data (McNeish, 2026). In addition, a multistage sampling design was employed in TOSCAneo, in which schools were first randomly selected with probabilities proportional to their size (PPS), followed by the selection of one class per school in academic tracks and up to two classes per school in comprehensive or vocational tracks. All students in the selected classes were invited to participate. In line with the TOSCAneo study's particular focus on comprehensive and vocational tracks, these tracks were oversampled. Survey weights that were based on nonresponse-adjusted selection probabilities at the different sampling stages were provided by the IEA. We used these survey weights to account for unequal selection probabilities and obtain population-representative estimates for students attending public schools at the beginning of upper secondary school in Baden-Württemberg.

Missing Data

Missing data were handled using pairwise-present observations, as implemented with the WLSMV estimator in Mplus (Muthén & Muthén, 2017). The estimator derives thresholds and polychoric correlations using the available observations for each pair of indicators. For additional information on missing data, see Table 1.

Results

Preliminary Results

First, we more closely inspected the measurement models of GenAI use. A two-factor confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) model reflecting the distinction between task-completion and learning-oriented use, estimated with categorical indicators (i.e., an IRT parameterization; de Ayala, 2009; Embretson & Reise, 2000), fit significantly better than a one-factor model, as indicated by the WLSMV-adjusted chi-square difference test, Δχ²[6] = 267.51, p < .001. The correlations between the task-completion and learning-oriented factors were high and positive at T1, r = .86, 95% CI [.83, .89], and T2, r = .81, 95% CI [.77, .85].

Both factors also showed positive cross-time stability, with correlations from T1 to T2 of r = .62, 95% CI [.55, .69], for the task-completion factor and r = .51, 95% CI [.43, .59], for the learning-oriented factor.

The two-dimensional longitudinal CFA model with categorical indicators and metric invariance, which is required when the goal is to meaningfully compare factors over time in CLPMs (e.g., Meredith, 1993; Mulder & Hamaker, 2021), achieved very good fit (χ²[71] = 283.53, p < .001, CFI = .98, TLI = .98, RMSEA = .03, SRMR = .03) (Hu & Bentler, 1998 , 1999; Marsh et al., 2004). For estimating mean changes in single items and in the learningoriented and task-completion factors (see Statistical Analyses section), threshold invariance was additionally required. We tested this assumption using the adjusted chi-square difference test for nested models estimated with the WLSMV estimator in Mplus. At the item level, six of the seven items showed full threshold invariance across time. For one item, partial threshold invariance was achieved by freeing one of its four cross-time threshold equality constraints. The resulting model showed very good fit, χ²[13] = 15.67, p = .268, CFI = 1.00, TLI = 1.00, RMSEA = .01, SRMR = .01. For the latent change score model of the learningoriented factor, three of 12 threshold equality constraints had to be freed to achieve partial measurement invariance, resulting in very good model fit, χ²[13] = 15.88, p = .256, CFI = 1.00, TLI = 1.00, RMSEA = .01, SRMR = .01. For the task-completion factor, four of 16 threshold equality constraints had to be freed to achieve partial measurement invariance, again resulting in very good model fit, χ²[24] = 31.47, p = .141, CFI = 1.00, TLI = 1.00, RMSEA = .01, SRMR = .01.

Next, we examined the frequencies of students' reported GenAI tool use in school for different academic purposes. Descriptive results are based on analyses incorporating survey weights and shown in Table 1. Unweighted sample characteristics and descriptive statistics are shown in Supplement S1. Correlations among study variables are shown in Supplement S5. As visible in Figure 1, at T1, GenAI use was highest for translating texts (50%), preparing presentations and essays (49%), and doing homework (49%). At T2, GenAI use was highest for preparing presentations and essays (69%), preparing for exams or tests (65%), and doing homework (62%). At T1, the percentage of never-users was highest for solving tasks during class (69%). At T2, the percentage of never-users was highest for further learning (56%).

Overall, about 5% of the students could be identified as "never-users" across both waves, reflecting that they reported never using GenAI at T1 and T2.

We then examined changes in use frequency and behavior between the two measurement time points. As shown in Figure 2, there were substantial increases in reported GenAI use across all seven purposes from the beginning to the end of the school year. The effect sizes ranged from d = 0.21 for translating texts to d = 0.65 for solving tasks during class (Cohen, 1988). We additionally specified latent change score models for taskcompletion GenAI use and learning-oriented GenAI use. The estimated standardized latent mean change was d = 0.35 for task-completion and d = 0.33 for learning-oriented use. All of these changes were statistically significant (p < .001).

Reciprocal Relations Between Types of GenAI Tool Use and Academic Achievement, Self-Concept, Value Beliefs, and Effort

We next examined reciprocal relations between types of GenAI tool use and academic achievement, self-concept, value beliefs, and effort. The results are presented in Table 2 and Table 3 as well as Figure 3. First, we inspected model fit statistics for the CLPMs of each subject. As is visible from Supplement S3, all models showed excellent fit. All values were in line with established criteria for evaluating model fit (Hu & Bentler, 1998 , 1999; Marsh et al., 2004).

Reciprocal Relations Between GenAI Use and Achievement

We examined reciprocal relations between GenAI use and achievement (see Table 2, Table 3, and Figure 3). Considering the predictive effects of prior GenAI use on subsequent achievement, we did not find any statistically significant association in any subject.

Considering the predictive effects of prior achievement on subsequent GenAI use, we found evidence that achievement at the beginning of the school year was negatively associated with GenAI use at the end of the school year, after controlling for GenAI use and all other variables at the beginning of the school year (-.28 ≤ β ≤ -.12, all ps < .05). For mathematics, science subjects, and German, this was true for both learning-oriented and task-completion GenAI use. In English, there was only a statistically significant association with taskcompletion GenAI use, but not with learning-oriented GenAI use. These findings suggest that students with lower achievement at the beginning of the school year reported using GenAI more often by the end of the school year for learning-oriented and task-completion purposes, after controlling for all other variables at T1.

Reciprocal Relations Between GenAI Use and Motivation

Considering reciprocal associations between prior GenAI use and later self-concept, we found only one positive association, namely prior task-completion GenAI use predicting later self-concept in mathematics (β = .17, p = .013). In addition, we found a positive association between prior self-concept and later task-completion GenAI use in chemistry (β = .15, p = .006), but no statistically significant associations in any of the other subjects.

When examining associations between prior GenAI use and value beliefs, we found a negative association between task-completion GenAI use and later value beliefs (β = -.21, p = .049) and a positive association between learning-oriented GenAI use and later value beliefs (β = .22, p = .020) in physics. The difference between these two coefficients was statistically significant (p = .035), implying that students with higher learning-oriented GenAI use at T1 reported higher value beliefs at T2, whereas students with higher task-completion use reported lower value beliefs at T2.

When examining the predictive effects of prior value beliefs on GenAI use, we found that prior value beliefs in mathematics were positively associated with subsequent learningoriented GenAI use (β = .12, p = .049), whereas there was no statistically significant association with task-completion GenAI use. In addition, negative associations were found between prior value beliefs in chemistry and both subsequent task-completion (β = -.17, p = .006) and learning-oriented (β = -.16, p = .008) GenAI use, as well as with task-completion GenAI use in biology (β = -.17, p = .021). These findings suggest that students with lower value beliefs at the beginning of the school year tended to use GenAI more often in chemistry and biology by the end of the school year. For German and English, we did not find any statistically significant associations between prior value beliefs and subsequent GenAI use after controlling for the other variables in the model at T1.

Reciprocal Relations Between GenAI Use and Effort

Finally, we examined reciprocal relations between GenAI use and effort. Regarding paths from prior GenAI use to later effort, we found a negative association for taskcompletion GenAI use in mathematics (β = -.24, p = .002), in chemistry (β = -.25, p = .003), in physics (β = -.42, p < .001), and in English (β = -.25, p = .008), whereas there was a positive association between prior learning-oriented GenAI use and subsequent effort in mathematics (β = .21, p = .011), chemistry (β = .19, p = .023), and physics (β = .39, p < .001).

This suggests that students whose GenAI use at the beginning of the school year leaned toward task completion rather than learning reported investing less effort in the respective subject by the end of the school year, after controlling for prior effort and the other variables in the model. The difference between the two coefficients (task-completion vs. learningoriented) was statistically significant in mathematics, chemistry, physics, and English (all ps < .05), but not in biology or German. Considering the reverse paths, we found negative associations between prior effort and subsequent task-completion GenAI use in physics (β = -.13, p = .015) and German (β = -.34, p < .001), suggesting that students who exerted less effort at T1 reported using GenAI more often for task-completion purposes at T2.

Discussion

Summary of Results

The present study examined reciprocal relationships between students' self-directed task-completion and learning-oriented GenAI use and the development of academic achievement, motivation, and effort across one school year. Two main patterns emerged: First, a GenAI use profile weighted toward task completion rather than learning predicted lower subsequent effort. The difference between the task-completion and learning-oriented coefficients was statistically significant in mathematics, chemistry, physics, and English.

Interestingly, with regard to the direction of the coefficients, a similar pattern was observed for value beliefs. However, this pattern was statistically significant only in physics. In contrast, we found little evidence that either form of GenAI use systematically predicted subsequent academic achievement or self-concept.

Second, in the reverse direction, prior achievement consistently predicted subsequent GenAI use, with higher-achieving students reporting less frequent task-completion and learning-oriented use and lower-achieving students reporting more frequent task-completion and learning-oriented use. Associations of prior motivational beliefs and effort with subsequent GenAI use were comparatively small and inconsistent. Together, these findings provide a nuanced picture of the reciprocal relationships between students' GenAI use and their academic development over the course of the school year.

How GenAI Use Predicts Academic Outcomes

For academic effort, in line with expectations, we found that students reporting more task-completion GenAI use showed lower effort in the respective subject by the end of the school year, whereas students reporting more learning-oriented GenAI use showed higher subsequent effort. These findings are consistent with the assumption that the educational consequences of GenAI use may depend less on whether students use GenAI and more on the purpose for which GenAI is used during academic activities (e.g., Y. Chen et al., 2026; Giannakos et al., 2025; Han et al., 2025; Panadero & Broadbent, 2025; Weidlich et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2024).

From a theoretical perspective, one possible explanation for this pattern concerns differences in learners' cognitive engagement when interacting with GenAI. Task-completion forms of GenAI use may reduce the need for learners to engage in retrieval, elaboration, or problem solving by shifting parts of the cognitive work to the GenAI system (Risko & Gilbert, 2016). In contrast, learning-oriented uses may support active processing when GenAI is used to obtain explanations or feedback or to facilitate reflection and elaboration (Chi & Wylie, 2014; Fiorella & Mayer, 2015). The observed differences in subsequent effort are therefore consistent with theoretical perspectives emphasizing that technologies can either reduce or support active engagement depending on how learners interact with them. More broadly, these findings illustrate why treating GenAI use as a unitary construct may obscure meaningful differences in students' interactions with these systems. This interpretation should take into account the strong correlation between the two GenAI factors (r = .86 at T1 and .81 at T2), which exceeds the stability of either factor across the school year (.62 and .51). Because the two forms of GenAI use were strongly correlated and entered simultaneously, their coefficients reflect associations with later effort conditional on the other form of use. The similar-magnitude, opposite-sign coefficients therefore suggest that differences in the relative composition of GenAI use may have been more relevant to later effort than students' overall level of use. This interpretation is also broadly consistent with the achievement results: both forms of GenAI use were predicted by prior achievement in the same direction, with overlapping confidence intervals, suggesting that lower-achieving students tended to report greater use of both forms rather than a clearly different balance between them. Whether the two forms of use would show the same pattern if assessed with items designed to distinguish them more sharply remains an open question.

At the same time, the present data cannot establish the cognitive processes underlying these associations. Task-completion and learning-oriented GenAI use may not only contribute to differences in cognitive engagement but may themselves reflect students' existing tendencies to engage with academic tasks. Students who seek to minimize cognitive effort may be more likely to adopt task-completion forms of GenAI use, whereas students who seek to engage more deeply with learning materials may preferentially use GenAI in learning-oriented ways. Although controlling for prior academic effort partly accounts for such preexisting differences, the present study did not directly measure cognitive engagement during GenAI use. Future research combining longitudinal designs such as this study with process measures of students' interactions with GenAI (e.g., Y. Chen et al., 2026; Pi et al., 2026) will therefore be important for distinguishing these mechanisms. Emerging theoretical frameworks such as the SYNC model (Fan et al., 2026) may be generative for this kind of empirical work.

Further, the relatively clear pattern observed for academic effort did not extend to the other academic outcomes considered (besides value beliefs in physics). For instance, we found no significant predictive associations of GenAI use with academic achievement across subjects and little systematic evidence that GenAI use predicted changes in students' selfconcept or value beliefs. This pattern is noteworthy given concerns that particularly taskcompletion GenAI use may reduce opportunities for active cognitive engagement and learning and, over time, contribute to deskilling (e.g., Fan et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2025). Several explanations for this pattern are possible. First, changes in effort may precede detectable changes in broader motivational beliefs or academic competencies. If repeated task-completion GenAI use reduces students' investment in learning activities, its consequences for skill development may accumulate gradually and become detectable only over periods longer than the single school year examined here (see the principle of cumulative continuity; Caspi et al., 2005). Second, effects may depend strongly on the particular tasks for which GenAI is used and, especially, on whether GenAI replaces cognitive processes that are necessary for the respective learning goal. Such task-specific effects may be difficult to detect using broad self-report measures of GenAI use and subjectlevel measures of academic outcomes.

At the same time, these explanations should be considered alongside the possibility that the detrimental consequences of students' self-directed GenAI use for broader academic outcomes are smaller and less pervasive than is sometimes assumed. Much of the concern about cognitive offloading and deskilling is based on studies examining relatively proximal learning outcomes or experimentally induced forms of GenAI use under specific task conditions (e.g., Kosmyna et al., 2025). In naturally occurring academic contexts, students may use GenAI more selectively and flexibly, such that potentially detrimental and beneficial instances of use coexist and may partly offset one another over time. Thus, although the present findings do not rule out negative consequences of GenAI use, they provide little evidence that students' GenAI use was associated with systematic declines in broader academic competencies or motivational beliefs over the period studied. Longer-term studies with more fine-grained measures of when, why, and how students use GenAI are needed to determine whether detrimental effects accumulate over time or remain primarily task-and context-specific.

How Academic Outcomes Predict GenAI Use

Turning to the reverse direction, prior academic achievement consistently predicted subsequent GenAI use across subjects, with initially higher-achieving students reporting less frequent GenAI use than lower-achieving students. Importantly, this pattern emerged for both task-completion and learning-oriented GenAI use. This finding only partly aligns with our hypotheses. We had expected lower-achieving students to engage more frequently in taskcompletion GenAI use. We had also expected higher-achieving students to engage more frequently in learning-oriented use. Instead, higher achievement was associated with less frequent use of GenAI for both purposes.

One possible interpretation is that prior achievement primarily predicts students' overall need or propensity to seek GenAI assistance rather than the specific purposes for which they use it. Lower-achieving students may experience greater difficulty completing academic tasks independently. They may therefore have more reasons to turn to GenAI, both to facilitate task completion and to obtain explanations or other forms of learning support (potentially as a result of lower self-regulatory competencies; e.g., Daumiller & Dresel, 2019; Fan et al., 2026; Tankelevitch et al., 2024). Conversely, higher-achieving students may need less external assistance because they can more readily complete academic tasks using their existing knowledge and (self-regulatory) skills. From this perspective, achievement may be relevant primarily to whether and how often students draw on GenAI as an academic resource, rather than to whether they use it to complete tasks or for learning. This pattern is particularly relevant from an educational equity perspective. Rather than demonstrating that GenAI necessarily exacerbates existing achievement differences, our findings indicate that GenAI may become a disproportionately important component of the learning environment of lower-achieving students. This greater reliance could potentially be beneficial if GenAI provides explanations, scaffolding, or other forms of support that help students overcome difficulties (e.g., Fan et al., 2026). Conversely, it could reinforce existing differences if GenAI is used in ways that reduce engagement in learning-relevant cognitive processes or if learners use AI differently depending on their misconceptions about AI, which have been shown to vary according to cognitive and motivational variables (Jaggy et al., 2026). Importantly, the present study cannot directly establish whether differential GenAI use led to widening achievement gaps over the period studied, but neither task-completion nor learning-oriented GenAI use was a statistically significant predictor of subsequent achievement across subjects. At the same time, the finding that task-completion GenAI use predicted lower subsequent academic effort may point to a potentially relevant longer-term pathway (e.g., Fan et al., 2026). Given that lower-achieving students also reported greater subsequent task-completion GenAI use, greater reliance on GenAI for task completion could contribute to differences in effort investment that may accumulate over longer periods (Caspi et al., 2005), although the present study cannot establish such a process. Thus, our findings identify differential uptake of GenAI according to prior achievement and a potential effortrelated pathway through which such differences could become educationally relevant. Future research should examine whether greater GenAI reliance among lower-achieving students ultimately reduces, maintains, or exacerbates existing educational inequalities over longer periods.

In contrast to academic achievement, associations of students' academic self-concept, value beliefs, and prior effort with subsequent GenAI use were small and inconsistent across subjects. Thus, our findings provide little evidence that established subject-specific motivational beliefs systematically translate into different patterns of subsequent GenAI use, at least as captured by the relatively broad measures employed in the present study. Given the absence of a consistent pattern across motivational constructs and subjects, we refrain from interpreting individual associations, which may reflect construct-or subject-specific variation rather than robust relationships between academic motivation and subsequent GenAI use.

Whether motivation becomes more relevant when GenAI use is assessed at the level of specific learning situations or decisions remains an important question for future research.

Theoretical and Practical Implications

Although significant associations emerged primarily between prior GenAI use and later academic effort as well as between prior achievement and later GenAI use, we believe these findings have important educational implications. Changes in academic effort may represent an earlier emerging process-level indicator that precedes detectable changes in academic achievement, particularly in older secondary school students whose competencies and motivational tendencies are already relatively established and who have already selected their learning environments accordingly (for example, course choices in upper secondary education; e.g., Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). Reduced cognitive and behavioral investment during learning activities may accumulate gradually over longer periods of time before translating into measurable achievement differences. This possibility may be especially relevant for younger learners who are still developing foundational competencies, learning strategies, and motivational tendencies (e.g., Eccles & Wigfield, 2000; Wigfield et al., 2021).

These findings also have implications for how productive GenAI use is conceptualized and for the consequences of GenAI use, which may depend less on whether students use GenAI and more on the purposes for which these tools are used (e.g., Y. Chen et al., 2026; Giannakos et al., 2025; Han et al., 2025; Panadero & Broadbent, 2025; Weidlich et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2024). Although task-completion GenAI use was associated with lower subsequent effort in the present study, task-completion use should not necessarily be considered maladaptive in all learning situations. Cognitive offloading can be adaptive when it frees cognitive resources for processes that are more relevant to the intended learning goal (Risko & Gilbert, 2016; Sweller, 2011). Thus, the distinction between task-completion and learning-oriented use examined here may represent a useful but necessarily broad approximation of educationally relevant differences in GenAI use. Ultimately, the consequences of GenAI use may depend more specifically on whether GenAI replaces cognitive activity that is necessary for the respective learning goal (e.g., Fan et al., 2026).

Future research should therefore move toward more fine-grained conceptualizations of GenAI use that capture not only students' general purposes for using GenAI but also what cognitive work is delegated and in which learning situations.

From a practical perspective, the findings suggest that educational guidance should focus not only on whether students use GenAI, but also on how they engage with these systems (e.g., Fan et al., 2026). The associations with academic effort indicate that encouraging students to use GenAI in ways that support their own engagement with learning materials may be particularly important. This does not necessarily imply discouraging all forms of task-completion use. Rather, students may benefit from support in recognizing when GenAI facilitates learning and when it functions primarily as a substitute for effort and engagement that would otherwise contribute to learning. Developing such metacognitive judgments may become an important component of GenAI literacy in the educational context (see OECD & European Commission, 2026).

Such metacognitive support may be particularly relevant for lower-achieving students.

Across subjects, these students subsequently reported more frequent task-completion as well as learning-oriented GenAI use, suggesting that GenAI may play a comparatively larger role in their academic activities. Lower achievement at T1 predicted higher GenAI use at T2 (after controlling for prior GenAI use at T1), which may indicate that students experiencing greater academic difficulties are more likely to turn to GenAI tools, potentially as a compensatory strategy. This interpretation aligns with prior work suggesting that students facing high academic demands and pressure (Abbas et al., 2024), as well as those with more executive functioning challenges (Klarin et al., 2024), may turn to GenAI tools to cope with these challenges. Our findings further align with prior experimental work (e.g., Stadler et al., 2024), which showed that GenAI tool use aimed at reducing cognitive load was associated with lower cognitive engagement during learning activities. Extending this work, the present findings suggest that these patterns may also persist over time in authentic educational contexts.

Although greater task-completion use was not significantly associated with lower subsequent achievement in the present study, its higher prevalence among lower-achieving students and its negative association with subsequent effort warrant further investigation. These findings do not justify recommending that lower-achieving students use GenAI less. Instead, they suggest exploring how educational support can help especially lower-achieving students use GenAI in ways that sustain productive engagement with academic tasks, given their comparatively more frequent interactions with GenAI.

Our longitudinal data also document the rapid emergence of GenAI use among secondary school students, highlighting the increasing educational relevance of the topic in line with descriptive findings from other countries showing, for example, that more than half of U.S. teenagers have used GenAI for schoolwork (McClain et al., 2026). In this sample, substantial increases in students' GenAI use emerged even within the relatively short time frame of a single school year, underlining how quickly GenAI tools are becoming integrated into students' academic practices. This pattern aligns with recent reports suggesting that GenAI has already been adopted by a large proportion of secondary school and university students internationally (Unterweger et al., 2026).

Taken together, these findings empirically support educational discussions surrounding GenAI in moving beyond binary questions of whether students should use GenAI tools and instead focusing more strongly on how these systems are used during learning (Giannakos et al., 2025; Panadero & Broadbent, 2025; Xiao et al., 2026; Yan et al., 2024). Educational guidance and GenAI literacy efforts may therefore need to emphasize forms of GenAI use that encourage explanation, reflection, revision, and active problem solving rather than uses primarily aimed at minimizing effort or rapidly completing tasks.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of the present study: First, the measures assessing students' Gen AI use were not subject-specific, whereas academic achievement, motivation, and effort were assessed separately across school subjects. Thus, although it could be argued that if students use GenAI tools, they may use them similarly across multiple academic subjects, we were not able to formally account for differential GenAI use patterns across subjects. For example, students may rely on GenAI differently in subjects in which they perceive themselves as less competent or experience lower intrinsic interest (e.g., Jansen et al., 2025). To partially address this issue, we conducted all analyses separately for each subject and controlled for the respective T1 measures. This approach ensures that the associations (i.e., between prior GenAI use and later academic outcomes as well as between prior academic outcomes and later GenAI use) are estimated by comparing students who are similar on the other variables at baseline. Nevertheless, the present study cannot fully determine whether the observed associations differ systematically across subject-specific contexts.

Second, the measurement and conceptualization of task-completion and learningoriented GenAI use are rather broad. Moreover, the two factors were strongly correlated within each time point, so the divergent effort findings rest on comparatively limited variation distinguishing the two forms of use and should be replicated with items designed to separate the two purposes more clearly. Although distinguishing between these two purposes provides a useful first approximation of how students engage with GenAI, the same form of GenAI use may have different implications depending on the specific task and learning context. In particular, task-completion GenAI use should not necessarily be understood as maladaptive or detrimental to learning. Offloading a specific subtask to GenAI may sometimes allow learners to allocate their cognitive resources more effectively to aspects of a task that are more relevant to the intended learning goal.

Third, although the theoretical framework of the present study centers on differences in active cognitive engagement with academic tasks, cognitive engagement itself was not measured directly. Instead, the study distinguished forms of GenAI use theorized to reflect differing levels of cognitive engagement during learning activities. Although this approach is grounded in educational psychology theory and consistent with emerging empirical findings on GenAI-supported cognitive offloading, the present data cannot directly verify the underlying cognitive processes through which these associations emerge. Future research should therefore assess GenAI use at a more fine-grained, situational level, capturing which strategies students employ for which components of a task and under what circumstances, including examinations of the role of cognitive engagement in these contexts. Such approaches could move beyond a general distinction between task-completion and learningoriented use toward identifying when and for which learning goals particular forms of GenAI use constitute adaptive and strategically appropriate tool use.

Finally, although the longitudinal design strengthens the interpretation of temporal associations, the assumptions required for causal interpretations (Angrist & Pischke, 2009) are strong and may not fully hold in practice. In addition, with two measurement occasions, the CLPM cannot separate stable between-person differences from within-person change (e.g., Hamaker et al., 2015). The cross-lagged coefficients therefore likely reflect a mixture of within-and between-person differences (e.g., Hübner et al., 2023). One particular advantage of our cross-lagged panel design is that longitudinal information is available for all variables whose associations we examined: we thus modeled associations between GenAI use at T1 and academic outcomes at T2 while controlling for the academic outcomes at T1. Controlling for prior measures of the outcome has been argued to strengthen causal inference from longitudinal observational data (VanderWeele et al., 2020).

Conclusion

GenAI is rapidly becoming part of students' everyday learning environments, yet the antecedents and consequences of secondary school students' self-directed GenAI use remain poorly understood. Across one school year, task-completion GenAI use tended to predict lower subsequent effort, whereas learning-oriented use showed more positive associations with subsequent effort. At the same time, lower-achieving students reported more frequent subsequent GenAI use across subjects, whereas prior GenAI use was not significantly associated with later achievement. Rather than being uniformly beneficial or harmful, the consequences of GenAI use likely depend on when, why, and how students use it.

Educational guidance should therefore move beyond simply promoting or restricting GenAI use and instead help students use it strategically to support sustained effort. Identifying how these patterns develop, and for whom they are beneficial or detrimental, will be important for supporting learning in increasingly AI-rich educational environments. Our findings suggest that lower-achieving students in particular, given their comparatively more frequent use, might benefit from interventions supporting GenAI use in ways that sustain productive engagement with academic tasks.
