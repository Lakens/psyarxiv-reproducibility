How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). Abstract

 Tracking children's vocabulary development in the language of instruction is of interest when classrooms are multilingual.

 Yet, few tests of receptive vocabulary have been specifically validated to function fairly over developmental time.

 A 50-item receptive vocabulary test drawn from local picture-and story books was validated.

 Items showed stable properties across three time points in one academic year.

The study provides a transferable template for fair tracking of vocabulary development in other similarly multilingual early childhood settings.

1. Introduction

A strong oral language foundation is arguably the bedrock for learning in school. A growing cross-national and cross-linguistic evidence base shows oral language to be an essential precursor to reading comprehension, with theoretical accounts converging on how language skills support comprehension processes (Perfetti et al., 2023; Snowling & Hulme, 2025).

However, despite its critical role, there is limited tracking of children's developing language skills globally. One reason for this imbalance may be the greater attention to, and investment in, tracking code-focused precursors to reading, such as reading fluency, compared to oral language skills, including vocabulary (Nag, 2016). The resulting neglect is problematic because, attainments in reading comprehension remain persistently low across multiple countries (PAL Network, 2025; see also Nakamura et al., 2023), compromising progress towards learning goals (e.g., United Nations, 2015 , 2025). A more sustained focus is needed on oral language assessments, their psychometrics, and approaches that allow meaningful comparison across languages and over developmental time.

Vocabulary knowledge offers a productive entry point for such work. It accounts for individual differences in reading comprehension (Nation, 2018; Wagner & Meros, 2010) and is closely associated with broader oral language development (Frank et al., 2021; Giazitzidou et al., 2025; Massonnié et al., 2022), while also lending itself to efficient and reliable measurement.

Yet vocabulary assessment raises difficulties that are still underexamined: the psychometric stability of items over developmental time has received less scrutiny than their stability across demographic or linguistic groups (AERA et al., 2014; Embretson & Reise, 2000), and in addition, items adapted from established instruments may function differently across languages and across the exposure profiles that characterize multilingual development (Bialystok, 2017; a Rasch-based validation of a contextually-situated Hindi receptive vocabulary test administered in a multilingual district in north India, considering item-level behavior across demographic, contextual, and developmental dimensions.

1.1. Vocabulary Knowledge

Children's word knowledge encompasses three interrelated components: vocabulary breadth, which refers to the number of words in the lexicon; vocabulary depth, which refers to the layers of meaning associated with those words; and morphological awareness, which refers to an understanding that words may be composed of smaller meaningful units. Two bodies of evidence make a particularly strong case for greater investment in the assessment of these components.

The first concerns the relationship with early reading: attainments in vocabulary breadth and depth account for individual differences in reading comprehension, either directly or indirectly through their association with morphological awareness (e.g., Giazitzidou et al., 2025; Nation, 2013), so that tracking these component skills carries clear instructional relevance for both language and literacy development. The second concerns the place of vocabulary within the broader oral language system. Statistical modeling of relationships among component language skills shows that vocabulary knowledge is closely associated with the development of listening comprehension, grammatical knowledge, and narrative expression (Frank et al., 2021; Massonnié et al., 2022), such that assessing vocabulary provides insight into the developing language system as a whole. Taken together, these findings position vocabulary as both a core component of language and a practical point of focus for assessment, particularly when the aim is to capture variation in the language of instruction.

1.2. Tests of Vocabulary Knowledge

Tests of word knowledge may tap either receptive vocabulary, indexed by what is understood when hearing a word, or expressive vocabulary, indexed by what is produced in spoken or written form (Nation, 2013). Picture-based tasks are typical of the former, while definition tasks are typical of the latter. In a picture-based receptive task, the child is presented with a small selection of images and indicates the picture that best matches a word spoken by the assessor. Because the response demand is restricted to picture pointing, the task accesses the child's language system without taxing speech, reading or writing, a feature particularly advantageous in contexts where children have had limited opportunities for verbal expression or are shy. Such formats are also straightforward to administer, making them especially suitable for multilingual contexts in which expressive demands and proficiency could vary considerably. In addition, picture-pointing tests yield more reliable scoring than expressive tests (Dunn, 2019).

1.2.1. Picture Tests for Receptive Vocabulary.

The seminal exemplar of receptive vocabulary assessment is the Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test (PPVT; Dunn, 1959), developed for Standard American English and subsequently adapted across multiple languages and contexts, with the current version organized by parts of speech and levels of lexical difficulty (Dunn, 2019). A common approach to extending the PPVT to new linguistic contexts is direct translation, but words that appear equivalent across languages may differ in frequency, familiarity, or semantic range, leading to imprecision in ability estimation and reduced comparability across contexts; cross-national adaptations have shown substantial proportions of items with poor fit in translated versions (Leon & Singh, 2017).

Alternative approaches to item development include common conceptual cores such as the Swadesh lists, the THINGS+ dataset, and the LITMUS-CLT lists of picturable nouns and verbs (Haman et al., 2015; Stoinski et al., 2024; Swadesh, 1971), age-of-acquisition norms (Dulay et al., 2026; Łuniewska et al., 2016), and tailor-made lists grounded in local language use. A twenty-year review identified 63 vocabulary assessments, of which 50 used picture-based formats across a wide range of language families, drawing variably on translated, common-core, and bespoke, contextually situated word lists (Nag, 2016). More recently, Rasch modeling has been used to develop new receptive vocabulary tests for multilingual primary school populations, demonstrating the value of iterative psychometric validation in achieving appropriate itemperson targeting (Morea et al., 2024). Together, these approaches highlight a central tension between cross-linguistic comparability and sensitivity to local language use-a tension particularly pronounced in multilingual contexts, where differences in exposure and language structure may influence how items function (e.g., Hoff et al., 2012).

1.3. Test and Item Performance

Establishing that a vocabulary test is fit for purpose requires evidence across several psychometric dimensions: that items span the ability range of the target population, that the test yields reliable ability estimates, and that individual items behave as expected under a measurement model. Recent Rasch-based validations have demonstrated that these standards are achievable for oral language instruments designed for young children. For English, Hulme et al. (2024) reported excellent reliability, good model fit, and well-calibrated item difficulty, and for French, German, and Spanish, Morea et al. (2024) showed that iterative Rasch validation can achieve appropriate item-person targeting.

Few tests of receptive vocabulary, however, have been validated to function fairly over developmental time, a requirement implicit in efforts to track learning. In addition, in multilingual settings where children bring diverse home languages and varying degrees of exposure to the language of assessment, the requirement for bias evaluation becomes especially pronounced. Items that systematically favor one group over another compromise validity (AERA et al., 2014; Camilli, 2016), and within an item response theory framework this concern is formalized as measurement invariance-the requirement that item difficulty remain stable across groups and over time, so that differences in observed performance reflect differences in underlying ability rather than artefacts of measurement (Chen et al., 2025; Embretson & Reise, 2000; Rasch, 1980). Three dimensions of invariance are particularly relevant to this paper.

First, sex differences in language performance are well documented in the early childhood years. Girls tend to outperform boys on vocabulary measures from infancy through the school years (Eriksson et al., 2012; Petersen, 2018), and at the item level, knowledge of particular words may differ by sex as a consequence of differential socialization and activity contexts (Coffey et al., 2025; Kachergis et al., 2023). If individual items systematically favor one sex, the test may yield biased ability estimates even when overall scores appear comparable.

However, sex differences go beyond language attainments and the direction of any bias is not fixed: globally, girls are more often disadvantaged in early-grade attainments, but opportunity structures in some contexts may produce the reverse pattern with more boys with lower reading attainments (Nag et al., 2014; UNESCO, 2025).

Second, when the language of assessment is not the child's home language, item functioning may be influenced by the degree of structural similarity between the two languages.

Cognate relationships, phonological overlap, and shared morphological patterns can facilitate performance for some language groups but not others (Bosma et al., 2019; Goriot et al., 2021), and direct translations of established instruments frequently produce substantial proportions of misfitting items (Leon & Singh, 2017). Beyond individual language profiles, ambient exposure to the target language represents a further source of variation. Traditional indices focus on parental language use and neighborhood exposure (DeAnda et al., 2016; Lindgren & Muñoz, 2013), but child-peer interactions are an understudied yet potentially influential source of language input. Available evidence suggests that peer exposure contributes to both receptive and expressive vocabulary development (Hürlimann, Lüdtke & Schmerse, 2026; Gámez et al., 2019), making it a relevant variable for evaluating measurement invariance.

Third, a requirement for any assessment intended to track development over time is that item parameters remain stable across assessment points-that is, the relative difficulty of items should not shift as children acquire new vocabulary (Millsap, 2010 (Millsap, , 2011)). If items show differential functioning across time, the meaning of change scores becomes ambiguous: an apparent gain may partly reflect changes in how items function rather than genuine growth in ability. Despite the importance of this requirement, temporal measurement invariance has received considerably less empirical attention than cross-group invariance in the vocabulary assessment literature, and we are not aware of any study that has explicitly examined item parameter stability over developmental time in a receptive vocabulary test for young multilingual children. A further psychometric consideration specific to picture-based vocabulary tests is the multiple-choice format itself. When items present four response options, children who lack knowledge of the target word have a non-trivial probability of selecting the correct picture by chance. Within item response theory (IRT), the three-parameter logistic (3PL) model explicitly accommodates guessing through a lower-asymptote parameter, but its estimation demands large samples per item and yields parameters that do not lend themselves to straightforward applied interpretation. For assessments intended to inform group-level research and tracking applications through total scores-the potential use case in child language research and educational settingsthe parsimony of the Rasch model offers a pragmatic advantage: a single difficulty parameter per item and direct interpretability of item-person targeting. The trade-off is that violations of model assumptions, including guessing, are not absorbed into the model but instead surface as misfit. A diagnostic strategy that pairs Rasch modeling with post hoc examination of item characteristic curves and multinomial distractor analysis can identify items affected by guessing or distractor interference, yielding actionable guidance for item revision without sacrificing the interpretive simplicity that applied use requires (Andrich et al., 2016; Shin et al., 2019; Thissen et al., 1989).

1.4. The Present Study

We examine how well a contextually situated test for receptive vocabulary captures variation in the language of instruction within a multilingual setting. Specifically, we evaluate the Picture Test for Receptive Vocabulary (TalkTogether -Hindi; PTRV[TT-Hin]), a subtest of the Sanjhe Bol Child Language and Foundation Learning Assessment Battery, designed for children aged 4 to 7 years. The assessment is administered in a multilingual district in north India, providing an opportunity to examine test performance under conditions of substantial linguistic diversity.

Using a Rasch modeling framework, we address four research questions: (1) To what extent does the PTRV(TT-Hin) yield reliable ability estimates and provide adequate coverage of the proficiency range represented in a linguistically diverse sample?

(2) Do individual items conform to the expectations of the Rasch model, and what patterns characterize items whose empirical response functions deviate from model expectations?

(3) Is item functioning invariant across sex, home language background, and level of peer exposure to Hindi?

(4) Are item parameters stable across three assessment points spanning one academic year, as required for the valid tracking of vocabulary development over time?

2. Method

>Method

2.1. Participants

The study was conducted in the final year of pre-school and Grade 1 in Hindi-medium public schools (locally called Balvatika and government schools) in Sirsa district in north India.

Children aged four to nine years (age at Time 1: M = 71.15 months, SD = 15.56; Mdn = 68 months) from 40 schools completed the test on three occasions at four-month intervals across one academic year (2023-2024). Recruitment was with permission of the government department for school education. Forty schools were randomly selected from the list of all schools managed by the government that already had seven or more children in target classrooms at the start of the academic year. Sample sizes were 756 at Time 1, 841 at Time 2, and 352 at Time 3. The variation in sample size across assessment points reflects continued school enrolment between Time 1 and Time 2, and reduced sample size at Time 3 to accommodate upcoming school closures. The wide age range reflects an admission policy of first enrolment of out-of-school and newly arrived children to Grade 1.

The analytic sample for this paper comprised 1,949 test performances on the PTRV(TT-Hin). Girls constituted 47.22%, 46.97%, and 48.58% of the sample at Times 1, 2, and 3, respectively. Approximately one-third (32.6%) of participating children reported speaking two or more languages at home. Hindi was one of the home languages for 69.18%, 51.72%, and 59.37% of children at Times 1, 2, and 3, respectively. For children without Hindi in the home, languages spoken included Haryanvi, Bihari, Punjabi, and Wagdi-ordered here from greater to lesser linguistic proximity to Standard Hindi. Some schools enrolled a higher proportion of Hindispeaking children, resulting in differing levels of Hindi exposure through peer interactions inside and outside the classroom. At Times 1, 2, and 3, peer exposure to Hindi was high for 57.80%, 57.91%, and 63.35% of children, respectively.

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Education Departmental Research Ethics Committee (DREC), University of Oxford (reference number EDUC_C1A_23_233), and

The Promise Foundation, India (reference number 11-11-2021). Parents or guardians could opt out of children's participation in the assessment. Opt-out was chosen because the focus on oral language aligned with the pre/primary school curriculum, with the centrality of oral language evident from daily scheduled lessons across the state and a teacher professional development training priority for the academic year.

2.2. Materials

Two sources informed the development of the preliminary word list: the Swadesh list (1971), and a Hindi child-directed print corpus of 38,133 sentences from 1,243 records spanning different genres and appearing in 236 books for 3-to-10-year-olds (Nag et al., 2025). We focused on nouns, verbs, and adjectives. For each word item, two distractors were (a) semantically close to the correct answer (close in definition and hence most plausible if unsure), and (b) phonologically close (overlap at word-beginning sounds or endings, and hence confusable).

The third distractor was semantically distant (loosely linked to the correct answer). This procedure is arguably more rigorous than approaches that leave one distractor unconstrained (completely a random unit), since such options quickly fall out of the test-taker's decision-making process.

Each item was presented alone and contained four full-color pictures with the target and distractor pictures placed randomly on a quadrant. A pre-pilot informed improvements to picture clarity and distractor quality (see SDM 1 in the Supplemental Digital Materials for issues resolved at this stage). The final 50-item word list comprised 27 nouns, 20 verbs, and 3 adjectives, covering content categories like actions, nature, and describing words. For exemplar items, including the target word and related distractors, see Table 1.

In addition to vocabulary performance, information was collected on each child's sex and home language (whether Hindi was one of the child's home languages). Peer exposure to Hindi was computed using school-level data (the proportion of Hindi-speaking children in the child's school).

2.3. Procedure

The test was administered in two sessions of 25 items each, which were ordered by difficulty based on estimates from the pilot study (for a brief report on item-level considerations see SDM 2). Sessions were held on consecutive days or within the working week, depending on the school timetable. Items were presented in one session using a standard laptop screen with display and recording using the Qualtrics platform, and using a paper-and-pencil format in another session. The two options of online and offline presentation were initially considered anticipating poor internet connectivity in remote locations across the district, with the Qualtrics set also available as paper-and-pencil material. In the end, connectivity issues disrupted 0.56% of online sessions (7 sessions at T1, 4 at T2, none at T3); these administrations were completed offline.

Testing was in a quiet location provided by the school. Instructions to the child were to prompt picture pointing ("I will say a word. Please show me the picture."). The sessions began with two practice items. A discontinuation rule of six consecutive incorrect responses was applied per session. Assessor training was through role plays and practice sessions with four-to seven-year-olds, using audio recordings for skills audit (see SDM 3 for training materials).

2.4. Data Analysis

Analyses were conducted within a Rasch modeling framework to examine reliability, item characteristics, and measurement invariance. Given the task's scoring structure, the data were analyzed using a dichotomous Rasch model, with parameters estimated via marginal maximum likelihood (MML). Score reliability was evaluated using Cronbach's α and Rasch person separation reliability, computed by pooling all time points into a single dataset. Item difficulty estimates were reviewed across the ability continuum, and item fit was assessed using infit and outfit statistics (with values between 0.8 and 1.2 indicating good fit) and the correspondence between the model and empirical item characteristic curves (ICCs).

To evaluate distractor functioning across the ability continuum, a semi-parametric approach was used to estimate option characteristic curves for each multiple-choice item. A multinomial logistic regression was fitted with response option (1-4) as the outcome and Rasch person ability (θ) estimate as the predictor, modeled using a natural cubic spline with three degrees of freedom. This approach was used for descriptive and diagnostic purposes and does not impose the parametric constraints of standard IRT category models. It allows visualization of how the probability of selecting each response option varies across ability and provides a flexible complement to the Rasch model. This is consistent with nonparametric and semi-parametric approaches to item response modeling that estimate option characteristic curves without assuming a specific functional form (e.g., Mazza et al., 2014). The resulting curves were used to identify potential violations of Rasch model assumptions, such as non-monotonicity in the correct response or irregular distractor behavior across ability levels.

Finally, measurement invariance was assessed by examining both uniform and nonuniform differential item functioning (DIF) via a logistic regression approach. DIF was evaluated across sex, Hindi as a home language, peer exposure to Hindi, and pairwise time points (T1-T2, T2-T3, T1-T3). All analyses were conducted in R, with full details including package and version details provided in the Supplemental Digital Materials (SDM 4).

4. Discussion

The aim of this study was to examine the tracking of receptive vocabulary over developmental time and across diverse linguistic experiences. The PTRV(TT-Hin), a picture-based test of Hindi receptive vocabulary, was evaluated to see if it can serve as a fair and reliable tool for tracking early vocabulary development across the diverse early childhood linguistic profiles found in multilingual north Indian classrooms. Such tracking is particularly relevant in contexts where low pace of learning has substantial downstream effects on educational attainments.

Using a Rasch modeling framework, we examined reliability, targeting, item fit, and measurement invariance across demographic, contextual, and developmental dimensions. Taken together, the findings indicate that the instrument meets the core requirements for a vocabulary tracking tool in this context. Two qualifications merit attention: a small subset of items showed misfit attributable to a combination of guessing, distractor effects, and-for two items-lower discrimination than the Rasch model assumes, and a small number of items exhibited differential functioning across assessment time points, though effects were predominantly small to negligible in magnitude.

At the test level, the PTRV(TT-Hin) demonstrated good internal consistency and reliability, meeting the threshold typically recommended for group-level research and tracking applications (Revelle & Zinbarg, 2009). Examination of item-person targeting revealed a good spread of person ability estimates in approximate alignment with the item difficulty distribution, with no evidence of floor or ceiling effects across any of the three assessment points. This is notable given the linguistic diversity of the sample, which ranged from children with Hindi as a dominant home language to those encountering it formally for the first time in school. That the test captured variation across this proficiency range suggests that the contextually situated construction approach-drawing on both the Swadesh list (Swadesh, 1971) and a child-directed Hindi print corpus (Nag et al., 2025)-produced a set of items appropriately matched to the breadth of early vocabulary knowledge present in this multilingual setting.

Five of the 50 items were flagged through inspection of item characteristic curves, and one further item (Item 43) exceeded the statistical fit criterion, yielding six items with some form of misfit-a proportion consistent with expectations for a newly developed 50-item instrument.

The contrast between the well-fitting items (Figure 2) and the misfitting items (Figure 3) was instructive: where the well-fitting items showed close alignment between the empirical ICC and Rasch model expectation, with monotonically declining distractors, the misfitting items showed systematic departures attributable to two distinguishable mechanisms. The first was guessing, observed in all five ICC-flagged items but most pronounced for the more difficult items (Items 42 and 49): an expected artefact of the four-alternative forced-choice format in which children selecting among pictures have a non-trivial probability of a correct response even when guessing (Andrich et al., 2016; Embretson & Reise, 2000; Jin et al., 2022). The second pattern, evident for Items 12 and 47, was reduced item discrimination, possibly because the target words occupied a narrower band of familiarity or because the pictorial stimuli did not clearly distinguish the target from children's everyday visual experience of the concept.

The multinomial response probability curves provided diagnostic insight into the mechanisms underlying misfit. Distractor interference-particularly from phonologically or semantically proximate options-contributed to the specific profiles of deviation for Items 4 and 42, suggesting that misfit was not simply uninformed guessing but reflected partial lexical knowledge: children with some familiarity with a word may have been able to eliminate one or two distractors without reliably identifying the target (Ludewig et al., 2023). Item 43, the only item exceeding the statistical fit criterion, showed a distinct pattern of response unpredictability rather than systematic guessing or reduced discrimination, possibly reflecting pictorial ambiguity or polysemy of the target word. Together, these six items constitute a tractable revision target.

The response probability data provide item-level diagnostic guidance: for the guessing-affected items, replacing proximate distractors with more clearly differentiated alternatives may reduce distractor-driven misfit; for Items 12 and 47, review of image clarity or conceptual distinctiveness is warranted; and for Item 43, review of the pictorial stimuli is indicated.

Perhaps the most substantively important finding for the validity of the instrument is the near-complete absence of non-negligible DIF across sex, home language, and peer exposure to Hindi. No item exhibited DIF by sex even though a female advantage in language performance is a robust finding across languages and cultural contexts (Eriksson et al., 2012; Petersen, 2018), and its absence at the item level here indicates that the content and format of the PTRV(TT-Hin) do not systematically favor either sex.

Two items showed statistically significant DIF by home language, though both effects were small to negligible in magnitude. Given that the sample included children from several distinct home language backgrounds-Haryanvi, Bihari, Punjabi, and Wagdi, differing in their structural proximity to Hindi-the near-absence of substantive DIF across these groups is notable. This stands in contrast to findings from translated instruments, where substantial DIF is a common outcome (Chen et al., 2025; Goriot et al., 2021; Leon & Singh, 2017), and supports the case for locally grounded item development over direct translation.

Extending beyond individual language profiles, one characteristic of a multilingual setting is the extent of exposure to a target language that children can gain through their peer group in class. Our study is the first to acknowledge peer-level exposure, quantifying this as a differential item functioning variable. Four items showed statistically significant DIF by peer exposure, but all effects were small to negligible in magnitude, and no pattern of systematic peer-exposure-related DIF was apparent across the full item set. Together, these results indicate that the PTRV(TT-Hin) calibrates ability in a way that reflects genuine variation in Hindi receptive vocabulary rather than artefacts of group membership.

A distinct and relatively underexamined validity question in language assessment concerns whether item parameters remain stable as children develop-a requirement for assessments intended to track longitudinal change (Millsap, 2010 (Millsap, , 2011)). Most statistically flagged items showed small to negligible DIF across all three pairwise time comparisons, indicating that their difficulty ordering was maintained across one academic year of development and instruction. Two items reached moderate effect sizes: Item 30 in the T1-T2 comparison and Item 46 in the T1-T3 comparison. These shifts in relative item difficulty are interpretable as reflecting differential rates of acquisition for specific words across the proficiency range, rather than systematic measurement failure, but they warrant monitoring in future administrations. Two further items showed non-uniform DIF in the T2-T3 comparison (Items 16 and 38), indicating a change in item discrimination rather than difficulty alone; this pattern may be consistent with increasing differentiation in word knowledge as children progress through the school year. Taken as a whole, these findings suggest that the PTRV(TT-Hin) functions with sufficient developmental stability for longitudinal tracking within the one-year window examined here and adds to the limited but growing evidence base on item parameter stability in developmental and multilingual assessment (Millsap, 2010; Park et al., 2025).

4.1. Broader Implications

The validation findings carry implications beyond the specific instrument examined here.

While it is well established that a picture-based receptive vocabulary test can be administered without literacy or expressive language demands, the PTRV(TT-Hin) demonstrates that a test built from locally relevant child-directed corpora can achieve the psychometric properties required for tracking early language development across a linguistically diverse sample. The absence of systematic DIF across a wide range of home language backgrounds lends support to the view that bespoke construction-calibrated to local language use rather than adapted from instruments developed for other linguistic contexts-offers a more measurement-invariant approach than translation (Leon & Singh, 2017). The test's efficient administration format and objective scoring are also relevant considerations for assessment at scale in low-and middleincome country contexts, where assessor training resources may be limited (Nag, 2016; Nakamura et al., 2023). More broadly, the findings reinforce the value of receptive vocabulary as an assessment target in multilingual settings: picture-pointing tasks access the developing lexical system without taxing speech, reading, or writing, providing a window into oral language development that is particularly informative where children's proficiency in the language of instruction is highly variable (DeAnda et al., 2016; Frank et al., 2021).

Questions of generalizability arise at two levels. At the sample level, the children in this study attended public schools in a single multilingual district in north India, and the home languages represented-Haryanvi, Bihari, Punjabi, and Wagdi-are all Indo-Aryan languages with varying structural proximity to Hindi. It is not known whether the near-absence of DIF by home language would hold in early childhood settings involving typologically more distant language pairings, or whether the psychometric properties observed here would extend to private-school populations with different socioeconomic and language exposure profiles. At the methodological level, however, the approach demonstrated here is designed to be transferable.

4.2. Limitations and Future Research

Several limitations bear on the interpretation of these findings. The tracking window covers only one academic year, and it is not known whether the developmental stability of item parameters observed here would persist over a longer period, or whether items showing moderate DIF by time would accumulate larger effects with further follow-up. In addition, the demographic variables were analyzed independently, and the study did not examine intersectional profiles-for example, girls from non-Hindi-speaking homes with low peer exposure-for whom the convergence of multiple factors may bear on vocabulary development in ways not captured by single-variable DIF analyses. The observation that Item 1 was the only item to exhibit DIF across both home language and peer exposure suggests that cross-factor item behavior warrants further investigation, particularly given that home language background and peer exposure are themselves correlated in this sample. Finally, the PTRV(TT-Hin) measures receptive vocabulary only. A more complete account of lexical development would additionally require expressive measures capturing productive vocabulary knowledge and morphological awareness (DeAnda et al., 2016).

Future research should examine whether the temporal DIF observed for a small number of items reflects transient or persistent instability. The item-level response data from the present study-including the multinomial response probability curves-offer a foundation for targeted revision of the misfitting items, and corpus-based resources such as child-directed print corpora (Nag et al., 2025) and age-of-acquisition norms (Dulay et al., 2026) provide candidate tools for selecting replacement distractors in languages for which psycholinguistic databases are not yet available. The novel use of peer-level Hindi exposure as a DIF variable also merits further investigation; more granular indexing of peer exposure-for instance, the proportion of classroom instructional time in Hindi, or network-level measures of language use among peersmay reveal threshold or interaction effects not detectable with the binary grouping variable employed here. Finally, the construction approach demonstrated in this study is exportable: applying it to other languages of instruction in multilingual contexts, such as in South Asia, would allow examination of whether item difficulty orderings transfer across typologically related languages, extending the evidence base for cross-linguistically comparable vocabulary assessment (e.g., Frank et al., 2021).

4.3. Conclusions

Receptive vocabulary, what is understood when hearing a word, underpins both developing reading comprehension and wider learning. The PTRV(TT-Hin) meets the requirements for a vocabulary tracking instrument for monitoring Hindi receptive vocabulary development in multilingual north Indian classrooms: adequate reliability, well-calibrated targeting across a wide proficiency range, and a largely small to negligible pattern of differential item functioning across sex, home language background, peer exposure, and developmental time.

The misfit observed in a small number of items is diagnostically interpretable and constitutes a tractable agenda for instrument refinement. Taken together, this study also offers a methodological template-combining Rasch analysis, DIF evaluation, and multinomial distractor modeling-for developing and validating vocabulary assessments in other early childhood multilingual settings where contextually appropriate language tracking tools are both urgent and underdeveloped.
