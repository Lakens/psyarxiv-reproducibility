Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Cooperation across social settings requires deciding whether to reciprocate a partner's helpful behavior or exploit it when incentives change. We examined this tension in an online experiment with 430 participants playing a Prisoner's Dilemma (PD), either alone or followed by a Snowdrift game (SD), where defection pays more against a sufficiently likely cooperator. We also placed programmed bots-computer-controlled players following a fixed rule-in some groups. By programming them to cooperate in PD and reciprocate their partner's PD action in SD, we created a consistent source of cooperation and reciprocity. This allowed us to ask whether humans also cooperated more with one another. Humans cooperated more in SD after their partner cooperated in PD, even when observed partner behavior made defection the better immediate material response. This pattern is consistent with reciprocity, although choices do not identify beliefs or motives. Human-human cooperation was higher in groups containing bots; coupling alone produced no detected increase in mean PD cooperation. Separate experiments with GPT language-model agents asked whether frequent cooperation was accompanied by similar responses across games. Despite high PD cooperation, partner PD cooperation predicted less GPT cooperation in SD in the primary pooled analysis. This association varied across configurations and did not generally indicate payoff-maximizing choices. Simulations using fitted human response rules illustrated the possible effects of alternative bot policies. These findings reveal responses to cooperative partners across contexts that overall cooperation rates can obscure.

Introduction

Like the individuals who participate in them, social interactions do not exist in isolation, and encounters vary in strategic form. Business partners may cooperate in both commercial and civic settings, teammates may coordinate across distinct tasks, and neighbors may work together on separate public goods. In each case, one potentially cooperative encounter exists alongside another, but the balance among cooperation, coordination, and free-riding can differ. This ecology-of-games perspective motivates models of interacting, rather than isolated, games in which behavior in one setting can alter cooperation in another (1) (2) (3).

Repeated-game research shows how people condition on prior actions, reciprocity, and reputation (7) (8) (9) (10) (11) (12) (13). Related work on multiple one-shot exchanges shows how simple partnerdirected heuristics can support cooperation (14). Coupled dilemmas ask what happens when information travels between settings with different strategic logics. Consider two familiar cases. In a shared project or costly agreement, mutual cooperation is best for the group, yet each person can gain by letting others bear the cost. This is the logic of a Prisoner's Dilemma (PD). In a task that must be completed, such as clearing a blocked path or handling an urgent problem, someone has to contribute. Once another person is already doing so, withholding effort can reflect anti-coordination as well as selfishness. In a Snowdrift game (SD), unilateral cooperation can prevent the worst outcome, while defection can exploit another's contribution (15) (16) (17) (18) (19).

Experiments also show that behavior can spill across games. An efficient coordination-game precedent can increase later PD cooperation when action labels remain descriptively similar (4). People can carry heuristics across simultaneous games played with different opponents (5). Concurrent donation games can raise or reduce cooperation depending on partner structure (6). Closest theoretically, Salahshour (3) models a PD followed by a different game, including SD, with second-game behavior conditional on the first action. The present experiment tests the stage-resolved PD-to-SD sign in human behavior and compares that cue use with GPT agents.

How social information carries across these contrasting settings is increasingly consequential as artificial agents enter social systems (20). Bots and language-model agents can serve as teammates (21), simulated participants (22), or strategic partners (23), changing what people observe and how cooperative the local environment appears (24, 25). For language-model agents, apparent continuity across encounters depends on the interaction history supplied in context, making the transfer of information between distinct tasks especially salient. Experimental work further shows that model behavior can change when trust-game choices carry actual rather than hypothetical incentives (26), and that tested early language models can produce context-sensitive completions resembling altruistic behavior (27). These findings motivate studying artificial agents in coupled social settings, while also cautioning that high aggregate cooperation does not establish human-like use of cross-context information (28, 29).

In such coupled dilemmas, two contrasting possibilities follow. Under a narrow payoffmaximizing account, behavior in one setting is useful mainly as evidence about what a partner is likely to do in another. The actor should then choose the best material response, even when this means exploiting a partner who appears likely to cooperate. For example, after directly seeing a colleague stay late to keep a shared project on track, a separate urgent task may arise that either person can handle. A narrowly strategic focal person might expect the same colleague to step in again and therefore withhold effort. Such a rule can be locally advantageous, but it gives cross-context information a trust-limiting role. Under a reciprocal or norm-based account, by contrast, observed cooperation is not only a forecast of future action. It can create social credit, while defection can invite punishment, so behavior in one context becomes a basis for rewarding or withholding cooperation in another. In the same workplace example, the focal person could instead treat the colleague's earlier willingness to help as a reason to share the later burden, even when letting the colleague handle it would bring a personal benefit. Cross-context information can then support moralized reciprocity rather than only strategic best response. This possibility fits broader models in which agents act on perceived or socially transformed payoffs rather than objective material payoffs alone (30, 31).

Distinguishing these alternatives requires a setting in which the same cooperative cue points toward opposing social and material responses. We examine these alternatives in a controlled online experiment involving humans and parallel GPT-agent simulations. Human participants played either a repeated Prisoner's Dilemma alone, a coupled Prisoner's Dilemma and Snowdrift game, or the same coupled game in mixed groups with cooperative bots. The programmed bots followed a simple cooperative rule, allowing us to test whether their stable behavior was associated with cooperation among humans elsewhere in the group. The GPT simulations served a different purpose. Unlike the fixed-rule bots, GPT agents made prompted choices, allowing us to ask whether high aggregate cooperation also produced the human direction of cross-game cue use. The central question is whether behavior in one dilemma transfers into another as positive social information, as a material cue for best response, or not at all. The results show that humans display positive cross-game transfer, cooperative bots are associated with elevated human-human cooperation under the specific bot rule, and the pooled tested GPT configurations, despite high aggregate cooperation in the Prisoner's Dilemma, show an opposite PD-to-SD association that varies across configurations.

Results

Theory: when the same cue implies exploitation or reciprocity

We use two standard social dilemmas. In the Prisoner's Dilemma (PD), mutual cooperation is best for the pair, but each player can gain by defecting against a cooperator. In the Snowdrift game (SD), unilateral cooperation still produces a positive outcome for both players, but a player can gain by defecting when the other player cooperates. We denote cooperation by C and defection by D. The entries below give the payoff to the focal player first and the partner second:

The coupled design, in which a pair plays a PD followed by an SD, turns a familiar question into a sharper test. Suppose a partner cooperates in the PD stage and the focal player then chooses its strategy in the SD stage. Under a narrow material best-response account, the partner's PD cooperation is useful because it helps predict what the partner is likely to do next. If it raises the expectation that the partner will cooperate in SD, then exploiting that likely cooperator can be materially attractive. Under a reputational or norm-based account, the same cooperative PD action can instead function as positive social information. The partner's earlier cooperation may give the focal player a reason to cooperate in SD, for example because the focal player treats the partner as having earned cooperative treatment.

The Snowdrift game makes these two interpretations point in opposite directions. With our SD payoffs, the material advantage of cooperating is 1 -3q, where q is the expected probability that the partner will cooperate. Cooperation is materially attractive only when q < 1/3. Above that threshold, an expected cooperator is materially exploitable. Thus, if a partner's PD cooperation only raises the expectation that the partner will cooperate again in SD, best response predicts less SD cooperation. If the focal player treats the same PD action as positive social information, reputational transfer predicts more SD cooperation.

The Reputational Coupling Model, introduced in Methods, makes this logic explicit without claiming that we observe private beliefs. A partner's action may change the focal player's strategic expectation q, the perceived probability that the partner will cooperate, and may also carry positive or negative social information across games, summarized by a transfer cue r. These channels are not separately identified from choices in this design. The model is therefore a diagnostic framework for sign predictions, not a structural decomposition of beliefs and social valuation.

We test this contrast with the PD-to-SD transfer coefficient, β P D→SD . The transfer coefficient β P D→SD measures the fitted change in the focal player's SD cooperation when the partner cooperated rather than defected in the same-round PD, holding fixed the previous-round PD and SD cues that were already visible before the SD choice. A positive value has the sign predicted by positive reciprocity. A zero value indicates no detectable transfer. A negative value means that a cooperative PD cue predicts less SD cooperation. It aligns with strategic anti-coordination only when the cue raises expected partner-SD cooperation and choices respond to the resulting material incentive. Behavior alone does not establish those conditions or uniquely identify the underlying mechanism. We arrange the four observed transfer coefficients, β x→y , into a transfer matrix, B: PD-to-PD, SD-to-PD, PD-to-SD, and SD-to-SD. Methods provides the full notation, the logit/sigmoid formulation, and the derivation linking the conceptual model to the fitted transfer coefficients, β x→y.

We also use behavioral simulation models, specified in Methods, to ask whether the estimated cue effects can reproduce the observed dynamics and compare alternative artificial-partner rules in counterfactual experiments. The Dynamic Logistic Transfer Simulation Model fits the observed transfer coefficients directly and uses them to generate predicted choice trajectories and simulated bot-policy comparisons. The Anchored Latent-State Transfer Simulation Model is a two-stage simulation model. It first assigns PD choices from visible prior cues, then approximates the expected partner-SD cooperation probability, q, and tests whether the residual partner-PD cue still has the sign predicted by reputational transfer. These simulations strengthen the behavioral test, but they still do not separately identify private expectations, reputational credit, or psychological parameters. Experimental design and guiding questions

In the PD-only control (PD-only; 26 groups, 156 humans), six participants played 25 rounds of a repeated Prisoner's Dilemma. In the two-stage conditions, each round contained a PD stage followed by a Snowdrift stage with the same opponent. One two-stage condition used six humans (all-human PD+SD; 25 groups, 150 humans). The mixed condition used four humans and two bots (mixed human-bot PD+SD; 31 groups, 124 humans plus two programmed bots per intended group). Before each PD choice in the two-stage conditions, participants saw the current opponent's previous-round choices in both games. Before each SD choice, those previous-round cues remained visible and participants additionally saw the same-round PD outcome with that opponent, including both PD choices and payoffs.

Participants played with the same opponent in both stages of a given round, but pairings were reshuffled across rounds. Because of this reshuffling, the design measures responses to visible behavioral cues about the current opponent rather than stable long-run partner reputation.

The preregistered experiment (32) asked whether coupling two dilemmas changed cooperation relative to a PD-only control, whether adding cooperative bots changed cooperation in mixed groups, and whether individual differences predicted cooperation. We organize those questions around the paper's central test. First, did the coupled PD+SD environment change cooperation dynamics? Second, did the cooperative bot rule merely add cooperative bot encounters, or was human-human cooperation also elevated in mixed groups? Third and most importantly, how did participants use the information shown to them across games? If the PD cue was used only to forecast the partner's SD action and maximize material payoffs, then a partner who seemed likely to cooperate would make SD defection more attractive. If actions also carried reputational or norm-based meaning, the same cooperative PD cue should increase SD cooperation. The stage-resolved PD-to-SD sign test reported below adapts the preregistered H3 question. Its preregistration status and the complementary preregistered proxy are reported in SI Section 2 and SI Section 4B. Finally, we asked whether the GPT-agent simulations analyzed here showed the same pattern of transfer as humans (SI Section 7C, Table S23, and Table S24).

The results show that the all-human PD+SD condition did not raise average PD cooperation relative to the PD-only control, but it changed the decline pattern. Mixed groups showed elevated human cooperation, including human-human cooperation under the specific cooperative bot rule studied here. The key result is that humans showed positive PD-to-SD transfer, whereas the GPT agents did not reproduce the human pattern. Model specifications and supporting estimates are reported in SI Section 1, Table S4.

Coupling slowed decline but did not raise mean PD cooperation

Relative to the PD-only control, the all-human PD+SD condition did not show higher average PD cooperation. Across all 25 rounds, PD cooperation in the all-human PD+SD condition was statistically indistinguishable from the PD-only control (∆ = -0.029, p = .685). The same null held in the last 10 rounds (∆ = +0.016, p = .749) and last 5 rounds (∆ = +0.030, p = .546; Fig. 2A-B; SI Section 4A, Table S5).

The time course differed more than the mean. Cooperation declined fastest in the PD-only control (odds ratio per round = 0.941, p < .001). Decline was shallower in the all-human PD+SD PD stage (odds ratio = 0.969, p < .001) and in both mixed-condition stages (PD: odds ratio = 0.983, p = .007; SD: odds ratio = 0.985, p = .013). The PD-only decline was steeper than each two-stage series (all slope-comparison p < .001; Fig. 2A-C; SI Section 4A, Table S5).

Mixed groups showed elevated human cooperation under cooperative bot exposure

Both mixed-vs-all-human contrasts were significant. Relative to the all-human PD+SD condition, the mixed condition showed higher cooperation in PD (∆ = 0.183, p < .001) and in SD (∆ = 0.137, p = .002; Fig. 2C-D; SI Section 1, Table S4; SI Section 4A, Table S5). This establishes the overall mixed-condition elevation, but it does not by itself identify the mechanism. The key question is whether bots merely added cooperative encounters, or whether their presence made the human cue environment more cooperative.

The human-only decomposition argues against a purely mechanical interpretation. Restricting the analysis to human choices, raw cooperation toward human opponents was higher in mixed groups than in all-human groups (Fig. 2E; SI Section 6, Tables S15-S16, and Fig. S5). Allhuman human-human cooperation was 0.432 in PD and 0.439 in SD. Among mixed-condition human-human choices without immediately preceding bot exposure, cooperation was 0.592 in PD and 0.525 in SD. Among mixed-condition human-human choices after recent bot exposure, cooperation was 0.601 in PD and 0.558 in SD. Raw human-bot cooperation was also high (0.633 in PD and 0.612 in SD), but the adjusted human-bot coefficient was not reliable (SI Section 1, Table S4; SI Section 6, Tables S15-S16).

A second observed-data check asked whether humans cooperated more with bots than with human opponents in both games, or mainly in one stage. Within the mixed condition, the difference between bot-facing and human-facing choices was small and not reliable in PD (∆ = 0.019, 95% CI [-0.014, 0.053], p = .339), but reliable in SD (∆ = 0.063, 95% CI [0.030, 0.096], p < .001; Fig. 2E; SI Section 6, Table S14). Thus, humans did not simply cooperate more with bots everywhere. The clearest direct bot-opponent difference appeared in the Snowdrift stage, consistent with bots changing the second-stage reciprocity environment.

A human-choice-only logistic model confirmed the human-human component. Adjusting for stage, round, previous partner cue history, the added same-round PD cue before SD choices, and group-clustered uncertainty, mixed human-human choices without recent bot exposure remained more cooperative than all-human human-human choices (log-odds coefficient = 0.315, OR = 1.37, 95% CI [1.04, 1.80], p = .024; Fig. 2F; SI Section 1, Table S4; SI Section 6, Table S15). The coefficient was similar and numerically larger for human-human choices following recent bot exposure (coefficient = 0.442, OR = 1.56, 95% CI [1.19, 2.03], p = .001; Fig. 2F; SI Section 6, Table S15). The two coefficients were not compared directly. These effects remained positive in a demographic/SVO/residence-adjusted check, a group-mean bootstrap comparison, and a participant-plus-group random-intercept model (SI Section 6, Table S17; SI Section 7C, Table S21). When we refit the model after omitting one group at a time (leave-one-group-out), the mixed human-human effect was positive in most refits, but a few omitted groups noticeably changed the estimate. We therefore report this analysis in SI Section 7C, Table S22 as a check for influential groups rather than as the main uncertainty interval.

Humans used cross-game information

We next asked how participants read the information shown to them. In plain terms, did they treat PD and SD as separate games, using only the cue that belonged to the current stage, or did they let information travel across stages? Cross-game information use means that an opponent's cooperation in one game helps predict the focal player's cooperation in the other game.

Pooling human choices from both two-stage conditions, the all-human PD+SD condition and the mixed human-bot PD+SD condition, participants used both within-game and cross-game cues. These models exclude the PD-only control, which had no SD stage, and we analyze the GPT experiment data separately. In the PD stage, cooperation depended on the opponent's previous-round PD choice (odds ratio = 1.73, 95% CI [1.46, 2.04], p < .001) and previous-round SD choice (odds ratio = 1.40, 95% CI [1.21, 1.62], p < .001), controlling for mixed-condition status and round (Fig. 3A-B; SI Section 4A, Table S5). In the SD stage, the previous-round PD and SD cues remained visible, and participants also saw the same-round PD choices. Conditional on that prior visible history, cooperation depended strongly on the opponent's same-round PD choice (odds ratio = 1.93, 95% CI [1.62, 2.31], p < .001). The previous-round PD cue was smaller (odds ratio = 1.18, 95% CI [1.00, 1.39], p = .047), and the previous-round SD cue was not reliable after we included both PD cues (odds ratio = 1.14, 95% CI [0.97, 1.34], p = .110; Fig. 3A-B; SI Section 1, Table S4; SI Section 4A, Table S5).

The cue-strength pattern is informative in two ways (Fig. 3C). First, participants weighted PD-derived cues more strongly than SD-derived cues. This was true even before PD choices, where the previous-round SD action was temporally more recent than the previous-round PD action but carried the weaker coefficient. Second, the strongest single cue effect appeared before SD choices, where participants used the immediately preceding PD behavior to choose their SD strategy.

Bot presence raised baseline cooperation more clearly than it amplified cue-use slopes. In all human choices, the mixed-condition interactions with cue use were not reliable in PD (previous PD cue × mixed: OR = 0.79, p = .280; previous SD cue × mixed: OR = 1.02, p = .907) or SD (same-round PD cue × mixed: OR = 0.78, p = .266; previous PD cue × mixed: OR = 1.02, p = .937; previous SD cue × mixed: OR = 1.08, p = .692). The same conclusion held when we restricted the analysis to human-human decisions. All-human and mixed human-human interactions both showed positive cue use, but mixed human-human cue slopes were not reliably larger than all-human slopes (SI Section 4C, Table S6). We do not treat human responses to bot cues as directly comparable to human responses to human cues because bot actions were generated by the programmed bot rule. After the first round, bot cue histories were generally visible, but bot PD cooperation was fixed by design and bot SD variation came from the mirroring rule rather than from an independent decision process. We therefore treat bot effects as changes in the cue environment and baseline human cooperation, not as evidence that bots strengthened every cue coefficient. This is the first of two behavioral signatures of cross-game transfer reported here. Partner PD cooperation predicted focal behavior in the subsequent Snowdrift stage, conditional on the included visible cues.

Positive human PD-to-SD transfer is consistent with reciprocity

The diagnostic result is the central entry of the Transfer Matrix, B: the PD-to-SD transfer coefficient, β P D→SD . The transfer coefficient β P D→SD is the fitted log-odds change in focal SD cooperation when the same-round partner PD cue changes from defection to cooperation, conditional on the previous-round PD and SD cues that were also visible before SD, condition, and round. In humans, the transfer coefficient β P D→SD was positive (β P D→SD = 0.658, 95% CI [0.480, 0.835]; OR = 1.93, 95% CI [1.62, 2.31]; p < .001; SI Section 1, Table S4; SI Section 4A, Table S5). In probability terms, focal SD cooperation in the all-human PD+SD condition rose from 0.354 after partner PD defection to 0.552 after partner PD cooperation. In the mixed condition, it rose from 0.461 to 0.605 (SI Section 5B, Table S9, and Fig. S2).

This positive PD-to-SD transfer coefficient, β P D→SD , becomes theoretically informative when combined with the Snowdrift payoff calculation in Methods. Let q D denote the observed probability that the partner cooperated in SD after defecting in the same-round PD, and let q C denote the same probability after cooperating in PD. These partner-cooperation probabilities, q D and q C , describe partner behavior, not the focal player's transfer coefficient, β P D→SD . In the all-human condition, q D = 0.314 and q C = 0.604; in the mixed condition, q D = 0.375 and q C = 0.639 (Fig. 3D; SI Section 5B, Table S9, and Fig. S2). With the SD payoffs, the material advantage of cooperation, ∆π SD (q), is ∆π SD (q) = 1 -3q (Eq. 4), so the PD cue changes the material incentive by ∆π SD (q C ) -∆π SD (q D ) = -3(q C -q D ). This quantity is negative in both conditions, at -0.870 in the all-human condition and -0.792 in the mixed condition. Under this one-stage material-payoff benchmark, a simple exploitation response would point toward less focal SD cooperation. The fitted human transfer coefficient moved in the opposite direction, with β P D→SD > 0 in the Reduced-Form Transfer Model (Eq. 5). The sign is therefore consistent with positive reciprocal transfer dominating that benchmark, while the design does not separately identify expectations, social valuation, or motives.

The full same-round PD outcome shows why this transfer is substantively richer than a single cue coefficient. Humans carried the relational pattern of the immediate PD encounter into the SD stage. Mutual PD cooperation produced the highest SD cooperation (CC, 0.713, cluster-bootstrap 95% CI [0.659, 0.762]). Being exploited after cooperating produced a lower rate (CD, 0.493, 95% CI [0.446, 0.540]). Exploiting a cooperative partner was lower still (DC, 0.397, 95% CI [0.352, 0.448]), and mutual defection produced the lowest SD cooperation (DD, 0.302, 95% CI [0.261, 0.347]; Fig. 3E; SI Section 5D, Table S11, and Fig. S4).

This ordered profile was not only own-action consistency. Among focal players who cooperated in PD, partner PD cooperation raised SD cooperation by 0.220 (95% CI [0.158, 0.280]). Among focal players who defected in PD, the corresponding partner effect was smaller but still positive (0.096, 95% CI [0.046, 0.143]; Fig. 3F; SI Section 5D, Table S12). A model-adjusted OwnPD × PartnerPD specification gave the same qualitative result (SI Section 5D, Table S13). Thus, human transfer was relational rather than merely individual. People did not only repeat their own PD action, but responded to what had just happened between the two players.

The later SD-to-next-PD association is weaker and should be read more cautiously. SD cooperation predicted next-round PD cooperation in the standard stage-resolved model (OR = 1.81, p < .001), but the within-person centered version weakened this result. Deviations around a participant's own SD average were not reliable. We therefore treat it as a cross-game association among participants or contexts, not as strong evidence that one SD cooperative act caused the same person to cooperate more in the next PD round (SI Section 4A, Table S5; SI Section 5A, Table S8; SI Section 7C, Table S20).

Pooled tested GPT configurations showed negative PD-to-SD transfer

The GPT agents in these experiments often cooperated at high rates, especially in PD, as shown by the GPT overlays and mean markers in Fig. 2A-D and consistent with evidence that particular LLM configurations can cooperate in repeated games (28, 29), but their aggregate pattern already raises a puzzle. GPT PD cooperation was higher than human PD cooperation in every parallel condition (Fig. 2). Yet the same agents often cooperated much less in SD than in PD. This is surprising if high PD cooperation is interpreted as a general cooperative disposition, because the SD stage is not simply a harsher cooperation problem. It uses a closely related payoff scale, but changes the strategic meaning of facing a likely cooperator. The Transfer Matrix, B, clarifies this contrast behaviorally. The diagnostic PD-to-SD transfer coefficient, β P D→SD , reversed. After we included the current opponent's previous-round PD and SD cues, that opponent's same-round PD cooperation predicted lower SD cooperation by the focal GPT agent (β P D→SD = -1.036, 95% CI [-1.225, -0.846]; OR = 0.355, 95% CI [0.294, 0.429]; p < .001). By contrast, the SD-to-SD transfer coefficient, β SD→SD , was positive. The opponent's previous-round SD cooperation predicted substantially higher focal GPT SD cooperation (β SD→SD = 1.181, OR = 3.26, p < .001). GPT SD cooperation also did not predict next-round PD cooperation once we controlled for previous PD behavior (OR = 1.04, p = .506; Fig. 4A-B; SI Section 1, Table S4; SI Section 5A, Tables S7-S8; SI Section 7C, Tables S23-S24, and Fig. S9).

This does not show that GPT agents lack any particular internal social state. It shows that their observed pattern of transfer was not human-like. Nor does the negative pooled coefficient identify a general best-response mechanism. In the all-GPT condition, observed partner SD cooperation was q D = 0.275 after partner PD defection and q C = 0.239 after cooperation, both below the 1/3 threshold. In the mixed GPT-droid condition, it changed from q D = 0.285 to q C = 0.678. The negative focal association therefore aligned with the one-stage material-payoff benchmark in the mixed-droid condition, but not in the all-GPT condition (SI Section 5B, Table S9). Family-by-temperature checks were also heterogeneous. Several temperature-0 cells had no partner-PD defection cue, and one cell had only a single defection cue. Among cells with substantial cue variation, the clearest negative association appeared for GPT-3.5 Turbo at temperature 1 (Fig. 4B-C; SI Section 7C, Table S24, and Fig. S9; prompt and response-coding details in SI Section 9A). The robust comparison is therefore descriptive. The pooled tested GPT configurations did not reproduce the positive human PD-to-SD association, and aggregate cooperation alone would have hidden that difference in cue use.

The full PD-outcome profile likewise differed. In the GPT experiment data, SD cooperation was 0.321 after CC (cluster-bootstrap 95% CI [0.292, 0.346]), 0.454 after CD (95% CI [0.419, 0.484]), 0.256 after DC (95% CI [0.228, 0.283]), and 0.340 after DD (95% CI [0.232, 0.503]; Fig. 4D; SI Section 5D, Table S11, and Fig. S4). The GPT outcome counts were highly imbalanced, especially because CC dominated the GPT data, so we interpret the non-CC comparisons cautiously. Conditional partner effects did not show the human pattern. The raw partner-PD effect was negative among GPT focal PD cooperators (-0.133, 95% CI [-0.176, -0.091]) and imprecise among GPT focal PD defectors (-0.084, 95% CI [-0.252, 0.030]; SI Section 5D, Table S12). The primary pooled result is the sign contrast between the positive human and negative GPT PD-to-SD coefficients. The GPT association is sensitive to configuration and specification. Model-adjusted partner-PD marginal effects conditional on focal PD action were positive but imprecise (0.022, 95% CI [-0.113, 0.125], for focal cooperators; 0.057, 95% CI [-0.097, 0.208], for focal defectors; Table S13).

Fitted choice models captured conditional behavioral patterns

The preceding analyses estimate cue effects directly, but cue coefficients alone do not show whether the same choice rule captures the observed round-by-round pattern. We therefore fit two choice models, specified in Methods. The first model implements the reduced-form transfer structure as a generative choice rule. The second asks whether the PD-to-SD result survives after we approximate the strategic expectation channel more explicitly.

Dynamic Logistic Transfer Simulation Model

The Dynamic Logistic Transfer Simulation Model asks whether the observed cue coefficients, own-action persistence, and round effects capture the main choice trajectories. The model maps stage-specific logistic equations to cooperation probabilities using cue histories and own-action states recorded in the observed sequence, together with round and the mixed-group environment (Fig. 5A). Figure 5B is therefore a conditional fit check rather than a recursively generated trajectory. In separate fivefold held-out SD-choice checks fit and evaluated within the human and GPT datasets, cross-game terms and own-action persistence improved prediction for humans.

For GPT choices, most improvement came from adding the previous-round same-game SD cue (Fig. 5B-C; SI Section 7A, Table S18, and Fig. S6).

Counterfactual bot-policy simulations

The human experiment used one particular cooperative bot rule. Bots cooperated in PD and mirrored the human partner's same-round PD choice in SD. This raises a more specific question. How would human cooperation change under alternative bot rules? We used counterfactual simulations to shed light on this question.

For this policy comparison, we fit a compact variant of the choice model to human behavior in the all-human PD+SD condition only. The PD equation used the partner's previous PD and SD choices, the focal player's previous PD choice, and round. The SD equation used the partner's same-round PD choice and previous SD choice, the focal player's same-round PD choice, and round. We then simulated 800 25-round groups under each artificial-partner rule. The no-bot reference uses six simulated humans, randomly paired into three human-human pairs each round. The mixed counterfactuals use four simulated humans plus two bots, paired each round so that two humans faced bots and two humans faced another human. Bots followed the rule shown in the left-hand chips. Simulated humans always followed the fixed all-human response model. We report simulated human cooperation only, while bot actions enter as opponent actions and visible cues (SI Section 7B, Table S19, and Fig. S7).

Under the fixed fitted response rule, the simulations predict the highest human cooperation with always-cooperative bots and the experiment-matched mirroring rule, weaker cooperation with random bots, and lower cooperation with always-defect bots (Fig. 5D). The white diamonds show the observed all-human and mixed-condition cooperation rates. The horizontal bars show the counterfactual simulations. The experiment-matched mirroring counterfactual lies below the observed mixed-condition diamond. Because the simulation transports an all-human response rule to bot-generated cue histories, that discrepancy may reflect omitted condition effects, model misspecification, sampling variation, or limits of the transport assumption. It does not identify an additional mechanism.

Anchored Latent-State Transfer Simulation Model

The Dynamic Logistic Transfer Simulation Model estimates total cue effects. As a predictive robustness check, the Anchored Latent-State Transfer Simulation Model first estimates partner SD cooperation from the visible history available before the same-round partner-PD cue and then includes that cue separately when predicting focal SD cooperation. Excluding the cue preserves its predictive coefficient, but also means that qt omits any expectation shift caused by that cue. The remaining coefficient therefore combines cue-induced strategic expectations with any additional social value. The model sequence is summarized in Fig. 6A and detailed in Methods. It is not a decomposition of beliefs and reputational transfer.

The Anchored Latent-State Transfer Simulation Model retained a positive partner-PD coefficient in humans (ρ res = 0.530, 95% CI [0.373, 0.687]), whereas the corresponding GPT coefficient was imprecise and spanned both signs (ρ res = 0.280, 95% CI [-0.392, 0.953]; Fig. 6B; SI Section 5C, Table S10, and Fig. S3). A sensitivity model that included artificial-opponent status yielded a negative GPT coefficient. Because decision makers were not shown opponent type, we designate the visible-information model as the primary specification. These results show predictive robustness of the human sign and uncertainty in the primary GPT specification. They do not isolate a belief-adjusted social-value parameter. The negative GPT loading on the predicted material contrast further cautions against reading this model as evidence of payoff-responsive best response.

Robustness and secondary analyses

We assessed the main results under alternative model specifications (SI Section 1, Table S4). Human PD-to-SD transfer remained positive in rounds-6-25 and late-round restrictions, humanhuman-only analyses, and random-intercept multilevel models (SI Section 7C, Tables S20-S21, and Fig. S8). The mixed-condition human-human effects stayed positive in demographic, SVO, and residence-adjusted models, group-mean bootstrap checks, and a participant-plus-group random-intercept model. We also refit the model while omitting one group at a time because a small number of groups noticeably changed the estimate (SI Section 6, Table S17; SI Section 7C, Tables S21-S22). We estimated a negative GPT PD-to-SD coefficient in the primary transfer matrix, in the self-consistency-adjusted model, in all narrow round windows, and within both all-GPT and mixed-droid conditions (SI Section 7C, Tables S20 and S23-S24, and Fig. S9). One broad rounds-6-25 pooled restriction attenuated to near zero, and family-by-temperature checks were heterogeneous.

Individual differences

Post-game measures included self-assessment items, the 15-item Social Value Orientation (SVO) slider, and survey items on conformity and horizontal/vertical individualism-collectivism to test preregistered individual-difference predictions (SI Section 3C). A one-standard-deviation increase in SVO angle predicted higher overall cooperation (b = 0.089, 95% CI [0.064, 0.113], p < .001), whereas competition orientation predicted lower cooperation (b = -0.031, 95% CI [-0.059, -0.003], p = .031; SI Section 4A, Table S5). Thus, prosocial orientation predicted cooperation across the coupled game environment, while competitive orientation pulled in the opposite direction.

A separate post-experiment self-assessment asked participants to reconstruct their own behavior. Mean discrepancies in conditional self-reports were generally near zero, whereas participants overestimated overall PD cooperation by about five percentage points (SI Section 8).

Finally, exploratory behavioral clustering summarized each participant's cue use, stage-specific cooperation, time trends, and within-round consistency. The resulting archetypes-steadfast cooperators, conditional cooperators, game switchers, and persistent defectors-provide an intuitive map of heterogeneity in the participant pool (SI Section 8 and Fig. S10).

Discussion

Coupled dilemmas reveal the direction in which cooperation moves across games. Cooperative PD behavior predicted more human cooperation in the following Snowdrift stage, even though observed partner behavior made defection materially attractive under the one-stage Snowdrift payoff benchmark.

This interpretation keeps the claim focused. We use a payoff feature of the Snowdrift game. When the expected partner-SD cooperation probability, q, exceeds 1/3, defection pays more. The human PD-to-SD transfer coefficient, β P D→SD , has the opposite sign, and the full PD-outcome analysis shows an ordered human profile. These patterns are consistent with positive reciprocity rather than simple strategic exploitation, while choices alone do not identify private beliefs, moral valuation, or motives.

The fitted choice models provide a compact account of the observed conditional patterns. The Dynamic Logistic Transfer Simulation Model captures the observed trajectories when evaluated on recorded histories and simulates alternative bot rules under a fixed fitted response rule. The Anchored Latent-State Transfer Simulation Model shows that partner PD cooperation still predicts higher focal SD cooperation in humans after adjustment for the cue environment predictable before the same-round PD cue. In GPT agents, the corresponding focal-SD effect is ambiguous under the visible-information adjustment. These fitted quantities show that the human pattern is not confined to a single reduced-form regression. Human-human cooperation was higher in groups containing cooperative bots than in allhuman groups. Under the fixed fitted all-human response rule, cooperative and mirroring bot policies increased simulated human cooperation, random bots produced smaller changes, and always-defect bots reduced cooperation (Fig. 5D). These observed associations and modelconditional comparisons suggest that cooperative, easy-to-interpret artificial partners can support a more cooperative social environment.

The GPT comparison shows why aggregate cooperation is insufficient. The GPT agents in these experiments often cooperated more than humans in PD (Fig. 2A-D), but the pooled PD-to-SD cue had the opposite sign. That association was heterogeneous across configurations and did not generally follow realized Snowdrift best response. The pattern inferred from the coupled game therefore differed even when overall cooperation looked high. Evaluations of artificial agents should test not only how often agents cooperate, but whether they use cooperative behavior in one setting as human-like social information in another.

In conclusion, coupled dilemmas can show whether cooperation transfers across contexts as positive social information or produces a contrasting cue response. Humans showed the positive-transfer pattern. The pooled tested GPT configurations did not, although their responses were heterogeneous. Mixed groups with cooperative, predictable bots showed elevated human cooperation, including human-human cooperation.

Materials and Methods

Ethics, recruitment, and sample

Participants were recruited through Prolific using a nonprobability, opt-in sample. The experiment received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the University of Konstanz (IRB statement 29/2023) and was preregistered on Open Science Framework (OSF) (32). The experiment was conducted online during September and October 2024, the experimental app was developed using oTree (33), and participants were recruited through the Prolific platform (34). Participants provided informed consent before participation. All participants had a Prolific approval rate of at least 98%. The participation fee was set on a £6/hour basis and ranged from £2.43 to £3.99. Approved Prolific submissions took a median of 30.6 minutes. The performance-based game bonus averaged £1.04 (range £0.34-£2.00; one point = £0.01).

We linked oTree behavioral data to Prolific demographic and payment records by participant ID, removed direct identifiers before analysis, and retained valid online groups with complete scheduled 25-round records. Timeout or invalid choices within otherwise retained records were coded as missing and omitted from choice models requiring observed A/B decisions. SI Section 1, Tables S1-S2 report the data sources, inclusion criteria, exclusions, anonymization, condition balance, and missing-choice handling.

Demographic information from Prolific was available for all 430 analyzed participants. Participants were 18-79 years old (mean = 30.78, SD = 9.94), and 223 were female and 207 were male. Participants resided in 34 countries. The largest residence groups were South Africa (n = 168), Poland (n = 48), the United Kingdom (n = 44), Portugal (n = 31), and Hungary (n = 29). Prolific demographics, SVO categories, and condition balance are reported in SI Section 1, Table S2.

Experimental design

Participants played 25 repeated rounds in one of three conditions: a PD-only control, an allhuman two-stage PD+SD condition, or a mixed human-bot PD+SD condition. The human sample included N = 430 participants in 82 session groups: 26 PD-only groups (156 humans), 25 all-human PD+SD groups (150 humans), and 31 mixed groups (124 humans plus programmed bots). The PD payoff structure was R = 3, T = 5, P = 1, S = 0. The SD payoff structure was R = 3, T = 5, P = 0, S = 1. Instructions described "multiple rounds" rather than disclosing the finite 25-round horizon. Choice screens displayed the current round number. Participants answered payoff-comprehension questions before grouping, and those who did not pass did not enter the main task.

In two-stage rounds, participants played both PD and SD with the same opponent within a round, and pairings reshuffled across rounds. Before choosing in PD, participants saw the current opponent's previous-round PD and SD choices. Before choosing their SD strategy, participants continued to see those previous-round cues and also saw the current opponent's same-round PD choice.

Human-bot procedure

The mixed condition used four humans and two programmed bots per intended six-player group. At the start of each round, two humans were paired with bots and the remaining humans were paired with each other. Each pair then played both the PD and SD stages of that round together.

Bots cooperated in the PD stage. In the SD stage, a bot mirrored the human partner's same-round PD choice. It cooperated in SD after a human PD cooperation and defected in SD after a human PD defection. If the relevant human PD choice was unavailable because of timeout or missing action, the bot chose randomly. The participant instructions described anonymous random pairing and did not explicitly label whether the current opponent was a bot or a human.

Human measures

After the game, participants completed self-assessment items, the 15-item SVO slider measure (35), and survey items measuring conformity (36) and horizontal/vertical individualismcollectivism (37). The control condition also included a five-item neutralization scale (38).

GPT experiment data

We also analyzed parallel GPT-agent simulations with the same PD-only, all-agent PD+SD, and mixed agent-droid PD+SD conditions. The GPT experiment data contained 533 simulation sessions and 137,400 decision rows across GPT-3.5 Turbo, GPT-4o, and GPT-4o mini at temperatures 0 and 1. GPT droids used the same broad rule as the human-condition bots: cooperate in PD and mirror the opponent's same-round PD action in SD. The GPT experiments included the main behavioral task, but no SVO survey was included in the GPT experiments. We report the prompts, procedural differences, and response-coding procedures in SI Section 9A.

Statistical models

Between-condition comparisons and mixed-group effects used group-level summaries or groupclustered uncertainty because the persistent session group was the independent social-environment unit. Trial-level cue-use models estimated associations with logistic generalized estimating equations and participant-clustered uncertainty. Group-level summaries and participant-plusgroup random-intercept specifications provided social-group-level checks. GPT models clustered by simulation session. GEE models used Statsmodels' independence working correlation and robust sandwich standard errors without a finite-cluster correction. Models used complete cases for the variables in each specification. Human PD-stage models included previous-round opponent PD cooperation, previous-round opponent SD cooperation, condition, and round. Human SDstage models included same-round opponent PD cooperation, previous-round opponent PD cooperation, previous-round opponent SD cooperation, condition, and round.

The human-choice-only bot model restricted the outcome to human decisions in the twostage human conditions. Predictors were interaction category (all-human human-human; mixed human-human without recent bot exposure; mixed human-human after recent bot exposure; mixed human-bot), stage, round, visible previous partner cues, same-round PD cues for SD choices, and group-clustered uncertainty. Recent bot exposure means the participant faced a bot in the immediately preceding round in either stage.

It then estimates partner-SD cooperation from information available before the same-round partner-PD cue, where X q i,j,t includes visible previous-round cues, self-known previous behavior, round, mixedcondition status, and GPT model/temperature indicators where relevant.

It excludes artificialopponent status because decision makers did not observe it. It also excludes the same-round partner-PD cue, O P D j,t , so that this cue can enter the focal SD-choice equation separately. Consequently, ρ res below retains both any expectation shift caused by O P D j,t and any additional social-value association. It must not be interpreted as a belief-adjusted reputational parameter. The model converts the first-stage prediction into the Snowdrift material contrast, and then models focal SD cooperation as logit(p SD,anc i,t ) = α + θ ∆π ˆ︃ ∆π SD i,j,t + ρ res O P D j,t + χC P D i,t +λ P D O P D j,t-1 + λ SD O SD j,t-1 + γ SD C SD i,t-1 + ζ ⊤ W i,j,t.

Here θ ∆π loads the predicted material contrast and ρ res is the remaining same-round partner-PD predictive coefficient. Because qSD i,j,t excludes that cue, ρ res is not adjusted for the full cue-induced expectation environment.
