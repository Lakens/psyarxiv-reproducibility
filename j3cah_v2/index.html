Based on the following text, we would expect an ethics approval statement, but it was not present:

In a cross-sectional survey conducted on Prolific, 299 Asian American and 284 Black American adult participants reported their definitions of ethnicity, race, and nationality using open-ended responses.

Since correlations of r = .10 stabilize at around 250 data points with 80% confidence (Schönbrodt & Perugini, 2013), 300 U.S. Asian Americans and 300 U.S. Black Americans were sampled on Prolific.com for a total sample size of 600 before exclusions to have adequate power for pairwise and correlational analyses.

We restricted our sample to adults residing in the U.S., including U.S. non-citizens, with at least a 95% approval rating on Prolific.com.

These participants were removed from all analyses, as pre-registered on OSF.

Multiracial participants were included in the sample, but were a minority in both the Asian American (n = 5; 1.6%) and Black American samples (n = 9; 3.2%).

That said, because all coders were Asian-identified, our subjective lens and data interpretation could be different from those of a team of multiracial or Black American coders.

The overrepresentation of East Asian participants and how participants were categorized may have influenced the themes that emerged, even though it was not our goal to detect within-sample differences.

Finally, several participants provided elaborate, similar nationality definitions, raising the possibility that they consulted external sources.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Code Check works

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Research on ethnicity, race, and nationality often conceptualizes and measures these identities independently, overlooking their interconnectedness. Perceptions of, and associations among, these identities could differ across racial groups, given distinct migration and racialization contexts in the U.S. Furthermore, nationality has not been thoroughly explored in ethnicity and race research. This study examined how individuals from groups with different migration and racialization histories (i.e., Asian and Black Americans) define ethnicity, race, and nationality. In a cross-sectional survey conducted on Prolific, 299 Asian American and 284 Black American adult participants reported their definitions of ethnicity, race, and nationality using open-ended responses. The definitions were thematically analyzed and reviewed between-and within-group variations. Definitions of ethnicity, race, and nationality shared similarities between samples: ethnicity by cultural components or family and ancestry; race by shared physical, genetic, and biological traits; and nationality by birthplace, belonging, and affiliation. Geographic locations also defined all three identities. However, Asian Americans commonly perceived ethnicity as narrower than race. Black Americans viewed the two identities as interchangeable and more frequently defined race as a social construct. Nationality had more ties to citizenship for Asian Americans than for Black Americans. Improved theorization and measurement of ethnic, racial, and national identity and the explicit inclusion of national contexts when discussing ethnic-racial identities are necessary.

How Asian American and Black American Adults Define Ethnicity, Race, and Nationality: A Qualitative Thematic Analysis

Despite being routinely theorized about, measured, and controlled for in psychological research, ethnicity, race, and nationality can be difficult to define as independent identities, due to overlapping conceptualizations in lay terms and as constructs. We argue that research needs improved conceptualization to address a) the distinct meanings of these identity domains and their permeable boundaries; b) the implied U.S. context in most ethnicity and race research and the subsequent omission of nationality from the broader intellectual discourse; and c) the unique migration and racialization (i.e., the social process of acquiring a racial identity) contexts of social groups in the U.S. Each of these issues contributes to potentially different understandings of ethnicity, race, and nationality among social groups. Approaching ethnicity, race, and nationality as independent identities that uniformly apply across social groups likely leads to fragmented theory and measurement, misleading group comparisons, overlooked within-group heterogeneity, and limited explanatory power for minoritized social groups' experiences (Helms et al., 2005; Schwartz et al., 2014). To move research towards more nuanced conceptualizations of ethnicity, race, and nationality, we examined how individuals from two minoritized groups with distinct migration and racialization contexts in the U.S. (i.e., Asian and Black American adults) understand these identities and their relationships.

Ethnicity, Race, and Nationality: Neither Independent nor Collapsible

Ethnicity often denotes groups sharing ancestry, culture, beliefs, and memories of migration or colonization (e.g., Vietnamese, Somali; Cornell & Hartmann, 2007). Race (e.g., Asian, Black) is a culturally structured, systematic way of understanding and experiencing reality, dividing humans into groups based on physical appearance, sociopolitical factors, and cultural backgrounds (Smedley & Smedley, 2007). Driven by oppression and hierarchy, racial formation is an evolving, dynamic process (Omi & Winant, 2014; Yamamoto, 1995). For example, until 2024, individuals of Middle Eastern descent were federally classified as "White" due to strategies by early immigrants to circumvent discriminatory U.S. policies restricting immigration to "White persons" (Takaki, 1989). Unlike ethnicity and race, nationality is characterized by formal rules, such as a government that sets boundaries and rules for membership. However, informal criteria (e.g., customs) can legitimize one's claim to national membership, and individuals can have allegiance with a nation without formal membership (Schildkraut, 2014). Nationality is also fluid, as the underlying land (and thus the people who live on it), can belong to different nations over time. For instance, the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848) transferred California from Mexico to the U.S., altering its residents' nationality.

While these formal definitions of ethnicity, race, and nationality are conceptually distinct, both lay people and researchers often connect these terms. Their distinctions blur further when ethnicity and nationality become racialized, as seen in the misconception that all Black people are from African nations, or that American-ness equates to White-ness (Devos & Banaji, 2005; Winston & Kittles, 2005). Also, these boundaries shift considerably with sociopolitical contexts: racial bans on citizenship for Black people were forced open after the Civil War, and later for Asian Americans following civil rights struggles throughout the mid-20th century (Takaki, 1989). However, existing research struggles to study ethnicity, race, and nationality in tandem, often treating them as separable. This separationist approach is rooted in Western psychology's empiricist traditions that, in pursuit of scientific credibility, operationalize interconnected psychosocial experiences as discrete, orthogonal constructs (Loevinger, 1957). Consequently, it prevents an intersectional understanding of racialization, such as why ethnic groups in the same racial category can experience discrimination differently (Bhakta, 2022).

With these issues in mind, some scholars have advocated for joint theorization of ethnicity and race, which are commonly entangled in people's minds. For example, immigrant and racially minoritized groups are simultaneously assigned racial labels by their host society and also maintain ethnic identification rooted in their heritage (Rivas-Drake & Umaña-Taylor, 2019). This integration represents a meaningful departure from previous decades of scholarship that treated ethnicity (e.g., Phinney, 1992) and race (e.g., Sellers et al., 1998) as two parallel processes. However, operationalizing ethnicity and race into a single construct often prevents discerning the distinct meanings people ascribe to ethnicity and race and articulating how people construct meaning by comparing and contrasting their ethnicity and race. A methodological example of this limitation is the Ethnic Identity Scale (EIS; Umaña-Taylor et al., 2004), which collapses ethnicity and race into one ethnic-racial identity, maximizing cross-group applicability but limiting insights into how individuals navigate these two distinct axes of identity.

Omission of Nationality and Implied U.S. National Context in Ethnicity and Race Research

Research on ethnicity and race frequently omits nationality, but national contexts determine the status of ethnic and racial groups. For example, Asian Americans are cast as the "perpetual foreigner" in the dominant U.S. narrative (Zou & Cheryan, 2017), rendering their claims to U.S. sociopolitical membership conditional. By comparison, while Black Americans' formal U.S. membership is less questioned, the root of that membership is forced migration and slavery, making Black American's relationship with their nationality strained (Ogbu, 1978).

Furthermore, Black American's civic and social participation remains hindered (Zou & Cheryan, 2017). Therefore, nationality is not a neutral backdrop, as it carries different meanings for different groups, and thus should be meaningfully included in ethnicity and race research.

From a metascience perspective, ethnicity and race research is epistemically bound to the national context in which it is generated. Rooted in settler-colonialism, the U.S. has justified privileges of select social groups by asserting their superiority over other groups (Takaki, 1989).

Early race research in the U.S. served White supremacy, supporting eugenics and claims of innate differences among racial groups to justify systematic mistreatment and oppression of racially minoritized groups (Gould, 1981). Even with the increased focus on ethnicity, culture, and socialization stemmed from the Civil Rights Movement and the shift from conceptualizing race as biologically determined to socially constructed, race continues to have an organizing role in sustaining power in the U.S. (Omi & Winant, 2014). To sum, the U.S. context dictates both the importance of ethnicity and race, and the research generated about them. Ironically, this U.S.-centric focus reinforces the invisibility of nationality in ethnicity and race research, limiting its utility across national contexts and amid shifting demographics in the U.S.

Migration History and Racialization as Contexts for Understanding Identities

Migration and racialization affect understandings of ethnicity, race, nationality, and their relationships. Consider the differing experiences of Asian and Black Americans: Asian migrants arrived in the U.S. for many reasons, including fleeing U.S.-imperialism-induced political turmoil and interethnic conflicts, and pursuing better opportunities (Lee, 2015). Given this migration history, Asian Americans often maintain an ethnic identity separate from their racial identity (e.g., Chinese and Asian; Ruiz et al., 2023). Some also may only identify ethnically and not racially, even though the pan-Asian label emerged as a political construct in the 1960s to represent and unite various ethnic groups with Asian ancestry under a single superordinate category (Takaki, 1989). In contrast, most Black Americans descend from enslaved ancestors, and their ancestral knowledge was systematically erased by the trans-Atlantic slave trade.

However, the "one-drop rule" historically categorized anyone with African ancestry simply as Black (Harris, 1964). In response, a shared history of resistance against oppression has fostered a collective Black diasporic and national identity, making ethnicity and race permeable (Cox & Tamir, 2022). Indeed, Asian Americans identify strongly with their ethnic groups, whereas Black Americans view race as central to their identity (Budiman & Ruiz, 2021).

Additionally, different racialization experiences determine how nationality is negotiated in the U.S. and within a broader transnational context involving individuals' ancestral nation-states. The perpetual foreigner stereotype against Asian Americans, for example, accentuates the boundary between race and nationality, conveniently justifying their exploitation and sociopolitical invisibility (Lee, 2015). Also, Asian Americans who maintain ties with nations besides the U.S. often navigate their national membership in global, transnational processes (e.g., shaping developments in their ancestral nation-states; Zhou & Gatewood, 2000). While the presence of asylum-and opportunity-seeking Caribbean or African migrants suggests similar ties with non-U.S. nations for Black Americans (Rutland & Batalova, 2025a , 2025b), there is a large population of Black Americans for whom connections to non-U.S. national contexts were involuntarily erased. Therefore, there are movements to define Black American nationality as a nation within a nation (e.g., Black nationalism; Robinson, 2001).

Current Research

Ethnicity, race, and nationality are intricately interrelated, and their manifestation and interaction vary across groups. The misconception that these identities and their relationship are constant and distinct across social groups prevents engagement with between-group and within-group differences in a nonessentializing, meaningful, and historically grounded way.

Despite recent movements to integrate race and ethnicity, the omission of nationality and the inadequate conceptualization of these identities in ethnicity and race research remain as critical gaps. To truly center the people at the heart of ethnicity and race research, it is necessary for empirical data to properly document and measure how individuals from different minoritized groups define ethnicity, race, and nationality.

In this study, we examined the lay definitions of ethnicity, race, and nationality by 299 Asian American and 284 Black American adults using codebook thematic analysis while integrating reflexivity. We chose these two groups because of their different migration and racialization patterns. Additionally, Asian Americans are the fastest-growing racially minoritized group in the U.S. but are invisibilized in psychological research (Vinluan & Remedios, 2024; Budiman & Ruiz, 2021). Relatedly, Black Americans are the second largest racially minoritized population in the U.S., but there is limited research on their, as well as Asian Americans', perceptions of ethnicity, race, and nationality holistically. We expected there to be both group similarities and differences in identity definitions and their relationships. Both Asian and Black Americans are considered racial minorities within the U.S., supporting some similarities in how they navigate the connections between ethnicity, race, and nationality. That said, with the differences in racial experiences and migration patterns, we also expected meaningful variations.

Methods

Participants

The sample sizes were determined before any data analysis. Sample size considerations were based on the minimum sample size required for quantitative data analyses, which yield higher sample size requirements than the qualitative data analyses described in this study.

Specifically, a sensitivity analysis was performed on G*Power 3.1. (Faul et al., 2009) using the smallest effect size from a pilot study on ethnic, racial, and national self-labeling and self-identification, and an a priori two-tailed t test for the difference between two independent means can detect an effect size of d = .27 with 80% power at a significance level of α = .05 with a sample size of N = 171 per group. Since correlations of r = .10 stabilize at around 250 data points with 80% confidence (Schönbrodt & Perugini, 2013), 300 U.S. Asian Americans and 300 U.S. Black Americans were sampled on Prolific.com for a total sample size of 600 before exclusions to have adequate power for pairwise and correlational analyses. This sample size satisfies the minimum appropriate sample size for codebook thematic analysis, which ranges anywhere from 5-60 participants depending on the complexity of data, length of survey or interview, and other factors (Wutich et al., 2024).

We restricted our sample to adults residing in the U.S., including U.S. non-citizens, with at least a 95% approval rating on Prolific.com. Participants received $2.67 USD in compensation for a 20-minute study. Of the 602 participants that finished our study, 10 participants failed our attention check, i.e., "How many fatal heart attacks have you had?" The correct answer was zero.

Six participants requested exclusion of their data. These participants were removed from all analyses, as pre-registered on OSF. An additional three participants who did not identify as Asian American or Black were removed, for a final sample of 583 participants.

Participants identified as Asian American (n = 299) or Black (n = 284). Multiracial participants were included in the sample, but were a minority in both the Asian American (n = 5; 1.6%) and Black American samples (n = 9; 3.2%). No participants identified as both Asian American and Black. Participants who identified as Asian American further specified their origin group as East Asian (n = 137; 45.8%), Southeast Asian (n = 89; 29.8%), South Asian (n = 56; 18.7%), West Asian (n = 1; 0.3%), other (n = 1; 0.3%), selected multiple groups (n = 14; 4.7%), or did not respond (n = 1; 0.3%). A majority of participants who identified as Black also identified as African American (n = 195; 68.7%), while a minority identified as Eastern African (n = 4; 1.4%), Central African (n = 1; 0.4%), Southern African (n = 7; 2.5%), Western African (n = 24; 8.5%), Caribbean (n = 8; 2.8%), Afro-Indigenous (n = 5; 1.8%), other (n = 6; 2.1%), selected multiple groups (n = 33; 11.6%), or did not respond (n = 1; 0.4%). The samples were comparable in gender, χ2(4, N = 583) = 6.52, p = .164, and political ideology, t(581) = 0.13, p = .895; however, the Asian American sample was younger, t(580) = -7.34, p < .001, more educated, χ2(5, N = 582) = 27.80, p < .001, and higher-earning, χ2(6, N = 580) = 45.00, p < .001, and had a greater proportion of first-(foreign-born) and second-generation (domestic-born with at least one foreign-born parent) participants, χ2(2, N = 583) = 335.69, p < .001, relative to the Black American sample (Table 1).

Procedure

After passing a botcheck and providing informed consent, participants defined ethnicity, race, and nationality in a random order following this instruction: "People talk about ethnicity, race, and national identity in how they identify. These identities are intertwined but they are also distinct. Ethnic, racial, and national identities can all be in line for some people but may not be the case for others. Briefly, define race, ethnicity, and nationality as you see them. There are no right or wrong answers." After providing their definitions, participants responded to additional exploratory items that were not discussed here. These items included an open-ended question asking participants to detail their "all-encompassing identity," after reading short definitions of ethnicity, race, and nationality for added clarity. Participants also reported whether they self-labeled using ethnic (e.g., Chinese), ethnic American (e.g., Chinese American), pan-ethnic (e.g., Asian), pan-ethnic American (e.g., Asian American), and American labels. In a random order, participants reported their ethnic identification, U.S. identification, and race centrality.

Participants reported their demographics and read a short debriefing form, where they could indicate whether they wanted to exclude their data without affecting their compensation.

Qualitative Data Analysis

We conducted a codebook thematic analysis, using a structured codebook to extract patterns while accounting for individual variation in participant responses (Braun et al., 2019).

Taking a subtle realist epistemological stance, we assumed that social experiences exist independently from the subjective mind but could only be detected and interpreted through lenses shaped by researchers' lived experiences (Hammersley, 1992). Researchers' positionality to the research topic is essential for helping readers evaluate the analytic process and results.

Two research assistants and the first authors (S.L. and D.J.S.) served as coders. The first authors were of East Asian descent: S.L. is a 1.5 generation Chinese American cisgender-woman and D.J.S. is a fifth-generation multiracial Japanese American and Irish American cisgender-man.

The two research assistants were of East (Chinese) and Southeast Asian (Filipino American) descent. Other co-authors (R.M.L. and S.T.J.H.) interpreted and revised the themes during manuscript preparation. R.M.L. is a second-generation Korean American cisgender-man and S.T.J.H. is a U.S. slave-descendant Black American cisgender-woman. This team composition was an asset to data analysis. First, our shared experience with participants as racially minoritized individuals in the U.S., without assuming we fully understood participants' narratives, helped preserve participants' voices during analysis. Second, our varied lived experience with these identity domains, combined with our academic expertise, ensured that participants' responses were considered through different perspectives and prevented essentialization. That said, because all coders were Asian-identified, our subjective lens and data interpretation could be different from those of a team of multiracial or Black American coders.

Thus, the results presented in this paper were one of many possible, valid interpretations; they are bound by the coders' lived expertise and blind spots. Our analysis involved (1) familiarizing ourselves with the data, (2) generating codes, (3) identifying themes, (4) reviewing themes, and (5) defining themes (Braun & Clarke, 2006).

Familiarizing with the Data

For each sample, a spreadsheet was created, containing participants' verbatim definitions.

Coders read through all responses at least once. During data cleaning (e.g., correcting spelling), responses were reviewed again by all coders. Coders met extensively to clarify the analytic goal: to understand how participants defined ethnicity, race, and nationality. The following procedures were conducted separately for each sample.

Generating Codes

For each identity, coders inductively extracted meaning units (basic ideas) from responses (Merriam, 2009). For example, "Ethnicity is about your culture and lineage" contains two meaning units: "Ethnicity is about culture" and "Ethnicity is about lineage." Codes were seldom organized to limit latent interpretations before a holistic understanding of the dataset was developed; only identical meaning units were merged for coding efficiency (e.g., "I am Asian" and "Asian"). To ensure consistent attention to details and depth across coders, S.L. discussed the first 50 coded responses for each identity with each responsible coder besides herself (Belotto, 2018). Finally, coders compiled meaning units into three codebooks, one for each identity.

Identifying, Reviewing, and Defining Themes

Once coders finished generating codes and developed a thorough understanding of the responses, they grouped related meaning units into categories, which were then organized into higher-order themes. The coding team met biweekly to discuss progress codebook development, themes, and diverse views over coding decisions, especially over ambiguous responses.

Generating Hit Counts

Next, coders developed coding manuals based on the codebooks. These manuals retained the category and theme headings, their rationale, and inclusion/exclusion criteria. For each identity, a coder who was not responsible for building the corresponding codebook conducted hit counts using the manual. This second coder reviewed the coding manual, read through all responses, and marked the presence or absence of each category using a dichotomized Yes/No ("1"/blank) scale. Responses were coded for multiple categories under one theme when reflecting multiple connected but distinct ideas. For instance, a participant received two hits for defining ethnicity in terms of cultural themes if they discussed cultural values and practices.

However, multiple meaning units from the same category within a single response were only coded once, to ensure that frequency represented the prominence of unique ideas rather than repeated phrasing. After hit counts were completed for all three identity domains, the coders calculated category-and theme-level frequencies for each sample.

Results

Notable themes were highlighted based on generalizability and ability to convey nuances within and between samples (Table 2). Frequency was a proxy for generalizability, and it was not the sole or superior criterion for selecting themes for reporting (Braun & Clarke, 2006; Braun et al., 2019); therefore, frequency counts were provided in Table 2 but were not discussed in text.

Themes prominent in only one sample also were discussed.

Lay Definition of Ethnicity

Across Asian American and Black American samples, we identified shared themes regarding ethnicity including cultural components; heritage, ancestry, or family; defining ethnicity in relation to race; and country or geographic location. Social traits (Asian American) and shared identity or belonging (Black American) were only prominent in one sample.

Overlapping Ethnicity Themes

Cultural Components. Across samples, cultural elements including language, customs, traditions, religion, values, clothing, food, and other culturally specific characteristics were key for understanding ethnicity ("a person's common cultural-linguistic traits"). Participants viewed ethnicity as rooted in cultural practices ("...cultural practices and beliefs people have and celebrate") and symbolic expressions that distinguish groups ("...cultural identification of different people in the different parts of the world.").

Heritage, Ancestry, or Family. Mentions of heritage, ancestry, or family were common, highlighting generational ties. Participants described ethnicity as passed down in families and embedded in lineage ("your heritage like parental background"). Emphases on ancestral or familial connections to culture, nation, or region suggested a relational view of ethnicity.

Defining Ethnicity in Relation to Race.

Both samples referenced race when defining ethnicity. Some saw ethnicity and race as interchangeable ("ethnicity is similar to race"), while others viewed them as distinct ("someone's race could be Asian, but their ethnicity is Indian") or linked ("similar to race but more focused on cultural belonging"). Multiple Asian American participants characterized ethnicity as a "subcategory" or the "breakdown" of race, conceptualizing ethnicity as more specific or narrow than race. In comparison, while Black American participants described ethnicity as "stemming from" or "based on" race, they were more ambiguous about the property of their relationship (e.g., one being narrower than the other). These patterns reflected shared efforts to define ethnicity through the lens of racial categorization, but the more nuanced understanding of their associations varied.

Country or Geographic Location.

Both samples defined ethnicity by referencing a specific country or region. Some codes mentioned both culture and geography; these were included under this theme when participants clearly conceptualized culture as existing within the confines of a country or geographic area (e.g., "ethnicity is defined by the culture of my country of origin."). This theme highlighted how ethnic identity could be anchored in a physical place.

Ethnicity Themes Prominent in Only One Sample

Social traits or processes (Asian American). Unique to the Asian American sample, the theme social traits or processes captured definitions of ethnicity that emphasized its social nature and function. Participants explicitly used the term "social" when describing ethnicity as a social identity, as a group-based belonging ("…the way we describe the social group…that one belongs to."), or as an identity maintained through interactions or shared experiences ("Ethnicity differentiates on the [basis] of…social events." ; "A term that refers to…experiences shared by a group of people."). This theme underscored the understanding of ethnicity as dynamic and shaped through social processes.

Shared identity or belonging (Black American).

Among Black American participants, this theme represented a sense of collective identity, belonging, and connectedness-being part of something larger than oneself. It included notions of community, systems of inclusion, and the emotional experience of being part of a shared group ("…a source of pride, solidarity, diversity, and belonging for many people."; "…a quality or fact of belonging to a population group or subgroup."). These responses emphasized the social and affective dimensions of ethnicity as a source of solidarity and mutual recognition within this sample.

Lay Definition of Race

Across samples, overlapping themes regarding race included physical, genetic, or biological traits; ancestral traits; region of origin; and social construct. The Asian American sample uniquely emphasized cultural traits, while the Black American sample highlighted the grouping of people and ethnicity.

Overlapping Race Theme

Shared physical, genetic, or biological traits. Participants commonly defined race in terms of physical or biological characteristics, including skin color, facial features, hair texture, and body shape. Responses explicitly referencing physical traits, biological features, or genetic makeup were included in this category. For some participants, race was linked to genetic composition or DNA ("... I am black because the majority of my genetic makeup is identical to the DNA of people from a few different regions in Africa"), indicating a biological understanding of racial categorization.

Ancestral traits.

Participants described race in terms of origin, history, and family background ("…an idea that splits humans into certain groups of origin based on ancestral origin"). Responses often referenced hereditary traits or lineages ("…how one categorizes themselves based on their parents"), and some referred to genetic inheritance passed through family lines ("...categorizing people into distinct groups based on physical characteristics such as skin color, facial features, and hair texture…linked to ancestry, heritage, and shared genetic traits amongst a particular set of people."). This theme was unified by the conceptualization of race as transmitted through ancestry and familial continuity.

Region of origin.

Participants in both samples defined race through regional origins.

Race was understood as reflecting "where one comes from" or the area associated with a particular group's heritage or genetic background. Some participants left the notion of "origin" unspecified, while others tied it to geography or the region from which one's lineage or genetic composition derived ("the different adaptations we've gained based on regional differences").

This theme highlighted the spatial and locational framing of race as a marker of group difference.

Social construct. This theme captured the understanding of race as a human-made, socially-defined concept. Participants described race as created by society, man-made, or socially constructed, reflecting awareness of sociopolitical processes ("...reinforced by social norms and institutions") that shape racial categories and their meanings. For example, one Black participant wrote, "[Race is] a social construct. We are all different colors, even what they call Black people, but I don't think it's a race." Another Asian participant wrote, "...race is primarily a social construct and lacks a universally agreed-upon definition. Different societies and cultures have developed their own ways of categorizing and understanding race." Interestingly, in both samples, this theme often co-occurred with themes like shared physical, biological, or genetic traits (e.g., "…a social construct based on skin color") and a grouping of people (e.g., "…a social construction to help better identify ourselves. For example, I am Black; someone I know is White."). On the other hand, whereas it often co-occurred with themes about discrimination in the Black American sample (e.g., "…a flawed and arbitrary concept that has been used to perpetuate inequality, discrimination, and social hierarchies"), there were no mentions of stereotype, prejudice, racism, or discrimination in responses coded for the social construct theme in the Asian American sample.

Race Themes Prominent in One Sample

Cultural Traits (Asian American). This theme reflected a conceptualization of race grounded in shared culture, independent from (although often concurrent with) biological or geographic features. Participants in this category defined race by common cultural backgrounds ("…the umbrella term for your cultural background." and "…the categorization of people based on common cultural traits."). Notably, mentions of culture in Asian American participants' race definitions were typically nondescript ("cultural identities", "cultural separation", "cultural ancestry"). This vagueness was noticeably different from the multidimensional descriptions seen in the ethnicity definitions. Combined with the fact that these respondents did not link culture to a particular country or nationality, the results suggested an understanding of race as overlapping with, but not reducible to, ethnic or cultural identity.

A Grouping of People (Black American).

This theme mainly included two types of responses. First was the function or purpose of the concept of race: namely, to categorize, classify, or divide people into distinct groups. The second type was more descriptive, identifying specific racial groups (e.g., "Asian," "Black") or explicitly defining race as "groups of people" or "racial gatherings." Together, these responses framed race as a structural and social mechanism for grouping or differentiating individuals.

Ethnicity (Black American).

This theme reflected an understanding of race as closely related to ("race goes hand in hand with ethnicity") or interchangeable with ethnicity ("race is whichever ethnicity you were born into"). Similar to earlier definitions of ethnicity, there was no consensus on how race and ethnicity were linked. One participant wrote, "Race is a more broad term than ethnicity.", whereas another wrote, "Race is similar to me as ethnicity is." The discourse extended to perceived mechanisms that connected or differentiated these two identity domains. Some believed that race was defined by genetically-or phenotypically-determined ethnic membership ("race is what ethnic group you are genetically a part of"); others believed the focus on shared biological characteristics-often along with a weaker focus on culture-was exactly what differentiated race from ethnicity ("race is a bit like ethnicity but I feel like it's slightly different because you can be black but not align with the certain cultures that represent the black community so I think race is more of a color thing"). One participant believed race is phenotypically-based but becomes closely connected to ethnicity because race-based perceptions has social and cultural impact: "Race is phenotype only THOUGH I think it can be considered an ethnicity too since [people] judge you based off your race and you may have certain cultural norms due to this that differ from others." One interpretation of this statement was that they perceived race-based experiences to shape ethnic identities. The codes under this theme indicated that some participants understood race by using ethnicity as a simile (i.e., equate the two), an analogy (i.e., highlight properties of ethnicity that also hold for race), or a reference point for derivation (i.e., use ethnicity as a starting point for conceptualizing race and then differentiate).

Lay Definition of Nationality

Across samples, overlapping themes included region of birth/origin; country of belonging or affiliation; legal and political factors; place of residence; and pledge, duty, and rights. One caveat was that, in this section, a few participants (n < 10) generated elaborate responses similar in structure (e.g., mentioning birth, descent, and naturalization in the same order); most came from the Black American sample. Although our data collection (early 2023) predated the popularization of AI-powered search tools (e.g., Gemini, OpenAI), it was possible that some participants researched the definition of nationality when answering this question. These responses were included for three reasons. First, both samples were well-educated and > 55% of the respondents had attained a college degree or higher, increasing the likelihood of sophisticated civic knowledge. Second, there were no identical responses, and removing responses based on similarity would be antithetical to using a codebook and conducting hit counts. Third, as long as the participants autonomously supplied the definition, they followed the instructions.

Overlapping Nationality Themes

Region of Birth/Origin. Region of birth or origin was a central way of defining nationality. Participants commonly described nationality as a birthright: something determined by where one was born or spent their early life ("If you were born in America, your nationality is U.S. American."). This definition emphasized nationality as fixed and established at the beginning of life, representing one's geographic and social origin ("Nationality can be [a] composite of regional location and national origins…where the person was born.").

Country of Belonging and Affiliation.

Both samples associated nationality with the sense of belonging, affiliation, or emotional connection to a country. Importantly, these meanings extended beyond legal or political status. For instance, statements such as "[Nationality is] where your heart belongs" and "Nationality is the country that you feel a sense of connection with" illustrated an internal, affective dimension of national identity. Participants distinguished between formal membership (e.g., citizenship) and an experiential sense of belonging, highlighting the subjective and identity-based aspect of nationality.

Legal and Political Factors. This theme captured definitions of nationality based on formal legal relationships with a country. Some participants mentioned abstract, general political ties and rights (e.g., "…the legal and political relationship between an individual and a particular nation-state."; "…the legal…cultural identity of a person that belongs to a specific country.").

Other participants referred to specific legal and political affiliations that signify national membership, such as having citizenship or holding a passport. Notably, Asian American participants referenced citizenship much more frequently than Black American participants.

Some responses containing this theme explicitly clarified that the conceptualization of nationality as grounded in legal or political factors may or may not have to do with ethnicity and race. One participant wrote, "Nationality would be of whatever country that one is a legal and permanent citizen of. This applies to a Black, Mexican, or Indian person regardless of ancestry."

In contrast, another participant wrote that, "Nationality is ethnicity." Besides sharing their own definitions, some participants believed nationality's perceived relation to other social identities differs across individuals and contexts. One wrote, "Depending on the laws of the nation, nationality may require ethnic inclusion or could be disconnected [from] any other form of social identity." Another person wrote, "[Nationality is] also wildly misunderstood as being ethnicity by a lot of people." This theme underscored the institutional dimension of nationality as defined through laws and governance structures, which sometimes occurred with different opinions on its tie to other identity domains, such as ethnicity.

Place of Residence.

This theme described where one currently lives or has lived ("...where one is residing"). Some participants emphasized the importance of having been raised or having lived for an extended duration in a country for residence to constitute nationality. This theme reflected an experiential understanding of nationality tied to residency-based familiarity.

Pledge, Duty, and Rights. In both samples, some participants defined nationality in terms of one's duties (e.g., abiding by national laws), responsibilities (e.g., civic participation), and rights (e.g., voting, access to social services). Some participants considered these elements interdependent, viewing rights and duties as mutually reinforcing. For instance, voting was mentioned as both a right of citizenship and a responsibility of national membership. This theme highlighted the civic and moral dimensions of nationality, positioning it as a privilege and an obligation. Notably, this theme was coded much more frequently in the Black American sample than in the Asian American sample. Examination of individual responses with relevant codes in both samples were similar in the degree of specificity and substantiveness.

Discussion

Psychological research and practice have largely treated ethnicity, race, and nationality as separable constructs, a default that constrains both the discipline's explanatory power and the validity of instruments built on it. To explore how this default approach may be revised, we thematically analyzed lay definitions of ethnicity, race, and nationality from 299 Asian American and 284 Black American adults. First, we found similarities and meaningful differences in these groups' identity definitions and perceived relationships among identities. The variations in ethnicity and race definitions may be understood by considering migration and racialization patterns and racial formation as a dynamic process (Omi & Winant, 2014). Many Asian American participants described ethnicity as nested within race because ethnicity may become how individuals differentiate within the "Asian" umbrella category, particularly in contexts where interethnic distinctions are salient (Espiritu, 1992). Conversely, many Black American participants conflated ethnicity and race as synonymous, which may be due to ancestral erasure through slavery and hypodescent (i.e., assigning individuals of multiracial ancestry to the racial group with lower social status), as well as experiencing "Blackness" as a culture rooted in resistance, strengths, and perseverance against racism (Johnson & Carter, 2020; Winston & Kittles, 2005). Similarly, defining race and ethnicity as grouping mechanisms underscored their perceived social functions to stratify and unite people in U.S. institutions and social life (Omi & Winant, 2014). The fact that some Asian American participants viewed race as a social construct but never explicitly named mechanisms that sustain racial hierarchies (e.g., racism) may be emblematic of Asian Americans' invisibilized, conditional minority status as a model minority and perpetual foreigner in the U.S. (Lee, 2015). Differential racialization experiences also help clarify how Asian and Black Americans distinctively defined nationality. For both, nationality included belonging, affiliation, residence, and political-legal elements. But Asian Americans' emphasis on citizenship or legal status and Black Americans' emphasis on civic duty and rights may originate from these groups being deemed foreign and being denied full civic and social participation, respectively (Zou & Cheryan, 2017). Critically, anchoring nationality in legal legitimacy and in civic relations are not mutually exclusive. Both demonstrate that national belonging has to be negotiated and could be weaponized to maintain the racial status quo (Ngai, 2004). Also, because most Asian American participants identified as first-(foreign-born) and second-generation (domestic-born but with at least one foreign-born parent), they likely had personal or vicarious experiences of being barred or discouraged from civic participation (e.g., inability to vote or use social services; Tran, 2018).

Second, we found that ethnicity, race, and nationality are interconnected, with blurred boundaries and content: geographic locations defined all three identities, and ethnicity and race were mutual reference points for defining each other. This finding aligned with research documenting the conflation of ethnicity and race, and the racial implications of nationality (Cornell & Hartmann, 2007; Devos & Banaji, 2005). Because the boundaries among these identities are socially constructed, theorizing them as having fixed or inherent boundaries could lead to fragmented knowledge across disciplinary and regional lines (Brubaker, 2009).

Together, these results called for conceptualizing ethnicity, race, and nationality in tandem. Additionally, Sellers et al. (1998) emphasized that racial identity takes meaning within group-specific contexts, and Yamamoto (1995) described how all racial groups provide dynamic identity reference points for one another. Our findings supported and extended these calls.

Although many responses described prejudice and discrimination, they rarely addressed Whiteness explicitly, suggesting that comparison with a majority group is one of several ways minoritized individuals make sense of their identities. Definitions in our samples were best understood through group-specific contexts, dynamic interactions with multi-group environments, and shared identity-formation processes (Kim, 1999; Phinney, 1992). This pattern underscored the need to balance these contexts and processes while integrating ethnicity, race, and nationality as a unit of analysis, work that remains much needed (Brubaker, 2009).

Implications and Future Directions

Our results have several theoretical implications: first, conceptualizing race, ethnicity, and nationality in tandem can strengthen the literature's explanatory power on U.S.-based civic engagement that varies across ethnic and racial lines. Given the varied definitions of these social identities, considering the shifting ethnic and racial composition of the U.S. population, research should explore the applicability of identity constructs across groups. For example, whereas race centrality is theorized as a crucial component of Black identity (Sellers, 1998), ethnicity centrality may be a more meaningful construct for Asian Americans. Also, an intra-minority perspective could be beneficial for the U.S. context by accounting for intra-minority processes that help maintain the status quo; an example is how Asian Americans are valorized to justify anti-Black racism, while being rendered foreign and civically ostracized compared to White people (Kim, 1999). Contemporary moments of heightened racial tension, including the intensified anti-Asian rhetoric during the COVID-19 pandemic or public discourse around affirmative action, illustrate how narratives about intra-minority relations can be activated to fracture solidarity or to maintain hierarchies (Jagpal & Schumacher, 2022). Critically, if intra-minority interaction is part of how racial meanings are reproduced, then it can also be a mechanism to build intergroup solidarity (Yamamoto, 1995). Although we did not measure intergroup attitudes, coalition behavior, or political participation, these perspectives are raised as implications and directions for future work rather than causal claims.

Second, nation or nationality provides an ecological context in ethnicity-and race-based processes that should be explicitly and extensively examined. Meaningful engagement with participants' identity definitions was made by considering U.S.-specific conditions of migration and racialization, suggesting that the national context shapes identity meaning-making. Recent scholarship has expanded to study ethnicity and race in non-U.S. and transnational contexts (e.g., experiences of racialization after migration; Juang et al. 2020). This trend warrants the explicit inclusion of nationality in ethnicity and race research because every national context is unique in its migration patterns, legislation, media representation, and popular cultural narratives pertaining to social group and hierarchy formation. Cross-national research is most likely to produce valid and relevant knowledge after examining the construct validity of ethnicity, race, and nationality in each new national context where U.S.-based ethnicity and race theories are adopted. A context-sensitive, linked-domains approach helps evaluate when and how U.S.-derived theories travel, require adaptation, or fail to capture local dynamics.

The results also have practical implications. First, they help explain the inconsistent connection between ethnic/racial identity development and well-being across races. Ethnic identity dimensions correlate with well-being and health outcomes, but the effect sizes vary across racial groups (Rivas-Drake et al., 2014; Yip et al., 2019), and ethnic-racial identity's protective effect on mental health after discrimination also varies (Woo et al., 2019; Yip et al., 2019). Construct nonequivalence may explain these variations: these identity domains are not stable with distinguishable boundaries or defined identically across groups (Harachi et al., 2006; Kline, 2005). Scales developed assuming construct equivalence may capture different processes, producing heterogeneous relations with health outcomes. Conceptualizing and measuring these identities as linked may reconcile mixed findings and increase data interpretability.

Second, nationality and racialization appear intertwined. Considering nationality in measures of organizational inclusion, political and institutional messaging, coalition behaviors, and interracial attitudes may clarify how perceived linkage among identity domains influences real-world participation professionally, socially, and civically. Implementation of this idea into practice could look like disaggregating ethnic, racial, and national data-preserving the distinct insights each provides for organizing and advocacy-while avoiding the assumptions that these identities function independently in measurement, clinical, educational, and workplace settings.

Simultaneously, raising public awareness of the socially constructed nature of these identities and developing coalition-building interventions and civic outreach that address intra-minority dynamics within a White-dominant society (e.g., comparative stereotypes, scarcity mindsets), may open door for solidarity and collective action toward equity for all.

Limitations

The Black American sample may not adequately reflect the experience of immigrants and refugees due to limited representation, constraining inferences about how ethnicity, race, and nationality are conceptualized across diverse Black American communities. Similarly, the Asian American sample was overrepresentative of East Asian participants relative to both Southeast Asian and South Asian participants and no ethnic information aside from regional categorization was collected (e.g., Filipino versus Southeast Asian; Vinluan & Remedios, 2024). The overrepresentation of East Asian participants and how participants were categorized may have influenced the themes that emerged, even though it was not our goal to detect within-sample differences. Future research should utilize larger, more diverse samples with more detailed demographic assessments and use additional methodologies (e.g., cognitive interviewing, quantitative methods) to investigate the relationship between lay definitions of different identities, perceptions of their relationships, and downstream outcomes (e.g., civic engagement, mental health, belonging), and capture heterogeneity within each racial group. Finally, several participants provided elaborate, similar nationality definitions, raising the possibility that they consulted external sources. If so, these participants' personal views could not be discerned from academic sources. However, seeking information is a normal and necessary learning activity to develop understandings of social identities and eliminating these responses may eliminate participants who attempted this question in good faith but felt they lacked relevant insights.

Overall, the present study indicates that ethnicity, race, and nationality are intertwined identities whose meanings and boundaries vary across and within racially minoritized groups in patterns that may be historically and socially situated. Attending to these linked meanings has important implications for developing more ecologically valid ethnicity and race frameworks, improving measurement validity, defying the assumed universality or total idiosyncrasy of identity processes, and informing better practices (e.g., programming, education, and healthcare).
