Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. Full text

Abstract

This study examines Jimmy Alcantara's Red ang Luha ni Michael and Blue ang Kobrekama ni Jake through a psychoanalytic lens, focusing on the manifestation of denial, displacement, fantasy, and projection in thoughts, emotions, and behaviors of Ricky. Using a qualitative literary analysis through the literary lens of psychoanalytic approach, the study applies the Hierarchy of Defense Mechanisms and Defense Mechanism Rating Scales Q-Sort (DMRS-Q) framework of Di Giuseppe and Perry (2021) as the basis for identifying and interpreting defensive patterns in the narratives. Object Relations Theory serves as a complementary perspective in examining Ricky's emotional attachment to Michael and the psychological significance of objects associated with their relationship. The findings reveal that Ricky employs denial in resisting threatening realities, displacement in redirecting emotional conflict toward objects and his surroundings, fantasy in constructing an idealized family through Jake, and projection in attributing his psychological conflict to Michael. The analysis further demonstrates that the scarlet paint, transformed apartment, blue bedspread, and Jake transcend their material functions and become symbolic extensions of Ricky's emotional attachment and desire for intimacy, stability, and belonging. Altogether, the findings suggest that Alcantara portrays psychological conflict through the interaction of defensive processes and symbolic attachment, gradually blurring the boundary between Ricky's internal desires and external reality. The study contributes to a psychoanalytic reading of contemporary Filipino literature by demonstrating how defense mechanisms and object relations can illuminate the representation of emotional dependency, attachment, and psychological fragmentation in literary narratives.

INTRODUCTION

Literature has long served as a mirror to human feelings, capturing the essence of sorrow, love, anger, and fear through written expression. For centuries, literature has been used as an avenue to communicate depth of human stories, enabling readers to connect to characters and narratives to a deeply emotional degree [1]. Beyond this, literary texts provide insights into the complexities of the human mind by portraying the unconscious motivations, emotional struggles, and interpersonal relationships that shape human behavior. In line with this, several literary characters have embodied complex psychological experiences allowing readers to examine how unconscious motivations and emotional conflicts have been artistically represented within literary narratives by employing psychoanalytic criticism.

Rooted in the psychoanalytic theories of Sigmund Freud, psychoanalytic criticism posits that human behavior is largely influenced by unresolved conflicts and repressed emotions laid in its unconscious mind [2]. Freud argued that when individuals encounter anxiety or emotional distress, the ego unconsciously employs defense mechanisms to protect itself from psychological pain. These mechanics include denial, displacement, fantasy, and fixation, which allow individuals to cope with excruciating and threatening experiences. Defense mechanisms, as explained by Freud, were aimed to redirect or disguise these conflicts through symbolic behaviors, recurring patterns, and unconscious expressions rather than eliminating the psychological conflict [3]. Consequently, literary texts become valuable sites for examining how characters negotiate emotional trauma through symbolic actions and psychological defenses.

While Freudian defense mechanisms explain how individuals respond to psychological conflict, later psychoanalytic developments provide a deeper understanding of why certain people and objects become emotionally significant. Object Relations Theory developed by Melani Klein and Donald Winnicott, posits that human beings establish profound emotional attachments to significant others, referred to as objects, and that these internalized relationships continue to influence perception, identity, and behavior throughout life [4]. Within literary criticism, this theory suggests that physical objects may acquire symbolic psychological value because they become extensions of interpersonal relationships and emotional security. This perspective complements Freudian psychoanalysis by dissecting how unconscious defenses become attached to symbolic objects that represent love, attachment, and fear of loss.

These psychoanalytic lenses provide a compelling framework for examining Jimmy Alcantar's paired narratives, Red ang Luha ni Michael and Blue ang Kobrekama ni Jake, which portray Ricky and Michael's long-term relationship alongside Ricky's increasingly unstable psychological state. In the first narrative, Ricky's affectionate act of repainting their refrigerator develops into an obsessive transformation of their home, while the sequel depicts his attempt to construct an ideal family through Jake and the symbolic blue bedspread. Across both narratives, objects such as the scarlet pain, transformed apartment, imagined family, and blue bedspread become psychological extensions of Ricky's unconscious desire for intimacy, stability, and emotional security. His responses to rejection, abandonment, and loss likewise reveal unconscious defense mechanisms, suggesting that the narratives extend beyond LGBTQ+ representation toward the psychological processes underlying love and attachment.

Thus, this study investigates how Freudian defense mechanisms and Object Relations Theory are revealed in the characters of Jimmy Alcantara's paired narratives. Specifically it answers to the following queries: 1.

How are Freudian defense mechanisms, particularly denial, displacement, fantasy, and projection, manifested in Ricky's thoughts, emotions, and behaviors in Jimmy Alcantar's Red ang Luha ni Michael and Blue ang Kobrekama ni Jake? 2. How do symbolic objects, specifically the scarlet paint, the transformed apartment, the blue bedspread, and Jake, function as psychological extensions of Ricky's emotional attachment to Michael through the perspective of Object Relations Theory? 3. How do Freudian defense mechanisms and Object Relations Theory collectively explain Ricky's psychological conflicts and emotional attachment as portrayed across the paired narratives?

RESEARCH METHODS USED

This study employs qualitative research using literary analysis on the psychoanalytic approach of Jimmy Alcantara's Red ang Luha ni Michael and Blue ang Kobrekama ni Jake. The analysis primarily uses the four (4) criteria of Freudian defense mechanisms based on psychological responses of Ricky namely: denial, displacement, fantasy, and projection, as avenues in dissecting his emotional conflicts and repressed behaviors. Further, Object Relations Theory serves as a complementary framework in interpreting Ricky's emotional attachment to Michael and the psychological significance of objects associated with their relationship.

The researchers conducted repeated close readings of the literary texts and identified relevant passages that demonstrate the four selected defense mechanisms. These textual instances were categorized according to hierarchical defense mechanisms scale of Di Giuseppe and Perry (2021) and interpreted within their narrative contexts. The analysis focused on characterization, behavior, symbolism, and narrative events without making clinical diagnoses of the literary characters in the paired narratives.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION OF THE TEXT

Rejecting Reality: Ricky's Denial in the Face of Emotional Threat

Denial emerges as a significant defense mechanism in the paired narratives of Jimmy Alcantara, particularly in Ricky's persistent refusal to acknowledge aspects of reality that threaten his emotional attachment to Michael. Di Giuseppe and Perry (2021) describe denial as a defense mechanism that enables the individual to refuse acknowledging an aspect of external reality of personal experiences that would otherwise be apparent to others. The two narratives reveal Ricky's denial as a progressive event as a way of preserving his preferred version of his relationship and domestic life with Michael.

In the first narrative, Red ang Luha ni Michael, Ricky was already established as a character whose perception does not correspond entirely with the perceptions of those around him. At the beginning of the narrative, Ricky explains that he and Michael have been together for six years and that their relationship is 'difficult to define' specifically saying, "di kami apektado kung di man namin ma-define ang relasyon namin." He presents their shared life as natural and harmonious despite acknowledging the fact that their relationship does not conform to a conventional category. Significantly, Ricky has previously lost his work as a production designer because others consider his ideas and use of colors irrational. This detail becomes important because it establishes that Ricky's unusual perception of color predates the central incident involving the red paint.

Initially, the response of Ricky to Michael's casual complaint about their aging refrigerator demonstrates an early form of denial. Michael merely remarks that the refrigerator is becoming rusty and that he feels embarrassed when visitors see it. This reaction from Michael was taken differently by Ricky, seeing it as an opportunity to surprise Michael by painting the refrigerator with a scarlet red paint. He convinces himself that Michael will appreciate the change, describing red as dramatic, romantic, and something Michael would "like." Ricky consequently interprets his own desire as the accurate understanding of Michael's desire. His interpretation becomes increasingly detached from Michael's actual response.

The denial becomes more evident when the act of painting expands beyond the refrigerator. Ricky initially intends only to repaint the appliance, but he progressively paints the cupboards, sink, lampshade, furniture, door, ceiling fan, shoes, garden objects, and other parts of the house.

He even recognizes moments when he should stop, telling himself, "Ayoko na, tama na," yet immediately supplies another justification for continuing: the house would look incomplete if only one part were red. This statement becomes a manifestation of the recognition of excessiveness of Ricky's behavior but suppresses the implication of that recognition by producing another explanation.

The strongest manifestation of denial occurs when Michael finally returns and reflects the transformed environment. This event prompts Michael to leave and tells Ricky that he is becoming insane, yet Ricky does not respond by reconsidering his behavior. Instead, he continues to interpret Michael's reaction through the framework of his own reality. When Michael says that he will leave and return to a friend in Fairview, Ricky remains concerned with the wet paint on Michael's luggage. More strikingly, when Michael accused him, "Totoo ngang nababaliw ka na," Ricky does not acknowledge the accusation. He responds by painting Michael's tears read. This act symbolically converts Michael's emotional rejection into another element of Ricky's red world, thereby allowing Ricky to preserve his preferred interpretation of the relationship.

The same defense becomes more explicit in the second narrative, Blue ang Kobrekama ni Jake. After Michael confronts Ricky about his behavior and eventually declares, "Hindi na ito anxiety, this is insanity," Ricky responds, "Magaling na ako. There is nothing wrong with me. Si Michael ang nababaliw." Here, denial becomes unmistakably present due to Ricky's rejection of his condition and reverses the accusation onto Michael. Instead of recognizing the conflict between his perception and Michael's, Ricky maintains his perception of self-stability and that Michael is the one who has become irrational.

Thus, denial functions across the paired narratives as a psychological barrier protecting Ricky from realities that threaten his emotional security. In Red ang Luha ni Michael, denial allows him to preserve his belief that his transformation of the home they shared is an expression of love that Michael must appreciate. Consequently, in the second narrative, it enables him to reject the possibility that his perception of Jake and his family life is fundamentally different from Michael's perception. The progression suggests that denial becomes increasingly consequential: what begins as resistance to Michael's rejection of the red environment eventually becomes resistance to the distinction between internal reality and the external world.

Painting Away the Pain: Ricky's Displacement of Emotional Conflict

Displacement is evident throughout Jimmy Alcantara's paired narratives as Ricky repeatedly redirects emotional energy arising from his relationship with Michael toward objects, spaces, and activities that he can more easily control. In psychoanalytic theory, displacement refers to the transfer of an emotion, impulse, or response from its original and potentially threatening target to a less threatening substitute. Di Giuseppe and Perry (2021) identify displacement as one of the defense mechanisms through which individuals manage psychological conflict by shifting emotional responses away from their original source. From this perspective, Ricky's fixation on painting may be understood not simply as an artistic preference but as a behavioral outlet through which interpersonal tensions are transformed into controllable material actions.

The process initially emerges from Michael's complaint about their rusty refrigerator. Although Michael's concern is relatively ordinary and practical, Ricky interprets it as an opportunity to demonstrate his affection, thinking, "Ito na ang pagkakataon para sorpresahin si Mike." Instead of addressing the concern through direct communication or simply replacing the damaged appliance, Ricky invests himself in repainting it. His choice of scarlet red gives the action an emotional dimension because the refrigerator becomes associated with his desire to please Michael. The appliance consequently shifts from being an ordinary household object to becoming a target for Ricky's emotional investment.

The displacement becomes more apparent when Ricky's activity extends beyond the refrigerator. After experiencing satisfaction from painting it, he begins repainting other parts of the household, including cupboards, the sink, lampshades, picture frames, flower pots, plants, and other objects. His admission, "Naa-addict na ako sa ginagawa ko," signals an important change in behavior. What began as a response to Michael's dissatisfaction develops into a self-sustaining activity. The original interpersonal concern becomes increasingly distant as Ricky transfers his emotional energy toward transforming the physical environment.

This expansion is particularly significant because many of the objects Ricky paints are connected to Michael. His decision to paint Michael's slippers, bags, belts, and other belongings demonstrates that the objects have acquired emotional significance through their association with his partner. This interpretation may be strengthened through Object Relations Theory, which emphasizes the psychological importance of internalized relationships and the ways individuals invest in people and associated objects with emotional meaning. Thomas et al. (2023) explain that object relations perspectives examine how relationships with significant others become internalized and subsequently influence emotional experiences and behavior. Thus, Ricky's manipulation of objects associated with Michael can be interpreted as an attempt to preserve an external representation of the relationship.

The scarlet paint consequently functions as more than a decorative medium. It becomes a vehicle through which Ricky externalizes and manages feelings that are difficult to negotiate directly within the relationship. By repeatedly applying the same color to objects connected to Michael and their shared domestic life, he creates a physical environment that reflects his preferred emotional reality. The act of painting therefore provides Ricky with a sense of agency: unlike interpersonal relationships, objects can be altered according to his wishes. The physical environment becomes controllable in a way that Michael's feelings and decisions are not.

The displacement becomes increasingly pronounced as Ricky begins painting objects that have little or no connection to the original problem. His activity expands to the garden, plants, telephone, and eventually even the postman. At this point, the behavior has moved substantially away from Michael's initial complaint about the refrigerator. Ricky's explanation that he paints the postman's face so that he will not appear anemic further illustrates how his preferred aesthetic reality begins to govern his perception of the world. The narrative thus presents a progressive movement from a specific interpersonal stimulus toward increasingly unrelated targets. Psychoanalytically, this progression suggests that the painting activity has become a means through which Ricky manages an emotional tension that can no longer be contained within the original situation.

The most significant manifestation occurs when Michael himself becomes incorporated into Ricky's symbolic world. After returning to the transformed apartment, Michael reacts with shock and distress and attempts to leave. Rather than responding to Michael's emotional reaction through direct communication, Ricky paints Michael's tears red. This moment marks a significant transformation in the pattern of displacement. The emotional response that should become the subject of interpersonal communication is instead absorbed into Ricky's established symbolic system. Michael's sadness is no longer treated as an independent emotional reality; it becomes another element that Ricky can transform through the color red. The action consequently demonstrates how Ricky's psychological strategy has shifted from modifying objects to modifying the visible expression of another person's emotions.

The second narrative reinforces this pattern by showing Ricky repeatedly altering Michael's domestic environment. Changes involving the living room, component system, bedsheets, and pillowcases suggest that the manipulation of physical surroundings has become an established means through which Ricky negotiates emotional tension. The refrigerator is particularly significant because it becomes a contested object: what initially served as an expression of Ricky's affection eventually becomes a source of conflict when Michael attempts to remove the paint. The object therefore embodies the changing nature of their relationship. Its transformation reflects Ricky's attachment, while Michael's attempt to restore it reflects his resistance to the reality that Ricky has constructed.

From an Object Relations perspective, this conflict illustrates the psychological significance that ordinary objects may acquire when they become associated with emotionally important relationships. The refrigerator, household belongings, beddings, and other domestic objects are not significant merely because of their material functions; within Ricky's psychological world, they become connected to his relationship with Michael. His repeated attempts to transform them can therefore be understood as attempts to maintain an external representation of attachment. By controlling the objects surrounding the relationship, Ricky symbolically attempts to preserve a sense of relational stability that he cannot guarantee through Michael himself.

Across the paired narratives, displacement therefore operates as both a psychological defense and a narrative device. Ricky's emotional conflicts are seldom confronted directly. Instead, they are transferred into colors, household objects, domestic spaces, personal belongings, and eventually representations of Michael's own emotional experience. The red paint becomes an external manifestation of an internal struggle: Ricky attempts to make the external world correspond to the emotional reality he desires. However, this strategy produces a paradox. The very mechanism that initially allows Ricky to express affection and manage emotional tension gradually distances him from Michael because the symbolic world he creates increasingly takes precedence over Michael's actual feelings and perceptions.

Thus, displacement in the paired narratives does not simply demonstrate Ricky's tendency to redirect emotion toward objects. It reveals a deeper psychological struggle between the desire to preserve attachment and the inability to control another person's emotional reality. Ricky can repaint a refrigerator, transform an apartment, and alter objects associated with Michael, but he cannot determine whether Michael will accept the world he has created. His increasing investment in controllable objects consequently reflects an attempt to compensate for the uncontrollable nature of human attachment. Through the combined perspectives of Freudian psychoanalysis and Object Relations Theory, the red-painted environment can therefore be interpreted as an externalized psychological space in which Ricky attempts to preserve, control, and materialize a relationship that is increasingly unstable in reality.

Creating the Family: Ricky's Retreat into Fantasy

Fantasy becomes the most prominent defense mechanism in Blue ang Kobrekama ni Jake, where Ricky constructs and sustains an imagined family life that compensates for emotional needs that cannot be adequately satisfied in his actual relationship with Michael. Di Giuseppe and Perry (2021) describe autistic or schizoid fantasy as the use of excessive fantasy as a substitute for human relationships, direct action, or problem solving. Fantasy may provide temporary gratification by allowing an individual to imagine a solution to an interpersonal conflict without confronting the actual difficulty. This concept is particularly applicable to Ricky's treatment of Jake, whose presence allows him to construct an idealized family that increasingly diverges from Michael's perception of reality.

The foundation for this fantasy is established before Jake's arrival. Ricky explains that he and Michael cannot have children and describes their inability to have a child as a consequence of their circumstances. After nearly six years of living together, however, Michael proposes adoption: "Ampon tayo, gusto mo?" Ricky immediately agrees. The adoption therefore becomes a concrete possibility for fulfilling a previously unfulfilled aspect of their relationship. Yet Ricky's subsequent behavior demonstrates that he invests the child with meanings that extend beyond the actual act of adoption.

When Ricky brings the child home, the baby is immediately incorporated into his imagined family structure. The child is placed in a prepared bed with a blue bedspread, and Ricky describes the baby as resembling Michael. He anticipates that Michael will be happy because the child is a boy, which is what he believes Michael wants. Ricky then addresses the baby directly and assigns him the name Jake. More significantly, he tells the baby, "Kami na ang mga magulang mo" and insists that the child is no longer an adopted child but belongs to them. These declarations demonstrate how quickly Ricky transforms the child from an adopted infant into the embodiment of an ideal family.

Ricky's fantasy becomes especially evident in his insistence that Jake's identity should conform to the family he imagines. He tells the baby to think of himself as having come from them and explains that they are his parents. The statement "Hindi ka ampon" is particularly significant because the reality of adoption is replaced with a more emotionally satisfying narrative of biological and familial belonging. The fantasy therefore does not simply invent a child; it reconstructs the meaning of the child's existence so that the child can occupy the precise emotional role Ricky needs.

Michael's response exposes the conflict between Ricky's internal fantasy and external reality.

Although Ricky interprets Michael's emotional response to the child as evidence of happiness, Michael eventually rejects the arrangement and tells Ricky, "isoli mo siya, hindi natin kailangan ng kasama." Ricky does not accept this statement. Instead, he dismisses Michael's reaction as another instance of "drama" and continues caring for the baby as though the family he imagines remains intact. This is important because fantasy becomes a substitute for direct engagement with the actual conflict. Ricky does not negotiate the disagreement with Michael; he retreats further into the imagined family.

The narrative's final revelation makes this defense particularly powerful. During the confrontation, Michael takes the baby from Ricky and throws it to the floor. Ricky experiences the event as the destruction of his child and responds emotionally to the injured body. He speaks to the baby, asking it to open its eyes and tell him that it loves its daddy. Only after this emotional scene does the narrator reveal, "Nangiti ako. Ibinalik ko ang mga baterya sa dating ayos." The batteries retroactively establish that Jake is not the living child Ricky believes him to be but an object that Ricky has incorporated into his psychological reality.

The revelation transforms Jake into the clearest representation of fantasy in the paired narratives.

The object becomes a substitute for the interpersonal relationship Ricky desires but cannot secure. Through Object Relations Theory, Jake may therefore be interpreted as more than an imaginary child: he becomes a symbolic object through which Ricky attempts to preserve an idealized relationship with Michael. The blue bedspread reinforces this interpretation because the color and domestic setting establish an environment associated with the peaceful family life Ricky wishes to construct.

Consequently, fantasy in the narrative functions not simply as escapism but as a psychological substitute for an unattainable interpersonal reality. Ricky's imagined family temporarily provides the intimacy, belonging, and companionship he fears losing. However, because the fantasy increasingly replaces rather than negotiates external reality, it ultimately deepens his isolation. Alcantara thus uses Jake to demonstrate how an internally constructed emotional world can become more compelling to Ricky than the reality recognized by everyone around him.

Blaming the Other: Ricky's Projection of Inner Conflict onto Michael

Projection is manifested most clearly in Ricky's attribution of his own psychological disturbance to Michael, particularly in the latter part of Blue ang Kobrekama ni Jake. According to Di Giuseppe and Perry (2021), projection occurs when an individual attributes one's own unacknowledged feelings, impulses, or thoughts to another person. In Ricky's case, projection becomes evident when his perception of Michael increasingly incorporates the very psychological instability that the narrative has been establishing in Ricky himself.

A significant basis for this interpretation appears in the first story. Ricky has already been described as someone whose artistic ideas and use of color have been regarded by others as irrational. He reports losing his work as a production designer because his employers considered his colors and concepts unacceptable and referred to him as "nahihibang." Yet Ricky narrates these events without critically examining the possibility that others' perceptions might contain some truth. Instead, he maintains his own interpretation of his behavior.

This pattern intensifies when Michael confronts him in Red ang Luha ni Michael. After seeing the completely transformed apartment, Michael tells Ricky that he is becoming insane and says that he will return him to the basement. Ricky does not explicitly acknowledge Michael's assessment. Instead, he responds by painting Michael's tears red. The act itself may be read primarily as displacement, but it also establishes the narrative condition necessary for projection: Ricky consistently treats Michael's responses as the problem while preserving his own perception as reasonable.

The clearest evidence emerges in Blue ang Kobrekama ni Jake. As Michael becomes increasingly distressed by Ricky's behavior and eventually attempts to remove the baby from the house, Ricky interprets Michael's actions as evidence that Michael himself has become irrational. When Michael declares, "Hindi na ito anxiety, this is insanity," Ricky immediately responds: "Magaling na ako. There is nothing wrong with me. Si Mike ang nababaliw." The structure of this response is significant. Ricky first denies the possibility that anything is wrong with him and then attributes the psychological problem to Michael.

This moment corresponds closely to the function of projection described by Di Giuseppe and Perry (2021), in which the individual externalizes psychological material that is difficult to acknowledge internally. Ricky's accusation that Michael is "the one going crazy" is especially revealing because the narrative has provided substantial evidence of Ricky's increasingly distorted perception: he interprets an object as his child, treats it as a living infant, and maintains that it belongs within his family even when Michael rejects the interpretation.

Ricky's projection also appears in his interpretation of Michael's motives. After Michael rejects Jake, Ricky interprets his partner's response as unreasonable hostility toward the child. He asks, "Di ba sabi mo'y okay ang bata sa bahay? Ngayon, bakit ka nag-iingay?" Rather than interpreting Michael's distress as a reaction to Ricky's behavior, Ricky frames Michael as the source of the conflict. Later, when Michael becomes aggressive and attempts to remove Jake, Ricky fears that Michael has also become mentally unstable: "huwag mong sabihing nababaliw na rin si Mike, huwag mong sabihing nahawaan ko siya." The latter statement is particularly interesting because it briefly introduces self-awareness-Ricky recognizes the possibility that his own instability has influenced the situation-but he quickly returns to viewing Michael as the one who is "crazy."

Projection therefore works together with denial. Denial prevents Ricky from acknowledging his own psychological disturbance, while projection allows him to relocate that disturbance onto Michael. The two defenses consequently reinforce one another. Ricky can maintain the belief that he is "well" precisely because the perceived problem has been transferred to someone else.

However, the textual evidence for projection should be treated more cautiously than that for denial, displacement, and fantasy. Ricky's statement that Michael is "crazy" can also be read as an extension of denial rather than pure projection. The distinction rests on whether the accusation is interpreted as Ricky attributing his own unacknowledged psychological state to Michael. For this reason, projection should be presented as a secondary but significant manifestation, rather than claiming that every instance of Ricky's disagreement with Michael constitutes projection.

Taken together, the evidence demonstrates that projection contributes to the progressive collapse of Ricky's relationship with external reality. By attributing his own disturbance to Michael, Ricky preserves the internal world he has constructed around their relationship. Michael consequently becomes not only the person Ricky fears losing but also the person onto whom Ricky transfers the psychological conflict he cannot acknowledge within himself.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, the analysis demonstrates that Jimmy Alcantara's Red ang Luha ni Michael and Blue ang Kobrekama ni Jake portray Ricky's psychological conflict through the defense mechanisms of denial, displacement, fantasy, and projection, which shape his responses to rejection, emotional insecurity, and the fear of losing Michael. His denial allows him to resist threatening realities, while displacement redirects his emotional conflict toward the objects and spaces associated with his relationship; fantasy enables him to construct an idealized family through Jake, and projection allows him to externalize his own psychological conflict onto Michael. Complementing these mechanisms, Object Relations Theory reveals how the red paint, domestic space, blue bedspread, and Jake acquire psychological significance as extensions or representations of Ricky's attachment to Michael. Taken together, the findings suggest that Alcantara uses these symbolic objects and defensive responses to portray the gradual entanglement of Ricky's internal desires with external reality, illustrating how an intense need for attachment and emotional security can transform ordinary objects into substitutes for human relationships and ultimately contribute to psychological fragmentation.
