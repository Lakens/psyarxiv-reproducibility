Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Memory is an adaptive cognitive function that enables individuals to use past experiences to guide future behavior. Prior research has demonstrated that goal-relevant, or rewarded, items are remembered more effectively, but the underlying mechanisms-ranging from neuromodulation of synaptic strength to increased semantic elaboration-remain debated. In this study, we investigated how different reward structures influence the temporal organization of free recall in mixed-item lists. Rewards were administered either during item encoding or were contingent on successful later recall. Our behavioral results reveal a striking mid-list primacy effect: when rewards are contingent on later recall, participants disproportionately initiate search at the onset of rewarded subsequences, effectively treating reward shifts as privileged access points. To account for these findings, we developed a novel hierarchical Bayesian implementation of the context maintenance and retrieval (CMR) model of free recall. By fitting the model to individual recall sequences, we adjudicated between competing accounts of memory enhancement. Our results demonstrate that motivated memory relies on two key mechanisms: the strengthening of context-to-item associations and the creation of accessible event boundaries. These findings suggest the mid-list primacy effect involves the reinstatement of a privileged context rather than solely the enhanced encoding of early list items. With regard to executive control and event segmentation theories, this suggests that participants exercise fine-grained control over their mental context, perhaps through various mnemonic techniques. We propose that motivated memory is not merely a product of enhanced associative binding, but also involves active, structured (re)organization of experience.

1. INTRODUCTION

Experiences carrying significance for future behavior are remembered more robustly than neutral ones. A large body of work links the learning of goal-relevant information to a dopaminergic reinforcement learning system that promotes the learning of behaviorally relevant, rewarding experiences (Dolan & Dayan, 2013; Schultz, 2015). However, reinforcement learning mechanisms typically rely on repeated exposure to reward before stable learning occurs (Niv, 2009; Sutton & Barto, 2018). By contrast, many adaptive decisions depend on remembering single episodes, a function that is served by the episodic memory system (Bornstein & Norman, 2017; Gershman & Daw, 2017). For the episodic memory system to contribute to adaptive behavior, it needs to preferentially encode and retrieve valuable information. This may happen through two distinct routes-a more automatic process that strengthens memory in the presence of salient events, and a more strategic process that applies more effective encoding strategies when information is deemed important (Knowlton & Castel, 2022). Here, we present experimental and computational evidence that people exercise fine-grained control over the temporal organization of memory when motivated by reward.

Reward modulation may occur via neuromodulatory signals that facilitate long-term potentiation in the hippocampus (Ergo et al., 2020; Miendlarzewska et al., 2016; Rouhani et al., 2023; Shohamy & Adcock, 2010). Behaviorally, this manifests as enhanced memory for items associated with reward anticipation or high incentive salience (Jang et al., 2019; Rouhani & Niv, 2021). Beyond simple strengthening, these signals may influence the organization of memory by promoting event segmentation (Rouhani et al., 2024). For instance, the release of norepinephrine in response to salient or surprising items can trigger event segmentation, signaling the end of one temporal unit and the start of another (Clewett et al., 2020; Nassar, 2024). Within these events, dopaminergic signaling may promote the integrative encoding of related stimuli (Clewett & Murty, 2019).

These reward effects are typically demonstrated in recognition and paired-associate learning paradigms, where strong retrieval cues are provided at test, potentially masking the contribution of more strategic encoding and search processes that flexibly link value to memory organization. In contrast, modulation of episodic memory organization has most often been examined in free recall paradigms, which place minimal constraints on retrieval cues and thus provide a sensitive window into the organization of memory.

Prioritization of high-value information often comes at the cost of low-value items. In the value-directed remembering paradigm, recall probability increases with item value (Castel, 2007; Stefanidi et al., 2018). This effect has mainly been attributed to strategic processes, such as deeper semantic processing or the use of interactive imagery (Cohen et al., 2014; Knowlton & Castel, 2022). Notably, this effect disappears on "pure" lists where all items are either high or low value, suggesting that reward does not merely enhance memory globally but instead drives competitive dynamics during encoding or search (Talmi et al., 2021; see also Popov & Dames, 2023).

These dynamics can be formally characterized within the retrieved context framework (Howard & Kahana, 2002), in which encoding involves binding items to a gradually evolving context representation, and retrieval proceeds by reinstating the context associated with recalled items, thereby guiding memory search along learned associations (Sederberg et al., 2008). By incorporating additional source information into the context (Polyn et al., 2009a) this can provide an explanation for why rewarded memoranda are preferentially recalled: reward signals may increase encoding strength or serve as a salient source cue that biases the search process (Horwath et al., 2023; Talmi et al., 2019 Talmi et al., , 2021)). Importantly, it is not clear if these effects depend on whether memorization is instrumental for reward, or whether the saliency of rewarding information is itself sufficient to yield these effects.

Reward can also fundamentally reshape the initiation of memory search (Stefanidi et al., 2018). In standard free recall, participants typically exhibit a primacy effect: the tendency to recall initial list items first. Originally, within the retrieved context framework, this was interpreted as enhanced encoding of early list items (Polyn et al., 2009a; Sederberg et al., 2008). Alternative theories exist, for example emphasizing temporal distinctiveness of early list items (Brown et al., 2007). However, there is increasing support for the idea that a dedicated start-of-list context representation can be formed during encoding, and reinstated at the start of the retrieval phase (Davelaar et al., 2005; Farrell, 2012; Healey & Wahlheim, 2024; Kragel et al., 2015; Lehman & Malmberg, 2013; Morton & Polyn, 2016; Osth & Farrell, 2019). This mechanism is a cornerstone of models of short-term memory and related serial recall paradigms (Brown et al., 2000; Burgess & Hitch, 2005; Henson, 1998; Lewandowsky & Farrell, 2008; Lewandowsky & Murdock, 1989) providing a theoretical bridge between models of free recall and serial recall (Gunn & Polyn, 2025; Logan, 2021; Logan & Cox, 2021; Lohnas, 2024; Ward & Beaman, 2025).

Importantly, from the perspective of retrieved context models, primacy initiation can be considered strategic. Recall is most effective when it is initiated from the beginning of a studied list and proceeds forward-a strategy adopted by highly successful participants (Romani et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2023). This type of cueing is also effective in naturalistic memory, where experiences are organized into temporally persistent events separated by discrete boundaries that serve as access points, guiding retrieval of the associated episodes (Michelmann et al., 2023). We suggest that strategic encoding techniques, such as interactive imagery or story generation, can shape how events are formed and where boundaries are placed, allowing participants to structure recall and enhance accessibility-principles that could also govern the prioritization of high-value or rewarded items.

If primacy reflects the initiation of recall from a privileged context state, then such effects need not be restricted to the beginning of an entire list. Instead, any segment of experience that is treated as a coherent and behaviorally relevant unit may function as a locally privileged starting point for memory search. In this sense, sequences of items associated with reward may resemble the onset of an event, motivating participants to establish a new contextual anchor and to preferentially initiate retrieval from that point. This predicts a mid-list primacy effect, in which recall disproportionately begins with the first item of a rewarded subsequence, even when that subsequence occurs in the middle of a longer list. By fitting retrieved context models to recall sequences, we can directly compare whether these effects are better described by enhanced encoding or contextual control, informing the broader debate about whether primacy effects reflect encoding gradients or contextual reinstatement.

Importantly, reward can also be delivered in ways that are salient and affectively engaging without instrumentally depending on memorization of the related information. To dissociate the effects of reward depending on successful memorization from more general effects of affective salience, we included a control condition in which participants received reward contingent on an immediate semantic classification of each item. In this condition, reward cues remained visually salient and reliably predicted monetary outcomes, but reward itself did not instrumentally depend on memorization of the item. From the perspective of retrieved context models, such a manipulation might produce salience-related effects on item processing that may manifest as a separate source context (Polyn et al., 2009a; Talmi et al., 2019) or a general increase in encoding strength (Horwath et al., 2023). Comparing this condition to recall-contingent reward therefore allows us to test the degree to which changes in recall organization depend on an instrumental relationship between reward and successful memorization, rather than general reward-related salience or arousal.

Understanding these mechanisms is particularly important because age-related memory decline disproportionately affects automatic encoding processes, whereas older adults often retain the ability to exert strategic control over memory (Castel et al., 2012). Identifying how reward and event structure interact with strategic control may reveal ways in which memory can be optimized despite overall reductions in encoding efficiency. From this perspective, studying reward-induced mid-list primacy and context reinstatement provides insight not only into fundamental mechanisms of memory organization, but also into potential avenues for mitigating memory decline.

The aims of the current paper are twofold. First, while prior work suggests that sourcecontext organization is a primary mechanism for reward-driven memory, we propose that this is not the only route (see also Horwath et al., 2023). By simulating control over contextual anchoring and recall initiation, we aim to show that memory organization is highly flexible, allowing participants to exploit environmental structure in service of goal-directed retrieval. More generally, we use our reward manipulation to inform a fundamental debate regarding the nature of the primacy effect as modeled within the retrieved context framework: is it driven by forming stronger associations for early list items during encoding, or do participants explicitly search for early list items when initiating recall? By analyzing recall sequences with retrieved context models, we use reward-induced mid-list primacy to distinguish between these competing mechanisms. Our results provide strong evidence for mid-list primacy for retrieval-contingent reward, best explained by tab divider-like markers in temporal context, enabling later recall to be guided according to these markers. Furthermore, we find that overall recall improvements also reflect encoding enhancements and source-context organization, conceptually replicating and extending earlier work.

2. METHODS

>METHODS

2.1 Experimental design

Thirty participants (age 18-45 years; M = 23.3, SD = 6.6) were recruited through the Vanderbilt University SONA platform. All procedures were approved by the Vanderbilt University Institutional Review Board, and informed consent was obtained prior to participation. The experiment was implemented in JsPsych (de Leeuw, 2015) and conducted in the laboratory. Participants received $20/hour plus up to an additional $10 per session based on task performance, with compensation provided in the form of Amazon gift cards. One participant was excluded for failing to comply with task instructions (not completing the distractor math problems between encoding and retrieval), leaving a final sample of 29 for analysis.

2.1.1 Free recall task

Participants were presented with lists of words and asked to remember these words. The words were drawn without replacement from a pool of 624 items that had been unanimously rated by a separate group as either smaller or larger than a shoebox (Diachek et al., 2023). During the presentation of the words, participants had to perform an encoding task, pressing the "F" or "J" key to indicate whether the word represented a smaller or larger object. After each list, participants completed a 15 s integer addition task, generating sums of numbers chosen randomly from the range of 10 to 50. Participants could type the answer into a text box and submit their answer with the <enter> key. Following the math distractor, participants were given 50 s to recall as many words as possible in any order by typing them into a text box and submitting with <enter>.

2.1.2 List construction

Task performance was rewarded. Participants could earn points, with every 10 points corresponding to $0.05 bonus payment at the end of the experiment. Participants always earned at least one point for correctly performing the encoding task on each word at study, one point for every correctly solved math problem, and one point for recalling each word at test.

In total, participants completed 16 trials, each consisting of a 20-word study list, a math distractor period, and a free recall period. For each list, we manipulated the reward contingencies so that some study words would earn 10 points rather than only 1 point (Fig 1A). These increased rewards were offered in contiguous sublists. These high-reward words could be present in the middle of the list (the middle sublist; items 5-12), or at the end of the list (the end sublist; items 13-20). The first four words were considered a burn-in sequence and were never associated with the higher reward. Two reward conditions existed, with the rewarded encoding condition (Fig. 1A, magenta) offering 10 points for correctly classifying the word as small or large, and the rewarded recall condition (Fig. 1A, blue) offering 10 points for recalling the word during the free recall period. This yielded four list types, each shown four times across the experiment for every participant in a fully randomized order.

To control potential order effects, we balanced the encoding task responses across and within sublists. Each correct response appeared equally often and was equally likely to be preceded by the same or the alternate response, for each serial position. This avoided systematic effects of response alternation, which are known to slow reaction times and potentially influence encoding. Four possible response sequences were created (Fig. 1B), and each list type was paired once with each sequence.

2.1.3 Procedure

Each trial started with a study phase, presenting the list of 20 words. Each word appeared for 3 s, and was preceded by a 750 ms reward cue and followed by 500 ms feedback (Fig. 1C). Response-key mappings for the encoding task were randomized across participants. After a response, a small square was presented below the word, indicating successful response registration.

Reward cues varied by reward condition. Words without additional reward were preceded by a black fixation cross. In the rewarded encoding condition, high-reward words were preceded by a magenta magnifying glass and displayed in magenta. In the rewarded recall condition, high-reward words were preceded by a blue camera icon and displayed in blue. Participants did not know the reward condition of the current list, nor the location of the highreward words, until the first high-reward word was cued by one of these icons.

Feedback during the study phase indicated points earned: +1 (green) for normal words, +10 (gold) for high-reward words in the rewarded encoding condition, and +0 (red) for incorrect or omitted responses. We selected these colors from the color universal design palette, meaning they appear visually distinct for any common pattern of colorblindness (Katsnelson, 2021). A cumulative point counter was displayed at the top of the screen.

After each study phase, participants completed a 15-second distractor integer addition task, generating random sums of numbers between 10 and 50. Each correct answer earned 1 point, indicated with green "+1" feedback.

Following the distractor task, participants recalled as many words as possible in any order by typing them into a text box and submitting with <enter>. Submitted words were matched to list items using a simple spelling-correction algorithm (edit distance ≤ 2) and immediate feedback was provided. Correct recalls earned +1 points (or +10 for high-reward words in the rewarded recall condition); incorrect responses yielded +0, and repeated recalls were flagged with "REPEATED." Each recall phase lasted 50 s, regardless of performance.

2.2 Computational models

We implemented a hierarchical Bayesian version of CMR in Stan (Carpenter et al., 2017) and considered three possible reward mechanisms: A general enhancement of associative strength for rewarded items, the construction of a separate reward source, and the formation of accessible event boundaries for the rewarded sublist. We describe CMR as it is typically implemented (Kragel et al., 2015; Polyn, 2024), followed by a description of these rewardsensitive modifications, followed by a description of the Bayesian modeling techniques. An overview of all the parameters is given in Table 1.

2.2.1 The Context Maintenance and Retrieval (CMR) model

CMR is a recurrent linear associative neural network (Fig. 2; Anderson et al., 1977; Elman, 1990) consisting of two representational layers bidirectionally connected by associative weight matrices. The input layer f is a representational space allowing for the activation of localist codes for each study item as it is encountered on the list. In Fig. 2 for example, the graphical icons indicate the featural element activated by the study items "dog", "mug", "car", and "vampire", respectively. This input layer projects to a context (hidden) layer c. Pre-existing associations link each featural element to a corresponding contextual element. The context layer additionally includes various representations of organizational features that can aid memory search, including start-of-list context (which is activated before the first study item is presented) and rewardrelated source features (which are activated for low-reward and high-reward items, respectively). An item-to-context weight matrix ( 𝐌 ) projects from f to c, allowing feature activation to influence the contextual representation. A context-to-item weight matrix (𝐌 ), projects from c back to f, allowing for the reactivation of feature representations during the recall phase. These memory activations of the study items are conceptualized as an output layer (𝐟 ), not depicted in the schematic figure. Learning is simulated as the formation of bidirectional Hebbian associations between items and context, on these weight matrices. Fig. 1). On the rest of the trials, it operates in a recency mode, and no such reinstatement takes place. During model fitting, we compute the likelihood of each recall sequence under each of these two modes and weight their likelihoods using the mixture parameter 𝑝.

2.2.4 Computational modeling procedures

We used a likelihood-based implementation of CMR which assigns a likelihood to each possible recall and termination option (Polyn, 2024). This allowed us to develop a hierarchical Bayesian framework to perform parameter estimation. We implemented a mixture mechanism within this framework, as described above, with the weighting controlled by the 𝑝 parameter. This is implemented during optimization by computing the likelihood of each recall sequence considering both successful and unsuccessful contextual reinstatement and weighting these likelihoods with the mixture parameter 𝑝 . All models were fitted with 4 chains of 1975 warmup and 4000 post-warmup transitions each, for a total of 16000 post-warmup transitions. The exact model structure, priors, convergence diagnostics, and a parameter recovery study, can be seen in Appendix A.

We conducted model comparisons using the loo package (Vehtari et al., 2020), which computes an approximation to the leave-one-out cross-validation score. This yields an expected log-predictive density (elpd) which can be interpreted analogously to an Akaike or Bayesian Information Criterion. A standard error for this elpd is also estimated, alongside difference scores for the elpd between any pair of models and a standard error corresponding to this difference score. We consider one model to be significantly better than another when the elpd difference is at least twice the standard error of this difference.

2.3 Statistical methods

We used the afex package in R (Singmann et al., 2024) to compute repeated measures ANOVAs as described in the results section. We described the temporal organization of the output recall sequence, by computing the lag-conditional response probability (lag-CRP; Kahana, 1996). The lag-CRP is based on tallying the serial-position distance (lag) of each observed recall transition and comparing it to the distribution of all lags that were available at that point in recall (with transitions counted as unavailable if the corresponding item had already been recalled). We also quantified overall temporal organization using the temporal factor score (Hong et al., 2024; Polyn et al., 2009a), computed by converting each observed transition into its percentile within the distribution of available (absolute) lags and then averaging across transitions. Scores range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating a stronger tendency to recall temporally adjacent items.

We developed single-trial regression models to test response times and accuracy scores on the encoding task, and recall performance. These models are further specified in Appendix B. When testing for significance of particular regression slopes, we used the bayestestR package (Makowski, Ben-Shachar, & Lüdecke, 2019) to compute the 95% highest density interval (HDI) and the probability of direction (pd)-the proportion of posterior samples on one side of zero, with pd > 0.975 roughly corresponding to a frequentist p < 0.05 (Makowski, Ben-Shachar, Chen, et al., 2019; but see Wagenmakers et al., 2020). We judged an effect to be significant when the 95% HDI excludes 0, and the pd is at least 0.975. We used the same criteria to judge the differences between model parameters as fit to the rewarded recall and rewarded encoding conditions.

3.1 Overview

Participants encountered different lists that manipulated i) the type of reward that was available-either for correctly performing a semantic encoding task or for successful later recall, and ii) the list positions in which this reward was available-either the middle (items 5-12) or the end (items 13-20) of the list. We found large differences in performance based on the reward type and its' position in the list. Participants performed the encoding task with high accuracy, but were slower and less accurate when encoding recall-rewarded items. Recall-rewarded items were recalled much more often compared to non-rewarded or encoding-rewarded items, and demonstrated enhanced temporal organization. Participants tended to initiate recall with the first recall-rewarded item, which proved highly constraining to the set of computational models we examined.

3.2 Encoding task performance

Performance on the binary semantic classification task was disrupted in the rewarded recall condition, possibly reflecting the engagement of cognitive processes to enhance the memorability of items. These effects were revealed by repeated-measures ANOVA on the mean response times within each sublist (Fig. 4, see Appendix B for full ANOVA table) with three factors: 1) the reward condition of that list, 2) whether the high rewards were in the middle (items 5-12) or at the end (items 13-20), and 3) whether the item itself was part of the middle or end sublist. Burn-in words (items 1-4) were excluded from this analysis. Participants responded slower overall in the rewarded recall condition than in the rewarded encoding condition (F(1,28) = 9.868, p = 0.004, η² p = 0.261). This was further modulated by an interaction between sublist location and reward location (F(1,28) = 7.482, p = 0.011, η² p = 0.211) which was qualified by a three-way interaction with reward type (F(1,28) = 16.033, p < 0.001, η² p = 0.364). Participants were slower when the item was part of the high-reward sublist, but only for the rewarded recall condition (F(1,28) = 19.250, p < 0.001, η² p = 0.407). Unlike the recall-rewarded items, the encodingrewarded items did not show a disruption of classification performance (F(1,28) = 1.318, p = 0.261, η² p = 0.045). This is consistent with the engagement of executive processes to enhance the memorability of recall-rewarded words, at the cost of fast encoding task performance. Note that we also conducted single-trial regression models of response times yielding identical results, but these models showed substantial misspecification and are therefore only reported in Appendix B.

It is possible that the high-priority study items from the rewarded recall condition could receive special treatment in the form of continuing rehearsal (Brodie & Murdock, 1977; Tan & Ward, 2000). We examined this possibility with a second set of analyses of classification performance. Continued rehearsal of (recall-rewarded) items adds a cognitive load to the participant which should disrupt task performance beyond the classification of the item itself. We compared response times in the end sublist when the middle sublist had high rewards, and did not find slower response times for these items in the rewarded recall condition relative to the rewarded encoding condition (F(1,28) = 0.020, p = 0.888, η² p < 0.001). This suggests that enhanced recall of high-reward words in the middle of the list is not due to rehearsal of those words, which would have interfered with task performance for low-rewarded words occurring at the end of the list.

A complementary set of analyses demonstrated that accuracy effects mirrored latency effects. Single-trial regression modeling provided a good fit for these data (see Appendix B for full model specification and posterior predictive check). We observed a similar three-way interaction between sublist location, reward location, and reward type (M = -1.344, HDI95% = [-2.245, -0.454], pd = 0.999): participants made more errors for high reward words in the rewarded recall condition than in the rewarded encoding condition. In order to determine whether continuing rehearsal could affect task accuracy, we carried out an analogous analysis to the latency analysis described above. Testing the accuracy in the end sublist when the middle sublist had high rewards again did not reveal a significant difference between the rewarded encoding and recall conditions (M = -0.232, HDI95% = [-0.708, 0.219], pd = 0.840) again not showing evidence in favor of continuing rehearsal processes.

3.1.3 Recall performance

The memorability of individual items was enhanced in the rewarded recall condition, specifically for the subset of rewarded items. We characterized these effects with a single-trial logistic regression model on the probability of recalling each word, using the same three factors as for the encoding task performance-reward condition, reward location, and sublist membership. Single-trial regression modeling provided a good fit for these data (see Appendix B for full model specification and posterior predictive checks). Participants recalled more items overall in the rewarded recall condition than in the rewarded encoding condition (M = 0.261, HDI95% = [0.114, 0.399], pd = 1.000). Recall was also generally higher for the end sublist than for the middle sublist (M = 0.181, HDI95% = [0.048, 0.321], pd = 0.995). Importantly, recall was enhanced specifically in the high-reward sublist, regardless of its position in the list (M = 1.356, HDI95% = [1.002, 1.712], pd = 1.000). This reward-location effect was further qualified by a threeway interaction, such that the advantage for high-reward sublists was substantially larger in the rewarded recall condition than in the rewarded encoding condition (M = 2.157, HDI95% = [1.419, 2.852], pd = 1.000).

These memorability effects can be seen in more detail in the serial position curves for each of the list types (Fig. 5). This figure also shows the posterior predictions from the modified CMR models incorporating all three reward mechanisms, which closely track the empirical data. This validates that the model provides an adequate account of the data, and we explore the unique contributions of individual mechanisms in section 3.3 Characterizing distinct reward mechanisms.

3.1.4 Recall initiation

A striking spike in recall initiation probability accompanied the first word of the rewarded recall condition, as shown in Fig. 6. Posterior predictions from CMR closely matched this behavior. We used a generalized linear mixed-effects model (logistic regression) to characterize this effect, predicting whether the first recall was the first word of the high-reward sublist (coded 1) or not (coded 0), with reward type (encoding vs. recall) as the independent variable. See Appendix B for the full model structure and posterior predictive check. The model revealed a significant effect of reward type (M = 1.993, HDI95% = [1.284, 2.824], pd = 1.000) indicating that participants were more likely to initiate recall with the first high-reward item in the rewarded recall condition than in the rewarded encoding condition. Interestingly, Fig. 6 does also reveal a small increased probability in initiating with the first rewarded item in the rewarded encoding condition, similar to Fig. 5 revealing a small increase in overall recall for (early) encoding-rewarded items. We address potential explanations for this in the Discussion section.

3.1.5 Temporal organization

Memory for the temporal structure of the study sequence was greatly enhanced for recallrewarded items. Fig. 7A,B demonstrate increased likelihood of short-range and forward-going transitions between study items during free recall, specifically for recall-rewarded items. The increased strength of temporal organization (i.e., the prevalence of short-range recall transitions) for recall-rewarded items is succinctly captured with the temporal organization scores depicted in Fig. 7C (Hong et al., 2024; Polyn et al., 2009a). Chance-level temporal organization is indicated by a score of 0.5 on this measure, and a score of 1 corresponds to always recalling the most nearby items. The increased tendency for forward-going transitions for recall-rewarded items (calculated as the difference between +1 and -1 transitions in the lag-CRP) is depicted in Fig. 7D. Memory for recall-rewarded items displayed especially strong temporal organization. We characterized reward-related effects on the temporal organization scores with a repeatedmeasures ANOVA, with high-vs. low reward items and reward condition (rewarded encoding vs. rewarded recall) as factors, collapsing across middle vs. end sublist position. This analysis revealed that high reward items showed stronger temporal organization than low reward items (F(1,28) = 10.500, p = 0.003, η² p = 0.273). The rewarded recall condition also showed stronger temporal organization overall compared to the rewarded encoding condition (F(1,28) = 9.011, p = 0.006, η² p = 0.243). Importantly, these effects interacted. Temporal organization was particularly enhanced for high reward items when recall rewards were at stake (F(1,28) = 9.860, p = 0.004, η² p = 0.260).

A similar analysis was applied to the forward asymmetry measure. Here the ANOVA showed a reliable effect of reward, with high reward items exhibiting stronger forward asymmetry than low reward items (F(1,28) = 12.493, p = 0.001, η² p = 0.309). However, there was no significant difference between the rewarded encoding and rewarded recall conditions.

3.3 Characterizing distinct reward mechanisms

We evaluated three proposed reward mechanisms-a modified attention gradient, a reward source, and contextual control (see Fig. 3)-and concluded that all contribute to enhancing the memorability of recall-rewarded items. In this section, we take a closer look at how these mechanisms contribute. We begin by comparing parameter estimates between the rewarded encoding and rewarded recall fits, to evaluate how the model accounts for their distinct recall dynamics. We then probe the unique contribution of each mechanism to recall performance by examining the predictions of subsets of the family of CMR variants omitting one of the three reward mechanisms each. 3.3.3 Improved encoding and contextual control work together to organize motivated recall

The enhanced temporal organization for recall-rewarded items reflects an increased tendency to recall the items in order, starting with the first rewarded item. We examined the first four recalled items in the rewarded recall condition (Fig. 10). Omitting the attention gradient mechanism does not disrupt this temporal organization, but does attenuate it: as overall support for the (early) rewarded items is decreased, the temporal organization cannot be as strongly expressed. This is especially salient when the rewards are in the end sublist.

Omitting the contextual control mechanism disrupts the temporal organization. Only a diffuse bias for early recall of early rewarded items remains, especially salient in output positions three and four. Overall, this indicates that contextual control is responsible for establishing the tight forward organization of recall, while the modified attention gradient determines the relative strength of the underlying memories. Only when both mechanisms are present do the posterior predictions reproduce the sharply ordered recall structure observed in the rewarded recall condition.

4. DISCUSSION

Memory is essential for daily functioning, yet our cognitive resources are finite. We must prioritize information that is most relevant for future behavior. In this work, we investigated the architecture of this prioritization, asking whether motivated memory is driven by processes such as the strengthening of memory traces, perhaps through neuromodulation, or by control over the mental context that can be used to search for specific information later on. By comparing rewards administered during encoding with those contingent on later recall, we observed that while simple reward salience provides a modest boost to memorability, the structure of memory is more fundamentally reorganized when memorization is instrumental to obtaining reward. Recall-based rewards, but not encoding-task rewards, produced highly structured free recall response sequences, with participants preferentially initiating recall at the beginning of the rewarded subsequence and then recalling items in forward order. We created a family of models incorporating three theoretically dominant reward-based mechanisms expressed within the Context Maintenance and Retrieval framework (CMR; Polyn et al., 2009a). We found that both encoding enhancements and contextual control were necessary to qualitatively reproduce the reward-based reorganization of memory.

4.1 The attention gradient

Classic theories of reward-enhanced memory focus on dopaminergic plasticity (Miendlarzewska et al., 2016; Shohamy & Adcock, 2010). Dopamine is released upon the anticipation of rewarding experiences (Schultz et al., 1997), and is known to enhance the strength of synaptic connections formed in the hippocampus (Lisman et al., 2011; Lisman & Grace, 2005; Otmakhova & Lisman, 1996). The modified attention gradient captures key characteristics of this neural mechanism: It enhances the associative strength for (early) rewarded items. However, reward-enhanced performance in free recall alternatively been interpreted as driven by strategic encoding processes, such as increased semantic elaboration for more valuable items (Cohen et al., 2014; Knowlton & Castel, 2022). The attention gradient as implemented in CMR is consistent with both views, in that it simply increases associative strength. Nevertheless, we observed a much stronger effect of the attention gradient when rewards were contingent on later recall compared to semantic classification performance, indicating a strong dependence on memorization being instrumental for reward acquisition.

It has been proposed that the memorability of items can be enhanced when participants engage in deeper processing of inter-item and item-context relationships (Craik, 2023; Dames & Popov, 2023; Hintzman, 2016; Popov & Dames, 2023). Notably, in CMR, context largely reflects previously presented items, blurring the distinction between inter-item and item-context encoding. Popov & Reder (2020) proposed that the formation of associations between items and context may consume a limited cognitive resource. The consumption of this resource may be experienced as costly, making participants reluctant to reliably engage this mechanism unless the reward structure of the task incentivizes it (Shenhav et al., 2013 (Shenhav et al., , 2017)). This could potentially explain why the modified attention gradient, enhancing the strength of the context-to-item associations, is stronger for recall-rewarded items. This suggests that resources may be allocated based on whether memorization is instrumental to reward acquisition, rather than simply sequentially. Indeed, Ma et al. (2026) recently extended this framework, formalizing a rule whereby associative strength is increased in proportion to item value, successfully capturing graded recall benefits in designs where each item is assigned a unique reward value (Knowlton & Castel, 2022). Nevertheless, our simulations also demonstrated that the attention gradient mechanism is not sufficient to explain the effect of reward on recall initiation. Participants showed a strong preference for initiating recall specifically with the first recall-rewarded item, that could not be captured solely by an increase in overall memorability for this item.

4.2 The reward source

We demonstrated that the reward source mechanism was not responsible for any of the qualitative organization features observed in the rewarded recall condition. When rewarded items are interspersed randomly throughout the list, participants still tend to cluster these items together during free recall-an organizational feature that can be captured by such a separate reward context guiding memory search (Horwath et al., 2023; Talmi et al., 2021). However, Horwath et al. (2023) already observed that this source context was not necessary to capture performance when all the rewarded items were presented in a continuous sequence. Instead, only enhanced context-to-item associations were necessary to produce the increase in overall recall as well as clustering of these items. Our model comparison still indicated that the reward source mechanism helps to improve the overall model fit of CMR to the empirical data, possibly by enhancing the overall contrast in memorability between high-reward items and low-reward items, while the attention gradient and the contextual control mechanisms emphasize differences in memorability within the rewarded subsequence.

We observed small effects on overall memorability and recall initiation in the rewarded encoding condition. Such effects have been observed in other free recall tasks as well. For example, Polyn et al. (2009b) asked participants to make semantic judgments during encoding, either size (smaller/larger than a basketball) or animacy (animate/inanimate). Several consecutive items required the same judgment before switching to the other task within a single list. At recall, participants tended to cluster items by judgment type and, importantly, showed a small but reliable increase in the probability of recalling the first post-switch item early, including as the first recall, resembling the results in our rewarded encoding condition. They were able to model these effects with a source context representing the task that had to be performed for each item. However, in our reward encoding condition participants occasionally may have engaged the same memorization processes as they may have used for recall-rewarded items, resulting in these signatures reflecting sporadic trials in which a stronger reward-modified attention gradient or contextual control mechanism would have operated. This is consistent with evidence that some individuals actively enjoy cognitively demanding tasks (cf. Inzlicht et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2023). Some participants indeed mentioned during the post-task debriefing that they enjoyed coming up with stories involving the words.

4.3 Contextual control

We observed a strong effect where participants preferentially initiated recall with the first high-reward item, extending beyond the overall increase in memorability of this item. This observation was made possible by the reward manipulation unique to our study. The contextual control mechanism is necessary to explain this effect, allowing participants to place tab dividerlike markers in temporal context, enabling later recall to be guided according to these markers (Michelmann et al., 2023). This reflects how real-world memory often targets coherent eventsmeaningful experiences unfolding over a brief interval (Kurby & Zacks, 2008; Wang et al., 2024; Zacks & Tversky, 2001). By manipulating the reward type, we demonstrated that reward motivation can enhance the segmentation of these events, as well as the integration of items within a single event. Participants initiated recall at the event boundary more consistently, as well as forming stronger context-to-item associations, specifically when they could receive rewards for later recall of the items.

Our results expand on other recent approaches that investigated the effect of reward prediction errors on event segmentation. Rouhani et al. (2020) asked participants to view photographs associated with various levels of reward, and to remember the reward values associated with each photograph so they could later select the most valuable ones. Reward levels were clustered in time, meaning that photographs associated with relatively high reward values all followed each other, and were clearly separate from photographs associated with lower reward values. After viewing these photographs, participants were tested for recognition memory, with some photographs being presented in the same sequential order as during encoding. This revealed sequential probing effects on recognition memory, where recognition memory performance is faster for photographs that were presented in the same sequential order as during encoding. Crucially, this effect was diminished for photographs that spanned an event boundary-specifically the items before and after a change in the mean reward value, excluding the first item of the new reward value. This was interpreted as evidence that reward prediction errors provide the crucial signal to trigger event segmentation. However, note that the participants were asked to remember the reward values of the photographs so that they could later select the most valuable ones. We demonstrated that our signature of event segmentation, the mid-list primacy effect, mainly affects such recall-rewarded items. This questions whether the results obtained by Rouhani et al. (2020) depend solely on the presence of reward prediction errors, or if memorization additionally needs to be instrumental for reward acquisition. Karagoz et al. (2025) investigated a paradigm where rewards were administered independent of later memorization, extending the work of Polyn et al. (2009a Polyn et al. ( , 2009b)). They offered participants rewards for correctly performing semantic judgment tasks on the words as they are presented. Crucially, the current rule for the semantic judgment task was not explicitly told to the participants and changed at hidden points during the encoding phase, which thereby required the participants to deploy a rule-inference process similar to that required by the Wisconsin card sorting test (Berg, 1948). Strikingly, they observed a decreased recall probability for items following rule changes. Rather than attributing this to an event segmentation mechanism, it is likely that the task design of Karagoz et al. (2025) required cognitive resources to infer the correct decision rule, preventing these resources from being allocated towards memory encoding processes following a rule change and leading to shallower processing of the item, resulting in weaker context-to-item associations. This is corroborated by their finding that decision certainty (derived from a reinforcement learning model of the rule-inference process) predicts recall success, potentially leaving more resources to employ strategic encoding processes, and providing some evidence that the formation of strong context-to-item associations requires focused attention.

Overall, this suggests that the increased engagement of contextual control and attention gradient mechanisms in the rewarded recall condition may be strategic. In line with Rouhani et al. (2020), we observed a signature of contextual control in the form of an accessible event segmentation at the start of a high-reward context, specifically when reward depended on remembering this information later. In line with Karagoz et al. (2025), we observed that participants were slower and more error-prone at performing the semantic classification task for recall-rewarded items compared to encoding-rewarded items. This suggests the engagement of capacity-limited cognitive processes to enhance the memorability of these items, at the cost of encoding task performance. These cognitive processes may take the form of participants coming up with a story involving the words as they appear, or placing the items together in a visual scene. Indeed, in the post-experiment interview, 17 out of 29 participants reported using strategies such as imagining a story that linked the words together or placing the words in a visual scene.

Application of such strategies has previously been argued to result in primacy initiation and forward organization (Romani et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2023).

These strategies map well onto event segmentation theory, which is often investigated with continuous movie watching or story listening paradigms (Zacks & Swallow, 2007). Such rich and long-form content would yield substantial interference if all details were equally committed to memory, resulting in ineffective memory search when specific content needs to be remembered later on. Event segmentation processes provide a crucial preprocessing step during (or shortly after) encoding, identifying what parts are important to remember and the best way to commit these to memory, in order to minimize such interference. Our results demonstrate that similar processes can occur in a more artificial setting such as learning lists of arbitrary words, and appear under strategic control when reward acquisition is dependent on successful recall. This provides a novel and simplified experimental technique for investigating the role of cognitive control in memory encoding and retrieval. Future work may further characterize the computations involved in deciding when and how to form these event representations, for example by combining the architecture of CMR with reinforcement learning algorithms that can learn to control (particular dimensions of) the context layer.

If such event segmentation processes help enhance memory performance, why do participants not always employ them in typical free recall tasks where the goal is to remember as many words as possible? One potential answer can be found in the literature on cognitive control. Better performance in cognitive tasks often comes with a perceived cost of exerting cognitive effort (Shenhav et al., 2017). Participants may not be willing to exert such effort, unless substantial rewards are contingent on it. Nevertheless, participants may occasionally employ such processes spontaneously. Indeed, Zhang et al. (2023) found that the best participants in a free recall task tend to show primacy initiation and forward organization (see also Romani et al., 2016). More generally, Farrell (2012) demonstrated that associations in memory may often cluster together in shorter chunks, which are accessed from the first item and recalled in a forward order. Such chunks may reflect the participant identifying richer relationships between these items, possibly through similar forms of imagery as we proposed play a role in generating the mid-list primacy effect.

4.4 Limitations and broader implications

We must consider the possibility that other executive mechanisms not considered here could also account for these behavioral effects. For example, there is a substantial literature linking both primacy initiation and forward organization to rehearsal strategies in which studied items are repeated covertly by the participant throughout the list (Brodie & Murdock, 1977; Laming, 2006). Both are hallmarks of serial recall, where participants are explicitly instructed to rehearse items in order, and there is ongoing debate about the extent to which serial recall and free recall rely on shared mechanisms, possibly involving rehearsal (Gunn & Polyn, 2025; Henson, 1998; Logan, 2021; Logan et al., 2021; Logan & Cox, 2021; Lohnas, 2024; Osth & Hurlstone, 2023; Solway et al., 2012; Tan & Ward, 2000; Ward et al., 2010; Ward & Beaman, 2025).

In our experiment, one might argue that the rewarded recall condition encouraged participants to rehearse the rewarded items in sequence, thereby producing primacy initiation and forward transitions. However, several observations argue against this interpretation. In the post-experiment interview, only six participants reported attempting rehearsal strategies, and two of these noted that they switched from rehearsal to a story/scene strategy later in the task, finding the latter more effective. Most participants thus did not admit relying on rehearsal, and among the six who reported attempting it, two spontaneously described it as inefficient. Second, we observed that recall rewards impair performance on the encoding task. If this reflects rehearsal, and if participants had engaged in ongoing rehearsal of the mid-list rewarded items (positions 5-12), this should have also impaired performance on the concurrent encoding task for later items (13-20). Instead, we observed no such persistent slowdown or accuracy cost, suggesting that participants did not continue rehearsing reward items into the remainder of the list. Therefore, while rehearsal could in principle generate similar organizational signatures, we believe it is unlikely to account for our results. Instead, the CMR framework provides a more parsimonious explanation, attributing primacy initiation and forward organization to the joint influence of enhanced context-to-item encoding and contextual control.

Beyond the influence of rehearsal, other mechanisms have been invoked to explain the primacy effect. It has been suggested that the early list items have a higher temporal distinctiveness and are therefore more salient and better encoded (Brown et al., 2007). Our midlist primacy effect demonstrates strong primacy-like effects can exist regardless of temporal distinctiveness, likely involving some form of strategic control over event segmentation and item processing. In this sense, we have demonstrated that such processes may play a role in generating the canonical primacy effect. However, we did not rule out that other factors such as temporal distinctiveness may play a role in generating the canonical primacy effect, in addition to the strategic processes we proposed.

Future experiments may further characterize the nature of the mid-list primacy effect and its relation to the canonical primacy effect. For example, it is not known what would happen if recall-rewards would be present from the first item onward. Strategic processes as we proposed may further increase the strength of the primacy effect by increasing the chance or strength of the contextual reinstatement process. This may overwrite original primacy generating processes, add to them, or these processes might interact in some way.

Additionally, participants in our design may have realized that the absence of rewards in the middle of a list predicts the presence of rewards at the end of that list, allowing them to prepare in advance for the onset of these rewards. More generally, the fixed and repeating nature of the reward sequences in our experiment would diminish influences from surprise, salience, or reward prediction error that may contribute to mid-list primacy effects. Nevertheless, we found this effect to be strongly present in both in middle-and end-reward lists. This is easier explained by potentially strategic processes, rather than effects of surprise, salience, or prediction error. We additionally investigated whether the mid-list primacy effects change between the first and last experimental half. If surprise, salience, or prediction error contributes more to these effects, they should be stronger in the first half, while strategic effects may become more reliable in the latter half. However, we did not find any such difference between early and late experimental halves.

Our design focused on investigating memory organization through free recall. Other reward effects have been studied using recognition memory (Jang et al., 2019; Rouhani et al., 2018; Rouhani & Niv, 2021). There, prediction errors often yield improved memory for items that were more surprising, even when the rewards are administered independent of later memory for the related items. The weak effects of such encoding rewards in our paradigm can be understood in two ways. First of all, as highlighted above, the predictability of our design may have attenuated the effect of prediction errors. Alternatively, encoding rewards may serve to make the items themselves more salient and therefore better encoded, but do not encourage the strategic processes we have emphasized that enhance the item-to-context binding strength. The increased saliency of individual items may make them more recognizable when the exact item is encountered later on, without making items more effective at cueing memory for other items on the list. Similar arguments may hold for cued recall tasks, where participants are offered rewards for example for studying specific word pairs (De Loof et al., 2018). These rewards encourage the formation of associations between the relevant items within a pair, but would not increase the efficacy with which words across different pairs would cue each other during recall. While CMR is classically applied to free recall, recent work has extended this to recognition and cued recall paradigms (Polyn & Woodman, 2025), although this work did not incorporate reward effects into the model.

The original formulation of CMR included an exponentially decaying attention gradient, which is limited in generality. In value-directed remembering paradigms, items are often assigned unique point values that are randomly interspersed within a list. Recall probability typically scales with these point values, a graded effect that a uniformly decaying attention gradient cannot reproduce. A more flexible framework, such as the one from Ma et al. (2026) discussed above, would address this gap. It may be possible to implement such value-based encoding mechanisms within CMR, supplanting the attention gradient mechanism and providing a more general account for the primacy effect as well as the value-based memory effects.

Our implementation of contextual control was likewise deliberately constrained to align with our experimental task by only considering event segmentation specifically when changing between high and low rewards, which simplified hierarchical Bayesian model fitting and comparison. The CMR model is a simplified form of a recurrent neural network using linear associative connections and a simple integration mechanism. This makes it part of a broader lineage of recurrent neural networks; training such a network with backpropagation has been shown to reproduce a wide range of complex memory-related phenomena (Botvinick & Plaut, 2006; Botvinick & Plaut, 2004; Elman, 1990; Franklin et al., 2020; Gershman et al., 2025; Holroyd et al., 2018; Jensen et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2022; Shahnazian & Holroyd, 2018; Wang et al., 2018). From this perspective, contextual control could take more flexible forms, dynamically shaping both encoding and retrieval according to task demands. However, the strength of these neural-network approaches-capturing behavior through optimized distributed representations-is also their limitation. They do not afford explicit likelihood-based fitting, and therefore cannot provide the same statistical interpretability that our constrained CMR extensions allow.

Our findings highlight CMR as a uniquely powerful framework for modeling how rewards shape episodic memory. By formalizing both enhanced context-to-item binding (via the modified attention gradient) and the manipulation of mental context during encoding and retrieval (via contextual control), CMR enables direct tests of competing hypotheses about encoding strategies and recall organization and revealed their unique, nuanced contributions. Crucially, CMR allows for explicit likelihood-based model comparison, making it especially well-suited for adjudicating between mechanisms that can produce similar behavior. Looking forward, reward-augmented extensions of CMR provide a promising foundation for integrating neural and behavioral data (Kragel et al., 2015; Nunez et al., 2024; Polyn, 2024; Turner et al., 2017). Such approaches could help identify neural signatures not only of strengthened context-to-item associations (cf. Ma et al., 2025) but also of the contextual control processes we considered here, advancing our understanding of how motivation organizes memory for rewarding experiences, and the primacy effect in general.
