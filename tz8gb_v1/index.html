Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Full text

Abstract

Personality change interventions (PCIs) offer promising opportunities for personal development, yet little is known about how different psychological layers of personalitythoughts, feelings, behaviors, skills, and identity-respond to intentional change efforts. This study addressed that question through a six-week smartphone-based intervention tailored to students' preparation for entering the workforce, an applied setting in which PCIs remain understudied. Final-year undergraduate clinical psychology students (N = 565) participated in one of three interventions designed to enhance resilience, social boldness, or punctuality, with a quasi-experimental control group for comparison (N = 99). Participants in all three conditions showed meaningful growth in the targeted facet, with gains exceeding changes in non-targeted facets, surpassing those observed in the control group, and persisting at the fourweek follow-up. Moreover, perceived feasibility of change moderated intervention effectiveness. Finally, participants targeting resilience and social boldness showed significant growth across psychological layers, whereas those targeting punctuality changed primarily in the behavioral and skill layers. The identity layer-representing how participants see themselves-exhibited the smallest degree of change. Collectively, these findings enrich volitional personality change (VPC) theory by illuminating how personality change unfolds across different psychological layers, while also demonstrating the feasibility of PCIs in supporting the school-to-work transition.

Transitioning from higher education to the labor market poses a dual challenge for students: they must acquire not only job-relevant knowledge, but also the personal competencies needed to navigate career demands (Blokker et al., 2023). Among these competencies, soft skills are particularly valued in workplace settings, yet graduates often enter the labor market with these skills underdeveloped (e.g., Succi & Canovi, 2019). Given the close relationship between soft skills and personality traits (Soto et al., 2022), personality change may offer an untapped pathway for supporting the school-to-work transition.

Personality traits play a central role in workplace functioning (e.g., Wilmot & Ones, 2019), and a growing body of evidence demonstrates that they can be meaningfully shaped through personality change interventions (PCIs; Haehner et al., 2024). However, most interventions have been designed for clinical purposes (Roberts et al., 2017) or have focused on general desires for personal growth (e.g., Hudson et al., 2019). Therefore, an important next step for PCI research is to examine how evidence-based personality development principles can be translated to specific contexts, such as education and work, particularly during major life transitions such as entering the workforce (Asselmann et al., 2026).

Building on this foundation, the present study applied a volitional PCI to the professional preparation context of clinical psychology students entering their first internships.

Within this setting, the study addresses a theoretical question that has received surprisingly little empirical attention in the PCI literature: what constitutes such personality change? Specifically, little is known about which layers of personality-such as thoughts, feelings, and behaviors (Wilt & Revelle, 2015)-are responsive to intervention. While this distinction is central to understanding PCIs broadly, its implications are especially pronounced in professional preparation contexts, where sustained changes across multiple layers are believed to offer unique advantages over traditional skill-based approaches (Bleidorn et al., 2019; Dupré & Wille, 2025).

To investigate these questions, the present study implemented a six-week, smartphone-based PCI tailored to the professional preparation context, providing a layered account of how personality change unfolds across different psychological layers.

Volitional personality change

The question of whether personality is fixed or amenable to change has long stood at the heart of personality psychology. Although traits show strong rank-order stability, there is also ample evidence that personality is malleable. Specifically, traits tend to follow a normative developmental pattern across the lifespan (Bleidorn et al., 2022), but individual trajectories often deviate from these trends (e.g., Schwaba & Bleidorn, 2018). Building on this dynamic perspective, scholars have recently begun to investigate a distinct process of change: volitional personality change (VPC; Haehner et al., 2024). This concept describes individuals' self-directed efforts to alter aspects of their personality. Research in this area has primarily focused on identifying the kinds of personality changes people wish to pursue and has more recently expanded to developing and testing PCIs designed to facilitate such changes (e.g., Hudson et al., 2019; Stieger et al., 2020 Stieger et al., , 2021)). These interventions are based on the premise that consistently practicing behaviors, thoughts, and emotional responses at the personality state level can gradually consolidate into enduring trait changes (e.g., Allemand & Flückiger, 2017).

Several theoretical frameworks have guided the design of these PCIs. Hennecke et al.

(2014) proposed three preconditions for successful self-regulated change: (1) perceiving the desired changes as both desirable and needed; (2) regarding these changes as realistically attainable or feasible; and (3) consistently practicing new behaviors until they become automatic and habitual. Building on this, Allemand and Flückiger (2017) proposed four common change factors as core mechanisms of PCIs: discrepancy awareness (e.g., through progress feedback), activating strengths and resources (e.g., through support and encouragement from a "change team" of trusted individuals), fostering insight into thoughts and feelings (e.g., through cognitive restructuring), and practice (e.g., through behavioral activation tasks).

Effectiveness of PCIs in clinical and everyday contexts

Most evidence for PCIs comes from clinical settings, where therapeutic approaches have produced enduring changes, particularly in emotional stability (Roberts et al., 2017).

More recent research has explored non-clinical populations, promoting personality growth in everyday life (e.g., Hudson et al., 2019; Stieger et al., 2020 Stieger et al., , 2021)). A recent review confirmed the effectiveness of such interventions (average d = .22 compared to normative change d = .04), with the strongest effects observed for extraversion, neuroticism and conscientiousness (Haehner et al., 2024). Moreover, intervention-induced personality changes appear both observable to others-albeit to a lesser extent than self-reported changes-and relatively durable, with effects maintained for up to one year after intervention completion (Stieger et al., 2020 (Stieger et al., , 2021 (Stieger et al., , 2023)).

Together, these findings demonstrate that personality traits can be meaningfully shaped through intervention. Building on this foundation, VPC may hold particular promise for educational and professional preparation contexts, where it could support the school-towork transition and facilitate ongoing employee growth (Asselmann et al., 2026; Dupré & Wille, 2025). Such interventions could be especially impactful during young adulthood-a developmental period characterized by normative personality plasticity, which may allow for greater capacity for change (Bleidorn et al., 2022). Moreover, PCIs may offer several advantages over traditional skill-based approaches to development (e.g., Bleidorn et al., 2019; Dupré & Wille, 2025). Specifically, personality traits predict a broad range of workplace outcomes (e.g., Berry et al., 2007), may better capture employees' typical day-to-day functioning than skills (Soto et al., 2022), and may produce more durable effects by targeting patterns of behavior, thoughts, and feelings (Bleidorn et al., 2019). Nonetheless, VPC has yet to be systematically applied or evaluated in professional preparation contexts. The present study addresses this gap by testing a PCI tailored to these settings.

Change across psychological layers of personality

Personality traits can be expressed through different psychological layers or components, including behavioral, affective, and cognitive aspects (Allport, 1961) (Olaru et al., 2022). Second, even if all layers were adequately represented, existing studies do not explicitly investigate whether change occurs across each layer of personality. As a result, it remains unclear whether observed changes reflect integrated change across layers or are driven primarily by specific ones. By adopting a deliberate, multi-layered approach to evaluating PCI effectiveness, this study seeks to provide a more nuanced understanding of which personality layers are most responsive to intervention.

The current study

Through a six-week smartphone-based PCI tailored to the professional preparation context, this study took a novel approach to disentangling how different psychological layers respond to intervention. Specifically, we examined VPC in final-year undergraduate psychology students preparing for their first clinical internship through a career preparation course that incorporated career exploration activities and a brief internship. Participants selected one of three personality facets-social boldness, punctuality, or resilience-as their personal development targets. These facets were derived from thematic analyses of focus group discussions with subgroups of course instructors, mentors, and former students and reflected recurring internship-related challenges. Participants were assessed at four time points: pre-intervention, mid-intervention, post-intervention, and after four weeks. A control group was also assessed repeatedly over a comparable timeframe. The intervention was delivered via smartphone, consistent with a growing body of research using digital tools for non-clinical interventions (e.g., Stieger et al., 2021). This format enabled flexible delivery in daily life while supporting scalable, cost-efficient implementation and continuous assessment.

The study had three primary goals. First, we examined whether desired changes occurred within the intervention group. In line with existing evidence, we hypothesized that participants would show increases in their targeted facet from pre-to post-intervention (Hypothesis 1) and that these gains would persist at the four-week follow-up (Hypothesis 2).

To further evaluate the robustness of pre-post intervention effects, we examined changes in targeted versus non-targeted facets within the intervention group and compared changes in the intervention group with those observed in a control group. We expected larger increases in targeted than non-targeted facets (Hypothesis 3), and larger increases than those observed for the corresponding facet in the control group (Hypothesis 4).

Second, we explored potential moderators of pre-to post-intervention changes.

Several frameworks of VPC have proposed determinants of successful change, some of which have received preliminary empirical support, although the evidence remains limited and inconsistent (e.g., Allemand & Flückiger, 2017; Haehner et al., 2024; Hennecke et al., 2014). Among the determinants most recurrently highlighted are engagement in the change process, motivation to change, and beliefs about the attainability of change. Drawing on these theoretical and empirical insights, we examined four moderators: intervention engagement, desire to change, perceived value of change, and perceived feasibility of change. Specifically, we expected greater intervention engagement to be associated with larger increases in the targeted facet (Hypothesis 5a), consistent with evidence that greater investment in change efforts facilitates goal attainment and personality development (Hudson et al., 2019; Stieger et al., 2021). We also expected motivation for change to facilitate personality development (e.g., Haehner et al., 2024; Hennecke et al., 2014). This was operationalized as desire to change (Hypothesis 5b), reflecting the extent to which participants wished to differ from their current facet level, and perceived value of change (Hypothesis 5c), reflecting the importance assigned to the goal. Finally, we hypothesized that perceived feasibility of change (Hypothesis 5d)-the belief that change is attainable-would positively predict increases in the targeted facet (Hennecke et al., 2014). Given that these moderators are conceptually related, they were examined simultaneously to assess their unique contributions to intervention effectiveness.

The final and third goal of the study was to investigate whether different psychological layers of personality vary in their rate of change. Given the exploratory nature of this question, we did not formulate any a priori hypotheses. In addition to the traditional layers of thoughts, feelings, and behaviors-components central to trait theories of personality (Allport, 1961)-we included two dimensions closely related to personality but not typically distinguished as core components; namely identity and skills.

While trait theories focus on patterns of thinking, feeling, and behaving, other perspectives emphasize the role of identity in personality functioning and development, capturing how individuals perceive and interpret themselves (McAdams et al., 2021). Identity is particularly relevant to VPC because development goals are argued to emerge from discrepancies between actual and desired selves (Hennecke et al., 2014), and because incorporating behavioral, affective, and cognitive changes into one's self-concept (i.e., assimilation) may help sustain long-term personality change (Jackson & Wright, 2024). By including identity as an additional outcome, we examined whether PCIs influence not only what individuals think, feel, and do, but also how they see themselves.

Finally, our investigation incorporated a skill layer. Whereas traits reflect typical patterns of thoughts, feelings, and behavior across situations (i.e., tendencies), skills reflect the capacity to enact specific behaviors when needed (i.e., capacities) and are generally considered to be more malleable (Soto et al., 2022). Because PCIs often employ strategies similar to-but more extensive and sustained than-those used in skill training, such interventions may foster growth in soft skills alongside personality change. Practically, these findings may inform scalable, digitally delivered PCIs that support students in preparing for occupational roles and guide PCI efforts in workplace settings.

Method Procedure

Ethical approval was obtained from XXX (institution blinded for review) University's Ethical Review Board (2024/020). At the beginning of the semester, participants in the intervention group received verbal information about the intervention and study and were invited to provide consent for their data to be used in research. To encourage honest reporting, participants were informed that their grades would be based solely on participation, not on the content of their responses. Interventions were timed to coincide with students' internships, facilitating application of intervention activities in practice. Participants selected one of three targets-social boldness (57%, N = 321), punctuality (16%, N = 93), or resilience (26%, N = 151)-based on their personal development goals. All three personality facets were measured in every participant at four consecutive time points: shortly before the intervention (pretest, T1), mid-way after three weeks (T2), immediately following the intervention (post-test, T3), and four weeks after the intervention (follow-up, T4). As a result, participants who focused on one target also served as a comparison group for those focusing on alternative targets. In addition to these within-intervention comparisons, a parallel control group was recruited concurrently and completed the same measures without participating in the intervention.

Participants

Intervention group

The intervention was delivered to final-year undergraduate students in clinical psychology at XXX (institution blinded for review) as part of a career preparation course. This course prepares students for professional practice through career exploration sessions, a brief internship, and other related activities. Participation was incentivized through higher course grades but was not required to pass the course. The sample was collected across two waves and consisted of 565 students at pre-intervention, of whom 541 participated at midintervention, 552 at post-intervention, and 275 at follow-up 1 . The majority of students (86%; N = 487) participated in all six intervention weeks. An additional 12% (N = 68) completed five weeks, and only 2% (N = 10) completed four weeks, consistent with the course-credit requirement that allowed missing at most one week. Participants were predominantly women (87%), with an average age of 21.64 years (range = 18-55 years).

Control group

A control group was recruited through convenience sampling among psychology students at various stages of their academic programs. Initially, 99 participants completed the pre-intervention assessment, with 91 participating at mid-intervention, and 78 at postintervention. Most participants were women (91%), and the average age was 22.28 years (ranging from 18 to 51 years). The demographic composition of the control group-in terms of gender and age-was thus broadly comparable to that of the intervention group.

Intervention

We designed a smartphone-based PCI informed by general change mechanisms and specific micro-interventions from prior research (e.g., Allemand & Flückiger, 2017; Hudson et al., 2019; Stieger et al., 2020 Stieger et al., , 2021)). The intervention was specifically tailored to the professional preparation context, with content adapted to participants' functioning in professional settings, including the campus, internships, and student jobs. Implementation occurred through m-Path (see example screenshots in Figure S1; supplemental material), a smartphone application for digital experience sampling and momentary interventions (Mestdagh et al., 2023). There is currently no consensus on the optimal duration or intensity for PCIs (Haehner et al., 2024), which can range from 2 weeks with daily dosage (Stieger et 1 The substantially smaller follow-up sample size is attributable to the fact that the four-week follow-up assessment was administered only in the second of the two data-collection waves (N = 292 at T1). al., 2020) to 15 weeks with lower weekly intensity (e.g., Hudson et al., 2019). For the present study, we adopted a six-week PCI with weekly dosage-a medium duration considered appropriate given the focus on narrower personality facets rather than broad domains, and on a single context rather than across multiple life domains. The content of an example week (Week 3) of the intervention is provided in Table S1 (supplemental material). Each Monday, participants received a notification to complete the weekly intervention. Nonresponders received up to two daily reminders until completion or until the session closed on Wednesday. Each week began with two app-based microinterventions, varying across weeks. Examples included psychoeducational videos to foster knowledge about personality change or reflection exercises such as cognitive restructuring exercises, guiding participants to identify, evaluate, and modify their thought patterns.

Upon completing these micro-interventions, participants received a behavioral activation task designed to promote behaviors relevant to their targeted personality facet.

Participants ranked six behavioral activation tasks from easiest to most challenging during the first week, creating a personalized hierarchy to gradually increase task difficulty. Each week, participants were instructed to translate the behavioral activation task into an implementation intention-a concrete if-then plan tailored to the professional preparation context participants were likely to encounter that week. Each subsequent Monday, students completed a brief assessment of their experiences before proceeding to the next intervention module.

Measures

Personality facets

Social boldness, punctuality, and resilience were assessed at all measurement occasions using a multi-layered, contextualized instrument developed through a four-step process. First, operational definitions of each facet were formulated in collaboration with course instructors to ensure relevance to the professional preparation context. Second, items were drawn from conceptually related scales in the International Personality Item Pool (IPIP; Goldberg et al., 2006) and the Behavioral, Emotional, and Social Skills Inventory (BESSI; Soto et al., 2022). Third, items were classified according to the psychological layer they reflected (i.e., thoughts, feelings, behaviors, skills, or identity). Finally, items were selected, adapted, or newly developed to ensure balanced representation of each layer while maintaining conceptual overlap across layers. Further details regarding measure development are provided in the supplemental materials (Supplementary Method S1).

Phrasing was standardized to reflect each psychological layer consistently. Feelings items referred to emotions (e.g., "I feel confident sharing my opinion" for social boldness).

Thoughts items were phrased to indicate beliefs or convictions (e.g., "I believe that planning is the key to good results" for punctuality). Behavior items explicitly referred to actions (e.g., "I give up quickly after a setback" for resilience). Identity items began with "I am" (e.g., "I am a procrastinator" for punctuality). Skill items were phrased using "I can" or "I am bad at" (e.g., "I am bad at expressing my opinion" for social boldness). All items were rated on a 5point scale from 1 (Strongly disagree) to 5 (Strongly agree). The full item set and instructions are provided in the supplemental material (Supplementary Method S2). Cronbach's alpha values at T1 indicated good reliability for 12 of the 15 scales (Kline, 1999), ranging from α = .70 (resilience -behavior) to α = .91 (social boldness -identity). Three scales scored lower: social boldness-thoughts (α = .65), resilience-feelings (α = .52), and resilience-thoughts (α = .61). Composite facet scores were calculated by averaging items across psychological layers.

Desire to change

At T1, participants rated their desire to change their targeted personality facet using the item: "On a scale of 1 to 10, how much [facet] do you want to develop compared to how you are right now?" (1 = a little more;10 = very much more).

Perceived feasibility of change

At T1, participants rated how feasible they perceived their development goal using the item: "On a scale of 1 to 10, how difficult does this development goal seem to you?" (1 = very easy;10 = very difficult).

Perceived value of change

At T1, respondents reported their perceived value of change using the item: "On a scale of 1 to 10, how important is it to you to reach this development goal?" (1 = slightly important;10 = extremely important).

Intervention engagement

Engagement was assessed for both reflection exercises and behavioral activation tasks. Following each reflection-based exercise, participants rated the depth of their reflection on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (I barely thought about it) to 5 (I thought about it very deeply). Behavioral engagement was assessed through self-reported completion of weekly behavioral activation tasks and, when applicable, completion of activities involving participants' chosen change team. Reflection and behavioral engagement scores were aggregated across the intervention period and modeled as indicators of a latent intervention engagement factor. Factor scores extracted from this model were used in subsequent analyses. The full measure development procedure is described in the Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Method S3).

Results

All research questions were examined using a series of linear longitudinal multilevel models (MLMs) in RStudio (version 4.4.2; R Core Team, 2023). MLMs were used to account for the nested structure of the data, with repeated measurements over time (Level 1: Time) nested within participants (Level 2: Person). For Hypotheses 1 and 2 concerning the degree of change and maintenance at follow-up, all four measurement occasions were included (i.e., pre-intervention, mid-intervention, post-intervention, and follow-up). Time was coded in weeks to reflect the unequal spacing between assessments (i.e., 3, 3, and 4 weeks, respectively). In these models, estimated slopes (b) represent unstandardized change per week. All other analyses focused on pre-to post-intervention change and therefore included only the first three measurement occasions. In these models, time was coded as 0, 1, and 2, such that slopes (b) represent unstandardized change per time step of 3 weeks. To facilitate interpretation, we additionally report standardized effect sizes (Cohen's d), calculated from observed scores, for the key effects in the main text 2 . All models included random intercepts and slopes to account for individual differences in baseline scores and rates of change over time. Descriptive statistics and intercorrelations for all study variables in the intervention and control groups at Time 1 are presented in the supplemental material (Table S2).

Within-and between-group comparisons

As a within-group control (H3), we tested whether, pooled across the three facets, intervention participants showed greater increases during the intervention period in their targeted facet compared with non-targeted facets. To examine this, we estimated a model predicting facet scores from fixed effects of Time, Target (target vs. non-target), and their interaction. Results are shown in Table 2. In line with Hypothesis 3 (H3), the Time × Target interaction was statistically significant (b = -.090 per 3-week time step, p < .001), indicating that increases from pre-to post-intervention were larger for targeted facets (b = .114 per time step, p < .001) than for non-targeted facets (b = .023 per time step, p < .001), although changes in the latter were still statistically significant. This difference corresponded to a small-to-moderate effect based on observed change scores (d = 0.35).

To test Hypothesis 4 (H4), we examined whether pre-post intervention changes in targeted facets were greater in the intervention group than in the corresponding facet in the control group. Analyses were conducted separately for each facet, with p-values Bonferronicorrected for three tests (padj). As shown in Table 3, the Time × Group interaction was statistically significant for all three facets. Intervention participants increased strongly in social boldness (b = .127 per 3-week time step, padj < .001), whereas the increase in the control group was smaller (b = .048 per time step, padj < .05). This between-group difference in observed change was small-to-moderate (d = .28). For punctuality, simple slopes indicated a statistically significant increase over time in the intervention group (b = .052 per time step, padj < .05) whereas the control group did not show a statistically significant change after Bonferroni correction (b = -.031 per time step, padj = .130). Cohen's d based on observed scores indicates a moderate difference between groups (d = .44). A similar pattern emerged for resilience: intervention participants increased in resilience (b = .136 per time step, padj < .001), whereas controls did not (b = .027 per time step, padj = .211). Cohen's d based on observed scores indicates a moderate difference between groups (d = .57). These results support Hypothesis 4 (H4), confirming that intervention participants showed greater increases in each targeted facet relative to the corresponding facet in the control group.

As a follow-up analysis, we compared changes in non-targeted facets between groups to determine whether the small but significant increases observed in non-targeted facets within the intervention group (see H3) reflected a byproduct of the intervention or natural fluctuations over time. As shown in supplemental Table S3, none of the Time × Group interactions reached statistical significance: social boldness (b = .036, padj = .090), punctuality (b = .017, padj = .828), and resilience (b = .014, padj = .056). These results indicate that changes in non-targeted facets in the intervention group were comparable to those in the control group.

Moderators of change

Next, we tested whether pre-to post-intervention changes were moderated by intervention engagement (H5a), desire to change (H5b), perceived value of change (H5c), and perceived feasibility of change (H5d). These variables were entered simultaneously in an MLM pooled across the three facets, allowing estimation of their unique contributions to personality change. All moderators were grand-mean centered prior to analysis; the latent engagement score was already centered at zero. A summary of the results is presented in the supplemental material (Table S4). In support of Hypothesis 5d, a statistically significant, albeit small, positive interaction emerged between Time and perceived feasibility of change (b = .012, p = .005), indicating that participants who perceived their development goal as more feasible (i.e., less difficult) showed greater increases in facet scores over time. This effect was small in magnitude (d = .14), reflecting the difference in pre-post change between participants high versus low in perceived feasibility (±1 SD), standardized by the pooled baseline standard deviation. No statistically significant interactions were found for the other moderators; thus, Hypotheses 5a, 5b, and 5c were not supported 3.

Patterns of change across psychological layers

Finally, we tested whether the psychological layers of personality exhibited differential rates of change over time from pre-to post-assessment. We first ran a pooled MLM across the three targeted facets, including a Time × Layer interaction. As shown by the simple slope estimates in Table S5 (supplemental material), all layers changed significantly over time (b = .086 -.136 per 3-week time step, all ps < .001). Pairwise comparisons suggested that the identity layer was among the weakest-changing layers, showing significantly smaller increases than the feeling layer (Δb = -.034, p = .026) and the skill layer (Δb = -.050, p < .001). Conversely, the skill layer was among the strongest-changing layers, showing significantly larger increases than the identity layer (Δb = .050, p < .001) and the thought layer (Δb = .035, p = .021). All remaining pairwise differences were nonsignificant.

Given the limited statistical power to detect higher-order interactions, we did not estimate a full Time × Layer × Facet interaction model. Instead, we conducted facet-specific analyses to examine whether the overall pattern generalized across facets (see Table 4 and Figure 2). P-values were Bonferroni-corrected to account for the three comparisons. For social boldness, all layers demonstrated statistically significant growth from pre-to postintervention (b = .090 -.173 per 3-week time step, all ps < .001). Consistent with the pooled analysis, the identity layer again showed the weakest growth, increasing significantly less than the behavior (Δb = -.056, p = .040) and feeling (Δb = -.083, p < .001) layers. Unlike the pooled analysis, the feeling layer was among the strongest-changing layers, increasing significantly more than the identity (Δb = .083, p < .001) and thought (Δb = .069, p = .005) layers.

Similarly, for resilience, all layers showed statistically significant change over time (b = .080 -.211 per 3-week time step, all ps < .001). Again, identity was among the weakestchanging layers, increasing significantly less than the skill (Δb = -.130, p < .001) and thought (Δb = -.076, p = .019) layers. Consistent with the pooled analysis, the skill layer was among the strongest-changing layers, increasing significantly more than the identity (Δb = .130, p < .001), feeling (Δb = .071, p = .032), and behavior layers (Δb = .109, p < .001).

For punctuality, only the skill (b = .085 per time step, p < .01) and behavior (b = .082, p = .006) layers exhibited significant change during the intervention. Additionally, no posthoc pairwise contrasts between layers reached significance, suggesting limited evidence for systematic differences in change rates across layers.

Evidence for PCIs in a professional preparation context

Our findings revealed significant increases in targeted resilience (d = .82), social boldness (d = .62), and punctuality (d = .22) over the course of the six-week intervention.

Whereas the effect for punctuality was comparable to the average effect reported in metaanalytic research on PCIs (d = .22), the effects for resilience and social boldness were substantially larger and approached the upper range of those observed in previous studies.

One possible explanation for these relatively large effects is that our intervention targeted rather narrow personality facets, which may be more amenable to intervention than broad trait domains (e.g., Allemand & Flückiger, 2017; Stieger et al., 2020). A second explanation may be the intervention's contextual specificity: both change efforts and outcome assessments were centered on students' preparation for the school-to-work transition. Such context-specific changes may be more readily achieved over a short period than broader personality changes that require generalization across multiple life domains (Jackson & Wright, 2024). Importantly, because the meta-analytic benchmarks used for comparison (Haehner et al., 2024) are largely based on similar young adult samples, the relatively large effect sizes observed here are unlikely to reflect age-related personality plasticity alone.

Several findings lend further support to the intervention's effectiveness. Improvements were stronger for targeted than non-targeted facets (d = .35) and changes in the intervention group exceeded those observed in the quasi-experimental control group (d = .43, on average).

Notably, small improvements in non-targeted facets within the intervention group did not differ significantly from corresponding changes in the control group, suggesting that they likely reflect normative changes (e.g., Bleidorn et al., 2022) rather than secondary effects of the intervention. Finally, consistent with both our expectations and previous research (e.g., Stieger et al., 2023), intervention gains were maintained at the four-week follow-up, with even further increases observed for social boldness (d = .19) and resilience (d = .09).

Perceived feasibility as a moderator of change

A second goal of the study was to examine potential moderators of intervention effectiveness, as prior work has yielded limited and inconsistent empirical support (e.g., Haehner et al., 2024; Jackson & Wright, 2024). When all moderators were examined simultaneously, perceived feasibility of change emerged as a small but statistically significant predictor of greater increases in targeted facets over time. This finding is consistent with theoretical models of VPC, which propose that individuals are more likely to initiate and sustain change efforts when they perceive change as attainable (Hennecke et al., 2014).

Although this finding appears inconsistent with previous studies reporting weak or nonsignificant effects of beliefs about personality malleability (e.g., Hudson et al., 2021; Olaru et al., 2025), those studies assessed general beliefs about whether personality can change, whereas our measure captured the perceived difficulty of achieving a specific development goal and may therefore be more closely linked to sustained change efforts. From a practical standpoint, these findings suggest that interventions may be more effective when they foster perceived feasibility, for example through psychoeducation and progress feedback that reinforce individuals' confidence in their ability to achieve their change goals.

Because the four moderators were conceptually related, they were examined simultaneously to estimate their unique contributions to personality change. As some moderators may operate through one another (Olaru et al., 2025), the present estimates reflect unique rather than total effects and may therefore be smaller than those reported in studies examining moderators in isolation. However, supplementary analyses examining each moderator separately produced the same overall pattern of results, suggesting that the null findings for intervention engagement, desire to change, and perceived value of change cannot be attributed solely to their simultaneous inclusion in the model. In addition, measurement considerations may provide context for the null findings. For example, because desire to change was assessed after participants selected a goal, it reflected goal strength rather than goal adoption, potentially reducing its predictive value compared with measures that also capture the absence of a change goal or desires in the opposite direction (Olaru et al., 2025).

Intervention engagement also failed to predict change, despite previous findings suggesting a positive association with personality growth (Hudson et al., 2019; Olaru et al., 2025). One explanation may be the reliance on self-reported and retrospective engagement measures. Future research could use more objective assessments, such as coded written reflections or experience-sampling measures of implementation enactment. Beyond frequency of engagement, qualitative aspects of intervention participation may also matter.

Recent evidence suggests that the experienced valence of implementation attempts may be associated with stronger personality change (Olaru et al., 2025). Different forms of engagement may also facilitate change at different psychological layers. Although the present study was not designed to test these differential pathways, future research could examine whether specific intervention components (e.g., reflection) preferentially facilitate change at particular psychological layers (e.g., thoughts and feelings).

Unpacking personality change across psychological layers

Finally, this study addressed a fundamental yet underexplored question in VPC research: which psychological layers of personality-thoughts, feelings, behaviors-are most responsive to intervention. Olaru et al. (2022) recently argued that personality scales in intervention research should be more balanced in terms of these layers, as many widely used measures emphasize certain layers unevenly across traits (Wilt & Revelle, 2015). The issue is that such imbalances can make it difficult to determine whether observed differences in change across personality domains reflect true differences in trait malleability or differences in the malleability of the layers assessed. Building further on this call, the present study explicitly distinguished between different layers within each facet.

Findings for social boldness and resilience indicated that the intervention produced change in all three traditional personality layers of behavior, thoughts, and feelings. In addition, the skill layer increased significantly, suggesting that the intervention also strengthened participants' capacities to perform behaviors when needed. This is consistent with expectations, as social, emotional, and behavioral skills are considered particularly amenable to development and are often targeted using similar intervention strategies (Soto et al., 2022 (Soto et al., , 2026)). Identity also showed significant growth, reflecting that participants partially assimilated the changes into how they perceive themselves in professional contexts.

However, a different pattern emerged for punctuality, with significant increases in the skill and behavior layers but not in the thought, feeling, and identity layers. This suggests that participants became more capable of being punctual and engaging in punctual behavior without corresponding changes in how they thought, felt, or perceived themselves. It is possible that-despite our efforts to develop a balanced measure of punctuality including indicators for each psychological layer-traits may nevertheless differ in the extent to which they comprise affective, behavioral, cognitive, skill-related, and identity-based processes (e.g., Bleidorn et al., 2026). From this perspective, punctuality may be less strongly grounded in recurrent patterns of thoughts and feelings than social boldness or resilience.

When examining differences in rates of change across layers within social boldness and resilience, a broadly consistent pattern emerged. In both facets, identity was among the slowest-changing layers, exhibiting significantly smaller increases than the two other layers. These patterns suggest that identity may be particularly resistant to short-term interventioninduced change, which aligns with theoretical models proposing that it develops through a gradual reflective process in which new thoughts, feelings, and behaviors are slowly recognized and integrated into one's self-concept (Jackson & Wright, 2024).

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations that point to promising avenues for future research.

First, the quasi-experimental design enhances internal validity relative to correlational designs without a control group (e.g., Hudson et al., 2019), but does not provide the full rigor of randomization. Specifically, self-selection into development goals resulted in baseline differences between the intervention and control groups, with intervention participants scoring lower on their targeted facet at baseline than control participants. Although the MLMs controlled for such baseline differences, regression-to-the-mean effects cannot be ruled out entirely. However, because self-selection into development goals is fundamental to VPC research, future research should retain it while using stronger controls, such as randomized wait-list designs that assign participants with similar development goals to immediate or delayed intervention groups (Haehner et al., 2025).

A second, related limitation of the present study concerns the potential influence of demand effects in the intervention condition, despite the inclusion of a control group. That is, placebo effects may have influenced participants' personality ratings, with expectations potentially shaping perceived rather than actual change. To mitigate demand effects, Hudson et al. (2019) have suggested using placebo tasks in the control group, but participants may notice that these tasks are unrelated to their development goals. Another alternative is to use measures beyond self-report. For example, informant reports may reduce self-serving bias and, although they can still be influenced by demand effects if informants are aware of the intervention's goals, this influence is typically less pronounced (e.g., Stieger et al., 2020 Stieger et al., , 2021)). However, the observability of different psychological layers should also be considered, as self-observer agreement is typically stronger for overt aspects such as behavior than for more internal aspects such as thoughts (Wiedenroth et al., 2024). Finally, longer-term follow-up assessments could help mitigate concerns about demand effects, as such influences may naturally diminish over time.

Third, it remains unclear whether the observed personality changes lead to measurable improvements in participants' professional functioning. While prior research has linked personality change to positive outcomes across life domains (e.g., Wright & Jackson, 2023), our study did not assess these outcomes. Future research should incorporate criterion measures-such as work performance or satisfaction-to determine whether personality change translates into meaningful gains in professional preparation settings.

A final limitation concerns the specificity of our sample. Participants were psychology students enrolled in the same course and were predominantly female, reflecting a broader trend in VPC research, which often relies on female-dominated psychology student samples (Haehner et al., 2024). Although this homogeneity supports internal validity, it may limit generalizability and may have increased receptivity to reflective intervention components. Moreover, despite completing short-term internships, participants had relatively limited exposure to professional settings. Future research should extend this work to students in longer internships and to working populations, who are typically older and may exhibit more limited personality plasticity (Bleidorn et al., 2022).

Conclusion

This study provides initial evidence that PCIs can be effectively implemented in professional preparation settings, producing meaningful improvements in resilience and social boldness and, to a lesser extent, in punctuality over a six-week period. Importantly, these effects were sustained at the six-week follow-up. Moreover, our multi-layered approach contributes to theory by offering a more nuanced framework for understanding what aspects of personality interventions ultimately change. Future research should replicate these findings in more diverse and professionally embedded samples to strengthen generalizability and assess longer-term impact. Ultimately, we believe this study offers a promising path toward facilitating professional development, both by preparing students for their future careers and by fostering meaningful change in employees within organizations. Yet, surprisingly little research has examined whether interventions actually produce trait change across these different layers. To date, psychological layers have primarily been examined as focal points of intervention design (Haehner et al., 2025) or theorized mechanisms of personality change (Bleidorn et al., 2026). However, much less attention has been devoted to examining these layers as outcomes of PCIs. Change is usually assessed at the level of domains or facets-and, in rare cases, at the level of personality nuances (Olaru et al., 2022)-using established trait inventories, such as the Big Five Inventory-2 (BFI-2; Soto & John, 2017). This approach introduces two key limitations. First, many widely used Big Five item sets provide uneven coverage across psychological layers, disproportionately reflecting certain components (e.g., conscientiousness items primarily capture behavior, emotional stability items largely assess affect; Wilt & Revelle, 2015), which may skew interpretations of what exactly has changed perspective, this work sought to contribute to VPC literature by applying existing frameworks of VPC to the professional preparation context and offering nuanced insight into how personality change unfolds across different psychological layers.

Although several researchers have argued that PCIs may be particularly relevant in professional preparation and work-related contexts (Bleidorn et al., 2019; Dupré & Wille, 2025), their effectiveness in these applied settings remains largely unexplored (Asselmann et al., 2026).
