Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Full text

Abstract

Political trust sustains democratic cooperation, but it may also reflect a broader tendency to align with those in power. If politically trusting individuals are more inclined to defer to political authority, can they remain critical of the information authorities provide? In this pre-registered survey experiment, participants (N= 1010) evaluated a series of political arguments from authority, classified as either normatively sound or fallacious. Political trust consistently predicted argument acceptability, without compromising evaluative accuracy. However, we found that trust was negatively correlated with soundness discrimination among participants who reported a stronger interest in politics. Exploratory analyses further showed that trust had a similar negative effect when participants registered identical ratings of trustworthiness for different political institutions. Together, these findings suggest that political trust may increase the persuasiveness of arguments from political authorities and that, under certain conditions, this effect may come at a cost of critical evaluation.

Introduction

Political trust, generally defined as the public's confidence in political institutions (Bobzien, 2023), is often framed as an essential condition for democracy (Easton, 1975; Mishler & Rose, 2001; Turper & Aarts, 2015). Several behavioral outcomes associated with political trust are conducive to democratic governance, such as greater voter turnout and stronger support for government policies (Devine, 2024; Levi & Stoker, 2000; Li et al., 2022). A broader pattern that emerges from the literature is that politically trusting individuals appear more willing to defer to political authority and comply with institutional directives. This pattern is also expressed attitudinally. Studies on authoritarianism and system justification find a positive association with political trust, such that politically trusting individuals are more likely to hold authoritarian attitudes (Dunn, 2020; Jamil & Baniamin, 2020; Miranda et al., 2022; Pernia, 2021; Pernia & Rye, 2025) and more likely to legitimize authority, hierarchy and systemic power (Friesen et al., 2018; Kelemen et al., 2014). Together, these behavioral and attitudinal correlates suggest that political trust is associated with a broader tendency to support social and political authority.

This association has the potential to undermine the democratic function of political trust.

While collective confidence in governance can enhance societal cohesion and facilitate effective policymaking, democracy also depends on a vigilant citizenry whose skepticism acts as a safeguard against the erosion of accountability and protects against undesirable state activities (Norris, 2022). If political trust implies a systematically positive attitude toward political authorities (Hadarics, 2025), it may also affect how individuals engage with these authorities in the information environment. When citizens are inclined to align with those in power they could be proportionally less inclined to question the claims, decisions and information communicated by the political status quo. To explore whether political trust can have such negative implications, we need to move beyond behavioral and attitudinal accounts and towards the domain of information processing and informal reasoning.

Certain studies suggest that political trust often predicts reduced evaluative scrutiny across various contexts (Hadarics, 2025; Rossini et al., 2023; Trumbo & McComas, 2008; van Zoonen et al., 2023). This line of research frames political trust as an epistemic cue with negative implications for the critical evaluation of political authorities and of communicated information. At the same time, research on misinformation and conspiracy theories indicates that trust reduces susceptibility to low-quality information (Giancola et al., 2025; Jedinger & Masch, 2025; Zimmermann & Kohring, 2020), such that politically trusting individuals are less likely to endorse false news and conspiratorial beliefs. These findings need not be empirically contradictory. Existing studies differ substantially in how they operationalize low-quality information and, consequently, in the normative expectations against which critical thinking is assessed. While in some studies, low-quality information consists of anti-establishment narratives and misinformation originating from non-authoritative sources (Jedinger & Masch, 2025; Zimmermann & Kohring, 2020), other studies consider political authorities as sources of misinformation (Rossini et al., 2023; Trumbo & McComas, 2008). Given the connection between political trust and support for authority, the same evaluative strategy -endorsement of authoritative sources in politics -could be interpreted as increased epistemic vigilance in some paradigms but as decreased epistemic vigilance in others. This makes it difficult to establish whether political trust is associated with sensitivity to information quality or simply determines receptivity to political authority.

In the present study, we address this gap in prior literature by exploring the trust-authority relationship in political communication. To that end, we focus on a particularly relevant communicative scenario, namely argumentation from authority. If political trust captures evaluative processes that place greater importance on the claims and opinions of authoritative sources in politics, then we can expect that arguments from authority, and in particular arguments from political authority, will reflect this trend. More importantly, the normative distinction between sound and fallacious appeals to authority allows us to explore whether political trust predicts the quality of reasoning in political communication. While previous paradigms obscure the distinction between receptivity to authority and sensitivity to information quality, the argumentation framework allows those dimensions to be isolated because authoritative standing can be preserved within an argument while the soundness of reliance on that particular authority varies. This approach provides a more direct test of the association between political trust and endorsement of authority, thereby clarifying its broader implications for critical reasoning in political communication.

Authority and trustworthiness as persuasive cues

Arguments from authority are defined as claims supported primarily by the status of the argument's source (Andone & Hernández, 2019). From a pragma-dialectical perspective, such arguments rest on a symptomatic scheme in which the acceptability of a standpoint is supported by presenting its endorsement by an authoritative source as a sign of its acceptability. In its basic structure, this scheme includes a premise stating that an authoritative source asserts or endorses a proposition and a conclusion stating that the proposition should therefore be accepted (van Eemeren & Grootendorst, 1992; van Eemeren et al., 2007). By consequence, the persuasive force of arguments from authority depends, at least in part, on the recipient's willingness to acknowledge the cited authority as reliable.

When people decide whether to accept a message as true or reject it as false, they often do so based on the characteristics of the message's source (Booth-Butterfield & Gutowski, 1993; Guillory & Geraci, 2013; Henkel & Mattson, 2011; Koh & Sundar, 2010; Priester & Petty, 2003). Generally, perceiving a source as trustworthy makes people more likely to perceive the information communicated by that source as reliable (Meinert & Kramer, 2022; Koh & Sundar, 2010). As such, the concept of trust is particularly relevant in the context of argumentation from authority. Because audiences ascribe greater epistemic weight to information communicated by trusted sources, they are more likely to accept argumentative premises endorsed by authorities they trust. Greater acceptance of an argumentative premise, in turn, provides stronger grounds for accepting the conclusion that follows from it. Hence, the acceptability of arguments from authority increases when an argument appeals to an authoritative source that is perceived as trustworthy.

Traditional measures of political trust are based on self-reported trust in political authorities such as the government, political parties and courts of justice, typically using Likerttype scales and combining several items into an aggregated trust score (Eurobarometer, 2023; European Social Survey, 2018; 2020; Smets et al., 2024). In political science and social psychology these measures reflect an evaluative attitude toward the political system and its various institutions (Levi & Stoker, 2000). Within the context of political communication however, the same measures could also capture an epistemic orientation. Given that source trustworthiness serves as an epistemic cue, measures of political trust should predict the perceived credibility of statements made or endorsed by political authorities. When these authorities appear as argument sources, political trust is expected to function as a source cue.

We thus hypothesize that political trust will positively predict the acceptability of arguments from authority which appeal to a political entity as an authoritative source.

H1: As political trust increases, arguments from political authority become more acceptable.

Trust and sensitivity to argument soundness

Reliance on source cues is often an efficient and rational evaluative strategy. Sometimes there is little choice but to accept information on the basis of someone else's claims. For instance, there are topics which require so much specialized knowledge that laypeople cannot independently verify them (van Eemeren & Snoeck Henkemans, 2017). However, this strategy can become problematic if used as a default response. That is, if reasoners outsource evaluative judgments to authority without considering the substance of the advanced claim (Meinert & Krammer, 2022). To derive accurate conclusions from authority arguments, an audience must sufficiently engage with their content to determine whether reliance on authoritative sources is warranted in the first place. This is especially important when arguments appeal to political authorities. By default, political actors possess authoritative standing in view of their recognized role in public decision-making. This form of authority is defined as 'deontic authority' and it describes individuals or institutions authorized to formulate directives (Brozek, 2013; Koszowy & Walton, 2019). Deontic authority is conceptually distinct from epistemic authority, which is grounded in a source's knowledge or expertise (ibid.). While the two types of authority may happen to overlap in some cases, epistemic authority cannot be automatically inferred from deontic authority. However, this inference could occur in the context of political argumentation if audiences perceive political actors as authoritative by default. It follows that reasoners should distinguish between deontic and epistemic aspects of authority when assessing argument acceptability.

Dialectical approaches to argumentation provide a framework for this exact task. The soundness of arguments from authority is determined through a series of critical questions aimed at identifying whether the cited source possesses genuine authority, whether their expertise is relevant to the topic under discussion and whether their claim has evidentiary support (Wagemans, 2011; Walton, 2014). If one or several of these criteria are not met, then the argument constitutes a fallacy. One such fallacy is 'abuse of authority' (argumentum ad verecundiam), where the cited source is credited with an authority they do not actually possess, or the authority's domain is not relevant in the particular context in which it is invoked (van Eemeren & Garssen, 2023; Wagemans, 2011). Another possible fallacy in authority argumentation relates to 'populist argumentation' (argumentum ad populum), upon which the view of the majority is presented as sufficient proof for a claim's acceptability (e.g. "If many people believe X, then X must be correct") while failing to demonstrate the relevance of the majority appeal (Andone, 2016; Godden, 2008).

The distinction between sound arguments and fallacies offers a normative benchmark for measuring sensitivity to variations in argument quality. When people evaluate arguments based on their merits, sound arguments should elicit more favorable evaluations than fallacies.

Understanding when and why individuals fail to make that distinction therefore allows us to determine the conditions that reduce argument scrutiny. Interestingly, previous studies point to source trustworthiness as one such condition. Priester and Petty (1995; 2003) found that participants were less likely to distinguish between strong and weak arguments when these arguments were advanced by trustworthy sources. By contrast, argument quality was more readily perceived when subjects were exposed to arguments from untrustworthy sources.

Research on epistemic vigilance explains this pattern by proposing that humans tend to avoid the cognitive cost of effortful processing unless they are motivated by situational factors to do otherwise (Sperber et al., 2010). In this context, trust in a source can reduce the perceived need to critically examine communicated information (Levy, 2022; Mayo, 2015). Given that source trustworthiness is generally translated as informational credibility, individuals presented with claims from trusted sources are not situationally motivated to scrutinize the substance of these claims and, instead, tend to accept information at face value (Mayo, 2015). An unreliable source on the other hand, invites more cognitive deliberation and increased attention to the content of communicated information (Ziegler et al., 2002). That is because information communicated by untrustworthy sources is more likely to be viewed as potentially untrue, and therefore reasoners are situationally motivated to remain epistemically vigilant so as not to accept erroneous claims.

Political trust, when conceptualized as a source cue, should elicit a similar trustworthiness effect in argumentation from authority. The proposed theoretical mechanism follows from previous studies. That is, higher levels of political trust should reduce the perceived need to scrutinize whether reliance on cited political authorities is warranted. Empirically, this should manifest as weak soundness discrimination. As political trust increases, we therefore expect that people will evaluate sound arguments and fallacies in increasingly similar terms.

H2a: As political trust increases, individuals are less likely to distinguish sound arguments from fallacies.

Because we presume that politically trusting individuals will be less motivated to scrutinize the arguments, we also expect that they will be more susceptible to fallacies from authority.

H2b: As political trust increases, individuals are more likely to endorse fallacies from political authority.

The moderating role of political interest

So far, we have discussed our expectations for the overall effect of political trust on argument evaluation. To understand the specific conditions that determine when and for whom this effect takes place, we turn to persuasion literature. Studies on persuasive communication have been dominated by dual-process theories of cognition (Crano & Prislin, 2006; El Hedhli & Zourrig, 2023; Luttrell, 2018). The Elaboration Likelihood Model (Petty & Cacioppo, 1984) is one such prominent example that falls under the dual-process umbrella. The ELM identifies two distinct routes to persuasion with distinct cognitive implications. The central route represents persuasion under high elaboration conditions -that is, when reasoners exert greater cognitive effort to process persuasive messages (Kitchen et al., 2014). Conversely, the peripheral route describes how persuasion occurs when elaboration is low. These two routes do not represent a binary system of argument processing but rather idealized extremes across one elaboration continuum (O'Keefe, 2013). The likelihood that one route is activated over the other varies as a function of processing motivation and cognitive ability (Petty & Wegener, 1999). When a reasoner is properly motivated and able to systematically analyze an argument, they are more likely to engage in elaboration and therefore more likely to process messages via the central route. Previous studies have identified several factors that influence motivation and capacity including Need for Cognition, mood, affective response and distraction (Kitchen et al., 2014).

Among the most widely studied motivational influences in the ELM is the degree to which one feels personally involved with the topic that is discussed. For example, elaboration likelihood is higher if the topic that is discussed is of high personal relevance to the reasoners, and if they as individuals have a higher propensity to engage in deep and intensive reasoning. By contrast, if the topic leaves them disinterested and they are not particularly keen to think, then elaboration likelihood is low and by extension, the peripheral route is activated.

Because the two ELM routes imply entirely different degrees of argument scrutiny, they also imply that different argument features become more or less effective depending on the specific elaboration conditions. Central-route persuasion involves extensive issue-relevant thinking and, as such, is highly sensitive to argument content and argument quality (Petty & Cacioppo, 1979). The ELM predicts that the more cognitively engaged an individual is, the more likely they are to be persuaded by strong arguments compared to weak ones. Several studies offer empirical support for this prediction, suggesting that argument quality is a key determinant of persuasive outcomes achieved via the central route and that its persuasive value diminishes when the audience moves towards the peripheral end of the elaboration continuum (Andrews & Shimp, 1990; Breves et al., 2024; Carpenter, 2015; Cyr et al., 2018; Susmann et al., 2021). By contrast, peripheral-route persuasion relies on simple decision procedures that require little information processing (O'Keefe, 2013). These procedures focus less on the communicated content within an argument and more on extrinsic argument features.

Source-related considerations are widely recognized as peripheral cues in ELM literature (Kitchen et al., 2014). As such, perceived source trustworthiness becomes more consequential for persuasion under low elaboration conditions. However, the influence of source cues is not strictly constrained to the peripheral route. ELM literature posits that one variable may serve multiple roles in persuasion depending on the amount of elaboration (Briñol & Petty, 2009; Petty & Briñol, 2014). Under high elaboration conditions, source variables can themselves function as relevant arguments. That is, reasoners take into account their impressions of argument sources as reasons for favouring or disfavouring a standpoint (Briñol & Petty, 2009).

When an individual uses a source as an argument, they are inclined to carefully examine how the characteristics of this source relate to the advocated position and whether they sufficiently support the claim. Although the ELM proposes different mechanisms through which source cues influence persuasion based on elaboration, both the aforementioned mechanisms lead to the same directional prediction in the context of the present study.

Previously, we hypothesized that political trust will predict the acceptability of authority arguments (H1) and patterns of soundness discrimination (H2). Here, we propose that these effects will be stronger under low levels of political interest. Drawing on the ELM, we treat political interest, i.e. the extent to which individuals are interested in politics, as a theoretically relevant variable to processing motivation. We therefore expect that the influence of political trust on argument acceptability and soundness discrimination should depend on political interest. Firstly, because the peripheral influence of trust decreases under high elaboration, politically interested individuals should rely less on their perceptions of the cited political authority and more on argument content. Secondly, because trust serves as an argument under high elaboration, politically interested individuals should be more attuned to the relevance of source attributes for the acceptability of the claim. Both of these mechanisms produce the same prediction: when they evaluate arguments from political authority, those with a strong interest in politics are less likely to be influenced by political trust and more likely distinguish between sound authority arguments and fallacies. Accordingly, we formulate our third hypothesis:

H3: The positive association between political trust and evaluations of fallacious authority arguments will weaken as political interest increases.

Diffuse and specific political trust as distinct cues

In conceptualizing political trust as a source cue, we presume that it elicits similar epistemic effects to those associated with source trustworthiness in communication and persuasion research. However, it is important to note that political trust differs from general source trustworthiness in one important aspect. Source trustworthiness typically concerns the trust allocated to the specific source of a message, whereas political trust (in the way that it is typically measured) reflects an aggregated assessment across several political authorities.

Individuals with high levels of overall political trust can nevertheless regard a particular political institution as untrustworthy. When that institution is invoked within an argument from authority, a high level of political trust may not predict either acceptance of the argument or the degree of scrutiny applied to its evaluation. To account for this possibility, we need to distinguish between political trust as a generalized attitude and political trust as a distinct evaluative judgment for distinct political authorities.

To that effect, we draw on Easton's (1965; 1975) seminal work on the different types of political support. Easton distinguishes between diffuse and specific support, with the former reflecting a generalized attitude toward the political system as a whole and the latter describing trust as an object-specific attitude directed at particular institutions based on their individual performance. Diffuse trust treats political systems as monolithic structures, wherein the trustworthiness of one type of authority can be inferred from the trustworthiness of another (Proszowska et al., 2023). Specific trust, on the other hand, allows for the differentiation of political authorities as it is distinctly calibrated to the perceived trustworthiness of each particular entity (ibid.). Wu and Wilkes (2018) draw on Easton's diffuse-specific distinction to identify three types of political trusters: critical trusters, compliants, and cynics. These profiles capture not only how much people trust political authorities, but also how they trust. Compliants and cynics represent two opposing forms of diffuse political trust that differ primarily in their evaluative direction. Both groups apply broadly consistent evaluations across political authorities; for compliants these evaluations are positive, whereas for cynics these evaluations are negative.

Critical trusters, by contrast, display specific trust. Rather than generalizing their perceptions across institutions or leaders, they assess each political authority independently. As a result, their trust in one authority does not automatically shape the perceived trustworthiness of others.

Our earlier predictions are rooted in the premise that political trust reflects a broader disposition toward political authorities. While this assumption is informed by previous studies (Friesen et al., 2018; Hadarics, 2025), Easton's distinction proposes a more nuanced approach. Specifically, that this premise holds when trust is diffuse but not when trust is specific. We therefore proceed to formulate an additional exploratory hypothesis: that trust differentiation reflects the extent to which one endorses a political entity by virtue of its authority status. While H1 and H2 reflect our expectations for the overall effect of political trust on argument evaluation, we also hypothesize that this effect is moderated by trust differentiation as an empirical indicator consistent with specific-trust profiles. We expect that as differentiation increases the effects predicted in H1 and H2 will be weaker whereas when differentiation is low these effects are stronger.

H4:

The effect of political trust on soundness discrimination will be stronger when trust differentiation is low rather than high.

The present study

To test our predictions regarding the influence of political trust on the persuasiveness of authority arguments, we conducted a pre-registered survey experiment. Participants were asked to evaluate a series of political arguments which appealed to various authoritative sources in politics as a means to support the advanced standpoints. Argument soundness was experimentally manipulated in accordance with the normative framework of pragma-dialectics.

We collected measures of political trust to test whether individual differences predicted argument acceptability (H1) and sensitivity to argument quality (H2). We additionally measured political interest (H3) and trust differentiation (H4) as moderators of the trust effect.

Methods

Participants

A national Swedish sample of 1,010 adults (473 female, 535 male, 2 other; age range: 18-84 years, mean age = 55 years) was recruited through a third-party organization (Novus Research, a Swedish probability-based survey panel). Probability-based sampling was employed to enhance representativeness (see: Supplementary Materials for detailed demographic information).

Materials

Our set of materials consisted of 12 arguments from authority, constructed by the authors for the purposes of this study. To account for inter-item variation, the set included two versions of every original item (n = 6) with each version corresponding to one of two experimental conditions (sound vs. fallacious arguments). Participants were presented with only one version of each argument. The allocation of items to conditions was randomized on a per-participant basis and the overall set of the 6 selected arguments was equally distributed across the two soundness conditions.

The argumentative content was held constant in every item pair, meaning that the sound and fallacious iteration of an argument had the same premise and the same conclusion (see Table 1). Items within a pair differed in terms of the referenced authority, which determined whether the argument was normatively classified as sound or fallacious. Specifically, we manipulated argument soundness by introducing either a match or a mismatch between the authority's domain and the domain of the topic under discussion. All sound stimuli referenced political sources that possessed relevant epistemic authority to the issue at hand. By contrast, all fallacious stimuli included inappropriate appeals to authority, either in the 'abuse of authority' variant or in the populist variant.

All appeals to authority across the two conditions included sources that could reasonably be regarded as authoritative in politics. This design follows directly from our research aims. We did not wish to contrast the persuasiveness of arguments appealing to authorities and arguments appealing to non-authorities. Rather, our focus lies on whether participants infer epistemic authority from deontic sources, even when the source's authority is not relevant to the topic at hand. To that end, authoritative standing was preserved in the sources of fallacious arguments and domain relevance was used as the benchmark for argument soundness.

In terms of content, the arguments were constructed to minimize the influence of prior policy attitudes and baseline disagreement with the conclusions. The six selected argument topics (renewable energy, gender equality, education, healthcare, public infrastructure, and cybersecurity) were adapted from issues identified as important to the Swedish population in Novus Research's 2023 survey (Novus, 2024). More importantly, both versions of each argument were grounded in policy positions expected to receive relatively broad public support (ibid.). As such, differences in argument evaluation were less likely to reflect disagreement with the policy position itself and more likely to reflect participants' responses to the experimental manipulation.

The structure of the stimuli was heavily standardized. Every argument contained one conclusion (the advocated claim) and one premise (the reasoning for accepting the claim) in a standard premise-therefore-conclusion order. The types of statements used to construct premises and conclusions were similarly standardized, on the basis of a widely used typology in debate theory (Freeley & Steinberg, 2008; Inch & Warnick, 1989) which distinguishes between statements of fact (F), statements of value (V) and statements of policy (P). Of these three types, F and P statements are of relevance to our stimuli. F statements contain factual observations, i.e. they state what is. P statements contain proposed actions, i.e. they state what should be done. All items included in our stimulus set contained an F statement as a premise (X said Y) and a P statement as a conclusion (therefore Y should be implemented). Despite linguistically grounding the materials to preserve naturalistic argumentative conditions, the use of the FP statement type configuration allowed us to uphold a high degree of standardization across items.

Measures

Argument Evaluation

Argument evaluation was measured on a 7-point scale ranging from 1 (not convincing at all) to 7 (extremely convincing).

Political Trust

We measured political trust using a 5-items scale adapted from the European Social Survey (2018; 2020) and the Eurobarometer (2023). Participants were asked to indicate their trust to several political institutions (the Police, Courts of Justice, the Media, the Government, Political Parties) on an 11-point scale, with responses ranging from "do not trust at all" (0) to "completely trust" (10). We calculated the mean score across all items as an index of overall political trust.

The five-item scale showed good internal consistency in our sample (Cronbach's α = .76, 95% CI [.74, .78]).

Trust Differentiation

Trust differentiation was subsequently operationalized as the standard deviation of each participant's trust ratings across the five institutions. Higher values indicate greater variability in trust across institutional targets, reflecting a tendency to discriminate between political authorities rather than applying uniform evaluations across them. Lower values indicate more uniform trust ratings, consistent with a diffuse orientation toward political authority. This approach treats within-person variability in trust ratings as a continuous index of evaluative discrimination Political interest Political interest was measured with a single self-report item. Responses were registered on a 4-point scale ranging from 1 ("not at all interested") to 4 ("extremely interested"; Prior, 2010; Stromback et al., 2013).

Ideology

Given that outcomes of political communication are often subject to ideological differences, we measured participants' political ideology for inclusion as a control variable in our analyses. We asked participants to self-place along two 11-point scales (ranging from "left" to "right" and "progressive" to "conservative") to indicate their political ideology (Jost et al., 2009).

Following our pre-registration, we used the mean across the two items as a composite score for political ideology (ρ = .69).

Sociodemographic variables

Demographic variables were collected, including gender (coded as1 = male,2 = female,3 = other), age, and educational background. Education was assessed using detailed items asking about participants' current study level or highest completed education, with responses ranging from primary through tertiary education. These were recoded into three more balanced ISCED categories: primary/lower secondary education, upper secondary education and higher education.

Procedure

Data collection was conducted by Novus Research in February 2026, using a Computer-Assisted Web Interviewing (CAWI) procedure. All subjects gave informed consent prior to participation. Prior to the task, we administered a set of instructions aiming to guide evaluation towards argument soundness and away from agreement-based considerations. Participants were told that in each argument, the speaker expressed an opinion and cited other people's claims as reasons for holding this opinion. They were then instructed to assign ratings focusing on whether the reason provided a convincing justification for the opinion. We explicitly instructed participants to disregard their personal views on the topics discussed. Following participation, participants were debriefed on the design and aims of the research.

Pre-registration

The design, hypotheses and planned analyses were preregistered on OSF Hypotheses 1-3 were formally preregistered, while Hypothesis 4 is considered exploratory.

H2 (pre-registered): Political trust and sensitivity to argument soundness

To assess whether political trust differentially predicts evaluations of sound and fallacious arguments, we estimated a linear mixed-effects model including political trust, argument soundness and their interaction with random intercepts per participant and item, using the lmer package in RStudio (Kuznetsova et al., 2017). Political trust was again positively associated with favorable evaluation, producing a modest but statistically significant effect (b = 0.12, SE = 0.02, 95% CI[0.08 0.16], p < .001). As a general trend, sound arguments were evaluated more favorably than fallacious arguments, although the effect did not reach conventional levels of statistical significance (b = 0.43, SE = 0.24, 95% CI[-0.03, 0.9], p = .10). Crucially, there was no evidence that political trust moderated the effect of argument soundness, indicated by a nonsignificant interaction term (b = -0.01, SE = 0.02, 95% CI[-0.06, 0.03], p = .49). Both the relative magnitude of these effects and their statistical significance was robust to the inclusion of sociodemographic and ideological covariates in the controlled model. Because the sound and fallacious arguments constituted matched pairs across six argumentative topics, we inspected stimulus heterogeneity with topic as the grouping variable. We used the emmeans (Lenth, 2025) package in RStudio to examine topic-specific contrasts and found that, across all item pairs, sound arguments were evaluated more favorably than fallacies, although the magnitude of this effect varied across topics (see: Supplementary Materials). Given the observed heterogeneity in the soundness effect across stimulus pairs, we estimated an additional linear mixed-effects model for the trust x soundness interaction, with a random soundness slope per topic. This specification was not included in our pre-registration and is reported as a robustness check accounting for stimulus-level heterogeneity. In this model, sound arguments were evaluated significantly more favorably than fallacious arguments (b = 0.43, SE = 0.11, 95% CI[0.19, 0.67], p = .013). Importantly, the political trust x soundness interaction remained non-significant (b = -0.015, SE = 0.021, 95% CI[-0.06, 0.03], p = .49), including after adjustment for age, gender, education, and political ideology. Overall, political trust predicted more favorable evaluations of authority-based arguments but did not predict lower sensitivity to argument soundness as expected.

H3 (pre-registered): Political interest as a moderator of soundness discrimination

To test whether the relationship between political trust and argument evaluation varied as a function of political interest and argument soundness, we estimated a linear mixed-effects model including argument evaluation as an outcome variable predicted by political trust, political interest, argument soundness and their interactions, with random intercepts for participants and items. Analyses were conducted in RStudio using the lmerTest (Kuznetsova et al., 2017) and emmeans (Lenth, 2025) packages.

The focal three-way interaction was statistically significant (b = -0.057, SE = 0.025, 95% CI[-0.11, -0.01], p = .022). The interaction remained virtually unchanged after adjustment for age, political ideology, gender, and education and under the alternative topic-based randomeffects specification (see: Supplementary Materials). As preregistered, we probed this interaction by estimating the effect of political trust on discrimination between sound and fallacious arguments at low (-1 SD) and high (+1 SD) levels of political interest. At low political interest, political trust was not significantly associated with soundness discrimination (Δb = 0.027, SE = 0.029, 95% CI [-0.029, 0.083], p = .344). However, at high political interest greater political trust was associated with weaker discrimination between sound and fallacious arguments (Δb = -0.060, SE = 0.028, 95% CI [-0.116, -0.005], p = .034). Moreover, the difference between these effects was statistically significant (Δb = -0.087, SE = 0.038, 95% CI [-0.162, -0.013], p = .022) suggesting that political trust differentially predicts soundness discrimination depending on political interest. At higher political interest evaluative accuracy decreases as trust increases whereas this association does not emerge at low political interest.

Further probing indicated that this interaction was driven by fallacious arguments.

Political trust was more strongly associated with favorable evaluations of fallacious arguments at high (b= 0.184) than at low (b= 0.069) levels of political interest, a difference that was statistically significant (Δb = 0.115, SE = 0.040, 95% CI [0.036, 0.194], p = 0.004). Conversely, its association with evaluations of sound arguments did not significantly differ between highand low-interest participants (Δb = 0.027, SE = 0.040, 95% CI [-0.052, 0.106], p = 0.498). Thus, although political interest moderated the relationship between political trust and sensitivity to argument soundness, the pattern was opposite to the hypothesized direction: political trust was more strongly associated with favorable evaluations of fallacious arguments among participants with higher, rather than lower, political interest.

H4 (exploratory): Trust differentiation as a moderator for the influence of political trust

To examine whether trust differentiation moderated the trust effect on argument soundness, we estimated a mixed-effects linear regression model with random intercepts for participants and items predicting argument evaluation from political trust, argument soundness, trust differentiation, and their interaction. Consistent with our exploratory fourth hypothesis, the trust x soundness interaction was qualified by a significant three-way interaction with trust differentiation (b = 0.07, SE = 0.03, 95% CI [0.01, 0.12], p = 0.02).

This effect remained significant in the controlled model and the alternative random slope models, indicating that the interaction was robust to demographic and ideological adjustments as well as stimulus heterogeneity. To interpret this interaction, we estimated conditional slopes of political trust for sound and fallacious arguments at low (-1 SD) and high (+1 SD) levels of trust differentiation using emtrends. The association between political trust and soundness discrimination differed significantly between low and high differentiation (Δb = 0.11, SE = 0.05, 95% CI [0.018, 0.198], p = 0.02). At low differentiation, the political-trust slope was slightly weaker for sound arguments (b = 0.115) than for fallacies (b = 0.156), although this difference was not statistically significant (Δb = -0.041, p = 0.155). At high differentiation, the political-trust slope was stronger for sound arguments (b = 0.14) than for fallacies (b = 0.08), with the difference approaching conventional statistical significance (Δb = 0.067, p = 0.060). Discussion

Throughout this study, we conceptualized political trust as a source cue in political argumentation from authority. Consistent with this premise, political trust emerged as a reliable predictor of argument acceptability across all tested models. What our data did not support, however, is the hypothesis that trust as a source cue determines patterns of soundness discrimination. While participants who overall trusted institutions more also tended to be more receptive to political arguments from authority, they were no less able to distinguish sound arguments from fallacies. We thus find no evidence that political trust compromises evaluative accuracy in authority argumentation. This does not, however, imply a total absence of trustrelated complications.

One rather surprising finding was the moderating role of political interest. Even though the effect of political trust on soundness discrimination varied across different levels of interest, the direction of this effect was opposite to what we had anticipated in our third hypothesis. Our prediction, informed by the ELM, was that greater interest in the topic of argumentation should predict more soundness-driven evaluations. On the contrary, we found that high-trust participants who also reported a strong interest in politics exhibited weaker soundness discrimination and greater endorsement of fallacies. In other words, evaluation became less normatively accurate and more subject to the influence of trust as political interest increased.

Interpreted against our earlier findings, it seems that political trust has a general persuasive function in political communication and that this persuasive function can interfere with soundness-based evaluation specifically under high levels of political interest. Driven by the discrepancy between our original hypothesis and our findings, we begin to speculate on the direction of the moderation by revisiting the theoretical implications of political interest as a proxy for processing motivation.

According to the ELM, argument quality becomes more consequential as individuals move toward the central end of the elaboration continuum. This is a key theoretical premise in traditional dual-process accounts, where accuracy-driven processing is associated with increased cognitive reflection. This premise has received substantial empirical support, and the pattern was found to replicate within political communicative settings as well (Aspernäs et al., 2023; Pennycook & Rand, 2019). However, competing accounts on political reasoning suggest that cognitive reflection may actually undermine critical information processing (Handley & Runnion, 2011; Kahan, 2013). Because political communication often revolves around topics that stimulate a reasoner's prior attitudes, either by confirming them or by contradicting them, it can activate a motivated reasoning process (Strickland et al., 2011). Thereby the goal of information processing is not to reach accurate conclusions, but to defend existing views.

Highly reflective individuals can be especially prone to this (Kahan, 2013). Interpreting new information in attitudinally consistent ways requires deep, effortful processing. As such, and contrary to traditional dual-process approaches, high elaboration conditions could in fact facilitate biased conclusions (Seremeta et al., 2023). This approach may be a possible explanation for the weaker soundness discrimination we observed among highly interested participants. Nonetheless, such an explanation implies that a simple source-cue account on political trust does not sufficiently explain the full pattern. Instead, our findings on the moderation of political interest appear more consistent with trust as a motivational influence.

Read this way, our results do not contradict ELM theory. In line with the ELM's multiple roles postulate, source cues can serve several different functions depending on the amount of elaboration (Briñol & Petty, 2009; Petty & Briñol, 2014). We have already mentioned that source factors function as a peripheral cue when elaboration is low but can serve as arguments when elaboration is high. The model also holds that under high elaboration source cues can additionally "motivate or enable people to either support or derogate the content of the information provided" (Briñol & Petty, 2009, p. 59). Put simply, source cues may bias the direction of argument evaluation when processing occurs via the central route. This is an equivalent mechanism to the one discussed earlier, where cognitive reflection was shown to encourage biased thinking rather than correct it (Kahan, 2013). Hence, a motivational explanation for the role of political interest in our study is supported by empirical research on motivated cognition as well as by ELM theory.

More importantly, ELM's multiple-roles postulate allows us to move beyond a narrow conceptualization of political trust as either a source cue or a motivational influence. Such a unidimensional interpretation cannot sufficiently explain the pattern of findings observed in our study. The analyses for H1 and H2 are consistent with a source-cue account, in that political trust predicted argument acceptability even though did not predict indiscriminate acceptance.

The unexpected moderation observed for H3, however, raises the possibility that the role of political trust changes under conditions of strong political interest. Taken together, these findings suggest that political trust could play multiple roles in political argumentation from authority. While it may generally operate as a favorable source-related influence, it can also become implicated in directional processing under particular conditions.

Our exploratory analysis of trust differentiation provides a further, though tentative, indication that the relationship between political trust and soundness discrimination is conditional. At low levels of trust dispersion, political trust was negatively associated with evaluative accuracy whereas the same association did not emerge when individuals differentiated their trust judgments more strongly across institutions. One possible interpretation for this finding is that dispersion patterns reflect the distinction between political trust as a generalized attitude and political trust as an institution-specific judgment. From this perspective, the effect of trust as a generalized attitude is more consistent with a motivational influence account, whereas institution-specific trust appears to align more with a simple sourcerelated influence. Here, it is important to note that our dispersion analysis can only capture metric differences rather than evaluative processes. For example, a participant with a low differentiation score may nevertheless have produced institution-specific judgments. While in our study, differentiation measures do not account for the cognitive mechanisms involved in political trust calibration, they may point to an additional boundary condition for the motivational role of trust in political communication.

The current research offers a nuanced understanding of political trust as a communicative cue, with implications for critical citizenship. We find that trusting citizens are inclined to engage with political institutions in good faith, without blindly accepting the claims and decisions made by these institutions. However, we also identify certain conditions where political trust positively predicts the acceptance of authority arguments even when they contain pragmatic irregularities. We therefore conclude this paper without assigning specific democratic correlates to political trust. Rather, we propose that its democratic implications may depend on when and how confidence in political institutions enters citizens' evaluations of political claims.

Understanding these conditions is essential for a broader account of political trust that recognizes both its role in sustaining cooperation with political institutions and the continued importance of critical scrutiny toward political authority.

Limitations and future research

This study offers a novel understanding of political trust as a cue in political communication, however, certain limitations should be acknowledged.
