Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Full text

Abstract

Multilingual children's language environments vary widely, yet they are often described by exposure proportions alone. In this exploratory paper, we describe how caregivers distribute and combine languages in speech during a child-directed picturebook narration task, using language-use visualizations, language proportions, language entropy, proportion of multilingual utterances, span entropy, and memory. We applied these measures to 146 narrations from caregivers in Singapore of children aged 0;8 to 3;4 and compared them to caregiver reports. Caregivers reported and used different linguistic varieties with their children, and reported language use and observed language-use during narrations showed moderate correspondence, indicating they characterise different facets of multilingual input. Among multilingual narrations, caregivers of older children used their languages less evenly and proportionally more English, whereas utterance-level mixing and span organisation showed no detectable age differences. We discuss methodological considerations, including segmentation and labelling decisions and provide code for researchers to compute these measures.

Introduction

Multilingual children vary in the languages they hear, how much they hear, and who provides that exposure (de Bree et al., 2017; Cychosz et al., 2021). Caregivers also differ in whether they use more than one language within an interaction, often described as language mixing (Byers-Heinlein, 2013). Characterising children's multilingual language experiences therefore requires considering not only which languages and how much, but also how caregivers use and combine languages (De Bruin, 2019; Arredondo & Castro, 2026).

Parental reports commonly estimate caregiver language patterns, mixing frequency, and relative exposure (for a review, Kašćelan et al., 2022). They provide breadth across contexts, but limited information about how caregivers use their linguistic repertoires within an interaction (de Bruin et al., 2019; Arredondo & Castro, 2026). Recordings complement parental reports by measuring speech directly, revealing differences among families with similar self-reported language-use proportions (Marchman et al., 2017; Orena et al., 2020), and actual child-directed language mixing (Bail et al., 2015; Kremin et al., 2022). Typical recording-based measures also summarise multilingual input into language proportions, rather than moment-to-moment dynamic language mixing. For example, a caregiver with 8:20 split between their languages might use each language in long, unbroken stretches, or they might deploy resources from both of their languages in a more blended way. Multilingual speech is thus an unfolding sequence of caregiver language resource choices, not merely a mixture in different quantities. Given that infants are sensitive to sequential regularities in speech (Saffran & Kirkham, 2018), how languages unfold in caregiver speech may matter in ways that broad comparisons miss. In this report, we explore how a variety of approaches capture the ways that caregivers dynamically deploy resources from their languages.

Many existing approaches to multilingual data focus on the type or position of 'switches' between named languages (Code-mixing: Muysken, 2000 , Code-switching: Poplack, 1980) or assume that one language provides a grammatical frame for within which a switch can be observed (Matrix Language Framework: Myers-Scotton, 1997). By contrast, alternative models focus on how linguistic resources are distributed and sequenced across an interaction without assuming that one language supplies a grammatical frame (Translanguaging: Garcia & Li, 2014; Polylanguaging: Ag & Jørgensen, 2013). These newer approaches are better suited to majority-multilingual contexts where speakers draw on multiple linguistic resources in their speech. In Singapore, speakers use linguistic resources primarily from English, Mandarin, Malay and Tamil (Leimgruber, 2012; Styles et al., 2022), alongside a rich shared vocabulary of lexical items from many language backgrounds (Tay, 1989; Alsagoff, 2010), often making code-switching models difficult to apply (Lee, 2003; Ong et al., 2013).

The current report documents translanguaging behaviour in 146 Singaporean parentchild dyads. Caregivers reported their language use at home and narrated a wordless picture book to their child (0;8 to 3;4 years) over Zoom. We addressed three questions: (1) how do different approaches capture translanguaging behaviour; (2) to what extent does reported home language use correspond to observed language use during narration; and (3) do these behaviours differ with child age?

Methods

Dataset

The data analysed in the present study were drawn from the Talk Together Study (Woon et al., 2021), a pre-registered micro-longitudinal study investigating parental language use for Singaporean families with preschool children aged between 8 to 40 months. The Talk Together Study includes a randomised controlled intervention designed to support caregivers in enhancing the quality and quantity of talk when interacting with their children (Styles, 2021).

Families were randomly assigned to either the intervention or control arm using a double-blind method. Families in the intervention arm received text messages containing evidence-based tips and interactive content aimed at enhancing child-directed talk twice a day over four weeks (binte Amran et al., 2021; Sundaray et al., 2021). Families in the control arm received less frequent, general information on child language development, as a kind of 'sham intervention' (Styles, 2021). Following four weeks, families switched conditions, such that all families received the full set of intervention materials.

To evaluate whether the effects of intervention on parental speech, caregivers and their children were invited to complete a shared picturebook narration task. The shared picturebook narration task was conducted remotely via Zoom video-conferencing software. Caregivers gave consent for the video calls (including their child's voice) to be recorded using the Zoom videoconferencing recording function. Participants were presented with the open-access wordless picture book "Little Orangutan: What a Scary Storm" (Styles, 2020). As the picturebook did not contain any words, caregivers were free to describe the book's illustrations using any language or language mix typical for use with their child. During the video call, the study researcher used Zoom's screensharing function to display the picture book two pages at a time, as shown in Figure 1. Caregivers were instructed to say "next" when they were ready to proceed, after which the researcher displayed the following pages. A jingling sound was played to signal each page turn.

As the current study is designed to explore the suitability of our proposed approaches for characterising caregiver translanguaging behaviours, the data analysed in this study uses only the recordings of the picturebook narration sessions completed in Timepoint 1, prior to exposure to the intervention. This restriction ensured that the analysed speech represented caregivers' baseline language use and avoided potential effects of the intervention on their speech behaviours. The Timepoint 1 corpus consists of a total of picturebook narrations from 146 parent-child dyads. At Timepoint 1, children's ages ranged from 8 to 40 months (Median=1;6). Note. Transcription Protocol

All audio recordings of narrations were transcribed in ELAN (Nijmegen: Max Planck Institute for Psycholinguistics) using a custom transcription protocol (BELA: Loh et al., 2022a; https://blipntu.github.io/belacon/). Each narration was transcribed by at least one transcriber and subsequently checked by a senior transcriber (Loh et al., 2022a). Video recordings were consulted when additional information was required for transcription.

The BELA transcription protocol uses a custom standardised transcription template in which each speaker in the recording was assigned a separate tier set comprising an Utterance tier, a Chunk tier, a Language tier, and a Translation tier. Utterances were defined as vocal output produced by a speaker and utterance boundaries were determined primarily by intonation patterns and pauses (Loh et al., 2022b). Transcribers were instructed to place speaker-turn boundaries as precisely as possible, including sounds occurring at the edges of words, such as final fricatives. In cases of overlapping speech, transcribers identified the onset and offset of each speaker's turn to the best of their judgement, resulting in individual timestamps per speaker.

Figure 2 shows the BELA transcription tiers in ELAN, illustrating an example of a multilingual utterance in our dataset, alongside its associated annotations. Utterances are further subdivided into smaller 'chunks' to mark either a change in language or a transition between speech and non-linguistic communicative acts, such as humming or clapping. Each chunk was assigned a label on a dedicated Language tier using a set of labels developed and refined iteratively, taking into account the languages commonly spoken in Singapore and those encountered in the study sample. Speech chunks were assigned a corresponding language label (e.g., English, Mandarin, Malay, Tamil), while non-linguistic chunks were assigned a label according to the type of communicative act, e.g., vocal sounds such as humming and whistling versus non-vocal sounds such as clapping. The full coding scheme is provided in Loh et al. (2022c). Non-English utterances were additionally provided with approximate English translations on the Translation tier. Note. Utterances are subdivided into chunks based on their language tags, and white spaces are placed between Mandarin words in the Chunk tier as outlined in the BELA transcription convention.

The BELA protocol also incorporated conventions to support consistent word-level segmentation across languages and enable the automatic extraction of word counts from the transcriptions (Loh et al., 2022b). First, spaces were used to mark word boundaries in all languages, even when this convention differed from the language's conventional writing system (i.e., Mandarin Chinese). Second, spelling was standardised across different pronunciations of the same lexeme to ensure consistent word-token counts. For example, although the informal Singaporean of already is sometimes spelled oreddy in informal contexts (cf. Gwee, 2017), the standard spelling already was used throughout the transcriptions. Third, multi-part words conventionally written as separate words were hyphenated (e.g., choo-choo) to facilitate consistent word-boundary identification. Together, these conventions enabled word counts to be extracted automatically from the transcriptions based on whitespacedelimited units. Further discussion of the methodological decisions underlying multilingual transcription is provided in Choo et al. (2026).

Our set of language labels also included two special language labels: "Languageless" vocabulary and "Red-Dot" vocabulary. Since filler speech and interjections such as 'um' and 'ah' can be used by speakers regardless of language backgrounds, in the Language tier, this speech was labelled 'Languageless' (i.e., lacking a specific language label; see Loh et al., 2022c for the full list of language tags). Vocabulary items such as 'mam-mam' (child-vocab 'to eat') which are commonly used and understood by local Singapore speakers across linguistic backgrounds were given the language label Red Dot in the BELA transcription protocol, as they do not belong to one language more than any other, regardless of the language of origin of the individual words (Woon & Styles, 2021; Wong & Siew, 2024; see full list in Loh et al., 2022c). Discourse markers such as 'lah' and 'ma' typically occur as monosyllabic clause-final particles to convey mood, attitude, solidarity or emphasis (Lee, 2003; Gupta, 2006), and are also labelled as Red Dot (Ho, 2023; see Loh et al., 2022c for the full list). The language tier labels 'Languageless' and 'Red Dot' were used to provide a language-model-neutral base dataset that could be analysed in a variety of different ways.

Rebadging. In the current report, we are interested in comparing metrics that capture a speaker's deployment of their multilingual resources across linguistic varieties. Since Languageless and Red Dot words do not constitute discrete language varieties (rather they can form a part of any utterances in any language), we rebadged Languageless and Red Dot chunks to coordinate with language of the surrounding speech, as follows: Clause-final Red Dot discourse markers were rebadged according to the language label of the preceding chunk.

When a Languageless or Red Dot chunk appeared at the beginning of an utterance, it was rebadged with the language label of the following named-language chunk. When a Languageless or Red Dot chunk appeared at the end of an utterance, it was rebadged with the language label of the preceding named-language chunk. When a Languageless or Red Dot chunk appeared between two chunks with the same named language label, it was rebadged with that language label. When a Languageless or Red Dot chunk appeared between two chunks with different named language labels, it was rebadged with the label of the language that accounted for the greater cumulative duration within the utterance. Finally, for utterances consisting entirely of Languageless or Red Dot chunks, all chunks in the utterance were rebadged with the language that was spoken for the longest duration by the speaker across the entire narration. Figure 3 illustrates this rebadging procedure for Languageless and Red Dot chunks in our transcriptions.

After rebadging the special language labels (Languageless and Red-Dot), the languagelevel annotations of 146 picturebook narrations provided the basis for the present analyses, allowing language identity to be tracked across each narration and were used to derive our proposed approaches.

Translanguaging Measures

Reported home language use. We used the Language Experience Overview (LEO; Woon et al., 2021) to capture caregivers' estimates of their children's multilingual experiences. For each family, one caregiver enrolled in the study completed the LEO survey, providing information about all individuals who cared for their child during waking hours on a typical weekday and weekend day. The participating caregiver identified these individuals and estimated the proportion of time each individual used different linguistic varieties with the child. For example, a single caregiver might be reported as using English 50% of the time, Mandarin 40% of the time, and Cantonese 10% of the time. For three of the 146 caregiver-child dyads who took part in the narration task language-use was estimated by their partner.

As all families in the study reported English as one of the languages used in the home, we investigated the proportion of English use at home, expressed as a proportion ranging from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater English use. We focused on English because it is the dominant language of education and early childhood care in Singapore. Of the 146 caregiver-child dyads, we computed the reported proportion of English use at home for 144 caregivers, excluding two caregivers who did not complete the LEO survey. To characterise the full range of diversity and balance of languages used at home, we also calculated home language entropy, which is reported in Supplement S1.

Observed translanguaging behaviours.

The rebadged transcriptions of the storybook narration were used as the basis of the observed measures of language use. Hence, all metrics in the current report are computed on the basis of word-count and utterance counts, excluding non-speech chunks. To enable an understanding of how long a parent speaks using resources from a single language before deploying resources from another language, we computed a 'span' of speech as a sequence of consecutive chunks assigned the same language label, including across utterance boundaries. The lengths of a language span and a language chunk are measured as the number of words. Figure 4 shows an example of how three language spans are defined across three utterances.

Observed proportion of English used.

For each picturebook narration, we computed the proportion of English used as the total number of English words spoken over the total number of words spoken, expressed as a value ranging from 0 to 1 with higher values indicating a greater proportion of English use. This metric allowed direct comparison with the reported home-language use proportions.

Proportion of multilingual utterances.

An utterance was classified as multilingual if it contained speech chunks assigned to at least two distinct language labels, and as monolingual if all speech chunks within the utterance were assigned the same language label. For each picturebook narration, the proportion of multilingual utterances was calculated by dividing the number of multilingual utterances by the total number of speech-containing utterances. Higher values indicate a greater proportion of caregiver utterances included more than one language during picturebook narration, whereas lower values indicate a greater proportion of utterances containing only one language.

Memory ranges from -1 to 1. For example, a caregiver with span lengths of [2, 3, 7, 8] would have a positive memory value, whereas a caregiver with span lengths of [2, 7, 3, 8] would have a negative memory value. Although both caregivers produced the same set of span lengths, their sequential arrangements differ: in the first sequence, short spans follow short spans and long follow long, whereas in the second sequence, shorter and longer spans follow one another. Thus, memory characterises the sequential organisation of language-span lengths, complementing span entropy, which characterises their distribution regardless of order.

Memory cannot be estimated reliably for speakers with fewer than three spans, because estimating a correlation between consecutive span lengths requires at least two pairs of consecutive spans. This included all speakers who used only one language, each of whom produced a single span. Memory was also undefined when span lengths showed no variation across consecutive-span pairs (e.g., when all spans consisted of the same number of words), because a correlation cannot be calculated when either variable has zero variance. In these cases, memory was recorded as missing rather than zero, since a value of zero would indicate an absence of correlation between consecutive span lengths, rather than an inability to estimate it.

Although a correlation can be calculated for speakers with exactly three spans, it is based on only two pairs of consecutive spans. With just two pairs, the correlation is necessarily either +1 or -1, provided neither variable has zero variance. These extreme values arise from the mathematical constraints of the calculation and make it uninformative as an estimate of the strength of sequential dependence between consecutive language span lengths. Accordingly, narrations with fewer than four spans, as well as narrations for which memory could not be estimated because of zero variance in span lengths, were excluded from analyses of memory.

Results

What combinations of linguistic varieties do parents report using with their child at home?

One family did not complete the self-report, while another family completed the selfreport for only one caregiver, resulting in a total of 144 completed reports of caregiver's language use. Almost all caregivers reported multilingual language use with their child (n = 127, 88%), with only a small proportion reporting monolingualism (n = 17, 12.0%). Among multilingual families, 108 caregivers reported using two linguistic varieties with their child, 13 reported three linguistic varieties, and six reported four or five linguistic varieties.

How does child age relate to multilingual metrics?

Figure 10 shows the relationship between child age and our six key measures of multilingualism, for those children where multilingualism was evident in the metric. Among caregivers who report multilingual use with their child (n = 127), proportion of reported English use was not positively associated with child age (ρ(125) = 0.037, p = 0.677; Figure 10). Among the 88 narrations in which caregivers used multiple languages, child age was positively associated with the proportion of English used during the narration task (ρ(86) = 0.23, p = 0.028), demonstrating that parents of older children tended to use a greater proportion of English during the narration task. We note that this p-value comparison does not survive correction for multiple comparisons, so it should be treated with some degree of caution. Child age was negatively associated with language entropy (ρ(86) = -0.32, p < 0.005; Figure 10), indicating that parents of older children tended to distribute their language use less evenly across different linguistic varieties, suggesting greater tendency towards one or more linguistic varieties relative to others. This aligns with our other observations that the parents tended to use more English with older children and that the longest monolingual spans were in English.

Together, these findings suggest that parents of older children exhibit biases towards long, unbroken spans of English during the narration, thereby increasing proportions of English overall.

Child's age was not associated with the proportion of multilingual utterances (ρ(86) = -0.151, p < 0.15; Figure 10), span entropy (ρ(86) = -0.146, p < 0.15; Figure 10) and memory (ρ(72) = -0.152, p < 0.15; Figure 10). Although parents of older children tended to distribute their language use less evenly across linguistic varieties, they did not differ systematically in how often they combined linguistic varieties within utterances, how consistently they sustained a linguistic variety before using another or whether they tended to produce consecutive language spans of similar or contrasting lengths. Discussion

Children growing up with more than one language experience different linguistic environments and capturing this variability requires measures that treat multilingual experience as continuous (Kremin & Byers-Heinlein, 2021). We examined how language-use visualizations, proportion of English use, language entropy, proportion of multilingual utterances, span entropy and memory, capture the unique ways caregivers distribute and combine their languages in speech. Our findings suggest that these measures provide complementary information about different patterns of multilingualism, and we provide tools to compute them (doi.org/10.21979/N9/QKG3CQ).

There was moderate correspondence between reported and observed proportion of English (See Figure S1 for similar findings for language entropy), with notable differences between reported and observed language use, consistent with earlier work reporting variable agreement (e.g., Marchman et al., 2017; Cychosz et al., 2021). Many parents used more English in the narration task than was reported used at home. This discrepancy may be due to the nature of self-report or the narration task being different to the context of the home environment.

Previous research has shown that language use varies with interaction context and interlocutors (e.g., Tare & Felman, 2011). In our case, narrating a wordless picture book over Zoom, with an unfamiliar off-camera researcher present, likely elicited patterns that differed from everyday home interactions. These discrepancies indicate that reported and observed speech may index different aspects of child language input. Our measures of observed language in narrations capture different aspects of how multilingual speech is structured. English was reported by all caregivers and observed in every narration, with 40.4% conducted entirely in English, consistent with the status of English as the primary language of education and governance in Singapore. Previous research has questioned whether multiple languages within an utterance hinder or support language learning (see Kaushanskaya, 2023, for a review). Some evidence suggests naturalistic mixing shapes learning (Adamou & Shen, 2019), and with repetition, does not impair novel word learning (Kaushanskaya et al., 2022). In our study, 55% of the caregivers use multilingual utterances at least once (2.4% of utterances were translanguaged), showing that within-utterance mixing is part of Singaporean children's input.

Given the high prevalence of English in our dataset, proportions of English can serve as a baseline metric for all caregivers, however it does not capture how language use might be balanced across different numbers of languages. We observed declining language entropy with child age, suggesting parents tended towards one language with older children. One advantage of language entropy is that it can be used when no common language exists among speakers within a dataset, making it language neutral. As entropy does not identify which language(s) are used most, it is best interpreted alongside language proportions and the number of languages spoken.

Span entropy and memory characterise the unfolding of language sequences over time.

Experimental work has suggested that abrupt language changes may carry short-lived costs for novel word learning (Libersky et al., 2024), while repeated exposure may enhance learning (Kaushanskaya et al., 2022). In this paper, we applied these metrics at one time point to characterise caregiver language behaviour, but links to developmental outcomes remain to be tested. The same span entropy value may reflect mostly short spans for one caregiver and mostly long spans for another (see Supplement S2), hence span entropy is best interpreted alongside span-length proportions and the number of spans. Memory is undefined for narrations with fewer spans, making it best suited to datasets with longer tasks, multiple time points, or samples where multilingual use is common.

In exploratory, cross-sectional analyses, age-related variation was evident in language distribution. Among multilingual narrations, caregivers of older children seemed to use their repertoires less evenly and used proportionally more English, with no differences in utterancelevel mixing or span organisation. Longitudinal research is needed to determine whether this reflects change within families or differences between families.

Measures that depend on segmentation and labelling require careful decisions, which are consequential where language boundaries are not straightforward, as in Singapore (Lee, 2003; Ong et al., 2013). Our transcriptions were designed to be language-model neutral, allowing analysis under models such as code-switching among discrete named-languages, or more flexible approaches like translanguaging. The current analysis took a permissive approach, rebadging specially labelled items (Languageless and Red Dot) according to the language labels of their nearest neighbours, or most frequently spoken languages. Hence 'wah' could be considered English in the utterance "Wah, look", or Malay in "Wah tiba-tiba" (see

Figure 3). This is an operational solution, not a definitive account of boundaries between linguistic varieties, and other approaches may be better suited to other communities or research questions. Rebadging affects span boundaries and utterance classifications, so span entropy, memory and proportion of multilingual utterances are most affected. Thus, comparison of the current study with others should take careful consideration of the language models underlying the data.

Since each metric highlights a different feature of multilingual language resource deployment, exploratory analyses are valuable for understanding complex dynamics of language use. We make available our code for rebadging, analysing and visualising multilingual data in this article's repository. The code is optimised for BELA transcripts, which use whitespace to delineate word boundaries, but includes an option to compute metrics using time-stamps rather than word counts. To avoid inflated experimenter degrees of freedom, selection of metrics for hypothesis testing should be justified by the research question: proportions of language-use for relative exposure, language entropy for balance, proportion of multilingual utterances for within-utterance translanguaging, and span entropy and memory for span organisation.

By describing multilingual input with continuous measures, our report and code repository aims to help researchers characterise variability within multilingual groups rather than treating them as homogeneous.

In this study, all observed measures were computed from one picturebook narration, so we cannot establish whether patterns generalise across activities or vary over time. Future research could apply these measures across contexts, communities and timepoints, and test whether the measures explain variation in language, vocabulary, communicative or pragmatic outcomes beyond exposure proportions, for example in designs that consider child age and caregiver proficiency.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates how different complementary measures (language-use visualization plots, language-use proportions, language entropy, proportion of multilingual utterances, span entropy, and memory) can characterise distinct aspects of multilingual speech.

Together, these measures capture the amount of languages used, how evenly languages are distributed across a caregiver's repertoire, whether multiple languages occur within individual utterances, how much language-span lengths vary, and the extent to which successive spans are related. Supplement S2 Worked Example of Span Entropy

To illustrate differences in span entropy, Figure S2.1 shows the distribution of English and Mandarin span lengths for three caregivers: those with the lowest and highest span entropy, and the caregiver whose span entropy was closest to the sample median. The caregiver with a span entropy closest to the sample median distributed spans across approximately 10 length categories, with each span length category accounting for around 10% of spans. The caregiver with the lowest entropy produced spans concentrated in three length categories, each accounting for approximately 33% of spans, indicating that they tended to sustain their use of English or Mandarin for a narrower range of consecutive-word lengths. In contrast, the caregiver with the highest entropy distributed spans across a greater number of length categories (n = 28), with most length categories accounting for no more than approximately 5% of spans, indicating greater diversity in how long they sustained each language before changing to another language.

As span entropy is calculated from the distribution of observed span lengths, its estimation depends on both the number of spans produced and the number of length categories.

Figure S2.2 shows the relationship between the total number of language spans and span entropy across caregivers. Span entropy increased more rapidly across lower span counts, whereas the relationship became more gradual at higher span counts. In addition, entropy values were more widely dispersed among caregivers with fewer spans, while the range of values was narrower among caregivers with more spans, although some variation remained.

When fewer spans are observed, individual spans may have a greater influence on the estimated distribution of span length, potentially contributing to greater variability in entropy estimates.

At the same time, the observed dispersion may reflect genuine differences in caregivers' spanlength distributions. Thus, this pattern highlights the importance of considering the amount of language data available when comparing span entropy across caregivers, as the observed relationship may reflect both differences in span-length distributions and variability associated with the amount of data available.
