Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Based on the following text, we would expect an ethics approval statement, and it was present:

As soon as a property had been encoded as part of a concept’s central form, participants were much more likely to report being certain of its presence in a new instance than when the same property had been introduced in a generic sentence, but not as part of the concept’s central form.

Participants. 80 adults participated online using Amazon’s Mechanical Turk (Mage = 46.4 years, range = 22-76, 52 females).

All participants were native English speakers residing in the United States and received a nominal payment for their participation.

As specified in the preregistration, participants were required to pass simple catch questions to ensure they were attending to the task.

At the beginning of the study, participants were told that three researchers had traveled to a city they had not previously visited, and that the researchers’ job was to make notes about some of the things they encountered while they explored the city.

Participants were then informed that they would hear about some of the specific encounters of the researchers, and they would be asked some questions.

On each trial, participants were told about one of the specific entities the researchers had encountered on one of the days they were exploring the city (e.g., “One day, the researchers came across an actor”).

On the presence question, participants were asked whether they thought the specific category member would have the property for that trial (e.g., after being told the researchers had met an actor, they were asked: “Do you think they portray characters?”

On the certainty question, participants were asked if they were certain that the specific category member encountered would have the property (e.g., “Are you certain that they portray characters?”

Participants completed 32 trials, involving 16 categories and two properties per category: a form property (e.g., portraying characters for an actor) and a generic property (e.g., having an agent for an actor).

In each domain participants were certain form properties would be present in category members from that domain: for the artifacts (76%, p < .001), events (74%, p < .001), places (75%, p < .001), and social categories (61%, p = .009).

And yet, when asked about a newly encountered category member, participants were only certain that it would possess its form properties, not these generic properties.

In Experiment 2, participants were told that a set of researchers had been exploring a remote island that they had not been to before.

On each trial, participants were told about a novel category the researchers had learned about while on the island (e.g., a place called a “bozon”) and they were told about a property of that novel category (e.g., that bozons serve milk).

On test, participants were told about a specific encounter the researchers had with a member of each category while exploring the island.

Methods 80 adults (Mage = 44.9 years, range = 20-78, 57 females) participated online using Amazon’s Mechanical Turk, as per the preregistration (https://aspredicted.org/ZRT_1D9).

Participants were presented with a scenario in which three researchers had traveled to a remote island.

On each trial, participants were told that on one day, the researchers had encountered some new members of a category (e.g., “On one day, the researchers came across some places called bozons”).

Participants were either in a form condition or a generic condition.

On test, participants were told about different days when the researchers had been exploring the island and encountering specific category members.

First, a set of adult participants (N = 80) received a version of the study which was identical to the current design, with the only change being that on test, participants were asked whether a bare plural generic involving the category and property was true (e.g., “Bozons serve milk”).

For example, in Haward (2022b), when participants were presented with properties in the form frame, they went on to exhibit the stability signature of central form, immediately endorsing the belief that the property would be present in category members forever on 89.22% of trials, compared to 30.0% of trials when the property was introduced in the generic frame (i.e., in the generic frame, they reject this strong stability belief 70% of the time).

Following the main phase of the study, participants completed a posttest, called the Form Measurement Phase, first developed in Haward (2022a) and adopted in related Form Theory studies (Haward, 2022b (Haward,, 2025a (Haward,, 2025b (Haward,, 2025c)).

At the beginning of the Form Measurement Phase, participants were told that we are now interested in how they were thinking about each of the properties of each of the categories they just learned about.

Then, participants were told (e.g., for bozons) to “Recall that in one of the scenarios you read about a kind of thing called a bozon.”

Participants were significantly inclined to answer “no” to the certainty question.

By encoding a property as part of a concept’s central form, the property goes from being understood as one which will likely exist in its members (on a graded scale), to one which participants are certain will exist in its members (a categorical distinction).

A further concern may be that in Experiment 2 participants were directly told about the conceptual identity of each concept (“what makes a fep a fep”).

Finally, (iv) when participants are asked to generate their own interpretations of sentences, they spontaneously generate form interpretations, and the properties they include in their interpretations are the exact same ones that, on separate questions, participants express “complete certainty” will exist in members, and which they report will be part of the concept “forever.”

Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. Abstract

Humans often exhibit more certainty than is justified. Drawing on recent work in Form Theory, a theory of conceptual structure, the present paper proposes and tests a novel hypothesis about one important source of psychological certainty. On Form Theory, concepts have a central form: a core structure within each concept that stores a subset of its information, gives that information default influence in cognition, and activates a distinctive set of strong form-generated beliefs. The present paper tests the proposal that psychological certainty is one such form-generated belief. In Experiment 1, participants judged which properties would be present in newly encountered instances of familiar concepts. While they generalized both form and non-form properties to new instances, they reported being certain only for form properties. In Experiment 2, participants showed the same pattern for newly learned concepts. As soon as a property had been encoded as part of a concept's central form, participants were much more likely to report being certain of its presence in a new instance than when the same property had been introduced in a generic sentence, but not as part of the concept's central form. These findings help reveal a deep connection between psychological certainty and the structure of human concepts. Further, they suggest that conceptual structure is one important source of psychological certainty and provide a framework for investigating whether strongly held beliefs documented in research on stereotyping, memory, clinical depression, and conflict may sometimes derive from how concepts have been structured.

1. Introduction

>Introduction

1.1. A Cognitive Puzzle: Psychological Certainty Is Common, While Epistemic Certainty Is Rare

Bertrand Russell (1933) famously suggested that the trouble with the world is that fools and fanatics are often certain of themselves, while wiser people are full of doubt. Though Russell was perhaps correct that fools and fanatics express their beliefs with certainty, the phenomenon of psychological certainty-a strong conviction that one of our beliefs is true-extends much deeper and more pervasively into the everyday beliefs that all humans share (Burton, 2009). People regularly feel certain about the opinions they hold (e.g., whether they are right or wrong about the political parties they support, Tormala & Rucker, 2018). They feel certain about their episodic memories, such as where they were when the World Trade Center was attacked on September 11, 2001, or where they were when they heard that JFK had been shot (Day & Ross, 2014; Hirst et al., 2009). Even when presented with evidence that counters their certain beliefs, people often hold onto them (Tormala & Petty, 2002; Tormala & Rucker, 2007).

In a way, this is surprising, since epistemic certainty-a belief that has the highest epistemic status and that we hold to be indubitably true-is quite rare. In his Discourse on Method (1637), René Descartes sought to identify beliefs he was justified in holding with certainty. He arrived at only one clear foundational belief, namely that he is thinking, and therefore that he, in some sense, also exists (Descartes, 1637 (Descartes, /1998). 1 For centuries philosophers have debated whether we can be certain of even the most established, well-supported laws of nature (Peat, 2002), with some arguing that we can only claim certainty in highly abstract and relational domains such as logic and mathematics (Hume, 1748 (Hume, /2007;; Ayer, 1936 Ayer, /1952)). Thus, there exists a striking gap between the degree to which people claim they are certain about many of their beliefs, and the degree to which they are justified in feeling certain.

Since humans exhibit more certainty than is justified, the source must be the human mind, which assigns a psychological sense of certainty to some of our beliefs. Considerable progress has already been made identifying sources of psychological certainty across domains such as memory, perception, and attitudes (e.g., Burton, 2009; Day & Ross, 2014; Hirst et al., 2009; Koriat, 2012; Pouget et al., 2016; Shekhar & Rahnev, 2024; Tormala & Rucker, 2018). For example, certainty is more likely to arise when a memory is vivid or easily retrieved (Kelley & Lindsay, 1993; Talarico & Rubin, 2003), when a thought or judgment feels familiar or easy to process (Reber & Schwarz, 1999; Reber et al., 2004), when people can generate reasons for an attitude (Tormala et al., 2002; Barden & Petty, 2008), or when an attitude is based on direct experience (Fazio & Zanna, 1978) or perceived social consensus (Clarkson et al., 2013). The present paper does not question the role of these sources. Instead, it asks whether one important source of psychological certainty originates from the very conceptual structures we use to think. Specifically, the paper tests a prediction from Form Theory (Haward, 2022a (Haward, , 2026a)), a theory of conceptual structure on which each concept possesses a central form: a special, unitary format that stores a compressed subset of conceptual information, can be recruited by other cognitive systems, and activates strong default beliefs about the information it encodes.

Here I propose that psychological certainty is one such form-generated belief. The present work is not intended to provide a complete account of every instance of certainty. Rather, it tests whether psychological certainty is part of this profile of formgenerated beliefs, and explores what this reveals about conceptual structure. In doing so, the paper aims to (1) identify a default sense of certainty that may be general across human concepts, and may therefore influence belief and judgment across domains; (2) clarify the relationship between psychological certainty and the structure of human concepts; and (3) help illuminate why such strong beliefs arise even when they exceed the evidence, and what functional role they may play in cognition.

1.2. Form Theory: Human Concepts Have a Central Form

One of the most striking features of human cognition is the vast stock of concepts we come to acquire, store, and then use to frame our experiences of the external worldconcepts like cat, crack, ribbon, professor, city, mountain, and universe. The typical person will acquire tens of thousands of concepts in their lifetime, maybe more (Bloom, 2000). They enter into almost every aspect of higher-level cognition: our memories are framed with them, we form our beliefs using them, they enter into planning and so human action, and we combine them together using human language. Understanding their structure, then, may lead to insights into how humans think, reason, and ultimately act.

Form Theory proposes that each concept possesses a hidden yet powerful structure, called a central form, a claim developed in recent theoretical and empirical work (Haward, 2022a (Haward, , 2026a; see also Haward, 2022b Haward, , 2025a Haward, , 2025b Haward, , 2025c)). Aristotelian ideas were later developed by early modern and Enlightenment figures (e.g., Cudworth, 1731), whose discussions of form subsequently influenced philosophers such as Immanuel Kant (1781 Kant ( /1998)). In contemporary cognitive science, elements of these older form traditions may be reflected in a range of later approaches to conceptual structure, including qualia structure (Pustejovsky, 1995), psychological essentialism (e.g., Gelman, 2003), principled connections (Prasada and Dillingham, 2006), and other related frameworks (e.g., Moravcsik, 1975; Witt, 1989). Form Theory adopts the notion of form explicitly, while giving it a distinct technical meaning: form is proposed to be a unitary, default structure within each concept that encodes a compressed core of information, activates a linked set of strong default beliefs, and has a common format across domains, allowing it to be recruited by higher-level systems involved in thought and language. Consequently, the framework generates novel predictions about the structure of concepts and its role in composition and reasoning (reviewed in the General Discussion; for further predictions, see Haward, 2026a).

A Compressed Subset of Information.

The central form represents a small subset of each concept's information in a highly specialized manner. Take the concept restaurant. A typical person will come to learn numerous facts about restaurants-that they serve food, employ servers and cleaners, have overhead lighting, windows, sound systems, air conditioning, wifi, security systems, and pictures on the walls, are built next to streets, and so on. Each concept has a central form: a unitary structure that stores a compressed subset of properties and captures the concept's conceptual identity. Prior work has shown that people treat form properties as permanent parts of the concept, present forever (Haward, 2022b). The present paper tests whether this same structure also generates certainty. It asks whether people exhibit a default sense of certainty that category members will possess form properties, while not exhibiting this sense of certainty for non-form properties.

The full collection of these facts for each concept is often referred to as our "generic knowledge" of the category (Gelman, 2003; Leslie, 2014).

Within our generic knowledge, however, exists a much smaller subset of properties which capture the concept's conceptual identity (Figure 1). For example, the property of serving food is not merely another fact about restaurants. It captures our intuition about what makes a restaurant a restaurant. Likewise, houses are for living in, architects design buildings, dogs bark, triangles have three sides, watches tell time, weddings involve a union of people. No matter the concept, drawn from any domain, a subset of properties can be identified that captures its conceptual identity, and which humans think about differently to the vast network of factual knowledge stored about the category.

Strong Form-Generated Beliefs.

People hold remarkably strong beliefs about form properties. I refer to these as form signatures: beliefs, inferences, or other qualities that are selectively activated for form properties and distinguish them from the broader generic knowledge stored about a category. For example, form properties are understood as permanent parts of the category, present forever. People endorse statements like: "even as the properties of restaurants change over time, restaurants will always serve food. They will be this way forever" (Haward, 2022a). Likewise, they believe that architects will always design buildings, that dogs will always bark, that triangles will always have three sides, that watches will always tell time, that weddings will always involve a union of people, and so on. But they do not endorse this strong belief about generic properties. They reject that restaurants "will always have wifi," "will always have windows," "will always have pictures on the walls." This is called the stability signature of central form, a strong belief activated for the central form of each concept, no matter its domain. Second, people believe that form properties should exist in category members, and there is something wrong with category members that lack a form property. For example, restaurants should serve food, and there is something categorically wrong with a restaurant that cannot serve food. But there is nothing categorically wrong with a restaurant if it does not have wifi, windows, pictures on the walls, and so on (called the normative signature; Haward, 2025b; see also Aristotle in Kirby, 2008; Prasada & Dillingham, 2006). Finally, form properties are conceived of as universal. For example, participants will endorse the strong belief that "all restaurants, without exception, serve food," "all architects, without exception, design buildings," and "all watches, without exception, tell time." They do not endorse equivalent beliefs about the generic properties of these concepts (called the universality signature; Haward, 2025a).

Immediate Activation.

Evidence suggests these strong beliefs are activated as soon as people have encoded a property as part of the central form of a new concept. For example, when people learn that having a handle is "part of what makes a blicket a blicket," they immediately endorse the full strong-belief profile (Haward, 2022b (Haward, , 2025a (Haward, , 2025b;; Haward et al., 2021). This pattern has been observed across domains, including newly learned animals, artifacts, spaces, institutions, and social categories. That a set of strong beliefs is activated like this, without being taught, captures a powerful type of one-shot learning (see Figure 2). It suggests that a central form is an intrinsic part of new concepts, a structure the mind uses to represent the concept's conceptual-identity information. All the mind needs to do is encode a property as part of it, then a suite of strong beliefs will be activated.

Differentiating Central Forms from Alternative Accounts.

Crucially, strong central form beliefs are overwhelmingly rejected for the other aspects of a concept's generic knowledge (rejected on 60-80% of trials). The strong beliefs are rejected even if these non-form properties are highly statistically prevalent / prototypical (e.g., being black for tires), play an important causal role (e.g., having an agent for an actor), or were designed to be in category members (e.g., having music players for bars, being rectangular for TVs, having restrooms for gas stations).

Thus, this approach is differentiated from other approaches to category structure which focus on degrees of statistical prevalence, association, or similarity (e.g., Nosofsky, 1986; Rosch, 1973 Rosch, , 1975) ) or degrees of causal importance (e.g., Ahn et al., 2000). 3 While these other approaches may capture how we reason with our categories in other ways, the strong central form beliefs are only endorsed for a small set of properties, for each concept, that participants believe "makes the thing what it is" (e.g., serving food is one of the things that "makes a restaurant a restaurant"). 4Though related, the notion of form is much broader than contemporary approaches to psychological essentialism which propose a belief in a hidden cause which generates surface level properties, a claim often restricted to living things and/or a subset of categories (Gelman, 2003). 5 An approach to forms, both traditional and contemporary, treats them as a structural component of each new concept, rather than a "stance," "schema" or "belief" that we hold about some categories and not others (Barrett, 2001; Cimpian & Salomon, 2014; Gelman, 2003 Gelman, , 2004)). Consistent with this, recent work has demonstrated that of the properties which satisfy an important causal schema for a domain-such as being present from birth for an animal or being intentionally designed for an artifact-only a fraction are encoded as part of the concept's central form. These properties then have the same status, and activate the same strong beliefs, no matter the domain (called the Common Format Hypothesis; Haward, 2025b).

A Default Structure Activating Default Beliefs.

Finally, while it may be tempting to think of these as simply "defining" properties, the specific proposals differ substantially. Consider again the universality and stability form signatures. These beliefs are surprising precisely because they are typically not justified. For almost all categories, there exist some members which lack even the most defining properties of the category, such as broken watches which do not tell time and war-torn houses which cannot be lived in (e.g., Fodor, 1998; Wittgenstein, 1953). Further, concepts can change quite markedly over time, both over human history and over conceptual development (for examples of conceptual change see Carey, 1985 Carey, , 2009)).

And yet, participants do endorse these strong beliefs for form properties, and often at high rates (e.g., 70-90% of trials), and for concepts from every domain so far tested (e.g., animals, artifacts, places, institutions, events, social categories). In all cases, these three strong beliefs-stability, normativity, universality-align for a small subset of the concept's properties, those that capture its conceptual identity. This pattern is present early in conceptual development (Haward et al., 2018), and in children crossculturally (Haward & Srinivasan, 2026).

Thus, central forms are not sets of "necessary and sufficient" conditions for reference-properties that must always be present in an entity in the external world for us to use our concept to pick out that entity in an act of reference (e.g., Locke, 1689 Locke, /1975;; Katz, 1997). Instead, each concept's form encodes information and then transforms how we think about that information-i.e., the central form is not primarily a rule for categorizing entities in the world, but a structure that gives selected properties a distinctive belief-generating status. As such, a good metaphor for thinking about central forms is as conceptual frames that shape how we interpret entities in the world, a view consistent with traditional notions of form (Cudworth, 1731).

Relatedly, Form Theory distinguishes itself from previous approaches to "defining" vs "characteristic" properties, since this notion of "defining" is largely based on the previous classical notion (e.g., Keil & Batterman, 1984; Smith et al., 1974). Thus, these approaches do not propose many of the specific hypotheses adopted within Form Theory.

For example, contemporary empirical approaches to "defining" properties concentrate on measures like categorization, typicality ratings, and property extension judgments. While these measures capture intriguing phenomena, they are not precise enough to assess a concept's underlying central form. Consider categorization judgments. Categorization involves a complex, long-range relationship where a mindexternal entity is referred to using a mind-internal concept (Figure 3). Many factors likely influence these judgments (Fodor, 1998; Wittgenstein, 1953), including whether an entity possesses the generic properties of a concept. For instance, black objects are more likely to be categorized as tires than pink objects, assuming all other factors are equal (categorization). Black tires will be judged as more typical tires than pink tires (typicality). Additionally, we would expect new tires to be black, not pink (property extension). However, being black is not what fundamentally makes a tire a tire; it is a generic property of tires. Focusing on these measures has led some researchers to classify what seem to be form properties (e.g., flying for a bird) as "characteristic," rather than defining, because they do not occur in every member and cannot be said to "define" categorization (e.g., Hampton, 1979; Smith et al., 1974). Many argue, based on these studies, that no sharp defining/characteristic distinction exists (e.g., Hampton, 1979; Rosch, 1973 Rosch, , 1978)).

In contrast, the central form has been proposed as a default structure that activates a default set of strong beliefs, which may then be overridden using other capacities, including contextual processing, pragmatic inference, memory, and metacognitive reflection (Haward, 2022a (Haward, , 2026a; see General Discussion for review). This makes the prediction that, even though they are typically unwarranted, humans will "fall back" on these beliefs when conflicting information is not present (or not processed). This default-structure proposal plays a crucial role in understanding how humans combine concepts together, and it may go a long way toward understanding many of the biased beliefs we come to hold (again, reviewed in the General Discussion). It is also in line with early modern and Enlightenment theories of form that took forms to be internal ideals, used primarily for thinking and reasoning, and not perfect descriptions of the external world (e.g., Cudworth, 1731).

1.3. A New Form Signature: The Certainty Signature

If all concepts possess central forms, and if central forms trigger strong beliefs which can shape how we interpret the world, then it is crucial to discover any additional formgenerated beliefs. Discovering the complete set of form-generated beliefs may help us better understand this structure, its role in human cognition, as well as the errors it can lead to in thinking and reasoning.

The present paper proposes a new form signature-called the certainty signature (call this the Certainty Signature Hypothesis). According to this hypothesis, the central form is one important source of psychological certainty in the human mind, as concepts are recruited for belief-generation and for interpreting the entities around us, which are almost always viewed through the lens of our concepts. Specifically, on the Certainty Signature Hypothesis, the central form generates an initial sense of psychological certainty that new category members will possess their form properties. This signature would seem suited to the roles that categories play. As we navigate through our environments, we often interact with thousands of new members of different categories in each space we occupy, at each moment (e.g., not just chairs and tables, but insects, lights, pipes, cracks, holes, people of different professions, different categories of hairstyles, different categories of institutions, roads, dwellings, and so on). According to this hypothesis, while people generalize many of a concept's factual/generic properties to new category members, they exhibit an initial sense of certainty that form properties will exist in newly encountered category members (for examples of generalization tasks, see Gelman, 2003).

Two clarifications are necessary. First, it is important to emphasize that it is psychological certainty, and not epistemic certainty, that is being proposed as a new form signature. As already noted, we often come across category members that lack their form properties, as was thoroughly documented in critiques of early classical approaches to concepts (e.g., Fodor, 1998; Wittgenstein, 1953). And so, it is patently true that humans do not possess epistemic certainty about most-perhaps any-of the properties that category members actually possess. Rather, the Certainty Signature Hypothesis proposes that a type of psychological expectation is activated for properties that are part of a concept's central form. This expectation is likely similar in character to some of the other sources of psychological certainty documented in the literature on certainty (e.g., attitude certainty), which though not justified on epistemic grounds, are felt "with conviction" (Burton, 2009; Tormala & Rucker, 2018).

Relatedly, and as a second point of clarification, this psychological certainty can clearly be overridden in particular circumstances, with additional processing of contextual information, memory, pragmatic information, or other case-specific evidence, which will come at a cost. For example, if a student claims they are certain that a new bird they are learning about in a classroom will fly is then reminded that penguins do not fly, they may revise their certainty belief in that moment. Alternatively, if someone walks into a watchmaker's store or a museum, they are unlikely to believe with certainty that every new watch they see will tell time. Thus, this signature has a similar default character to the other form signatures, which are activated, but which can be overridden with case-specific, contextual processing. This default character of form-generated beliefs makes sense given a cognitive system where conceptual structure is only one factor that determines any judgment or inference, which will of course be the output of many different factors. It raises interesting questions about when humans may be more likely to fall back on central form beliefs, such as under heavy load or stress, but I leave a discussion of some of the deeper implications of this new form signature for human cognition to the General Discussion.

The primary aim of the present paper is to test a focused prediction of Form Theory, namely that psychological certainty is a signature of central form. Will participants say they are certain that newly encountered category members will possess their form properties, but uncertain that they will possess their non-form properties? Given that category members can lack even their most defining properties (e.g., broken or damaged category members), it might seem surprising that people would be willing to say they are certain that category members will possess any of their properties. Then again, the other form beliefs are strong and often unwarranted, and we often observe psychological certainty expressed in our daily lives, especially in contexts which do not promote contextual or metareflective reasoning (e.g., social media platforms). To test whether certainty is activated by the central form of each concept, Experiment 1 probed whether participants endorse certainty beliefs for form properties of familiar categories, but not non-form properties. Experiment 2 then probed more directly whether this strong belief is immediately activated as soon as a property is encoded as part of the central form of a newly learned category.

2. Experiment 1

Experiment 1 tested participants' generalization of form properties and generic properties to new category members. Both sets of properties were highly generalizable properties of the categories tested. Experiment 1 tested whether both properties would be generalized to new category members, as in classic generalization tasks (Gelman, 2003; Keil, 1989), but whether participants would only exhibit certainty that form properties would exist in new category members.

Participants. 80 adults participated online using Amazon's Mechanical Turk (Mage = 46.4 years, range = 22-76, 52 females). Sample sizes for both experiments in the present paper were determined in advance (Preregistration: https://aspredicted .org/QQC_6VQ). As specified in the preregistration, participants were required to pass simple catch questions to ensure they were attending to the task. Those who failed these questions were thanked for their time and exited from the survey.

Stimuli and Procedures. Introduction.

At the beginning of the study, participants were told that three researchers had traveled to a city they had not previously visited, and that the researchers' job was to make notes about some of the things they encountered while they explored the city. Participants were then informed that they would hear about some of the specific encounters of the researchers, and they would be asked some questions.

Trial structure. On each trial, participants were told about one of the specific entities the researchers had encountered on one of the days they were exploring the city (e.g., "One day, the researchers came across an actor"). They were then asked two test questions about this entity/category member-a presence question and a certainty question. Both questions were about a potential property the entity/category member might have (e.g., portraying characters). 1. Presence Question. On the presence question, participants were asked whether they thought the specific category member would have the property for that trial (e.g., after being told the researchers had met an actor, they were asked: "Do you think they portray characters?" ; yes/no response options). The presence question is therefore a test of property generalization-whether participants generalize a specific property to a specific category member, given the context of researchers exploring a new city and encountering objects/people/events in that new city.

2. Certainty Question. On the certainty question, participants were asked if they were certain that the specific category member encountered would have the property (e.g., "Are you certain that they portray characters?" ; yes/no response options). 6 The presence question was always asked first, followed by the certainty question. This ordering allowed participants to indicate that they expected the property to be present without also endorsing certainty. Further, in the certainty question, the word "certain" was bolded and italicized to emphasize the strength of the belief being assessed.

Participants completed 32 trials, involving 16 categories and two properties per category: a form property (e.g., portraying characters for an actor) and a generic property (e.g., having an agent for an actor). Experiment 1 tested categories from different domains, since the present paper proposes a hypothesis about the central form of concepts, and the central form is, by hypothesis, part of the structure of concepts from any domain. 2.3. Discussion

Experiment 1 provided evidence in support of the claim that form properties possess a psychological certainty signature. Participants went beyond simply generalizing a form property to a new category member, to instead indicating that they were certain a form property would be present in a newly encountered category member. They did not make such a judgment for generic properties. Many of these generic properties are prototypical for category members (e.g., being black for tires), play an important causal role in category members (e.g., having an agent for an actor), or were designed to be in category members (e.g., having music players for bars, being rectangular for TVs, having restrooms for gas stations). Further, these generic properties are habitually present in category members, reflected in the high rates of generalization for these properties. And yet, when asked about a newly encountered category member, participants were only certain that it would possess its form properties, not these generic properties.

In many respects, this is a surprising finding. In psychology and philosophy, much has been made of the fact that category members often lack the properties that seem to define/identify them (e.g., Fodor, 1998; Wittgenstein, 1953). For example, we encounter watches that do not tell time, and we can still think about them as watches. And we can encounter houses that are not livable, and we can still think about them as houses.

Crucially, in the present paper we are investigating something quite different to the epistemic certainty discussed in previous theories of conceptual structure. Rather, Experiment 1 provides evidence for the psychological phenomenon of people feeling certain that form properties will be present in any given category member. And, of course, the beliefs people hold can be inaccurate. Experiment 1 suggests that people do indeed possess an initial degree of certainty, all else equal, that the objects they encounter will possess their form properties. That is, as someone gazes over at a set of chairs and tables outside a café in the distance, they may initially possess an expectation of psychological certainty they will be able to sit on the chairs and place objects on the tables.

Nevertheless, while Experiment 1 demonstrated that this type of psychological certainty tends to be present for form properties, but not for generic properties, it did not demonstrate that this certainty belief is intrinsically tied to the property's status as a form property. After all, these are familiar categories with which the participants will have had extensive experience. To investigate whether this is a genuine signature of the central form of human concepts, it is crucial to explore whether this signature follows directly and immediately as soon as a property is encoded as part of a concept's central form, by utilizing a novel category paradigm.

3. Experiment 2

Experiment 2 provides a more direct test of the Certainty Signature Hypothesis. The aim of Experiment 2 was to test whether the proposed certainty belief is immediately triggered as soon as a property has been encoded as part of the central form of a newly learned category.

In Experiment 2, participants were told that a set of researchers had been exploring a remote island that they had not been to before. On each trial, participants were told about a novel category the researchers had learned about while on the island (e.g., a place called a "bozon") and they were told about a property of that novel category (e.g., that bozons serve milk). Importantly, they were either introduced to this property with evidence that it was part of the category's central form, or with evidence that it was generic-licensing, but not part of the central form. On test, participants were told about a specific encounter the researchers had with a member of each category while exploring the island. Participants then completed the two test questions from Experiment 1-the presence question and the certainty question. If the proposed psychological certainty belief is indeed a signature of central form, it should be automatically adopted by participants in the form condition but not in the non-form condition. This would show that psychological certainty beliefs are intrinsically tied to newly encoded form properties.

Stimuli and Procedures.

Introduction. Participants were presented with a scenario in which three researchers had traveled to a remote island. These researchers were interested in studying the animals, artifacts, places and people on the island.

Trial structure. On test, participants were told about different days when the researchers had been exploring the island and encountering specific category members. Form Measurement Phase.

Following the main phase of the study, participants completed a posttest, called the Form Measurement Phase, first developed in Haward (2022a) and adopted in related Form Theory studies (Haward, 2022b (Haward, , 2025a (Haward, , 2025b (Haward, , 2025c)). The Form Measurement Phase measures whether participants have actually encoded each property as a form property, for each of the four properties they learned about (e.g., serving milk, for a bozon). At the beginning of the Form Measurement Phase, participants were told that we are now interested in how they were thinking about each of the properties of each of the categories they just learned about. Then, participants were told (e.g., for bozons) to "Recall that in one of the scenarios you read about a kind of thing called a bozon." They were then asked: "Did you think that serving milk was part of being a bozon (i.e., one of those things that makes a bozon a bozon)?"

The Form Measurement Phase does not play as crucial a role here as it has in prior studies. In prior work, it has been used to measure individual differences in which properties people encode as part of the central form of familiar social concepts such as government, country, and religion-for example, whether one person encodes "having a strong leader" as part of what makes a country a country, whereas another encodes "providing public services" as part of what makes a country a country (Haward, 2026b). In those studies, it predicts individual differences in the strong central form beliefs/convictions people hold. It has also been used with newly learned categories to assess whether participants encoded particular properties as part of a category's central form (Haward, 2025b).

These prior studies highlight that two distinct questions can be asked about form encoding. First, what cues lead people to encode a property as part of a concept's central form? There are likely many, including pedagogical cues, linguistic cues, domain-specific biases, and other higher-level constraints (see Haward, 2026a, for discussion). Given that humans learn tens of thousands of concepts under different circumstances, this will at times lead different people to encode different properties as part of the central form of the same concept. In prior work, the Form Measurement Phase allowed these differences to be identified.

3.3. Discussion

Experiment 2 provides a key piece of evidence that the proposed certainty signature follows from encoding a property as part of a concept's central form. Recall that there are two distinct questions about form encoding: first, the cues people use to encode a property as a form property; second, having encoded a property, which beliefs about it immediately follow. By including clear cues to form encoding, the focus of Experiment 2 is the second of these questions. It provides evidence that psychological certainty follows immediately once a property is encoded as part of a concept's central form. As noted in Section 3.1, however, there are likely many routes to form encoding-some more explicit, such as direct linguistic or pedagogical cues, and others more implicit, such as domain-specific biases and other non-linguistic factors-and future work should continue to investigate these possibilities more directly (for further discussion, see Haward, 2026a).

In Experiment 2, participants either received clear evidence that a property was part of a concept's central form, or evidence that the property was generic-licensing but not part of its central form. Stimulus checks demonstrated that both conditions lead properties to be generalized to new category members, conceived of as genericlicensing, regarded as causally important, and present in category members most of the 11 Most participants encoded the property in the manner they were introduced to it, either as (i) a form property or (ii) a generic property but not part of the concept's central form. For example, participants introduced to a property within the form condition indicated that they encoded that property as a form property on 96% of trials (SD = 11.16%), compared to 29% of trials when the property was not introduced as a form property (SD = 39.04%; t(78) = 10.41, p < .001). Furthermore, in Experiment 2 in both conditions participants subsequently generalized this property to a new category member most of the time (replicating the result of one of the four stimulus checks). And yet, only when the property was introduced as a form property did participants then say they were certain that it would be present in a new category member. The rate of endorsement of this strong certainty belief was striking-participants said they were certain on 92% of trials in the form condition, compared to 34% of trials in the generic condition.

Moreover, as in Experiment 1, the same pattern of results was observed for categories drawn from four different conceptual domains (which otherwise have quite different structure: see for example , Gelman, 2003). This pattern of results provides further evidence that this is indeed a signature of central form, a common structure across concepts which activates a common set of strong beliefs (for further discussion on how this common structure not only activates a common suite of domain-neutral inferences, but also provides a common format which is then utilized by central computations like natural language, see Haward, 2022a Haward, , 2026a)).

Finally, while it may seem intuitive-even self-evident-that psychological certainty about a property's possession in future members would follow directly from evidence that a property is part of "what makes an X an X," it is important to acknowledge two points. First, conceptual identity and certainty about future possession are distinct inferences, and many literatures have been at pains to stress that even for the most seemingly defining properties of categories, we should not be certain of their presence in members (e.g., Fodor, 1998; Wittgenstein, 1953). Second, a strong intuition like this is exactly what Form Theory would predict, since on this theory conceptual identity and certainty are tightly interlinked (see Section 4.1 for further discussion).

4. General Discussion

The present paper introduced and provided evidence for a novel signature of central form, called the certainty signature. On this proposal, people exhibit a default sense of psychological certainty that form properties will be present in category members, which they do not exhibit for the broader generic/factual knowledge they have about the category. The data suggest that: (i) as soon as a new concept is learned and a property is encoded as part of that concept's central form, this strong certainty belief is immediately activated; (ii) this strong certainty belief remains, even for richly structured, familiar concepts like novel, football game, and actor, which will have been part of these participants' stock of concepts for decades. In both cases, participants exhibited psychological certainty for the properties that capture the concept's conceptual identity (e.g., what makes a fep a fep, what makes a novel a novel). They sharply differentiated these form properties from generic/factual properties.

Given the generative nature of human concepts, in the sense that we can form an indefinite number of concepts during our lifetimes which are then used by the systems of the mind for reasoning, planning, and ultimately human action, the present paper raises critical questions: (i) What is the nature of the psychological certainty belief activated by the central form of each human concept? (ii) Since other systems utilize concepts, what are the consequences for how humans think and reason? (iii) Why might a mind build in a sense of certainty for a subset of each concept's properties? (iv) How might this proposal relate to existing research on conceptual representation and psychological certainty?

Form-Generated Certainty Shares Characteristics With the Other Form Signatures.

The current results complement a recent body of work which demonstrates that strong, often unwarranted beliefs are activated as soon as a property is encoded as part of a concept's central form (Haward, 2022b (Haward, , 2025a (Haward, , 2025b)). In each case, a qualitative shift occurs in the knowledge acquired about the newly encoded property. Take the present signature. By encoding a property as part of a concept's central form, the property goes from being understood as one which will likely exist in its members (on a graded scale), to one which participants are certain will exist in its members (a categorical distinction). Likewise, the property goes from being understood as persisting in category members temporarily (on a graded scale), to existing in category members forever (a categorical distinction; Haward, 2022b). And it goes from being understood as existing in some members (on a graded scale), to instead existing in all members (a categorical distinction; Haward, 2025a). Further, these transitions are "strong" not merely because they are categorical, but because they often involve large inferential jumps-toward certainty, permanence, or universality-that go well beyond the evidence available. Indeed, as noted in the introduction, people often encounter cases that directly contradict these absolute beliefs, yet still endorse them.

Extensions Beyond the Present Paradigm.

Given the typically unwarranted nature of these form-generated beliefs, it may be tempting to imagine that they are only expressed in the present paradigms. And yet, extensions of these lines of work show that children and adults exhibit them in culturally and socially rich contexts where their judgments matter for their own lives, exhibit them cross-culturally, and endorse them using other response measures beyond the certain/not certain options used in the present studies (Haward, 2026b; Haward & Srinivasan, 2026).

For example, when a child in India says that "not eating pork" is part of "what makes a Muslim a Muslim," they are much more likely to report being "completely sure" that a Muslim person they are about to encounter will not eat pork, compared to children who did not see this property as part of what makes a Muslim a Muslim. The same child will be more likely to believe this is what all Muslims do, that Muslims will continue to not eat pork in the future, and to believe that there is something categorically wrong with a Muslim who does eat pork. Both Hindu and Muslim children aged 12-15 demonstrate this pattern for a variety of religious concepts (e.g., Hindu, Muslim) and a variety of traits (e.g., "not eating beef/pork," "believing in a deity"), only exhibiting the strong beliefs if they had encoded the property as part of the religious concept's central form, its conceptual identity. This form-measurement approach also predicts the strong judgments adults possess about socially relevant concepts like government and country, showing that individual differences in the endorsement of strong form-generated beliefs can be predicted by whether any given property (e.g., providing public services, having a strong leader) is seen as part of "what makes a government a government" or "what makes a country a country."

In these lines of work, participants often endorse these beliefs using even stronger language than was used in the present experiment (e.g., "I'm completely certain," "I'm completely sure") and with scales that present other options (e.g., "not sure," "a little sure," "completely sure"). The present work is also consistent with the documentation of psychological certainty beliefs in the political domain, in religious contexts, in memory retrieval, during conflict, as well as in many other domains of life (Burton, 2009; Talarico & Rubin, 2003; Rios et al., 2014; Tormala & Rucker, 2018). Form Theory therefore raises the possibility that future research could link some of these deeply held beliefs to the internal structure of our concepts.

Clarifying the Logic of Experiment 2.

A further concern may be that in Experiment 2 participants were directly told about the conceptual identity of each concept ("what makes a fep a fep"). Therefore, isn't it natural they would then be certain the property will exist in any given category member?

Ultimately, this is precisely the point. This line of work seeks to establish: (i) what logically follows, in the mind, from conceiving of a property in this manner, as part of what makes a fep a fep (strong certainty, stability, normativity, and universality beliefs); (ii) that these strong beliefs are restricted to properties that are part of the concept's conceptual identity, part of its central form; (iii) that this cluster of interrelated strong beliefs continues to be activated for the conceptual identities of familiar concepts like actor, gas station, protest, Hindu, and government (what makes a protest a protest, what makes a government a government). That this pattern has been shown to be present for all concepts so far tested, both novel and familiar, drawn from many different domains, is strong evidence that each concept possesses a central form that activates these strong (often unwarranted) beliefs.

Alternative Accounts and the Structural Interpretation.

As is often the case within the sciences-and particularly within a relatively young field like cognitive sciencedifferent approaches to these issues exist, within different frameworks (e.g., Bayesian approaches, causal-model approaches, connectionist approaches; Tenenbaum et al., 2006; Sloman, 2005; Rumelhart & McClelland, 1986). For example, an alternative explanation for the results may be that participants' certainty judgments reflect threshold-based processes. Specifically, participants may estimate the prevalence of properties across category members and report certainty only when these estimates exceed a high threshold (e.g., 90%).

While integrating these results into alternative frameworks may hold independent value, it is critical to consider these data in the broader context of findings on central form. As noted: (i) a whole cluster of strong beliefs are activated for form properties, not just one; (ii) these strong beliefs are activated immediately, as soon as a property gains the status of a form property; (iii) they are then maintained at high rates for familiar concepts which participants have likely possessed for decades; and (iv) this takes place for all types of concepts, across different domains. Even within alternative frameworks, these data reveal highly systematic patterns that would need to be integrated and activated should a property pass a designated threshold. Furthermore, positing a threshold like this for every concept that the mind generates, and proposing that it automatically activates a whole cluster of beliefs, is inherently a proposal about mental structure.

In this sense, the present work holds considerable value for alternative frameworks by providing systematic patterns that any model of conceptual representation and reasoning must explain. 12 Fully differentiating between entire frameworks goes beyond the scope of this paper, and future work may explore these ideas. 13 Form Theory, however, treats the central form as a specific type of structure, part of each concept, which is consistent with historical approaches to form. It is part of an approach to the mind that considers there to be distinct types of mental representation (e.g., visual representations, linguistic representations…) with distinct structure (e.g., verbs have roles). This approach to the mind is highly consistent with the rest of biology, which proposes distinct systems and component parts, each with distinct structure. Form Theory adopts this structural interpretation for concepts and their central forms because central forms display all the empirical hallmarks of a specific type of structure: they are present in concepts early in life and across the lifespan, present in entirely new concepts, remain present as concepts encode more information over time, and are present in concepts from any domain. Moreover, without learning anything specific about a new kind of thing-say, when hearing a new word in conversation-we likely have the intuition that there is "something about it that makes it what it is." Systematic patterns like this, although abstract, warrant an explanation. This too is consistent with the central form being a preexisting structure within each concept, capturing its conceptual identity, but requiring information to be encoded within it.

By characterizing the central form as a distinct structure, Form Theory also makes it possible to ask deeper questions about how this structure functions in thought, including whether it operates as a default mental structure that humans may at times fall back on, subsequently activating strong form-generated beliefs. 12 In the case of a statistical threshold, the threshold would need to either vary by concept or be very high. In the present study, generic properties like being black were included which are extremely prevalent in category members, well above 90% (consider how often you come across non-black tires). In fact, across all properties in Experiment 1, the generalization of form properties and generic properties to new members was near identical (98.75% vs 92.66% respectively). 13 One way to approach this question is to ask: Is the threshold inherently statistical? Three considerations are worth noting. First, the statistical beliefs activated as part of the suite of form-generated beliefs are internal beliefs that neither accurately reflect the external world nor are graded. For example, participants accept statements about form properties like "all watches, without exception, tell time" and "all motorcars, without exception, have an engine," even though the external world includes watches and motorcars that lack these properties. Second, while it is plausible that statistical information plays a role in encoding a property as a form property, many other cues likely contribute, such as pedagogical cues, causal cues, and form-signature cues (e.g., hearing a speaker express certainty that a category member possesses the property). Third, once encoded, a whole cluster of form-generated beliefs is activated, not all of which are statistical in nature. In fact, taking the form-generated beliefs together-certainty, permanence, normativity, and universality-the central form may represent our "internal ideals" for each concept, a proposal consistent with traditional approaches to form (Cudworth, 1731).

Form-Generated Beliefs Are Likely "Defaults" for Thinking and Reasoning.

But surely form-generated psychological certainty can be overridden? We can entertain the thought that a particular watch might not tell time, that a particular house might not be livable, and that a particular architect might not be able to design buildings. Entertaining these thoughts appears to be easiest when salient external causes exist that account for why a category member lacks a form property. For example, if we visit a watchmaker's store, we are unlikely to be certain that any given watch we encounter will tell time. Similarly, if we are walking through a war zone, we are unlikely to believe with certainty that any given house we encounter will be livable. Consistent with this, Form Theory does not claim that form-generated psychological certainty must be rigidly maintained over time. 14 Rather, as noted in the introduction, it is proposed as a type of default belief that can be overridden with additional processing (Figure 8). That is, it provides the mind/brain with an initial expectation in the absence of contradictory contextual information. The present paper lays the foundations for future lines of work exploring the interplay of these factors, such as the cost associated with bringing in additional context, and the situations in which people may be more likely to fall back on form-generated certainty (e.g., in conflict, under stress, under time pressure; see Section 4.3).

The other form signatures also seem to possess this default character. For example, central forms impose the normative expectation that category members should possess form properties and there is something wrong with a member lacking a form property. A watch should tell time, a strawberry should be red, and a bird should be able to fly. Both adult and child participants make these judgments (including normative judgments about these specific form properties; Haward et al., 2018). But, upon reflection, there is nothing necessarily wrong with a category member that lacks a form property. There is nothing wrong with a watch that has been taken apart to be cleaned, or an unripe strawberry, or a baby chick. In these cases, additional causal/contextual knowledge justifies the absence of a form property, and inhibits the default judgment that there is something wrong with the category member. For each of the form signatures, there appears to be a distinction equivalent to what researchers working on certainty call the psychological/epistemic distinction (between psychological certainty and epistemic certainty; Burton, 2009). Namely, each of these signatures is a psychological expectation, held with conviction by an individual, and so will not always be well justified.

The nature of these signatures, as psychological and default expectations triggered by a concept's central form, suggests interesting avenues for future research. For example, people often exhibit certainty about their beliefs during conflicts (Rios et al., 2014), during clinical depression (Miranda & Mennin, 2007), and when reflecting on their memories (e.g., Day & Ross, 2014; Hirst et al., 2009). The results of the present paper lay the foundations to test, for any expression of psychological certainty in these domains, whether the strong beliefs derive from how a person's concepts have been structured. Consider, as another example, stereotype representations. Humans possess a tendency to acquire overly simplified concepts of men, women, Black people, White people, Asians, Mexicans, and so on. When reasoning using these stereotype representations, people appear to exhibit the psychological signatures of central form, often in a default manner and without adequate justification.

Take the well-documented stereotype of Asians and the trait of being competent at mathematics (e.g., Shih et al., 1999). Future work might explore whether some people-those who hold the stereotype, who have encoded "being good at math" as part of what makes an Asian an Asian-exhibit an initial sense of certainty that any given Asian person they encounter will be good at mathematics. If the same person (i) believes that Asians should be competent at mathematics, and that they are a "bad Asian" if they are not (ii) believes Asians will be this way forever (iii) expresses that "they're all like that," then this would be strong evidence that these pernicious beliefs arise from human concepts more generally possessing a central form. Should this be true, it would align with an expanding body of research which suggests that humans have a natural tendency to form and perpetuate stereotypical beliefs (e.g., Cimpian & Markman, 2011; Gelman et al., 2004; Rhodes et al., 2012; Roberts et al., 2017; Roberts & Rizzo, 2021).

Form Theory extends this body of work by: (i) making predictions about additional inferences, including psychological certainty; (ii) predicting the default character of these inferences; and (iii) predicting that this kind of reductive thinking is not limited to concepts treated as having an underlying nature, but may appear across concepts of many types, including political identities such as liberal or conservative; institutional roles such as politician or police officer; deviance labels such as criminal or deserter; status concepts such as rich person or poor person; information concepts such as conspiracy theory or fake news story; and event concepts such as riot or revolution.foot_7

4.2. Why Might Concepts Possess a Certainty Signature?

Deep questions remain about why human concepts are structured this way. Why would a cognitive system build into each concept a central structure that imposes such strong beliefs on the information it encodes? Benefits for Thinking About Local Environments. One possibility is that when navigating our environments, we encounter thousands of category members at any moment which we need to process to determine whether and how to engage with them. When walking down the street, we encounter not only roads, cars, and pedestrians, but also dogs, lampposts, crosswalks, chairs and tables, cracks and potholes, banks and cafés, clouds and rain droplets, different kinds of sounds, professions, hairstyles and spaces. For each concept, we represent a vast number of properties potentially existing in individual members (e.g., for a café, that it will have wifi, places to work, restrooms, chairs and tables). We surely, at times, process the likelihood that these properties will be present in any category member we are about to encounter (e.g., drawing on statistical, contextual, and pragmatic processing). But this additional processing will come at a cost, and computation is costly for an organism (Harris et al., 2012; Mink et al., 1981). Consequently, if the cognitive system can presume the existence of a small subset of properties for each concept, with certainty, it can offload some of that computational cost. These certainty judgments will do well much of the time. For example, if you are walking along the sidewalk and notice a cluster of chairs outside of a café in the distance, it makes sense to presume with certainty that these will allow for sitting, thus preserving computational resources to process the likelihood that the chairs will be comfortable, or whether it will rain soon, or what time the café will close, and so on.

Benefits for Combinatorial Thought.

Another possibility is that the existence of a central form within each concept makes distinctively human thought computationally tractable (Haward, 2022a (Haward, , 2025c; Figure 9). Humans can combine concepts at a rapid rate in thought and language to produce complex structures of a length and complexity unimaginable by any other species on Earth (100-200 words are combined in a typical minute of speech; Chafe, 2015; Yuan et al., 2006). Since each concept stores such a vast amount of generic/factual information, even simple combinations are consistent with hundreds of meanings. For example, the sentence/thought "the restaurant was efficient" does not specify which property of the restaurant was efficient. Is it the restaurant's restrooms, plumbing, electricity, overhead lighting, air conditioning, or any of the many other properties understood as part of our generic knowledge of restaurants and often present in restaurants?

In Haward (2022a), people showed a striking preference for form interpretations, interpreting sentences in terms of form properties 80-90% of the time (in this case, interpreting the sentence to mean that the restaurant's serving of food was efficient, one of those properties that makes a restaurant a restaurant). This tendency is present (i) when concepts are recruited and combined from any domain (e.g., concepts for artifacts, social agents, animals, events, natural objects, places, and so on); (ii) as soon as a new concept is learned and a property is encoded as part of its central form. Most revealingly, (iii) participants go as far as to say that non-form interpretations are impossible if sentences are presented without context. This supports the hypothesis that the central form is a default structure which when combined yields a set of default interpretations. These are essentially available for free-since form information is represented with certainty and permanence-while non-form interpretations may require additional contextual support before they are deemed possible. Finally, (iv) when participants are asked to generate their own interpretations of sentences, they spontaneously generate form interpretations, and the properties they include in their interpretations are the exact same ones that, on separate questions, participants express "complete certainty" will exist in members, and which they report will be part of the concept "forever." This supports the hypothesis that people do indeed fall back on stable, certain central forms when interpreting sentences. Within Form Theory, this proposal is called Form Composition.

Form composition provides a deep explanation for linguistic creativity. Markedly different concepts, like person and mountain, drawn from different conceptual domains, can be combined quite naturally to produce an interpretation. Since the mind prioritizes combining the central forms of each concept, it strips away the "generic/factual noise" and provides interpretations like the person's body (a form property of the concept person) is tall (a form property of the concept mountain; Haward, 2025c). Further, form composition generates interpretations not only for familiar metaphors (e.g., "The person is a mountain"), but also newly created metaphors (e.g., "The cow is a megaphone," "Her hair is a pancake," "The ballerina is a feather"). More generally, while the causal structure and generic/factual details will differ radically by domain, the central form provides a common format, present within every concept, which can be quickly and easily combined in language and thought. By representing most of the concept's information in an uncertain manner, and restricting certainty beliefs to a central core of information, the process of combining concepts together is greatly simplified, computationally less costly, and will do well most of the time, with specifications of non-form interpretations of sentences possible with additional linguistic markers, or with the additional processing of contextual cues.

Benefits for Our Broader Belief Systems.

The certain, stable, and normatively guiding format of central form may also help establish stable organizing structures within a person's broader belief system or worldview (Haward, 2026a). On this view, humans face at least two related pressures. First, we must construct some relatively stable and certain reality in a world that, behind the veil of our conceptual structure, is marked by impermanence and complexity (Harvey, 2013; Siderits, 2021). Second, even within what we do conceive, life has become increasingly complex as societies have expanded in scale and organization (Tainter, 1988; Schwartz, 2004).

Form Theory proposes that one way the mind may counter these destabilizing pressures is by drawing on form structurefoot_8 -which naturally activates certainty, stability, and normative guidance-and encoding more beliefs with that structure. In line with this, recent work suggests that when children encode more beliefs as part of the central forms of important worldview-organizing concepts, they then view those concepts as playing a more stabilizing and protective role in their lives. For example, among children in India who share a religious faith, those who encode a broader range of beliefs and practices as part of what makes that religion what it is (i.e., its central form) (i) display the full range of form signatures for those specific beliefs and practices and, crucially, (ii) are considerably more likely to report that their religion makes them feel safe and secure in an uncertain world where bad things can happen (Haward & Srinivasan, 2026). This suggests that form-generated beliefs may not only help solve cognitive problems, but may also help satisfy deeper existential needs. More broadly, it raises the possibility that strongly held beliefs about religion, nation, political identity, social status, family, and other identity-bearing concepts may partly arise when deep needs for stability, certainty, and guidance about how to act lead people to encode more worldview-organizing content in form.

4.3. Differences in the Expression of Form-Generated Certainty

As already noted, different people may encode different properties as part of a concept's central form, and this may partly explain why psychological certainty is expressed for different beliefs across individuals (see Sections 3.1 and 4.1). But this raises a further question: once an initial default sense of certainty has been generated by the central form of a concept, do people and contexts differ in how readily that certainty is overridden?

There are at least two broad cases in which this question arises. First, the context may be such that other reflective systems are not even activated, allowing formgenerated beliefs to dominate by default-as appears to be the case in the present experiments. In everyday life, this may occur in contexts that do not encourage reflective reasoning, such as fast-paced social media environments built around brief, bite-sized responses, or when environmental or habitual factors tax other cognitive systems, such as when people are making decisions quickly, under stress, or with too little sleep. Under such conditions, the processes required to override form-generated beliefs may not be activated at all.

Second, there may be cases in which the conditions are, in principle, ideal for revision, and yet people still cling to form-generated beliefs-such as psychological certainty about a belief-even when they have time to reflect, recruit other cognitive systems, and consider competing information. Why might this be?

The answer likely lies in the particular functional roles the signatures of form are serving for that individual in that situation. Recall that Form Theory places no fixed limit on the number of benefits these signatures may provide for the human mind. Central forms are proposed to be intrinsic to human concepts and to activate a family of automatic beliefs, but, as noted in the previous section, there are many ways in which systems -and, more broadly, people-may utilize them. Consider the role form plays in complex thought. There, form may help massively restrict the initial hypothesis space of sentence meanings. In that context, readily revising an initial default assumption in light of further evidence, such as new referents in context or pragmatic information, may often be the most rational response, particularly when little depends on maintaining that assumption. By contrast, if for some beliefs form plays an important stabilizing role by providing people with a sense of certainty and structure within their social frameworks for understanding the world, then the costs of abandoning these beliefs may be much higher, especially when they are foundational and support a wider hierarchy of commitments. In such cases, even when other cognitive systems can in principle be activated to override form-generated beliefs, people may nevertheless cling to them because of the other functional roles they play.

Understanding the interaction of these factors may motivate a new line of research on when and why humans fall back on form-generated beliefs. Indeed, Form Theory predicts that, as social life becomes more complex, people may come to express more of their beliefs with certainty. Under these conditions, both of the pressures described above may intensify. First, greater numbers of choices, demands, and competing sources of information may increase the frequency with which people operate under conditions that tax reflective systems (Roetzel, 2019), leading to greater reliance on form-generated beliefs and, with them, greater expressions of certainty. Second, as traditional social and civic frameworks fragment (Putnam, 2000), people may come to rely more heavily on the other functions of form in establishing certainty, stability, and guidance within their broader belief systems. On this view, Form Theory may help explain a seemingly paradoxical pattern. Even as people gain access to more information, and so more opportunities to consult alternative perspectives, they do not always respond with more measured reflection, but often with greater certainty. Further, Form Theory predicts that these effects should be most visible in contexts where complexity, time pressure, stress, and cognitive load make reflective systems less likely to be recruited, including on social media platforms, during political or institutional crises, during public-health emergencies, and in other situations where people must respond quickly to complex and uncertain information.

4.4. Further Implications

Form Theory offers a set of specific proposals about the structure of human concepts. Each concept contains a single, unitary, preexisting central form: a compressed format that stores a subset of conceptual information, provides a common structure across domains, activates strong default beliefs that make sense given its proposed structure and role, and can be recruited by other cognitive systems. Because this format is recruitable by other systems, it can support powerful computations in thought and language, yielding distinctive benefits for cognition. At the same time, because the beliefs it activates are default, simplified, and often unwarranted, the same structure can also generate characteristic costs. These claims, taken together, constitute the distinctive core of Form Theory.

These commitments raise questions about how Form Theory relates to other theories of conceptual representation, especially approaches that emphasize causal structure, domain-specific knowledge, and the distinction between type concepts and concepts of individual entities.

As with other major approaches to concepts-such as prototype theories (e.g., Rosch, 1975; Rosch & Mervis, 1975), definitional theories (e.g., Smith et al., 1974; Katz, 1997), theory-theory approaches (e.g., Murphy & Medin, 1985; Gopnik & Meltzoff, 1997), and causal approaches (e.g., Sloman et al., 1998; Ahn et al., 2000)the central explanatory target is the vast class of type concepts humans acquire, likely numbering in the tens of thousands across development (e.g., Bloom, 2000). Form Theory is therefore already a broad theory of concepts, and in some respects broader than approaches focused on natural kinds, since it is intended to apply to all concepts, including artifacts, mathematical concepts, abstract ideas, events, places, institutions, occupations, political identities, and even concepts with temporary application, such as phase sortals. For example, watch, triangle, democracy, wedding, restaurant, government, architect, nationalist, and passenger all appear to possess forms and to activate form signatures. More broadly, the pressures that motivate this frameworksuch as the need to compress large amounts of information into a small usable set, and to represent that set in a format suited for composition-are not unique to any narrow class of concepts.

Integration With Existing Approaches to Conceptual Representation.

One particularly important point of contact is with causal approaches. Form Theory does not suggest that the central form replaces domain-specific causal knowledge. Rather, it suggests that a subset of causally or functionally privileged information from different domains may be extracted and encoded into a common format for thought and language. Indeed, in novel category studies of the type introduced in Experiment 2, domain-specific knowledge has been shown to play a role in which properties are encoded as form properties. But crucially, a property only activates form signatures if it has been encoded as a form property (Haward, 2025b). For example, when participants learn that a new artifact was designed to have a property, or that a property of an animal is innate, they only exhibit form signatures (e.g., certainty and stability) if they subsequently report on a posttest that they conceive of that property as part of the concept's central form. This view is further supported by evidence that the central form provides a common format that can be rapidly combined across domains, despite large differences in their underlying causal structure (Haward, 2022a).

This offers a plausible route for integrating Form Theory with causal approaches to conceptual representation, including psychological essentialism (e.g., Medin & Ortony, 1989; Gelman, 2003), which overlaps with Form Theory in some respects but differs from it in several important ways. 17 For example, in the case of psychological essentialism, one important next step is to determine which measures commonly used in essentialism research reflect a domain-neutral representational structure within all concepts, and which instead reflect domain-specific knowledge.

Further, since central forms are proposed as a default structure, it is possible that people preferentially recruit the form properties of the entities they are reasoning about when constructing causal models, making those properties more likely to occupy important causal roles in those models. This possibility is consistent with the body of work on form composition, which suggests that central forms are recruited as defaults when concepts are combined. It may help explain some of the biased reasoning discussed in Section 4.1, if, at times, traits of social groups become form-encoded. If, for example, aggression is encoded into the central form of a social group concept, people may then be more likely to treat aggression as an explanatory cause when interpreting an event involving a member of that group. Fully integrating these findings into causal and other existing frameworks of conceptual representation goes beyond the scope of the present paper, but it is an important direction for future work. A further question is how Form Theory interfaces with concepts of individuals (e.g., Blok et al., 2005), which may operate differently in some respects, but which may nevertheless come to exhibit some of the same signatures of form as knowledge about them accumulates-for example, in judgments such as "I'm certain Mary will behave that way," "Mary always does that," or "That's just who Mary is."

17 Form Theory differs from psychological essentialism in at least six ways. (1) Scope: Form Theory proposes a structure within all concepts, rather than focusing primarily on biological or natural kinds and extensions to social kinds. (2) Cognitive type: Form Theory is a theory of representational structure, whereas psychological essentialism is typically studied as a theory of essentialist beliefs, construals, or schemas. (3) Outputs: Form Theory focuses on domain-neutral signatures such as certainty, permanence, normativity, universality, and possibility/impossibility. Psychological essentialism, by contrast, primarily characterizes beliefs about hidden essences and the inferences those beliefs support. (6) Position: Form Theory proposes central form as a default structure within cognitive architecture, with default outputs that can be overridden by other cognitive systems, and places forms, and therefore concepts, at the interface of combinatorial thought and non-linguistic cognition. Psychological essentialism does not make these architectural claims.

Integration With Existing Certainty Literature.

As noted, the present account is not intended to explain every instance of psychological certainty, but rather to reveal a natural expression of our conceptual structure. Even so, by identifying a possible representational source of certainty, Form Theory may lead to new questions about how this source relates to previously identified sources of certainty. For example, because form-encoded information acquires the status of default conceptual knowledge, it may be easier to retrieve, processed more fluently, experienced as more coherent, and accompanied by a pre-reflective "feeling of rightness"-factors that have each been linked to increased subjective certainty or confidence (e.g., Schwarz et al., 1991; Reber et al., 2004; Alter & Oppenheimer, 2009; Koriat, 2012; Thompson et al., 2011). Intriguingly, the certainty with which people hold their attitudes is a powerful predictor of attitude stability and behavior (Berger & Mitchell, 1989; Fazio & Zanna, 1978; Glasman & Albarracín, 2006; Krishnan & Smith, 1998; Rucker & Petty, 2004; Tormala & Petty, 2004). Guided by the present paper, future work might ask whether formgenerated certainty is a distinctive predictor of attitude stability and behavior, over and above general high confidence. After all, form-generated certainty, belief stability, and normative understanding-which includes instructions for how we should act-are intrinsically connected within the present framework. This would be in line with the broader view developed in Section 4.2: that form helps establish stable, guiding frameworks for understanding and navigating the world. Future work may also explore how form-generated certainty, proposed here as a type of categorical certainty, relates to graded measures of confidence, reliability, self-consistency, and other metacognitive judgments in the existing metacognition literature (e.g., Koriat, 2012; Pouget et al., 2016; Shekhar & Rahnev, 2024). The present paper is one contribution to this broader literature.

A Deep Resolution to an Old Puzzle About Concepts.

The present account helps resolve a classic puzzle about concepts. It aligns with critiques of the classical theory of concepts, which correctly argued that there are few, if any, properties we can be epistemically certain will be possessed by category members (e.g., Fodor, 1998; Wittgenstein, 1953). We often encounter houses that are not livable, watches that do not tell time, and birds that do not fly. Nevertheless, Form Theory argues that these concepts still possess a central form, with a suite of distinctive signatures, including the activation of a default psychological certainty belief about the information encoded within that form. In this way, Form Theory preserves the central insight of the anticlassical critique while showing how concepts can still contain a privileged internal boundary.

4.5. Conclusion: The Human Mind Needs to Take Some Things for Granted

The present work shows that it is not only fools and fanatics who possess certainty about their beliefs (cf. Russell, 1933). Rather, psychological certainty enters deeply into how we conceive of the entities that structure our lives. Complementing recent proposals, the present paper reveals another benefit of concepts possessing a central form. It allows the human mind to presume the existence of some properties of the entities we encounter, which much of the time will be correct. In going beyond mere likelihood, and in fixing these properties as certainly present, this signature of form may help the mind preserve cognitive resources, support the rapid construction of complex thoughts, and establish stable, guiding frameworks for understanding the world. And yet, it may also introduce misplaced certainty into some of these beliefs, particularly within the complex, cognitively taxing environments of modern life. Future research should aim to test these ideas more directly, exploring both the benefits and potential costs of the mind falling back on the central form in thinking and reasoning. Finally, by approaching psychological certainty through a broader theory of conceptual structure, we can see more clearly why it occupies such a deep place within human cognition.
