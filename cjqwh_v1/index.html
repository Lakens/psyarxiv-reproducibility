Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Abstract

Although viewing art reportedly elicits profound physiological and psychological responses, few studies have examined both domains concurrently in ecologically valid realworld exhibition settings. This study aims to investigate the temporal trajectories and concurrence of autonomic responses-specifically heart rate (HR) and heart rate variability (HRV)-and affective responses across pre-, during-, and post-viewing phases of a naturalistic art-viewing experience. This observational study was conducted at the Yokohama Triennale 2024. In total, 86 adults participated in the on-site procedures; 73 participants with valid HR data across all three on-site phases were included in the primary analytic sample. HR was recorded continuously, from which HRV (indexed by the root mean square of successive differences: RMSSD) was derived. Self-reported affect, wellbeing, resilience, and interest in art were assessed at prespecified time points before, during, and after the visit, as well as at 1-day and 1-week follow-ups. The results revealed divergent trajectories between autonomic and affective responses across the exhibition visit. Mean HR decreased from pre-viewing to post-viewing, while RMSSD increased-a pattern consistent with lower autonomic arousal and greater parasympathetic modulation. Conversely, self-reported negative affect increased (mean change = 5.29, 95% confidence interval [3.91, 6.67]), whereas positive affect exhibited little descriptive change. Broader psychological states-including well-being, resilience, and interest in art-exhibited no consistent patterns of change. Decreases in mean HR, increases in RMSSD, and increases in self-reported negative affect were observed over the same pre-to post-viewing period during a real-world contemporary art-viewing experience. This contrasting pattern may reflect the multidimensional complexity of naturalistic aesthetic experiences, in which physiological and affective measures may exhibit different patterns of change over the same observation period. Future studies should incorporate control conditions, standardized measurement periods, and additional physiological and experiential measures to clarify the mechanisms underlying these divergent patterns.

Introduction

A growing body of evidence indicates that engagement in artistic and cultural activities can meaningfully contribute to mental and physical health [1, 2]. Past empirical work has predominantly focused on active forms of artistic participation, such as music making, dancing, and creative art production [1], overlooking receptive forms of engagement, particularly visual art viewing, which represent an accessible population-level resource for well-being that requires no specialized training and has a low threshold for entry [3, 4]. Its relatively low barrier to participation may make it an accessible form of art engagement.

Understanding these multifaceted aesthetic encounters necessitates the examination of art viewing within ecologically valid, naturalistic settings rather than relying solely on restricted laboratory paradigms. Although traditional laboratory studies-in which art viewing is controlled by protocols-offer high internal validity and robust evidence of how people view artworks, they strip away the essential spatial, physical, and contextual affordances that define genuine aesthetic experiences-including ambulatory freedom, selfselected viewing distances, physical materiality, and curatorial sequencing [5] [6] [7] [8].

Conceptualizing art engagement in real-world exhibition spaces is therefore crucial for empirical aesthetics, as the authentic museum environment restores the viewer's embodied agency and active spatial navigation, fundamentally shaping both cognitive appraisal and real-time psychophysiological processing [9, 10].

In ecologically valid exhibition settings, visitor experiences begin with assessing their overt physical and spatial behaviors. Unlike laboratory participants who view art while seated, museum visitors exercise active spatial agency by freely walking through galleries, adjusting their viewing distances, and choosing where to focus their attention [6, 7, 11]. Observational and visitor-tracking studies monitoring physical movement and viewing durations reveal that naturalistic art viewing involves complex behavioral patterns, such as surveying a room, approaching an artwork from multiple angles, and revisiting preferred pieces [6, 7]. In naturalistic environments, viewing durations are far more variable than in laboratory trials, often averaging 15 to 40 seconds per artwork depending on canvas size, placement, and personal interest [6, 12, 13]. Crucially, the decision to stop in front of a specific artwork represents a foundational behavioral transition that enables sustained aesthetic contemplation and deeper cognitive processing [14, 15].

Although behavioral tracking studies have clarified patterns of physical navigation and viewing durations, psychological and affective responses to visual art are still assessed predominantly via post-visit self-report questionnaires. Empirical studies conducted in museums [10, 16], healthcare environments [17, 18], and experimental settings [19] [20] [21] have documented various psychological benefits of art viewing, including enhanced mood, emotional stimulation, and stress reduction. However, neither overt behavioral observation nor subjective self-reports alone can capture the implicit, moment-to-moment autonomic fluctuations that accompany real-time aesthetic encounters [4, 8, 22, 23].

To address this limitation, an increasing number of studies have incorporated objective physiological metrics alongside psychological assessments, including blood pressure and heart rate (HR) [16], heart rate variability (HRV) and skin conductance [10, 24, 25], and salivary cortisol [20, 26]. However, findings regarding the alignment between physiological and psychological responses remain inconsistent, with some studies reporting concordant reductions in stress, salivary cortisol, blood pressure, or HR following art viewing [16, 26] and others reporting significant psychological improvements, such as reductions in subjective stress without corresponding autonomic shifts, or vice versa [24, 27]. Because these physiological and psychological responses to art viewing may not always change in parallel, assessing both concurrently becomes necessary.

These conflicting results may be attributed to the fact that previous research has largely relied on comparisons between evaluations before and after a museum visit [26, 27] or on isolated exposures to artworks under laboratory experimental conditions [16, 25]. Few studies have examined how physiological and psychological responses unfold over the course of aa naturalistic exhibition visit, in which individuals navigate freely and engage with challenging contemporary artworks in ways that may induce cognitive friction or semantic instability [12, 28, 29]. Consequently, the temporal correspondence between autonomic activity and subjective affective states during unconstrained art viewing remains unclear.

To overcome these empirical gaps, the present study characterizes the temporal trajectories and concurrence of physiological and psychological responses during an unconstrained, naturalistic museum visit that did not involve experimentally controlling participants' exposure to artworks. Conducted at the 8th Yokohama Triennale 2024, a contemporary art exhibition, this observational study used a repeated-measures design, assessing participants across pre-, during-, and post-viewing phases, with 1-day and 1-week follow-ups. In this study, framed as an exploratory investigation rather than as a confirmatory hypothesis-testing trial, we aimed to describe overall empirical trends, estimate effect magnitudes, and evaluate their associated uncertainties to identify meaningful patterns for future research. Therefore, to avoid arbitrary dichotomization based on null-hypothesis significance testing, power-related misinterpretations, and multiplicity issues, we adopted an estimation-focused analytical approach centered on point estimates and two-sided 95% confidence intervals (CIs) rather than p-values [30, 31]. Because the analyses were exploratory, CIs were interpreted descriptively and were not adjusted for multiplicity. Specifically, we evaluated whether a naturalistic exhibition visit would elicit autonomic stabilization and physiological calming-reflected by a decrease in mean HR and an increase in HRV from the pre-viewing to the post-viewing phase. Conversely, given the cognitively challenging, provocative, and semantically complex nature of contemporary art, combined with cumulative attentional demands over time, we also examined whether subjective affective trajectories would diverge from autonomic calming, manifesting instead as heightened affective friction or negative affect during the visit. Finally, we conducted an exploratory evaluation to determine whether these acute, on-site psychophysiological shifts would extend to broader, sustained changes in subjective wellbeing, resilience, and interest in art at 1-day and 1-week follow-ups. By capturing both continuous cardiac metrics and subjective states throughout an unconstrained exhibition visit, this study sought to clarify whether autonomic and affective trajectories change in parallel or diverge during real-world engagement with contemporary art.

Methods

Study design and setting

This single-group, repeated-measures observational study was conducted during the 8th Yokohama Triennale, "Wild Grass: Our Lives," held from March 15 to June 9, 2024, across five venues in Yokohama, Japan (https://www.yokohamatriennale.jp/2024/en/). The exhibition addressed themes related to contemporary social and economic systems, environmental issues, and challenges in modern society. To minimize between-participant variability in the exhibition environment, data were collected from May 17 to 19, 2024, at the Yokohama Museum of Art. The exhibition at this venue comprised contemporary installations, paintings, sculptures, and video works. S1 Table provides a complete list of the displayed artworks. Participants explored the venue freely in a naturalistic, nonexperimental art-viewing environment. Exposure conditions-including viewing order, viewing duration per artwork, and social context-were completely unconstrained. official website of the Yokohama Triennale Research Initiative. The Yokohama City Government published a public recruitment announcement on February 29, 2024. Online registration remained open through March 4, 2024. The website described the study procedures and eligibility criteria. Eligible individuals were adults aged 18-59 years with no self-reported cardiovascular disorders, specifically arrhythmia. The website also stated that participants would receive complimentary admission to the Yokohama Triennale as compensation. Registration closed after 295 individuals had registered, as the target sample size was 100.

Participants

All registered participants received an email with an online eligibility screening survey and an informed consent form. They selected one preferred visit period among six available options: morning or afternoon on May 17, 18, or 19, 2024. Of these participants, 19 were excluded for not returning the screening survey and/or a signed consent form, and 12 were excluded for self-reporting of arrhythmia. Of the remaining 264 eligible participants, 130 were randomly selected and invited to complete the baseline questionnaire and visited the exhibition. The invitation target exceeded the planned sample size by 30 to account for non-attendance. To balance attendance across the six visits, 25 participants were selected for each of the four periods on May 18 and 19, and 15 for each of the two periods on May 17, for which fewer individuals were available. Participants were selected for each period using the random-number function in Microsoft Excel. Of the 130 invitees, 86 actually attended the exhibition and completed the on-site procedures.

Measures

Physiological measures

Continuous HR-quantified as beats per minute (bpm)-and HRV-quantified as beat-to-beat interval (BBI [ms])-were recorded by the Garmin vivosmart 5 (Garmin Ltd., Olathe, KS, USA) worn on participants' nondominant wrist. The data from this device were transferred to the Fitrockr platform (mHealth Pioneers GmbH, Germany) and subsequently exported as spreadsheet files for analysis. HR values of 255 bpm (hereafter, 255 errors) indicated recording errors and were therefore treated as invalid. When such errors occurred intermittently during a recording session, individual 255 errors were excluded, and the remaining data were retained for analysis. Mean HR was calculated separately for the previewing (T2), during-viewing (T3), and post-viewing (T4) phases. Participants who lacked valid HR data across all three phases were excluded from the physiological analyses (details provided in the Data Analysis section). In addition, BBI data were utilized for HRV analysis. BBI values of 0 ms or greater than 3,000 ms were identified as invalid artifacts and excluded without interpolation.

PERMA Profiler

Subjective well-being was evaluated using the Japanese version of the PERMA Profiler [32], based on the original version developed by Butler and Kern [33]. The scale is a multidimensional self-report measure of well-being based on Seligman's model [34]

The 14-item Resilience Scale (RS-14)

Psychological resilience was measured using the Japanese version of the RS-14 [35] based on Wagnild and Young's original scale [36]. The RS-14 assesses positive adaptation to adversity across personal competence and acceptance of self and life. Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale and summed to yield a total score ranging from 14 to 98, with higher scores indicating greater resilience. Participants responded to this scale during the previewing phase (T2), the 1-day follow-up (T5), and the 1-week follow-up (T6). We compared changes in psychological resilience, which has been defined as a potentially modifiable factor [37] and one that might be improved through intervention [38]. Although not designed as an intervention study, we assumed that this study would be meaningful to explore changes in resilience through art appreciation.

The University College London (UCL) Museum Wellbeing Measures Toolkit

Subjective positive and negative affect were assessed using Japanese measures corresponding to the UCL Museum Wellbeing Measures Toolkit [39, 40], which was administered in Japanese, using a translation previously applied to an art-viewing study in Japan [41, 42]. Each measure comprises six emotion items rated on a 5-point scale. Item scores are summed to yield scores ranging from 6 to 30, with higher scores indicating greater positive or negative affect. In addition to the scales assessing positive and negative affect, we evaluated participants' museum experience using the Generic Wellbeing Questionnaire, which is included in this toolkit. This scale refers to an aspect of emotion or quality of life experienced by a participant while participating in an activity (e.g., "I felt yielding scores ranging from 2 to 14, with higher scores indicating stronger expression of the trait. Participants completed this scale only at the pre-visit phase (T1).

Statistical analysis

In accordance with recent statistical recommendations for exploratory research [30, 31], we adopted a descriptive, estimation-focused analytical approach centered on point estimates and CIs instead of null-hypothesis significance testing. The analyses reported in this study were based on a prespecified statistical analysis plan prepared before data analysis. No additional post hoc inferential analyses were conducted.

Physiological and psychological trajectories across the art-viewing experience were evaluated without relying on p-values. The target sample size was derived from the expected precision of the estimated within-participant change in mean HR, as reflected by the width of its 95% CIs. Assuming a standard deviation (SD) of 12 bpm for withinparticipant HR changes, a sample size of 89 participants was expected to yield a two-sided 95% CI with a total width of approximately 5 bpm under a normal approximation. To allow for potential data loss, the target sample size was set at 100 participants.

Discussion

This study characterized the temporal trajectories and concurrence of physiological and psychological responses during an unconstrained, naturalistic art-viewing museum visit. In accordance with recent statistical recommendations emphasizing estimation over rigid null hypothesis significance testing [30, 31], our analytical approach focused on point estimates and two-sided 95% CIs to describe overall empirical trends, quantify effect magnitudes, and evaluate associated uncertainties. This estimation-focused exploratory paradigm is particularly valuable for ecologically valid real-world field research because it avoids arbitrary dichotomization based on p-values, power-related misinterpretations, and multiplicity issues, thereby establishing a transparent foundation for generating empirical hypotheses for future research.

Primary findings and overall interpretation

Physiological and psychological responses exhibited different group-level trajectories across the art-viewing experience. Physiologically, mean HR decreased substantially, and HRV (RMSSD) increased from the pre-viewing (T2) to the post-viewing (T4) phase, potentially suggesting lower autonomic arousal and greater parasympathetic modulation. Psychologically, however, subjective affect did not follow a parallel trend of generalized relaxation; instead, negative affect increased markedly from pre-viewing to 29 post-viewing, whereas positive affect exhibited minimal change. Broader psychological constructs, including subjective well-being (PERMA Profiler), psychological resilience (RS-14), and interest in visual art (VAIAK), revealed no consistent directional shifts in their descriptive profiles across the assessment timeline. These findings suggest that realworld engagement with contemporary art may be accompanied by a complex, multidimensional state in which physiological changes consistent with autonomic calming are observed alongside increased self-reported negative affect at the group level. These different group-level patterns underscore the need to evaluate multiple physiological, emotional, and cognitive domains simultaneously, as relying solely on single-domain or purely hedonic metrics would fail to capture the nuanced dynamics of aesthetic experiences in naturalistic settings.

Physiological trajectories: autonomic stabilization in naturalistic viewing

Continuous physiological tracking revealed that mean HR decreased and RMSSD increased from pre-viewing (T2) to post-viewing (T4). In previous psychophysiology studies, this pattern was broadly compatible with lower autonomic arousal and greater parasympathetic modulation, although HR and HRV are influenced by multiple physiological and contextual factors [48, 49]. The direction of these changes aligns with previous reports of reductions in physiological stress-related indices, including systolic blood pressure and salivary cortisol, following museum or gallery visits [4, 16, 26].

In this study, when examining phase-to-phase transitions, mean HR changed minimally during art viewing (T3 relative to T2), whereas RMSSD exhibited a descriptive increase during the art viewing phase, becoming more marked during the post-viewing rest period (T4). These phase comparisons should be interpreted with caution because of the fundamental physical differences across conditions. Although participants were seated during the pre-viewing (baseline; T2) and post-viewing (T4) phases, they engaged in unconstrained walking and postural changes throughout the exhibition (T3) [49].

Ambulatory movement increases metabolic demand and HR, which can attenuate or mask underlying vagal recovery during active viewing [10, 48, 49]. The physiological changes observed at T4 were consistent with lower autonomic arousal. Although active exploration involves physical and cognitive engagement, the post-viewing phase may be associated with reduced physiological arousal following the cessation of movement. Accordingly, the study suggests that physiological changes can be measured in a naturalistic exhibition setting, although its observational design does not establish that art viewing caused the observed changes.

Affective friction and the paradoxical benefits of negative affect

In contrast to the autonomic stabilization observed in the post-viewing phase (T4), subjective affective responses were characterized by increased negative affect but little change in positive affect. This pattern reflects a complex affective response to contemporary visual art and may provide insights into the nature of engagement with such experiences. Contemporary art exhibitions, such as the 8th Yokohama Triennale 2024, often explore provocative, socio-political, and existential themes that challenge viewers' preconceived cognitive frameworks. Recent research in empirical aesthetics emphasizes that viewing art regularly evokes mixed or negative valence states [23, 50]. Recently, Dhallu et al. [51] demonstrated the paradoxical benefits of engaging with tragic or challenging art. Exposure to artistic depictions with negative valence not only induces distress but also causes viewers to feel deeply moved, become more empathetic, and find profound personal meaning.

According to the Distancing-Embracing model [50, 52], framing negative or disturbing content within an art context provides psychological distance that helps protect against distress and allows viewers to safely embrace negative affective states (e.g., sadness, discomfort, or cognitive friction) without feeling threatened in real-world practical terms. Thus, the increase in negative affect observed in our study likely reflects meaningful emotional resonance and active interpretive processing elicited by the challenging exhibition content, rather than a negative shift in affective state or dissatisfaction with the museum visit itself.

Broader psychological constructs and long-term follow-ups

Broader psychological constructs, including well-being (PERMA Profiler), resilience (RS-14), and interest in art (VAIAK), revealed no consistent directional shifts in their descriptive scores from pre-viewing to 1-day (T5) or 1-week (T6) follow-ups.

Although mean PERMA scores were numerically lower at the follow-up (T5, T6) than at the on-site assessments, these descriptive differences should be interpreted with extreme caution because of the incomplete follow-up responses and the exploratory nature of the study design. Because this study adopted a non-confirmatory, estimation-based analytical framework, these descriptive patterns should not be misinterpreted as evidence of no effect. These results do not provide clear evidence of short-term changes in broader psychological constructs following the art-viewing experience. Traits such as psychological resilience, general interest in visual art, and multidimensional well-being may reflect relatively stable psychological characteristics or broader states that are less likely to reveal marked short-term variation. A single, brief exhibition visit, even if emotionally evocative, may not be sufficient to produce immediate, detectable shifts in trait-like constructs or global well-being over a 1-week period [4, 53]). This pattern aligns broadly with recent empirical assessments on the temporal dynamics and persistence of psychological responses to art viewing. In a 2-week daily diary study tracking visitors before and after an exhibition on challenging societal themes, Pelowski et al. [54] observed that although acute emotional and prosocial responses frequently return to baseline within 24 hours, certain reflective cognitive processes-such as self-reflection and considering others' perspectives-can exhibit subtle, sustained elevations over the following week. Furthermore, Pelowski et al. [55] reported that while socially focused exhibitions were associated with attitude and value shifts mediated by emotional resonance, population-level effect sizes from a single visit remain small to moderate, with substantial inter-individual variability.

Collectively, these insights suggest that while single-session art encounters may be accompanied by acute psychophysiological and affective engagement, macro-level psychological traits and sustained changes in well-being may be more likely to emerge with repeated exposures or cumulative engagement over time [54] [55] [56]. Future longitudinal research should employ ecological momentary assessment or repeated-visit interventions to clarify the precise decay trajectories, individual moderating factors, and potential sleeper effects of real-world aesthetic experiences.

Theoretical implications for aesthetic reception and museum engagement

The different group-level patterns in physiological and affective responses observed in our study may have theoretical implications for major frameworks in empirical aesthetics and human flourishing [3, 57, 58]. Here, human flourishing can be understood as a multidimensional state of optimal functioning and holistic well-being, which encompasses not only hedonic positive affect but also eudaimonic dimensions such as meaning and purpose in life, personal growth, and social connectedness [58] [59] [60]. Examining our findings through the combined lenses of the Distancing-Embracing model [50], the Vienna Integrated Model of Art Perception (VIMAP) [29, 61, 62], and the flourishing museum framework [3, 63, 64] may provide a coherent, multi-layered perspective on the naturalistic art-viewing experience.

First, Menninghaus et al. [50] conceptualize the reception of negative valence from artworks through two complementary mechanisms: psychological distancing, facilitated by the protective "art frame," and the active embrace of emotional friction. In our study, the co-occurrence of physiological restoration (decreased HR, increased HRV) and heightened negative affect may be consistent with this dual dynamic in a real-world setting. The observed physiological calming (reduced autonomic arousal) may be related to the psychological distancing, although the latter was not directly assessed in this study. This may provide a stable physical baseline, potentially enabling viewers to engage with and cognitively process provocative and challenging contemporary art content with reduced real-world physiological distress.

Limitations

Several methodological limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the absence of a control condition prevents the observed psychophysiological changes from being attributed specifically to the artistic content, as opposed to general venue characteristics, novel social settings, or simple rest periods.

Second, the convenience sample was predominantly female (79.5%) and comprised volunteers who chose to attend the exhibition. This self-selection bias limits the generalizability of the findings to broader, non-volunteer populations or visitors with lower baseline interest in art. Third, 13 participants (15.1%) were excluded from the primary analytic sample because of complete or severe invalidity in continuous HR recordings for all three on-site phases. Although these exclusions were strictly driven by technical 255 errors, the loss of data may introduce some selection bias. Fourth, affect was assessed only during the pre-viewing (T2) and post-viewing (T4) phases, preventing real-time tracking of affective dynamics during artwork exposure.

Fifth, exposure conditions were unconstrained. Because participants could select their own viewing routes and choose which artworks to contemplate, their total viewing duration (ranging from under 60 minutes to over 180 minutes) and social context varied widely. Sixth, in this field setting, a conceptual and terminological clarification is warranted regarding our use of the terms "real-world", "naturalistic", and "ecologically valid" throughout this paper. As methodological critiques in psychology and empirical aesthetics have noted, these descriptors are frequently used interchangeably without strict operational definitions, even though ecological validity encompasses multiple distinct dimensions, including the nature of the research setting, the stimuli, and the behavioral task. To reflect these multidimensional aspects, we used real-world to denote the physical and institutional context of an operating public museum outside the laboratory, naturalistic to characterize participants' unconstrained, self-paced ambulatory and viewing behaviors, and ecologically valid to describe the broader methodological alignment between our field paradigm and authentic aesthetic encounters [67]. Nevertheless, as Schmuckler [66] and Holleman et al. [68] emphasize, artificiality and naturality exist along a continuum rather than as a simple binary opposition between laboratory and field settings. Although conducting this study at the Yokohama Triennale preserved essential contextual affordances [67], participants were explicitly recruited for research, wore physiological sensors, and recorded their routes on a map. These procedural requirements inevitably introduced a degree of research-induced artificiality, meaning that the observed engagement represents a close-to-life approximation rather than an entirely unmonitored visit in everyday life [67, 68]. Seventh, the measurement conditions differed fundamentally across phases.

Because pre-viewing (T2) and post-viewing (T4) phases involved rest while being seated, whereas T3 involved continuous walking and standing, postural shifts, physical activity, and movement artifacts inherent to wrist-worn photoplethysmography (Garmin vivosmart 5) may have influenced HR and HRV metrics, particularly while viewing artworks (T3) [49]. Finally, continuous HR and RMSSD metrics reflect peripheral autonomic activity and should not be interpreted as direct, unequivocal indicators of specific central emotional or mental states.

Conclusion

This study characterized the physiological and psychological trajectories of visitors during an unconstrained, naturalistic visit to a contemporary art exhibition. Using an estimation-focused, non-confirmatory analytical approach centered on point estimates and two-sided 95% CIs, different group-level patterns were observed for autonomic and subjective affective measures: while mean HR decreased and HRV increased from the previewing to the post-viewing phase, negative affect increased and positive affect remained largely unchanged. Global constructs of well-being, resilience, and interest in art did not indicate consistent short-term descriptive changes over the assessment period. These findings generate the empirical hypothesis that naturalistic engagement with contemporary art facilitates somatic and autonomic calming while simultaneously provoking affective friction and cognitive challenge. Because this study was exploratory and lacked a control group, its results should be interpreted as descriptive trends rather than as causal effects. Controlled, experimental studies incorporating standardized resting conditions, continuous multi-modal physiological tracking, and detailed artwork-specific appraisals are needed to test the mechanisms underlying this pattern of autonomic and affective responses.
