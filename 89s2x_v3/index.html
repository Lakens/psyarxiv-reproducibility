Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

Purpose: Common mental disorders (CMDs), including depression, anxiety, and adjustment disorders, are leading causes of long-term sickness absence (SA) and work disability in highincome countries. Despite the significant burden, evidence-based interventions to support return to work (RTW) remain limited. This study aimed to investigate the predictive role of selected modifiable psychological factors on RTW among individuals on sick leave for CMDs, with particular emphasis on temporal dynamics during the early phase of the RTW process.

Methods: A longitudinal observational study of 165 participants on sick leave for CMDs was conducted with bi-weekly self-reported questionnaire measurements for 3 months and quarterly follow-ups for 12 months. Outcomes were self-reported work status (working to the full extent of one's employment, RTW) and registry-based net SA days (NSAD) in each quarter. Analyses included robust linear mixed models to classify improvement in seven focal predictors during the first three months, Bayesian logistic regression with a regularizing horseshoe prior for the probability of RTW, and ordered beta regression for NSAD. All models were adjusted for the baseline values of all focal predictors and for covariates.

Results: Over 3 to 12 months, improvement in perceived competence was associated with a higher probability of RTW, with a risk difference (RD) of 17.6 percentage points (95% credible interval, CrI [5.2, 27.1]). Improvement in workplace phobia (RD = 15.5, 95% CrI [0.7, 25.4]) and RTW self-efficacy (RTW-SE, RD = 14.1, 95% CrI [-0.1, 25.6]) showed similar differences.

At 3 months, RTW-SE improvement was associated with RD = 21.5 (95% CrI [0.1, 32.8]). No focal predictor was associated with NSAD in any quarter.

Early improvement in work-related psychological factors was associated with later work status, but not with compensated sickness absence. Whether strengthening these factors can support RTW is a question for further study.

Background

Common mental disorders (CMDs), including depression, anxiety, and adjustment disorders, are leading contributors to work disability worldwide, placing a substantial burden on individuals, employers, and healthcare systems (World Health Organization 2022; OECD 2023). In Sweden, CMDs represent the most prevalent diagnostic group associated with long-term sick leave, particularly among women and younger individuals (Försäkringskassan (Swedish Social Insurance Agency) 2024).

Returning to work (RTW) following sickness absence (SA) related to CMDs is a complex process, influenced by both individual and contextual factors (Nieuwenhuijsen et al. 2006; Etuknwa et al. 2019; Duchemin and Hocine 2020). Previous studies have identified several predictors of RTW for individuals with CMDs, including younger age, higher education, fewer prior sickness absences, lower symptom severity, greater self-efficacy, positive RTW expectations, better sleep quality, and support from supervisors and colleagues (de Vries et al. 2018; Etuknwa et al. 2019; Fisker et al. 2022; Villotti et al. 2024).

Modifiable psychosocial factors are of particular interest as potential targets for intervention (Villotti et al. 2024). Among these, RTW self-efficacy, defined as an individual's belief in their ability to resume and manage work despite mental health challenges (Lagerveld et al. 2010), has emerged as a key predictor of successful RTW (Volker et al. 2015; Nigatu et al. 2017). Research indicates that stable or increasing self-efficacy over time is associated with earlier RTW (Lagerveld et al. 2017; Horn et al. 2022); however, current evidence is based on measurement intervals usually spaced 3 months apart. To date, no studies have incorporated more frequent assessments during the critical early period (0-3 months) post-baseline.

Predictors of RTW may also vary depending on the duration of the SA episode. For instance, factors that are influential early in the absence period (< 3 months) may differ from those relevant in later phases, yet few studies have examined this potential temporal variation (Fisker et al. 2022). Furthermore, it remains unclear whether predictors maintain a consistent influence throughout the RTW process or if their predictive value changes over time.

Accurately capturing RTW and SA outcomes presents further methodological challenges. Sick leave may vary in duration, frequency, extent (part-or full-time), and recurrence with different metrics used across studies, complicating comparability (Hensing et al. 1998; Duchemin and Hocine 2020). Sole reliance on metrics such as counting days based on registry data may obscure the complexity of the RTW process. Registry data needs to comply with regulations in the country of origin, which may make comparisons between countries difficult.

Also, variables predicting registry data could be more closely related to system level and health care factors, such as policies and physician guidelines, that are difficult to quantify and include in a statistical model. Focusing on individual level factors only is less challenging but will likely be limited in explaining variation in registry data.

There are also important methodological concerns in the field. One relates to the treatment of data, where non-categorical variables are most often treated as interval level data in statistical models, even if a variable is clearly ordinal in nature, such as a single item measure. This may influence inference significantly (Liddell and Kruschke 2018). Multi-item ordinal sum scores have less clear statistical properties, but visualizing the distribution of the data is important to choose a correct statistical model, rather than just assuming data to be normally distributed. Number of sick days is a count variable with a lower bound, which also has a known upper bound since the time span is known and thus the maximum number of days possible. In the methods section, we will describe a statistical model that handles bounded data properly.

Another important aspect is psychometric quality of measures used. A paradigm shift is ongoing in psychometrics, moving from rule-of-thumb model fit cutoffs that are known to be inappropriate for most analyses to simulation-based cutoffs (Goretzko et al. 2024; Johansson 2025). This calls for re-analyses of old scales, no matter which psychometric method has been applied previously, to evaluate and likely modify measures according to findings from correctly applied methods. As a final methodological note, we want to mention the "Table 2 fallacy" (Westreich and Greenland 2013; Bandoli et al. 2018), which briefly stated is related to the interpretation of regression coefficients. Since a statistical model is usually constructed to evaluate the total effects of a focal predictor (such as a treatment) on the outcome (for instance RTW), it does not follow that the coefficients of other predictor variables can be interpreted as total effects as well. This is especially important to consider if one's statistical model uses the hazard ratio or Odd's ratio, since these estimands are non-collapsible and will change depending on the set of covariates included in the model (Cummings 2009; Dumas and Stensrud 2025; Colnet et al. 2025).

To inform the development of effective RTW interventions, a deeper understanding of how modifiable psychosocial predictors function and evolve over time is essential. In this exploratory longitudinal observational study, we aim to investigate the role of selected modifiable psychological factors in explaining RTW among individuals on sick leave for CMDs, with a particular focus on temporal dynamics during the early phase of the RTW process. We used two outcomes that describe different aspects of recovery. Self-reported work status describes whether participants were working to the full extent of their employment at each assessment, and registered net sickness absence days (NSAD) describe the amount of compensated sickness absence in each quarter of the year after inclusion.

Methods

Study design and setting

The study was designed as a longitudinal observational study and is reported in accordance with

The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. The Swedish Ethical Review Authority approved the study protocol (Dnr:2021-05022). Participants were recruited between April 2022 and February 2023 via advertisements in social media and posters displayed at primary healthcare centers in Region Stockholm, Sweden.

The advertisements contained a link to a secure website with information about the study and how to register to participate. Following registration, participants were contacted by phone for an interview where inclusion criteria were checked to confirm their eligibility. At the interview, participants reported the diagnosis stated on their medical certificate as the reason for their sick leave, and checked the certificate when needed. The certificates were not collected, so diagnostic eligibility was based on self-report. Self-reported data were collected using the digital research platform BASS from the eHealth Core Facility at Karolinska Institutet. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. To promote adherence to the measurement protocol, participants were offered the option of receiving an incentive at each quarterly assessment point.

They could choose between a cinema voucher or lottery tickets as a form of compensation.

Participants

The inclusion criteria were: age 18-60 and currently (for a duration of less than six weeks) on full-or part-time SA due to a common mental disorder (CMD). The accepted diagnoses according to the criteria of the ICD-10 (World Health Organization 2019) were: F32 depressive episode; F33 recurrent depressive disorder; F34 persistent mood [affective] disorders; F38 other mood [affective] disorders; F39 unspecified mood [affective] disorder; F41 other anxiety disorders; F43 adjustment disorders and reactions to severe stress; and F48 other neurotic disorders. All forms of comorbidities, both physical and psychological, were accepted, as well as all kinds of medication. The follow-up measurements require a good understanding of written Swedish, participants who did not meet this inclusion criteria were thus excluded.

Study size

The sample size was determined before the analyses presented here were planned. Based on Arend and Schäfer (Arend and Schäfer 2019), we estimated that 150 to 200 participants with 6 to 8 measurement occasions would give a power of 80% to detect medium standardized effects (0.20-0.30) at the between-person level and small effects (0.10-0.20) at the within-person level in multilevel models. This calculation does not apply to the improvement classification or the outcome models reported here, which were not powered for a specified effect. We therefore interpret the results by their estimates and credible intervals.

Variables and measurement

Demographic data and background variables (such as age, gender, type of work, level of education, etc.) were collected at enrollment. Assessments were conducted weekly during the first 10 weeks and then quarterly until one year after inclusion. Weekly assessments alternated between health and work measures on a biweekly basis. Register data were obtained from the Swedish Social Insurance Agency database MiDAS (Micro-Data for Analysis of the Social Insurance System) 12 months prior to inclusion to 12 months after inclusion. All residents in Sweden with income from work, unemployment, or parental leave benefits can receive SA benefits if their work capacity is reduced due to disease or injury. For employees, the employer pays SA benefits for the first 13 days of a SA period after the first qualifying day. A medical certificate is required from day eight. After the 14th day, the Social Insurance Agency pays the benefits, covering approximately 80% of lost income up to a certain level. All residents aged 19-64 years with a long-term or permanently reduced work capacity due to disease or injury can be granted disability pension (DP). Both SA and DP may be granted full-or part-time (25%, 50%, 75%) of ordinary working hours. Data also included main diagnosis (according to ICDclassification) from the sickness certification granted.

Outcome variables

Two outcomes were included. First, self-reported work status, for which participants were asked about their current level of work activity at each assessment, with response options of 0, 25, 50, 75, and 100 percent of their own employment. The variable was dichotomized into working to the full extent of one's employment (100%) or less. For participants employed part-time, 100% thus means working the full extent of that employment. Contracted hours were not recorded. In the following, RTW refers to working to the full extent of one's employment at an assessment.

The outcome describes work status at each assessment and does not show when a participant first returned to work or whether the return was sustained. Second, net sickness absence days (NSAD), for which we calculated SA/DP net days; e.g., two days of part-time absence for 50% were summed to one net day. NSAD was calculated for each quarter after enrollment (months 1-3, 4-6, 7-9 and 10-12), each with a maximum of 90 days, and each quarter was used as the outcome in separate statistical models.

Covariates

All models included the same covariates, all measured at or before enrollment. They included demographic variables, ongoing treatments, pain, general health, and single-item assessments of sleep quality, employer facilitation of workplace adjustments (EFWA), the Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2, (Kroenke et al. 2003)), and the Demands, Control and Social Support Questionnaire (DCSQ, (Sanne et al. 2005)). Sleep quality and EFWA were assessed using single questionnaire items with 7 and 11 ordered response categories, respectively. While the sleep quality item provided descriptive labels for all response categories, the EFWA item included labels only at the endpoints. The distribution of responses for the sleep quality item was approximately normal. In contrast, the EFWA response distribution was very irregular, with the most frequently selected categories being 1 (30%), 5 (13%), and 10 (11%). This distribution posed challenges for statistical modeling. To address this, the EFWA responses were collapsed into three ordered categories: 0-3 (53% of responses), 4-7 (24%), and 8-10 (22%). PHQ-2 contains two items, each with four ordered response categories scored from 0-3, and was dichotomized using ordinal sum score ≥ 3 as a cutoff for risk indication (Manea et al. 2016).

Two register-based variables described sickness absence during the 12 months before inclusion: any sickness absence (yes/no) and the number of net sickness absence days (log-transformed) (Lorenz et al. 2017). All covariates and their coding are listed in the Additional materials.

Focal predictor variables

Our focal predictors consisted of six multi-item questionnaires and one single-item measure, assessed biweekly from enrollment to 3 months after enrollment. We use the term "focal predictor" to clarify that statistical models and post-modeling effect estimations are designed to investigate the effect of the focal variables on the outcome (Arel-Bundock et al. 2024; Rohrer and Arel-Bundock 2025), to avoid the "factors associated with" issue if we were to interpret model coefficients of all variables included in the model (Lewer et al. 2025). The focal predictors were: Return-to-work self-efficacy (Lagerveld et al. 2010); Workplace Phobia Scale (Linden et al. 2008; Muschalla and Linden 2009); Perceived Competence Scale (Williams et al. 1998); two subscales, physical fatigue and cognitive weariness, from the Shirom-Melamed Burnout Questionnaire (Melamed et al. 1992 (Melamed et al. , 2006;; Lundgren-Nilsson et al. 2012; Sundström et al. 2023); General Anxiety Disorder (Spitzer et al. 2006). The single-item measure was Work Ability Score (El Fassi et al. 2013).

Quantitative variables

Psychometrics

All multi-item measurement scales were subject to psychometric analysis using Rasch measurement theory (Rasch 1960), assessing the basic psychometric criteria of unidimensionality (for each subscale in multidimensional scales), local independence of items, ordered response categories, and invariance (Christensen et al. 2021). For each latent variable, interval level respondent scores were estimated using weighted likelihood (Warm 1989), based on the set of items for each scale found to have acceptable psychometric properties, i.e. some of the focal predictors were modified. The retained items, response categories, score ranges and measurement occasions for each scale are listed in the Additional materials.

Statistical analyses

Missing data

Repeated measurements of the focal predictors were not imputed. The robust growth models (see below) used all available observations, and each participant's baseline value was the mean of their available scores at the first two assessments. Missing data among the baseline covariates were limited to five binary treatment variables (2 to 7 missing values each), and 15 participants (9%) lacked at least one. Missing covariates and work status were handled using multiple imputation with chained equations, as implemented in the R package ´mice´ (van Buuren and Groothuis-Oudshoorn 2011). For RTW, data were imputed in wide format with one row per participant, so that work status at all ten assessments entered the imputation model as separate variables. The treatment covariates and work status were imputed by logistic regression, with predictors selected by a minimum correlation of 0.1. Twenty datasets were imputed, which meets the recommendation of at least as many imputations as the percentage of incomplete cases (White et al. 2011), using 50 iterations and visually inspected for convergence. Imputed work statuses were retained in both the cross-sectional and the longitudinal models, since work status at other assessments, including those before 3 months, informs the imputed values and is not part of the analysis models. For NSAD, only the treatment covariates were imputed, with the NSAD outcomes as predictors. All statistical models were fitted to each of the 20 datasets separately.

Since we used Bayesian outcome models, results were pooled by combining the posterior draws from all submodels. The RTW analyses thus included all 165 participants, and the NSAD analyses the 141 with registered sickness absence (see Results).

As a sensitivity analysis, all models were also fitted to complete cases, the 150 participants with complete covariate data. The cross-sectional RTW models then included those with an observed work status at each time point (147 at 3 months, 140 at 6 months, 135 at 12 months), the longitudinal model all observed assessments at 3 to 12 months (148 participants, 560 observations), and the NSAD models 127 participants. These analyses assume that missingness does not depend on the outcome given the variables in the model. Complete-case results are reported in the Additional materials.

Statistical models of predictors

Each focal predictor was used in two ways: as a baseline measurement using the mean of the two first assessments and as a dichotomous variable indicating either participant improvement or lack of improvement during the first three months. For each of the focal predictors, a robust linear mixed model (LMM) with random slopes and intercepts was fitted using the R package `robustlmm` (Koller 2016), with time in weeks since inclusion. The models used the assessments from enrollment to 3 months, with the mean of the first two assessments as the first time point. The robust method for this unconditional growth model was chosen after checking non-robust models and finding issues with outliers. Improvement was classified from each participant's full estimated slope, that is, the fixed slope plus the participant's random deviation. Participants whose slope exceeded half the standard deviation (SD) of the random slopes in the favourable direction (an increase for RTW-SE, PCS and WAS, a decrease for WPS, SMBQ and GAD-4) were coded as 1 for "improved", and the others as 0 for "not improved".

The threshold is thus defined relative to the model-estimated variation in slopes between participants, which is in the same unit as the classified quantity and is not inflated by residual measurement error. R code and a worked example are given in the Additional materials. The improvement categories were treated as fixed in the outcome models, so the reported intervals are conditional on the classification and do not include its uncertainty. Results from all robust LMMs and slope categorizations are summarized in Table 2, which also gives the threshold in score units and includes a heterogeneity ratio, calculated as the SD of the random slopes divided by the absolute fixed slope (Vuorre et al. 2024) to describe the relative variation in change over time, as well as the ICC. Bolger and colleagues (Bolger et al. 2019) suggest that heterogeneity is "noteworthy" when the H-ratio is 0.25 or larger.

Each focal predictor was also modeled as a baseline measure, using the average score from the two first measurement points to minimize potential influence of the "initial elevation effect" (Anvari et al. 2023) and to attain a more stable value than a single measurement. The first two assessments were one week apart for the work-related measures and two weeks apart for the health measures, so the baseline value describes status during the first one to two weeks. Since the baseline value is also the first time point of the growth models, the baseline value and the estimated slope share this information. Summary statistics and histograms of baseline values are summarized under Additional materials.

Statistical models of return to work

We chose to model the probability of RTW using both cross-sectional and longitudinal models.

Cross-sectional models used data from 3, 6, and 12 months. The 3-month assessment is the last point of the period in which improvement was classified, so associations with RTW at 3 months are concurrent with improvement. Associations at 6 and 12 months are prospective but may reflect work resumed during the improvement period. Since the categorical variable "RTW-SE improved" had quasi-separation at one time point, a regularizing model was needed. The crosssectional analyses were modeled with Bayesian logistic regression with a regularized horseshoe prior (Piironen and Vehtari 2017) using the R package `brms` (Bürkner 2021). The regularizing horseshoe prior imposes shrinkage towards zero on predictor coefficients, except in cases where a predictor exerts a substantial influence on the outcome. In such instances, the prior has little impact. For all focal predictors in all models, we report posterior medians with 95% equal-tailed credible intervals (CrI). Equal-tailed intervals were used since they remain valid when coefficients are transformed into odds ratios (OR) or probabilities. We also report P, the posterior probability that an association is in the expected direction (a higher probability of RTW, or fewer NSAD). P is not a frequentist p-value. It is the probability, given the data and the model, that the association has the expected direction, so values close to 1 indicate strong support for that direction and values close to .5 indicate no support for either direction.

For the longitudinal analysis of RTW, a Bayesian mixed logistic model with random intercepts and slopes was fitted (Barr et al. 2013) to the assessments at 3, 6, 9 and 12 months. All covariates except time had a regularized horseshoe prior, with the prior guess for the number of relevant variables set to half of the number of predictor variables, as in the crosssectional models.

We report the average probability of RTW at each assessment, and for each focal predictor the average difference in the probability of RTW (risk difference, RD), in percentage points (pp), over the 3 to 12 month period and at each assessment. The model has no interaction between the focal predictor and time, so the conditional OR is constant over time, while the difference in probability varies because the probability of RTW changes over time.

Each improvement model included the improvement indicator for one focal predictor together with the baseline values of all seven focal predictors and the covariates, so that each improvement association is adjusted for starting level. Improvement in the other focal predictors was not included, since it occurred during the same period as the focal improvement and is not a baseline confounder. For the same reason, the baseline models included the baseline values of all seven focal predictors and the covariates but no improvement indicators, since improvement may lie on the path from baseline status to the outcome. The two model sets thus address separate questions: whether status at entry is associated with later work outcomes, and whether early improvement is associated with them beyond status at entry. The same adjustment sets were used for RTW and NSAD. Effects are estimated using g-computation (Robins 1986; Snowden et al. 2011; Chatton and Rohrer 2024), as implemented in the `marginaleffects` package (Arel-Bundock et al. 2024). Each participant's predicted outcome is computed with their own covariate values, and in the longitudinal models their own estimated random effects, under each of two values of the focal predictor: improved and not improved, or for baseline values the 25th and 75th percentile. The difference between the two predictions is then averaged over the participants in the data. All figures and tables use this procedure. All baseline covariates using ordered response categories were treated as ordinal monotonic predictors in the statistical models (Bürkner and Charpentier 2020).

Statistical models of net sickness absence days

Net Sickness Absence Days (NSAD) is a bounded variable with limits defined by the observation period. Within each quarter, possible values for NSAD range from 0 to 90 days.

Figure 2 displays the observed distribution of NSAD in each quarter, where the rightmost column in each panel represents the upper bound. In each quarter, many participants had 0 days and some had all 90 days. We therefore used ordered beta regression (Kubinec 2023), which models bounded outcomes including values at both bounds. The R package `ordbetareg` (Kubinec 2025) implements ordered beta regression using the `brms` package (Bürkner 2017).

NSAD was divided by 90, and the default normal prior on the coefficients was replaced by the regularized horseshoe prior used for the RTW models.

Separate statistical models were fitted for each quarter. For months 4-6, 7-9 and 10-12, the improvement and baseline models used the same adjustment sets as the RTW models.

For months 1-3, only the baseline models were fitted, since baseline scores precede that period while improvement was classified during it. R code with model specifications is included in the Additional materials. Effects were estimated by g-computation as for RTW and are reported as average differences in days per quarter. The NSAD analyses include participants with registered NSAD during the year (see Results), and therefore represent participants whose sickness absence was compensated by the Swedish Social Insurance Agency.

All models' R² values and Root Mean Squared Errors (RMSE) were calculated using the R package `performance` (Lüdecke et al. 2021). For the longitudinal models, marginal R² includes the fixed effects only and conditional R² both the fixed and the random effects.

Convergence was assessed by R-hat, bulk and tail effective sample size, divergent transitions and tree-depth warnings, and the reliability of approximate leave-one-out cross-validation by Pareto k (Vehtari et al. 2017). With imputation, each imputed dataset was fitted separately and diagnostics were assessed per fit, since diagnostics on the pooled posterior reflect differences between imputed datasets rather than convergence. Imputed fits with R-hat above 1.01 or more than 10 divergent transitions were refitted with a new seed, more iterations and a higher adapt_delta setting. Posterior predictive checks followed current recommendations (Säilynoja et al. 2025): for RTW, PAV-adjusted calibration plots with 95% consistency bands, and for NSAD, the checks of the `ordbetareg` package of the shares at both bounds and the distribution between them. Diagnostics for all models are reported in the Additional materials. All figures in the paper were created with package `ggplot2` (Wickham 2016).

Discussion

Key results

In this exploratory longitudinal observational study, we investigated modifiable psychological variables as predictors for RTW after sickness absence due to CMD, focusing on temporal dynamics of different variables during the early phase of the SA episode. Two different outcomes were evaluated: self-reported RTW and net sickness absence days (NSAD).

The RTW analyses showed that work-related psychological factors had the clearest associations with RTW. In the cross-sectional models, a more favourable baseline RTW-SE score and RTW-SE improvement were associated with RTW at 3 months, which is concurrent with the period in which improvement was classified. At 6 and 12 months, RTW-SE improvement at 6 months and PCS improvement at 12 months were probably positively associated with RTW, with intervals that included zero, while baseline RTW-SE showed no association. These later associations are prospective but may reflect work resumed during the improvement period. In the longitudinal models of RTW at 3 to 12 months, improvement in PCS, WPS and RTW-SE was associated with a higher probability of RTW, with RDs of 14 to 18 pp over the period, and among the baseline scores WAS had the largest association. The intervals were wide, so the size of these associations is uncertain. The 3-month RTW-SE association in particular rests on few participants without improvement who had returned to work. In contrast, no focal predictor was associated with NSAD in any quarter after the improvement period, and no baseline score was associated with NSAD in months 1-3.

Improvement and baseline scores describe different contrasts, improved versus not improved and the 75th versus the 25th percentile of the baseline score, and their relative size does not show which provides better predictions. Information on improvement is also available three months later than the baseline assessment. We therefore do not interpret these results as a comparison of predictive performance. The associations between early improvement and work status at 6 to 12 months are exploratory findings that may be of prognostic relevance.

The unconditional growth models for each of the seven focal predictor variables also led to some interesting findings. We summarized results in Table 2, where notable levels of both heterogeneity and ICC are shown. In combination with the need to use robust models to accommodate outliers, this indicates a large variation in trajectories between individuals, while the residual scatter around each individual's own trajectory was small compared with the differences between individuals. This highlights the importance of using random intercepts and slopes to correctly model heterogeneity. The heterogeneity ratio (H-ratio) is a relatively new metric that, to our knowledge, has not been previously reported in studies in this field. Bolger and colleagues (Bolger et al. 2019) recommended 0.25 as a reasonable cut-off for "notable heterogeneity" but also highlighted the need for context specific knowledge to better understand expected levels. By reporting ICC as well as regression beta coefficients and SD, we provide useful information for future power calculations and comparisons with other studies.

Interpretation and relation to previous literature

Similar findings have been reported in previous studies, where higher initial levels of RTW-SE and increasing RTW-SE were positively associated with RTW (Nieuwenhuijsen et al. 2013; Lagerveld et al. 2017; Horn et al. 2022). Among these, Lagerveld et al. (Lagerveld et al. 2017) included assessments at baseline and after one and three months. Our study adds weekly assessments during the first ten weeks, which allow individual trajectories to be estimated within the first three months. By using the average of the first two measurements our baseline measures should also be robust. In our data, improvement during the first three months was associated with later work status beyond status at entry, but this does not show that improvement is a better predictor than baseline status.

In planning the study, focal predictor variables were selected to represent two distinct domains: symptomatology and work-related psychological factors. Work-related psychological factors (RTW-SE, WPS and PCS) had the clearest associations with RTW, whereas the symptom measures did not. Work-related factors have also been associated with sickness absence in previous research (Fisker et al. 2022; Frantz et al. 2025). These findings further underscore the importance of emphasizing work-oriented processes in RTW interventions. Most participants exhibited improvements in symptom severity during the initial three-month period compared to baseline. However, neither improvement nor baseline levels of physical fatigue, cognitive weariness or worry had clear associations with RTW or NSAD in the adjusted analyses. Self-reported cognitive complaints have previously been linked to reduced odds for RTW in depression (Bergdolt et al. 2024). Future research might focus on the influence of cognitive complaints as a moderator on the association between behavioral determinants and RTW.

For WPS and PCS, our analyses show that both were associated with a higher probability of RTW over 3 to 12 months. The WPS has been described as a potentially relevant factor in RTW outcomes (Muschalla and Linden 2014; Vignoli et al. 2017). The version of WPS used here was shortened from 13 to five items, which improved its measurement properties but may cover the concept of work phobia less fully. In an RCT comparing cognitive behavioral therapy with an RTW-focused intervention, increased perceived competence mediated the greater reduction in burnout symptoms with cognitive behavioral therapy (Santoft et al. 2019).

That finding concerns burnout symptoms and not RTW or sickness absence. Worry and anxiety have been indicated as being related to non-RTW (Jones et al. 2021; Coutu et al. 2024), but GAD-4 worry showed no clear association with the outcomes in our models. These results concern the shortened scales used here, the two work outcomes and an adjustment set that included the baseline values of all focal measures. Weak associations with these outcomes do not establish low value for other clinical purposes, such as assessing symptoms or treatment needs, and any suggestion to reduce questionnaire burden applies to RTW research only.

Exploration of the temporal dynamics of the RTW-process has been requested in a previous review article (Fisker et al. 2022) showing that variables important for RTW probability after, e.g., < 3 months of sick leave are not necessarily equally important after > 12 months and vice versa. This suggests that the predictors of RTW probability vary depending on the duration of sick leave. It is important to distinguish between modifiable predictors, such as psychosocial factors, and non-modifiable variables, including sociodemographic characteristics, which remain We chose to include two distinct outcome measures: one based on participants' self-reported work participation, dichotomized into working to the full extent of one's employment or less, and another derived from registry data indicating the number of days compensated by the sickness insurance system. The findings differed between the two outcomes.

Early improvement and baseline status were associated with working to the full extent of one's employment, but not with the amount of compensated absence later in the year. Notably, a portion of the sample had no recorded sickness benefit days, possibly due to ending their sick leave within the initial 15 days or not meeting eligibility criteria for compensation. These participants were excluded from the NSAD analyses, so those results may not apply to people whose absence ended before compensation began. The excluded group differed from the others mainly in age, and was not more often working at 3 months, so an early end to sick leave does not seem to explain most of the missing register data. The dichotomized RTW variable is unable to reflect gradients of increased work participation, which may be better reflected in the NSAD variable. This highlights the importance of employing a broader set of measurement tools to capture diverse trajectories within the population. While self-reported data may offer more nuanced insights into individual-level processes, registry data are influenced by external factors such as the physician's assessment and certification, as well as the case officer's evaluation of the medical certificate and its qualification for sickness benefit. The combination of highfrequency measurements and self-reported data enabled the identification of intermittent patterns in sickness absence, revealing both "on" and "off" statuses over time. While this provides valuable insight into the temporal dynamics of absence, it also presents methodological challenges related to data interpretation and classification.

Methodological considerations

Net sickness absence days (NSAD) is a bounded variable, with lower and upper limits given by the observation period. In our data, many participants were at the lower bound and some at the upper bound in each quarter. Ordered beta regression (Kubinec 2023) accommodates values at both bounds, and posterior predictive checks were used to assess how well the models reproduced the shares at both bounds and the distribution between them (Additional materials).

Two different types of data were used to assess RTW. The method section provides a thorough description of the statistical analyses used and the reasons for each decision. Together with the model specifications provided in the Additional materials, this increases transparency and the possibility of replication in future studies. Other researchers can both criticize and perhaps learn from our chosen methods. While we do not assert that the assumptions for interpreting results from g-computation as causal effects are fulfilled, the method allows us to present results in an easily interpretable way on the scale of the outcome variable (probability of RTW and number of days). This method also reduces risk of the "Table 2 fallacy" (Westreich and Greenland 2013).

Limitations

It is important to note that while we are interested in causal relationships between predictor and outcome variables, our non-randomized uncontrolled data does not allow us to make strong causal claims. In particular, the associations do not show that producing improvement in a focal predictor would increase work participation.

The chosen analysis strategy was exploratory rather than theory driven, as we included a large number of predictor variables and used data-driven methods to examine the associations between multiple variables and the outcomes. A stronger causal inference methodology would include drawing causal diagrams, often referred to as directed acyclical graphs (DAGs, (Shrier and Platt 2008; Rohrer 2018), which illustrate á priori assumptions about causal relationships between variables used to determine appropriate adjustment sets for each pair of outcome and focal predictor.

Our question concerned being at work at each assessment, for which models of work status are appropriate. Time-to-event models address the timing of transitions into or out of work, which is a different question. Models for recurrent events, for example time-to-event models that can handle multiple event occurrences with different durations (Amorim and Cai 2015), could be used to study such transitions.

Including only baseline measurements of the focal predictor variables rather than using them as time-varying covariates limits the possibility of understanding co-variation between variables across time.

The large heterogeneity of change in the focal predictor variables between individuals during the first 3 months of the study was perhaps coarsely captured by the "improved" or "not improved" dichotomous variable created. Further investigation of this heterogeneity and how it relates to outcomes such as RTW is warranted. A statistical modeling method that could have been used is to allow predictor variables to vary across time rather than using only the baseline measurements and the categories (improved/not improved), or the categorization could have used more than two levels. We did not conduct any sensitivity analyses regarding the amount of change occurring during shorter time spans. The improvement categories were treated as fixed in the outcome models, so the reported intervals do not include the uncertainty in the classification and are likely too narrow, and misclassification near the threshold would tend to attenuate the associations. The baseline value and the estimated slope share the first time point. Improvement was classified during the first three months, so associations with RTW at 3 months cannot establish whether improvement preceded work resumption or followed it.

Missing covariates and work status were handled with multiple imputation, which assumes that missingness depends only on observed variables. Complete-case analyses gave similar but somewhat smaller estimates for RTW and similar estimates for NSAD.

As previously noted, for 13 participants the diagnosis stated in the sickness certificate was not one of the accepted diagnoses for inclusion. This discrepancy may be due to the nature of the registry data only reporting the initial main diagnosis. Secondary diagnoses in the certificates are not shown in register data. Since diagnostic eligibility was based on self-report and the certificates were not collected, we cannot rule out that some participants did not have an eligible diagnosis.

Generalizability

These results apply to people with CMD who were recruited within six weeks of the start of sick leave and followed for 12 months, and may not apply to people already on long-term sick leave when first assessed. Participants were self-selected through advertisements and were treated in different settings, mainly primary care, which may limit generalizability to other recruitment routes and settings. The results are relevant for Sweden and other countries with similar health care and insurance systems. Only small proportions of the included participants were male, which may limit the generalizability to men. The reported analyses could inform future studies in terms of power analysis and setting priors in Bayesian statistical models.

Conclusions

Improvement in work-related psychological factors during the first three months was associated with a higher probability of working to the full extent of one's employment during the following year, while symptom measures did not show a significant relationship with RTW. None of the focal predictors was associated with compensated sickness absence days. Our findings suggest that interventions aiming to support RTW should target work-related factors and monitor their development, especially during the early phase of sick-leave. Whether strengthening these factors can support RTW is a question for further study.
