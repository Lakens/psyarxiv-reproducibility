Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Modern models of preferential decision making have achieved substantial success in predicting choice behaviour both qualitatively and quantitatively, but often overlook related aspects such as response time or confidence. In parallel, recently proposed mental sampling frameworks have offered broad explanations for multiple aspects of behaviour including probability judgments, perceptual estimates and numerical forecasts, but have not yet been applied to preferential choice. Here, we seek to unite these lines of research by extending one such framework, the Autocorrelated Bayesian Sampler (ABS; Zhu et al., 2024), to decisions between alternatives which differ in the value of their potential outcomes. We present a new version of the ABS adapted to such decisions, the Autocorrelated Bayesian Decision Maker (ABDM), able to predict not just choices, but also reaction times and confidence in selections.

We compare the predictions of the ABDM against extant choice and response time data in risky choice tasks, finding strong correspondence in both. We also contrast the new model against leading explanations for behaviour in such tasks, showing the ABDM is competitive in choice and ahead in response times. We thus propose this model as a broader approach to decision making capable of explaining multiple measures from a common process.

The Autocorrelated Bayesian Decision Maker: Mental Sampling Processes Capture Preference, Confidence and Response Time in Risky Choice

Introduction

We often must make decisions between alternatives where an objectively superior option is difficult to define or entirely absent: for example, which meal to order at an unfamiliar restaurant, whether a new phone's features are worth the price, or whether a new medication will cure a symptom. Determining how we make choices in such scenarios is thus of vital importance both to deepen our understanding of human behaviour as well as to help guide people toward better decisions.

Naturally, then, many potential theories have been offered to capture how people make such choices, with almost 100 decision making models proposed over the last 70 years (He et al., 2022). In many cases, these theories have been designed to accommodate observed behavioural patterns which violate earlier models: expected utility theory offers reasons for violations of expected value via subjective transformations of value (Bernoulli, 1954), prospect theory offers reasons for violations of expected utility via reweighting of probabilities (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979), cumulative prospect theory offers reasons for violations of prospect theory via distinct accumulation processes for gains and losses (Tversky & Kahneman, 1992), and so on. The result of this has been a series of models steadily improving in their ability to capture choice behaviour, both in terms of reducing quantitative error between predicted and empirical choice rates and matching qualitative choice phenomena (Bhatia, 2014; Birnbaum, 2008; Erev et al., 2017; Kellen et al., 2020). This is particularly evident in recent models leveraging machine learning techniques to provide high predictive accuracy in large-scale data (Peterson et al., 2021; Plonsky et al., 2025).

At the same time, the overarching focus on choice within these models has arguably led to a deep but possibly narrow understanding of decision making: modern models certainly offer strong performance in predicting choices, but predictions of related aspects such as decision confidence or time are rarer, while others such as estimates and valuations of prospects are often entirely distinct. Such a focus is perhaps understandable given that choices are ultimately the main consequential aspect of decision making, but these other responses can themselves offer insight into the choice process. A key example is the time taken to make a decision: more difficult choices often take longer, providing an indication of the time course of the underlying cognitive systems (Ayal & Hochman, 2009; Glöckner & Betsch, 2008).

Consideration of these measures can thus offer a more complete understanding of choice behaviour via the relations between choice and other response types, which risks being overlooked in models strictly focused on predicting selections.

A contrasting approach to this line of work is to attempt to provide a more general model of behaviour which includes decision making as one of the many actions a person can take. This principle underlies the Autocorrelated Bayesian Sampler (ABS), a general purpose cognitive framework that can be applied to a wide range of potential tasks (Zhu et al., 2024).

The ABS proposes that much of behaviour may be based on a consistent underlying mental sampling process in which agents repeatedly simulate possible cases in their mind and use these simulations to guide responses: for example, when faced with a simple perceptual stimulus like a field of dots, people may iteratively imagine a series of potential counts of the actual number. This set of samples can then be used in various ways to answer questions on the original stimulus: the mean of the samples provides an estimate of the target value, the relative proportion of samples above or below a boundary value (e.g., higher vs. lower than 25) can act as a judgment of the probability of that classification, and the number of samples needed to make a classification can predict the response time. Importantly, this means that samples collected for one query can be reused for another, creating links between responses which may be missed by narrower models focusing on one response at a time. This trades on the flexibility of mental sampling as a cognitive process: simulated samples offer rich sources of information able to answer multiple queries from a common source, while the generality of sampling allows for applications to a broad range of domains.

The ABS draws upon a series of recent studies offering sampling-based explanations for observed behaviours in varying tasks, including perceptual decisions, probability judgments and numerical estimates (Dasgupta et al., 2017; Lieder, Griffiths, Huys, et al., 2018; Spicer et al., 2022 Spicer et al., , 2024;; Zhu et al., 2020). The findings of this work inform the specific sampling algorithm at the core of the ABS: for example, samples are assumed to be autocorrelated due to common violations of independence in human response series (Castillo et al., 2024; Castillo et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2022), and samples are moderated by a prior on beliefs to avoid overly polarised judgments when relying on small sample sizes (Sundh et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2020). More generally, the ABS uses the consistency of these patterns between tasks to propose a common underlying psychological system supporting varying aspects of behaviour from low-level perception to high-level reasoning.

There is however a key caveat to this model: the ABS uses sampling to approximate the probability of particular responses within an individual's mental distribution, but does not consider how these responses may differ in their value. This may be sufficient in simple objective cases where the decision maker need only consider the accuracy of their choice (as in the field of dots example above), but may not contend well when the available alternatives offer substantially different rewards: for example, a monetary gamble may have a low probability of paying out, but if the potential win is high enough, it may still be worth playing.

As such, despite the breadth of its framework, the ABS is not well suited to capturing preferential choice in its current implementation. This is not to say that sampling cannot be applied to such choices: in fact, mental sampling models have become increasingly prominent in recent decision making research.

Key examples include Decision Field Theory which uses sampling to capture the allocation of attention to potential outcomes (Bhatia, 2014; Busemeyer & Townsend, 1993), 'Decision by Sampling' approaches which draw samples from memory to determine the relative rank of an outcome within the decision maker's psychological distribution (Noguchi & Stewart, 2018; Stewart et al., 2006), the Best Estimate and Sampling Tools (BEAST) model which combines biased sampling mechanisms with expected value when evaluating options (Erev et al., 2017), and Utility Weighted Sampling which prioritises sampling of outcomes which would have the greatest impact on the decision maker (Lieder, Griffiths, & Hsu, 2018). Much like the ABS, these models assume decisions are driven by mental simulation, in this case generating the potential outcomes of a particular choice in the mind and selecting according to the results of these simulations. As previously referenced, however, these models principally focus on choices, with little to no consideration of related behaviours such as confidence or valuation.

There thus remains a gulf between even these sampling models of decision making and the broader sampling framework of the ABS, preventing a more unified description of choice behaviour.

In this paper, we seek to bridge this gulf by extending the ABS to preferential choice, connecting the existing framework with established models of value-based decision making to create an even broader depiction of behaviour. We suggest that such a combination offers benefits for both individual lines of work: expanding the ABS to such choices not only increases the scope of this particular model, but also provides a more general account of decision making, including potential relationships between response types overlooked by existing choice-focused models. In the following sections, we first introduce the current sampling framework of the ABS, then describe how this can be extended to preferential choice scenarios. We then assess the adequacy of this model by comparing its predictions to available data, as well as contrasting its performance with other leading models of decision making.

Crucially, we examine the model's predictions on multiple response types (choice, response time and confidence) to assess its breadth of coverage, drawing all measures from a single common posterior distribution. Finally, we close with discussion of the implications of this newly developed model for both theories of preferential choice and sampling approaches to cognition.

The Autocorrelated Bayesian Sampler

The Autocorrelated Bayesian Sampler (ABS) proposes a consistent rational framework underlying a number of human behavioural responses (Zhu et al., 2024). The ABS approximates full Bayesian inference via a sequential sampling process in which samples are drawn from an internal psychological distribution over potential hypotheses. These samples are assumed to be able to answer multiple potential queries, including estimates, probability judgments and basic decisions between alternatives (for example, whether a particular target value is higher or lower than some boundary). In the case of such decisions, each sample is treated as evidence for one particular selection, with an accumulation process aggregating this evidence as more samples are taken. This results in a posterior distribution approximating the probability of each choice within the psychological distribution. We shall first detail the current process of the ABS to provide a foundation for its modification for value-based choices, separating the model into three distinct components for clarity: the sampling space, the autocorrelated sampling algorithm, and the evidence accumulator.

The Sampling Space

Before beginning sampling, it is first necessary to define the space from which samples will be drawn. In the existing definition of the ABS, this is a psychological distribution within the mind reflecting the probability of particular states of the world according to that individual's mental representation. The definition of this distribution is purposefully left open to allow the framework of the ABS to be applicable to multiple potential tasks, scenarios and queries: states could reflect perceptual properties of a given stimulus (e.g., the number of dots on a screen), empirical distributions of particular values (e.g., the ages of a group of people) or inferred beliefs (e.g., the future price of a financial asset). Similarly, the source of this psychological distribution is deliberately undefined within the ABS as the model is only concerned with how the mental representation is used once it has been acquired.

The Autocorrelated Sampling Algorithm: MC 3

As its name suggests, a key tenet of the ABS is that samples are not independent of one another but are autocorrelated, with each new sample being related to its predecessors.

For this purpose, the ABS uses a particular sampling algorithm named Metropolis-Coupled Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MC 3 ) in which multiple sampling chains run in parallel, differing in their propensity to explore the psychological distribution. On each timestep, each chain proposes a new potential sample given its most recent sample, and probabilistically decides whether to accept or reject this proposal. This can itself be divided into separate subcomponents: the starting point of the sampler, the proposal function determining new values, and the swapping of chains as a distinct exploratory aspect of the MC 3 algorithm.

Starting Point

Because the autocorrelated sampling algorithm proposes similar values to the preceding sample, sequences are inherently biased towards their starting point under a limited number of samples as there is less potential for movement away from this initial value. As such, the setting of this value can substantially impact the resulting series of samples, and therefore any decisions based on them. A basic setting for this value is simply to draw an initial random sample from the space, meaning any starting point bias is eliminated across simulations. One notable alternative to this however is to set the starting point to prominent values offered by the query (for example, a boundary between responses), as in this case the resulting starting point bias offers an explanation for anchoring effects where estimates are drawn towards presented values (Lieder, Griffiths, Huys, et al., 2018; Spicer et al., 2022).

While the multiple chains of MC 3 allow for greater exploration of the sampling space, the sampler ultimately only produces one key sample on each timestep, taken from the undistorted chain. In order to make use of this explorative ability, the sampler compares the values produced by each chain and probabilistically determines whether to swap their positions using a similar decision rule to that determining sample acceptance above. This then allows for larger movements of the undistorted chain where a higher temperature chain discovers a high probability region and these positions are exchanged.

Evidence Accumulator

In order to make a choice, samples are converted into evidence towards a potential selection, with an accumulator aggregating this evidence as the sampler progresses. Samples are thus converted into single units of evidence for the prospect favoured in that sample (e.g., 'higher' or 'lower'), essentially counting the number of 'wins' for that option across the sequence. This is a purely ordinal comparison with no consideration of magnitude: for example, a sample of 26 dots and a sample of 30 dots are equally supportive of the 'higher than 25' response. This results in a posterior distribution approximating the probability of each response within the psychological distribution, being either a Beta distribution for binary decisions or a Dirichlet distribution for multialternative decisions: where p(h i |s) is the probability of response i given samples s and the β parameters reflect the counts of samples supporting each of k possible responses.

Prior on Responses

While the evidence accumulator tracks the output of the sampling algorithm, it is also initialised with a prior to capture pre-existing expectations on the likelihood of each response before sampling begins. These priors can be viewed as 'pseudocounts' of assumed observations before actual sampling begins, with their relative proportion reflecting pre-existing expectations of the likelihood of each considered response. This prevents extreme polarization of the evidence accumulator when only a few samples have been taken: for example, after a single sample, all evidence will naturally favour one response, which may lead to overconfidence that this response is correct (Zhu et al., 2020). This prior can then be adapted according to experience with the task, for instance favouring a response which has occurred more frequently in past trials.

Stopping Rule

The stopping rule determines whether sufficient evidence has been accumulated to cease sampling and make a selection. As the accumulator tracks wins against losses, the stopping rule is defined by a fixed threshold measuring the difference in evidence count between options (or between the top two options in multialternative cases), with this threshold being an additional free parameter of the model. Despite the relative simplicity of this rule, such a heuristic offers a reasonable approximation of the optimal stopping rule in such decisions (Dragalin et al., 1999 (Dragalin et al., , 2000)), and has been suggested to be used by actual learners (Brown et al., 2009).

In addition to this choice, the set of collected samples also predicts both the time taken to reach this decision and confidence in the selection. Decision Making in the ABS

Decisions in the current ABS are thus based on an approximation of that option's probability within the internal distribution, where higher sample counts provide better approximations but are restricted by the optional stopping rule. As previously noted, this suffices for basic perceptual or cognitive tasks where the available options carry equivalent values as decisions need only consider the expected accuracy of selecting the most likely alternative. Such a definition fails however to account for cases in which the available options carry differing values, meaning the ABS is currently unable to effectively simulate preferential choice tasks.

Defining the Task: Risky Choice

Before attempting to apply the ABS to value-based decision, we should first specify the format of the choices which it must solve as this will determine some aspects of the new definition. For this purpose, we use the risky choice paradigm in which decision makers must choose between options varying in both monetary outcomes and the probability of receiving those outcomes (e.g. 100% chance of £10 vs. 50% of £20, 50% of £0). We shall initially focus on scenarios in which both the possible outcomes and their associated probabilities are provided ahead of the choice (so-called 'decisions from description') rather than being learned through observation ('decisions from experience'), though we will return to the influence of experience later. We shall also focus exclusively on choices between two risky prospects rather than multialternative choice as this is the more common paradigm in previous work.

Such choices have been a focus of extensive work in decision making research, commonly using behavioural patterns in these tasks to identify underlying systems and discriminate between potential decision models (Bhatia, 2014; Birnbaum, 2008; Busemeyer & Townsend, 1993; Erev et al., 2017; Glöckner & Pachur, 2012; Kellen et al., 2020; Tversky & Kahneman, 1992; Zhao et al., 2020). In addition, the probabilistic nature of these choices offers a closer match to the existing probabilistic nature of the ABS, allowing for easier application.

The Autocorrelated Bayesian Decision Maker

Having set the task, we next offer a new definition of the ABS adapted to risky decisions. We name this the Autocorrelated Bayesian Decision Maker (ABDM) to distinguish this new definition from its predecessor, though we view this as an extension of the same underlying sampling framework rather than an entirely separate model. The ABDM samples the potential outcomes of an actual choice, with an accumulator tracking the evidence towards each selection as more samples are collected. This follows the approach of many existing mental sampling models treating samples as mental simulations of possible outcomes (Bhatia, 2014; Busemeyer & Townsend, 1993; Erev et al., 2017; Lieder, Griffiths, & Hsu, 2018). Such a change does however require alterations to many of the components of the ABS outlined above, including the addition of new model functions and parameters. In some cases, these changes are intended to offer greater flexibility in model behaviour, allowing for interpolation between specific model settings rather than static rules. The following section thus redefines the components of the model, noting any changes from the previous definition. Creating a Joint Event Space

To provide a singular distribution for sampling, the ABDM uses a joint event space across options, as in Busemeyer and Townsend (1993) and Bhatia (2014), essentially sampling differences between outcomes rather than separate samples for each prospect (see Figure 1D).

Such a representation of the sampling space is also important for capturing scenarios where outcomes are correlated and so must be considered as pairs (Diederich & Busemeyer, 1999).

Events are defined by the difference in utility between each possible pairing of outcomes from the two gambles. Where gambles are uncorrelated, the probability of each event is simply the product of the probability of its component outcomes. For correlated gambles, event probabilities are determined by aligning the inverse cumulative density functions of each gamble and identifying the density within each unique pair of outcomes.

The Autocorrelated Sampling Algorithm

While the core of the ABDM remains the MC 3 sampling algorithm, two adjustments were made to this process to suit the risky choice task: first, we consider several options for the starting point, treating this as an additional model parameter; and second, we replace the continuous proposal function with a discrete function better suited to the specific outcomes of a particular risky choice. Starting Point

We consider four potential starting rules for the sampler: first, the max rule begins at the event which reflects the highest absolute outcome of each gamble, following the suggestion that decision makers should prioritise consideration of the most impactful events in a set, whether positive or negative (Lieder, Griffiths, & Hsu, 2018). Second, the min rule begins at the lowest absolute outcomes of each gamble, assuming that the initial expectation of any choice is that closest to no change, reflecting a form of status-quo bias (Johnson et al., 2007; Zhao et al., 2020). Third, the most likely rule begins at the event with the highest probability given that this is most likely to occur (Azab et al., 2016; Falbén et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026). Fourth, the random rule simply selects one of the possible events at random under uniform probability. For simplicity, we use a common starting rule for all chains within the sampler, meaning each chain in a given simulated choice begins at the same point even under the random rule.

Proposal Function

As the sampling space is now divided into discrete events, the proposal function is here defined using a Markov transition matrix where the probability of proposing each event is determined by the similarity between that event and the most recent sample (see Figure 2B).

Evidence Accumulator

For simplicity, samples are again converted into single units of evidence for the prospect with the better outcome in that sample. This does mean however that evidence is based primarily on the probability of each gamble outperforming the other with no consideration of the degree of difference in value. As such, we include an additional parameter here determining how often this conversion is performed: rather than converting each individual sample into evidence, conversion instead occurs every n steps using the mean of the last n samples (illustrated in Figure 3), with this count being a free parameter. This then allows the ABDM to approximate the expected difference between options, with n controlling the fidelity of this approximation: high ns provide more accurate estimates leading the model to tend towards expected utility, while low ns continue to focus on the probability of advantage as in the original ABS.

Prior on Responses

As in the previous definition of the ABS, the evidence accumulator is initialised with a Beta prior to capture pre-existing expectations on the likelihood of each response, fitting with the continued accumulation of discrete units of evidence for each choice. This is initially fixed and uniform across options for all choices to reflect no pre-existing bias, but can be updated as experience with these options is gathered.

Stopping Rule

As the accumulator continues to track wins vs. losses, the stopping rule is here again defined by a fixed threshold measuring the difference in evidence count between options, with this threshold being an additional free parameter of the model. For practical purposes, we also include an absolute limit of 1000 total samples to avoid indefinite sampling where evidence is indecisive; if this limit is reached, the model selects the option with the higher win count at that point regardless of the threshold, or selects randomly if counts are equal.

The final set of samples can then be used to direct choice according to the maximum of the posterior distribution as in the ABS. This also includes predictions of response time based on the final sample count (following Equation 7) and confidence based on the posterior mean (following Equation 8).

Additional Noise

As with many other models, there remains the possibility that the above processes are not perfectly executed, leading to additional noise beyond the stochasticity inherent within the sampling algorithm. The ABDM includes two sources of such noise: within the accumulator, and when selecting responses. Accumulator noise reflects errors when converting samples to evidence, captured by adding Gaussian noise to the average over recent samples, with the variance of this Gaussian being a further free parameter: meaning evidence is more likely to be miscalculated where the mean of the sampled values is closer to 0.

Response noise reflects errors when making the final selection such as typing errors, captured by a fixed probability of choosing the alternative gamble to that favoured by the sampler. Following previous model definitions (Erev et al., 2017), accumulator noise is not included for dominated choices where one option is clearly superior to the other, but response noise is included in these cases as this is independent of actual values.

Competing Models

To evaluate the predictive ability of the new ABDM, we must compare it against current leading theories from the literature. We therefore selected four competing models to provide a contrast with the ABDM, chosen to reflect key models of decision making proposed in previous work, including existing sequential sampling models. We next briefly introduce these models before comparing their performance with the ABDM.

BEAST

'Best Estimate and Sampling Tools' (BEAST) is an alternate sequential sampling model aiming to capture the qualitative choice phenomena in both description and experience (Erev et al., 2017). In brief, BEAST also offers a sampling explanation for choice behaviour, but with several notable differences from the ABDM: first, BEAST switches between four sampling methods within its sampling process, each reflecting particular behavioural biases (unbiased sampling from the true probabilities, uniform sampling reflecting equal weighting, focus on the lowest available outcome to capture pessimism, and focusing solely on the sign of outcomes). Fourth, BEAST resamples for every trial: feedback is instead incorporated into the unbiased sampling tool, using the store of observed outcomes as a source for mental samples in place of the listed probabilities. As the number of observations grows, proportions within this store will eventually converge to the true probabilities, though greater discrepancies will appear in earlier decisions. BEAST also increases its likelihood of using the unbiased tool as the amount of feedback increases (see Equation 16), meaning later choices will more closely follow objective probabilities. BEAST offers a valuable standard for model evaluation as this provided strong performance in the comparison of Erev et al. (2017), with even the better performing models in that competition using BEAST as a foundation.

Model Evaluation Choice

We next evaluated the predictive ability of the ABDM by fitting the model to empirical data, beginning with choices themselves. This also includes comparison against the other candidate models to measure the ABDM's relative performance against current leading descriptions of choice behaviour.

Method

Dataset

We assessed the performance of the candidate models by simulating choices on a set of 150 risky choice problems taken from Erev et al. (2017). We use this dataset for model evaluation as it provides empirical choice rates for several major choice phenomena from a common participant sample: 30 of the problems capture prominent behavioural effects observed in previous decision making research, reflecting common criteria for model assessment in this field (Bhatia, 2014; Birnbaum, 2008; Busemeyer & Townsend, 1993; Erev et al., 2017; Kellen et al., 2020; Tversky & Kahneman, 1992). The remaining 120 problems meanwhile provide a generalisation to a wider set of choices for a broader perspective of choice behaviour. Another advantage of this data set is that problems were answered both with and without feedback: each choice is repeated 25 times, with feedback on actual outcomes from each gamble beginning on the sixth trial. Choice rates are thus separated into five blocks, each collecting five trials, where block 1 represents choices from description alone and subsequent blocks reflect increasing amounts of experience. This then allows for examination of behaviour in both description and experience, and the transition between.

Model Criteria

Model performance was assessed both qualitatively and quantitatively: qualitative fit was measured according to the model's ability to produce the choice phenomena targeted in the gamble set, such as preference for certainty in gains but not losses, aversion to risk and losses, and differences in choice when outcomes are split into separate components. Each effect is defined by choices between monetary gambles, with model targets being set according to empirical choice rates across participants, either reflecting preferences for particular options, or preference patterns between two related choices. Effects are thus measured at the aggregate level rather than the individual level based on whether the majority of decision makers select a particular option. 14 total effects are included in the gamble set, with 10 occurring in description and 4 occurring in experience. Descriptions of these effects are given in Table A1.

Quantitative fit meanwhile was assessed using numerical measures of the difference between empirical and simulated choice rates. We use two quantitative fit metrics: first, we examine the mean squared deviation (MSD) between the average choice rate across model simulations and the empirical choice rate across participants, collapsed across problems, matching the metric used by Erev et al. (2017). Second, we calculate the marginal log likelihood (MLL) of the model by determining the probability of each choice made by each participant and averaging across parameters, allowing for individual level fitting. We use marginal likelihoods as opposed to maximum estimates to control for the size of the parameter space within the likelihood itself, meaning no further adjustments for model complexity are required.

Modelling Procedure

The candidate models were simulated by sampling 100 independent parameter sets from their prior distributions (listed in Appendix B) and using these to predict selections in each of the 150 target problems. To allow fitting of individual participants, choice probabilities were defined for each problem for a given parameter set; for the sampling models (ABDM, BEAST and DFT), this was done by repeating simulations 100 times and taking the proportion selecting an option as its likelihood, thus reflecting the inherent stochasticity of the sampling algorithm. This also included response noise described above for these models to prevent likelihoods of 0 where all simulations favoured one option. For CPT and TAX, this used the softmax function from Equation 23.

Following the task format of Erev et al. (2017), the models predicted a sequence of 25 choice probabilities for each gamble pair at each parameter set: for the ABDM, the full sampling process produced predictions for the first trial, and subsequent trials were predicted by updating the results of this initial trial according to received feedback as described in Equation 15. To further support individual-level fitting, we updated the ABDM's predictions using the actual feedback sequence viewed by each participant to capture their exact experience in the task. BEAST resampled on each trial to allow its increasing use of feedback (see Equation 16). CPT, DFT and TAX meanwhile all offer no method to incorporate feedback information and so were only simulated for the first trial of each gamble pair, with subsequent trials assuming the same choice probability. CPT, DFT and TAX also have no method to account for ambiguous scenarios where explicit probabilities are not given, though in this case these models are completely unable to make predictions. As such, we assume in these cases that these models predict indifference (i.e., choice probabilities are 0.5). We use this assumption to maintain equal numbers of empirical observations for each model to aid comparison of likelihoods, though we also examine model performance when excluding ambiguous cases in Appendix C as a robustness check.

Once calculated, these probabilities were compared against each participant's choices to determine likelihoods, which were then aggregated across trials to produce their likelihood at that parameter set and subsequently marginalised across simulations. Individual-level marginal likelihoods were then logged and summed across participants to produce an overall measure of model performance.

To calculate MSD, we produced posterior predictive choice rates for each participant: individual-level likelihoods were normalised across simulations to produce posterior distributions for each participant, which were then used to weight the predicted choice rates of each simulation to create posterior predictive rates for each individual for each model.

We then averaged these posterior predictive rates across participants to produce group-level predictions. As in Erev et al. (2017), predicted rates were also averaged in blocks of 5 trials before being compared to equivalent empirical measures. We then took the mean squared deviation between the simulated and empirical rates as a general measure of model error.

Response Time

As previously noted, a particular advantage of the ABS framework is that it produces multiple response types from a consistent sampling process (Zhu et al., 2024). This strength is inherited by the new version of the model: in addition to predicting choice, the ABDM also predicts the time taken to make that choice, as dictated by the number of samples drawn before the accumulator passes its threshold parameter. This uses the same Erlang distribution of response time given in Equation 6: by assuming a particular sampling rate λ, the ABDM can also simulate RTs alongside predicted choices. What is more, this means the ABDM can predict decision times even without directly fitting equivalent empirical data, instead inferring these times directly from choice simulations.

The competing models meanwhile vary in their ability to predict choice response times. As noted above, DFT also assumes response times reflect the number of samples taken to reach the threshold, meaning this count can be used to estimate RT by assuming a particular sample rate. A similar process can be applied to BEAST: while this model was not intended to produce response times, as this also assumes a mental sampling process, the sample count can again be used to predict response times under a given sampling rate. Unlike the ABDM and DFT, however, BEAST assumes this sample count is fixed for all trials and gambles for a particular decision maker, meaning variations can only emerge between individuals. In contrast, CPT and TAX do not natively offer any predictions of response time, providing only valuations of each option. As such, to allow these models to compete on this measure, we supplemented them using Drift Diffusion Models (DDMs) translating choice parameters to predicted response times: this converts model valuations into a 'drift' term reflecting the tendency towards a particular selection which can be used to estimate the expected time to reach a threshold for choice (Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008; Ratcliff et al., 2016). Comparing the models on their correspondence to empirical response time data thus offers an alternate lens to evaluate their performance, supplementing our previous model comparison results.

Mean RT across all participants and trials was 1.35s (SD = 4.19), though due to the repetition of choice within the task, RTs are substantially slower in the first trial of each pair (M = 11.9s, SD = 15.1s) than subsequent choices (M = 0.91s, SD = 2.00). As a precautionary measure, we excluded any response times below 0.2s or above 60s as these are unlikely to fairly reflect the time course of the related decision process, removing 340 (<0.1%) data points.

Modelling Procedure

Rather than directly fitting the candidate models to empirical response time data, we instead predicted RTs from the models' posterior distributions after fitting them to choices; this acts as a form of generalisation test in which models are fit to one task and then tested in another to prevent overfitting (Busemeyer & Wang, 2000), though in this case this uses different measures from the same task rather than entirely separate designs. This includes the supplementary DDMs added to CPT and TAX: to avoid altering the base behaviour of these models, we only use DDMs to map choice rates to RTs rather than directly fitting DDM parameters. Full details on this procedure are given in Appendix D.

The candidate models were applied to the choice problems of Plonsky et al. (2025) using the same fitting process described above to provide predicted decision rates and model likelihoods for each participant. RT estimates were then derived from these fitted simulations using mixed-model regressions predicting empirical RTs according to model-specific factors reflecting their assumptions on the decision process: for the ABDM, BEAST and DFT, this used the posterior mean number of samples drawn for each problem, meaning the fitted coefficient estimates the time taken to produce each sample. For CPT and TAX, this used the estimated time from the supplementary DDM, translating the arbitrary units of the diffusion process to meaningful time. In addition, to capture the time needed to read the available options when first presented, all regressions included the number of joint outcomes in that gamble pair as a predictor, reflecting the complexity of that choice problem; this was assumed to only impact the first trial of each pair, with the count for subsequent trials set to zero.

Regressions also adjusted their predictors according to the assumed time course of each model: as the ABDM assumes sampling only occurs in the first trial for a particular gamble pair, sample count was set to 0 for all subsequent trials. In contrast, BEAST and DFT assume sample counts are stable across repetitions and so use the same count for all trials, though BEAST also assumes sample count is stable across problems for a given individual.

The DDM estimates used by CPT and TAX were also assumed to be stable across trials but could vary between problems and individuals. All regressions included random intercepts and slopes for the considered predictors to capture individual differences in pace.

Confidence

In addition to predicting the time taken to make a decision, the ABDM also inherits its predecessor's ability to predict the decision maker's confidence in their selection. This follows the ABDM's use of sampling to approximate the probability that an option is better than its alternative, captured by the posterior distribution produced after sampling is terminated: the final sample counts for each alternative form a Beta distribution whose mean represents the expected probability of advantage (see Equation 8). This means that, as with response times, the ABDM automatically produces a confidence estimate alongside each predicted choice, derived from the same set of samples used to direct decisions. As such, the ABDM simulations used in the preceding model comparisons also include confidence judgements for these choices as well, though again the empirical datasets do not include equivalent measures from human participants to compare the model against. Empirical ratings of confidence in risky choice are in fact relatively rare, though there are some recent exceptions (da Silva Castanheira et al., 2021; da Silva Castanheira & Otto, 2026; Hu et al., 2026; Lejarraga & Lejarraga, 2020). Even within these studies, there are differences in task set-up, elicitation method and measurement scale which make this data difficult to use: for example, some studies focus on comparisons of confidence between description and experience formats (Camilleri & Newell, 2019; Lejarraga & Lejarraga, 2020), while others use simplified response scales which are harder to contrast with the probabilities produced by the ABDM (da Silva Castanheira et al., 2021; da Silva Castanheira & Otto, 2026).

Model evaluation on confidence is also complicated by the fact that the competing models make no formal predictions regarding confidence at all, offering no point of comparison with the ABDM. Furthermore, unlike the previous comparison of response times, it is difficult to even define a baseline for these models: while we could assume new methods to extract confidence measures from the operation of these models, these would be highly speculative and not reflective of the original definitions of these theories. This then prevents a direct model comparison of the form used in the previous sections.

As such, rather than directly comparing predicted confidence judgments from each of the candidate models to empirical data, we instead here evaluate the ABDM's predictive ability in confidence by examining particular qualitative patterns in the model's simulations. This targets three main relationships between confidence and other measures raised by previous research: the relation between confidence and choice, the relation between confidence and RT, and the relation between confidence and the value of the stimuli. We assess each of these using the posterior predictions on these measures across both datasets from the previous simulations, where the posteriors are again defined at the individual level based only on fits to each participant's choices.

While confidence is always derived the same way from the model, we use two different codings of confidence depending on the comparison: option coding provides the confidence in a given alternative even if that is not what was ultimately selected, while choice coding provides the confidence in the actual selection, creating a lower bound of 0.5. As choices here are all binary, choice coding is simply the complement of option coding where that alternative was not favoured (i.e., con f (A) = 1con f (B)). For simplicity, we restrict these analyses to only the first trial of each gamble to focus on the internal samples before incorporating feedback. In addition, we also exclude model predictions for three gamble pairs from the dataset of Erev et al. (2017) as these led to extreme outliers in response time: all three cases include common outcomes between alternatives, leading the model to become stuck sampling indifferent events and therefore never reaching a decision (these gamble pairs did not affect the RT comparison above as these did not have empirical equivalents).

Confidence and Choice

Studies of value-based decisions without risk have found negative relationships between choice confidence and variability: for example, less confident decisions are associated with weaker preferences (De Martino et al., 2013) and are more likely to change when repeated (Folke et al., 2016). We assessed whether this pattern held in the ABDM by testing the correlation between predicted confidence and choice rate. For this test, confidence was coded by option rather than choice to match the definition of choice rate as preference for option B used above. Figure 6A illustrates this relationship: confidence was strongly positively related to choice rate, with a correlation of 0.986 (p < .001). As a result, when preference is more consistent, confidence is more polarised, whereas where choice predictions vary, confidence falls closer to indifference.

Confidence and RT

Decisions in both risky and risk-free choice have shown negative relations between confidence and response time: slower choices commonly demonstrate lower confidence ratings (da Silva Castanheira et al., 2021; De Martino et al., 2013; Folke et al., 2016). We assessed this within the ABDM by examining the correlation between predicted confidence and RT, where RT was calculated using the same sampling rate recovered above (see Table 3).

In contrast to the previous analysis, confidence is in this case choice-coded as RT cannot be coded by option. Figure 6B shows this relationship, demonstrating a moderate negative correlation of -0.395 (p < .001). This again results from both measures being driven by the sampling process: longer response times result from higher sample counts, which are more likely where evidence is more conflicted and confidence is therefore lower.

Confidence and Value

In addition to relations to other responses, confidence also shows sensitivity to the features of the options that are being decided between: confidence is higher with larger differences in the value of the available options, demonstrated in both direct subjective valuations (De Martino et al., 2013; Folke et al., 2016), estimates from model fits (da Silva Castanheira et al., 2021) and objective expected values (da Silva Castanheira & Otto, 2026). We assessed this pattern here using the correlation between confidence and the difference in expected value (EV) between the target gambles in each pair. This used option coding, focusing on the confidence in selecting option B according to its advantage in EV.

Figure 6C illustrates this relationship, showing a moderate positive correlation of 0.575 (p < .001): larger differences in EV lead to more confidence in the selection. This is because the conversion of samples to evidence in the ABDM approximates the difference in EV of the considered alternatives, meaning the accumulator will tend towards the higher EV option; greater differences in EV thus lead to more consistent evidence and so higher confidence.

These results thus show the ABDM is able to capture key effects in confidence from the decision making literature, further illustrating the breadth of its coverage. This stands in sharp contrast to the competing models which all offer no predictions of confidence at all.

Discussion

The above comparisons demonstrate the Autocorrelated Bayesian Decision Maker's ability to describe human decision making, showing that it not only accurately predicts choice behaviour in general terms but is also competitive with other existing leading models, even outperforming some well-established theories. This correspondence is both quantitative and qualitative: the ABDM demonstrates good numerical fit to empirical data and captures most of the key phenomena observed in choice, again demonstrating an advantage over previous models. What is more, our results show that the ABDM in fact offers more accurate predictions of the time taken to make such choices than these competitors, thus better explaining the time course of these decisions. We have also shown the ABDM can describe key qualitative phenomena in confidence, further outperforming its competitors which do not consider this measure.

These results illustrate the breadth of this approach described in the Introduction to this paper: by proposing a detailed sampling process underlying choice, the ABDM provides a more multifaceted depiction of this behaviour, considering how sampled evidence determines not just selections but other related responses like response time and confidence. This breadth does not end with these responses, however: as with its predecessor, the ABDM is also able to predict valuations of the alternatives as well as confidence intervals around these valuations (Zhu et al., 2024). Such responses are in fact available within the current model predictions, though the considered datasets do not provide equivalent empirical values for comparison.

Further contrasts with such data are thus a key avenue for future work, particularly where all response types are given from a single task and participant sample to allow for examination of their relations to one another. This is contrasted with the behaviour of the more specialised models in our comparisons: considering choice alone, BEAST provides the best description, demonstrating the advantage of focusing on one particular aspect of behaviour. Such an advantage could in fact be even greater for more recent models which use modern machine learning techniques to provide even closer matches to empirical choice rates (Peterson et al., 2021; Plonsky et al., 2025). Evaluation of the other response types show where these models can fall down, however: none of the competing models match the ABDM's ability to predict the time course of decisions and offer no predictions of choice confidence, revealing the limitations of their approach. The ABDM thus provides a better description of behaviour when considering more aspects than just the immediate choice rate, so offering a more 'complete' account. In fact, a similar argument could be applied to the more specialised models as well: BEAST is itself a broader model than the other candidates, considering the additional influence of feedback and ambiguity on decisions. From this perspective, the ABDM takes this a step further by expanding not just the set of choice problems that can be modelled, but also the range of responses drawn from these problems. This is not to suggest that the ABDM completely captures all behaviour in these tasks, of course: as with any computational model, some detail must be lost to make it tractable, inevitably leading to an imperfect description. At the same time, the ABDM does provide substantially wider coverage of behaviour than other leading models, not only predicting multiple aspects of the choice process, but also linking this to other behaviour in perception and judgment from its prior definition (Zhu et al., 2024).

The value of this broader approach is not just in providing a more complete theory of behaviour, but also in identifying the practical implications of the processes actually supporting decision making: understanding the underlying cognitive systems reveals the possible interactions between responses which may be missed if focusing purely on choice.

The response time analyses provide a demonstration of this: because RT in the ABDM depends on the number of samples needed to make a choice, this model links RT to the difficulty of a decision, with longer RTs reflecting more conflicted evidence and hence harder choices. These patterns are however ignored by base CPT and TAX which generate immediate estimates of choice without considering how the difficulty of that comparison might impact its speed (Ayal & Hochman, 2009; Glöckner & Betsch, 2008). This is not however an inherent trait of process models, also demonstrated by our comparisons: both BEAST and DFT propose sequential sampling processes support choice but fail to fully account for variation in RT. The reasons for this are more specific to these models: DFT does attempt to predict the time course of decisions but ignores the influence of experience, predicting consistent behaviour across repetitions, while BEAST uses a fixed sample count for a given person, preventing variations in RT across both trials and problems. Similarly, confidence in the ABDM reflects the relative proportion of samples favouring each choice, meaning more mixed evidence results in more moderate confidence, as well as longer RTs as more samples are needed to meet the threshold.

Identifying these underlying systems also allows for new predictions of likely behaviour where the process is constrained by external factors: for example, adding a time limit to decisions may further restrict the amount of considered evidence, thus strengthening the influence of the starting point and prior on responses and so leading to different decisions.

Our results also indicate that the narrowness of choice-focused models cannot be easily solved by simply appending these models with additional mechanisms: we find that supplementing both CPT and TAX with a Drift Diffusion Model to allow predictions of response time does not lead to a better description of this data. This is particularly important given such mechanisms have become increasingly common as general purpose explanations for behaviour in this field: DDMs have had substantial success in recent years in matching both the choice and timing patterns of human decision makers in perceptual (Drugowitsch et al., 2012; Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008) and preferential tasks (Krajbich et al., 2010; Krajbich & Rangel, 2011). Moreover, DDMs have also been extended to allow for judgments of confidence (Pleskac & Busemeyer, 2010) and continuous valuations (Kvam & Busemeyer, 2020), providing their own form of breadth. The present results however suggest that DDMs are not a universal solution for capturing such behaviour, offering little additional explanatory power for the response times observed in the tasks examined here. This being said, our comparisons consider only a fairly basic form of DDM without starting point bias or variation in parameters across trials; this was a deliberate decision to avoid diverging from the predictions of the base choice models, but may limit the predictive ability of these approaches.

Continued contrasts with DDMs are thus a key avenue for future work.

There are also more theoretical distinctions between DDMs and the form of breadth discussed here: while DDMs are highly versatile, no single setting captures all response types, instead requiring separate diffusion processes for each modality. As such, while DDMs may capture the relation between a response and its timing, they do not predict direct connections between differing responses such as choices and valuations. This is in contrast to the use of a consistent sampling process for multiple responses in the ABS, leading to relations between responses, for example decision-contingent biases in estimates like anchoring (Spicer et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2024). More generally, DDMs do not fully specify the processes supporting choice, offering an abstracted vision of evidence accumulation to facilitate RT predictions as opposed to the concrete sampling framework offered by the ABDM.

The breadth of the sampling framework is not just across responses within decision making, but also across tasks: while we have focused here on extending the ABS to value-based choice, the model retains its prior applications to other domains, continuing to predict a number of empirical phenomena in estimation (e.g., anchoring, repulsion), probability judgments (e.g., conservatism, unpacking effects), response times (e.g., speed-accuracy trade-offs, slow vs. fast errors) and confidence (e.g., metacognitive inefficiency), as originally outlined in Zhu et al. (2024). This reinforces that the definition of the ABDM given here is an extension of the existing framework to a new domain rather than an entirely new model, linking choice processes to a common sampling system used across a range of tasks. This then opens the possibility of examining the performance of this framework across these domains, for example fitting the model to a perceptual task and then using this fit to predict behaviour in preferential decision making (e.g., Frydman and Nave, 2017).

Moreover, the generality of sampling itself allows for further applications to new domains by simply switching out the target distribution according to the task at hand: distributions could reflect perceptual features, semantic memory, or linguistic space, with the same sampling algorithm being used to access these distributions to support varying responses. This particular aspect of the sampling framework is a further distinction from the more focused decision making models which are not intended to be applied outside of choice contexts.

We should note that the definition of the ABDM presented here is not the only method to adapt this sampling framework to value-based choices, as previous models have offered differing answers to both what is sampled and accumulated: for example, 'Decision by Sampling' suggests that samples are not limited to potential outcomes from the presented options but also draw from the decision maker's memory of all experienced values (Stewart et al., 2006), while DFT accumulates differences in outcomes between options rather than the wins and losses used here (Bhatia, 2014; Busemeyer & Townsend, 1993). These may then present alternate forms of the ABDM to explore in future comparisons, though such possibilities are more complex than the current model, and reflect greater deviations from the initial definition from Zhu et al. (2024). This would also increase the space of models to evaluate, potentially making full comparisons of all settings intractable.

There are however some limitations of the present study which should be noted. First, while we evaluate the candidate models in multiple response modalities, we do not perform any joint fitting across these response types, with RTs and confidence judgments instead being inferred from fits to choice. Such joint fitting can prove challenging to ensure fit metrics over differing datasets are evenly weighted, but could provide greater assurance that the ABDM truly offers the better explanation across measures. As previously referenced, however, this approach does offer a form of generalisation test (Busemeyer & Wang, 2000), and so could in fact be seen as a strength of this study: the ABDM demonstrates high accuracy in predicted RTs even when its parameters are fit to other data (though from the same task), providing some assurance that this is not simply the result of overfitting. Fits are also limited by the number of simulations used to evaluate the models: we restricted comparisons to 100 parameter sets per model for tractability, but this may not be sufficient to fully cover the relatively large parameter space of the ABDM. More extensive simulations are thus advisable to verify the robustness of these results, though these will naturally carry greater computational costs.

The current comparisons are also entirely focused on risky choice, potentially limiting the generality of our conclusions. Risky choice is a dominant paradigm within decision making research, and has provided the basis for many key models, including those considered here (Bhatia, 2014; Birnbaum, 2008; Erev et al., 2017; Glöckner & Pachur, 2012; Kellen et al., 2020; Rieskamp, 2008; Tversky & Kahneman, 1992), but extensions to other forms of choice will provide valuable tests of the true breadth of the ABDM. The generality of the sampling framework should allow application to other forms of choice, though further minor adjustments to the model to suit these tasks may be required. Even within this paradigm, we have focused on a particular task design from Erev et al. (2017) and Plonsky et al. (2025) in which choices are directly repeated multiple times, which may not offer a fair reflection of wider choice behaviour. This particularly applies to the RT analyses: such repetition leads to particular patterns in choice timing, with much slower RTs in the first instance of each decision. This could lead to suggestions that the advantage of the ABDM in these results is specific to this task; indeed, a supplementary analysis found model performance was more even if only considering the first trial (see Appendix E). At the same time, our results show that these tasks offer a method to distinguish these models based on their account for repeated choice, illustrating the value of this design. Relatedly, while the current model does account for feedback, it does not capture decisions made from experience alone, which will require further adjustments to the definition of the sampling space in the absence of objective descriptions. Similar criticisms do however also apply to the other candidate models which also lack concrete predictions when nothing is initially known about the choice options.

Finally, these comparisons also consider only a subset of the many choice models from the existing literature: the current candidates were specifically chosen given their prevalence in past research, reflecting key theories for comparison with our approach, but are certainly not exhaustive given the number of models available in this area. Further contrasts with such candidates will thus help place the ABDM within the wider space of decision making theories.

Conclusion

We have presented the Autocorrelated Bayesian Decision Maker, a new application of mental sampling to value-based choice further expanding the scope of this already broad framework to this domain. Our results show that this model offers a wider coverage of choice behaviour than many existing theories, accurately describing human choices and response times in both absolute and relative terms, as well as key effects in choice confidence. This represents a broader perspective on human decision making not only capturing a wider array of behaviours within these tasks but also linking theories of decision making to other domains.

(2017) and Plonsky et al. (2025): these tasks both involve a series of immediate repetitions of each choice, leading to substantial differences in RT in the first trial of each pair, beginning much slower and subsequently settling at a faster level.
