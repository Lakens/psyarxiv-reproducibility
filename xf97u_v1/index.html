Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Abstract

The human brain readily captures regularities in the environment.

It is not clear, however, whether and how this skill-statistical learninginteracts with attention. We addressed this issue by leveraging neural entrainment, the synchronization of brain activity with statistical structure. In Study 1, we asked whether sensitivity to a frequencybased regularity, created by embedding low-frequency items among higher-frequency items, competes for attentional resources with a concurrent task. Participants viewed structured or random visual streams while performing a 3-back color matching task. Neural entrainment emerged only for structured streams, demonstrating sensitivity to the frequency structure. Improvement in the concurrent task, indexed by decreasing response times, unfolded more slowly when structure was present, suggesting that detecting statistical regularities drew attention away from the 3-back color matching task.

[div-01]

In Study 2, we tested the reverse connection-whether taxing attention with a more difficult task impairs the neural tracking of statistical structure. We compared entrainment during an easier color-change detection task and a more difficult 3-back color matching task. We observed no detectable effect of attentional load on neural entrainment. These findings reveal an asymmetric relationship between attention and frequency-based statistical learning: detecting structure imposes a modest attentional cost on a concurrent behavioral task, whereas the online neural tracking of regularities appears to be unaffected by increased attentional demands.

Our sensory world is extremely complex. At any given moment, we are hit by a multiplicity of sights, sounds, and movements that form a tangled web of cues and signals. Interpreting this complexity is very challenging.

Yet, our brain detects recurring patterns in the environment, often without our conscious awareness-a capacity known as statistical learning. Although interest in statistical learning dates back nearly a century (Frost et al., 2019), it surged following demonstrations of its fundamental role in language acquisition (Saffran et al., 1996). Since then, statistical learning has been observed across sensory modalities (Conway & Christiansen, 2005; Frost et al., 2015), stages of development (Forest et al., 2023), and even across species (Conway & Christiansen, 2001; Santolin & Saffran, 2018), establishing it as a pervasive mechanism through which the brain extracts and represents structure in the environment (Sherman et al., 2020).

Far less is known about whether (and, if so, how) regularities are prioritized for processing amid the vast array of competing environmental information. Answering this question requires considering the relationship between statistical learning on the one hand, and attention and cognitive load on the other.

Quite interestingly, the interaction between attention and statistical learning can be examined from two complementary perspectives. First, structured input may influence how we allocate our attention by allowing statistical regularities to prioritize certain aspects of the environment over others. Indeed, statistical regularities can bias selective attention toward predictable targets and away from distractors (Ferrante et al., 2018; Geng & Behrmann, 2002 , 2005; Jiang et al., 2013; Wang & Theeuwes, 2018). Accumulating evi-dence also suggests that the mere presence of statistical regularities attracts attention (Forest et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2013) and stabilizes moment-tomoment attentional states (Greatti et al., 2026). To date, however, it remains unknown whether this attentional attraction persists when statistical regularities compete with an ongoing task for attentional resources. If the processing of statistical structure competes for limited attentional resources, then task-irrelevant regularities should draw resources away from a concurrent task, resulting in measurable costs to task performance. Testing this possibility requires paradigms in which structured input and a concurrent task compete for attention while remaining otherwise independent, allowing the attentional cost of statistical learning, if any, to emerge.

Second, the availability of attentional resources may determine the extent to which statistical regularities are detected and learned. Research on both auditory and visual statistical learning has produced inconsistent evidence about whether learning requires attention or can occur automatically: some studies suggest that attention facilitates statistical learning (D. Duncan et al., 2025; Greatti et al., 2026; Mattys & Palmer, 2015; Toro et al., 2005; Turk-Browne et al., 2005), whereas others indicate that learning can happen (or is even enhanced) in the absence of focused attention (Batterink & Paller, 2019; D. Duncan & Theeuwes, 2020; Saffran et al., 1997), during mind wandering (Simor et al., 2025; Vékony et al., 2025), under conditions of cognitive depletion (Smalle et al., 2022), and even during sleep (Batterink & Zhang, 2022; Fló et al., 2022).

In addition, almost the entirety of this research has investigated the relationship between statistical learning and attention through structure based on transitional probabilities (TP), that is, by manipulating how reliably one item predicts the occurrence of the next (Frost et al., 2019). Yet, these sequential dependencies represent only one form of statistical structure available in the environment. Regularities can also arise from differences in the frequency with which individual events occur, for example. Such frequency information can provide an important source of structure, contributing, for instance, to the discovery of grammatical categories during language learning (Mintz, 2003; Reeder et al., 2013). Yet, whether the interaction between attention and statistical learning generalizes beyond sequential dependencies to frequency-based structure remains unknown.

In the present work, we address these open issues in the literature through neural entrainment-the brain's synchronization with statistical structure. This approach offers an online, objective measure of neural sensitivity that allows researchers to observe whether the participants' brains are capturing structure during exposure, in the absence of any behavioral assessment. Indeed, in De Rosa et al. (2022), participants viewed rapid sequences of base elements presented at a high frequency (F). Oddball stimuli were inserted at fixed intervals (every n items), thus creating a second regularity (periodic at F/n) based on the appearance of a lower frequency item. Importantly, the higher and lower frequency items were of the same sort (e.g., words in words), thus making their relative frequency of occurrence within the stream the only possible cue to the presence of a statistical structure. Indeed, EEG data revealed neural entrainment at F/n, indicating implicit discrimination between the higher-and lower-frequency groups. These findings broadened the scope of entrainment to statistical structure beyond TPs. Although this emerging evidence demonstrates that the brain can reliably track frequencybased statistical structure online, very little is known, again, about how this process interacts with attention. This interaction may operate in both directions also for univariate cues, of course: frequency-based structure may draw attention away from competing tasks, while the availability of attentional resources may constrain the neural processing of such structure.

The present study addresses the relationship between statistical learning and attention, combining attentional (cognitive workload) manipulations with neural entrainment as an online index of learning. Study 1 manipulates the presence vs. absence of statistical structure and checks whether this yields effects on performance in a concurrent task. Participants were exposed to visual streams of either structured or randomly organized linguistic stimuli (i.e., words and pseudofonts) while performing a demanding visual 3-back matching task on the color of a central fixation cross. If statistical regularities attract attention, we predict that performance on the 3-back task will be impaired during structured streams compared to random streams. Alternatively, no difference in performance between conditions would suggest that the detection of frequency-based regularities does not affect attention, at least to the extent that this can be captured in a concurrent behavioral task. Study 2 manipulates attentional demands to determine whether they influence the neural processing of statistical structure. Participants viewed the same structured visual streams from Study 1 while performing either a high-load (3-back matching) or low-load (mere detection) concurrent task on the color of a cross superimposed on the stream. This within-subject design allows us to isolate the effect of attentional demands on the neural representation of statistical structure. A similar design was adopted by Batterink and Paller (2019), which, however, paired a visual 3-back task with auditory patterns, thus addressing the relationship between attention and SL across modalities. Our study extends Batterink and Paller (2019)'s results using a concurrent task in the same modality. Arguably, this constitutes a stronger test of independence between attention and statistical learning, as attentional interference is typically stronger within than across sensory modalities (J. Duncan et al., 1997; Soto-Faraco & Spence, 2002; Treisman & Davies, 1973; Wickens et al., 1984). If neural entrainment to structured patterns does not require focused attention, we would expect to replicate prior findings that suggest neural entrainment to be unaffected by performing a concurrent task (Batterink & Paller, 2019). If, instead, neural entrainment to distributional regularities does require some significant amount of implicit attention, we might observe impaired neural entrainment under higher cognitive load.

Discussion

In Study 1, we examined whether the mere presence of a statistical pattern reduces the attentional resources available to perform a concurrent task. In a within-subjects design, participants completed a 3-back, color-matching task while viewing either a random stream or a structured stream. We hypothesized that if statistical regularities compete for attentional resources, concurrent task performance would be impaired when such regularities are present.

As expected, robust neural entrainment at the oddball frequency (1.2 Hz) emerged only in the structured condition and for both words and pseudofonts, replicating De Rosa et al. ( 2022) and confirming that participants successfully tracked the embedded structure. This validates the effectiveness of our manipulation: neural sensitivity to structure appeared exclusively when the said structure was present. Behaviorally, no main effect of condition was observed on the 3-back task performance, either in terms of accuracy or overall RTs, indicating that the presence of statistical regularities did not directly disrupt participants' ability to perform the concurrent task. However, we observed a significant interaction between condition and trial number for RTs: although participants became faster over the course of the study in both conditions, the rate of their improvement was slower in the structured condition, where a statistical pattern was embedded.

Overall, these findings demonstrate that the brain is remarkably sensitive to frequency-based structure, detecting such regularities even while attention is engaged by a cognitively demanding concurrent task. However, processing this structure did not consume sufficient resources to impair overall task accuracy or response speed. Instead, the regularities imposed a subtler attentional cost: although participants successfully performed the concurrent task, their practice-related improvements in response speed unfolded more slowly in the presence of structured input. Thus, frequency-based regularities appear to compete modestly for attention without substantially disrupting ongoing performance.

General Discussion

In two experiments, we examined the relationship between attention and the neural sensitivity to frequency-based statistical structure. In Study 1, we manipulated the presence of task-irrelevant visual regularities during a demanding 3-back, color-matching task to test whether processing statistical structure imposes a measurable cost on concurrent behavior. Participants showed robust neural entrainment at the frequency of the embedded structure, indicating that their online sensitivity to structure persists even when participants are challenged with a competing, attention-demanding task.

Behaviorally, we found no measurable difference in the 3-back, colormatching accuracy and mean RTs when frequency-based regularities were present, suggesting that the presence of a frequency-based structure does not disrupt participants' ability to perform the concurrent task. Yet, improvements in task performance, as indexed by decreasing RTs across trials, unfolded more slowly when regularities were present than when the stream was random. This finding suggests that attention and statistical learning may not be fully independent: the presence of a structured stream might have attracted attention (or cognitive resources more generally) away from the concurrent task, reducing the rate at which performance improved with practice. Note that the structured input was entirely task-irrelevant, and our instructions even encouraged participants to ignore it; thus, our findings are consistent with the idea that our attentional system is automatically attracted toward structure once regularities are detected in the environment, in line with previous work (Greatti et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2013). The present study extends these findings by showing that the attentional capture exerted by statistical regularities can also be based on item frequency, instead of transitional probabilities -that is, univariate statistical information rather than bivariatefoot_1 . The effect is, however, rather mild; it affects the speed of learning and improvement, not performance per se. Therefore, the presence of frequency regularities in the input might behave as a mild attentional pull, drawing enough resources to flag the presence of learnable structure, but leaving sufficient capacity for successfully completing other concurrent tasks. This balance is likely adaptive: because regularities are ubiquitous in natural environments, their detection must remain sufficiently sensitive to support learning without compromising the goal-directed behaviors in which we are concurrently engaged.

In Study 2, we reversed the causal question: rather than asking whether statistical regularities capture attention, we tested whether taxing attention with a more difficult concurrent task constrains the online neural sensitivity to frequency-based structure. Participants viewed the same structured visual streams as in Study 1 while performing either a low-load (color detection) or high-load (3-back color matching) task. This design allowed us to manipulate cognitive load and, consequently, the attentional resources available to process statistical structure. Behavioral results confirmed the effectiveness of the manipulation: participants were clearly slower and less accurate in the high-load condition than in the low-load condition. Yet, neural entrainment at the oddball frequency (1.2 Hz) did not differ between conditions, suggesting that our neural sensitivity to statistical regularities may operate independently of the cognitive load imposed by a competing task. This dissociation suggests that the mechanisms supporting online statistical learning are resource-tolerant; they can extract and integrate regularities even when attention is engaged elsewhere. These findings are nicely in line with previous work on the relationship between attention and statistical learning. For example, Batterink and Paller (2019) also showed that entrainment to regularities is unaffected by the manipulation of attention through a concurrent task. Our work, however, extends the theoretical remit of Batterink and Paller's results in at least two important ways.

First, the regularity implemented in our stream of stimuli was based on a frequency difference between groups of items (A and B occur more often, C and D occur more rarely), rather than on TPs, that is, predictive relationships between individual stimuli (A predicts B, C predicts D). These forms of structure place different computational demands on learners. Frequency learning requires accumulating evidence across relatively extended periods to estimate how often individual events occur, whereas TP learning depends on detecting local relationships between successive events. However, frequency statistics are representationally less complex because they concern individual items rather than the associations between them; they are based on univariate, rather than multivariate information. The two forms of regularity also afford predictions at different levels of specificity. In frequency-based streams, learners could anticipate whether the next stimulus was likely to belong to the frequent or infrequent category (i.e., bases or oddballs), but could not predict the identity of the particular stimulus. In TP-based structures, by contrast, the occurrence of one element constrains which specific element is likely to follow, enabling item-level predictions. As previously proposed (Greatti et al., 2026), these statistics may therefore serve complementary computational functions, with frequency information providing a coarse representation of the distributional properties of the environment, and TP-based information supporting more precise models of its sequential organization. Despite this methodological shift, the convergence of our findings closely parallels results obtained with TP-based statistics. The persistence of neural entrainment under these conditions suggests that statistical learning, at least as measured through its early neural index, may rely on a common mechanism that generalizes across different forms of structure (Growns et al., 2020).

A second way in which the present work extends our knowledge on the relationship between attention and statistical learning is in the role of the perceptual channel. Prior research focused on acoustic statistical learning (see, e.g., Batterink and Paller, 2019), while we assess learning in the visual domain. Our results suggest that, at least in the present paradigm, vision and audition share a common learning mechanism -or at least they feature mechanisms that are similarly independent of attention and cognitive load.

Moreover, learning occurred here in the same perceptual modality as the competing task, while in previous work the attentional manipulation was deployed cross-modally (e.g., statistical regularities were implemented in the acoustic modality while the concurrent task was visual). Because attentional interference is typically stronger within than between modalities (J. Duncan et al., 1997; Soto-Faraco & Spence, 2002; Treisman & Davies, 1973; Wickens et al., 1984), this finding provides a particularly conservative test of the robustness of statistical learning under reduced attention, and it indicates that the lack of an attentional load effect on neural entrainment cannot simply be attributed to cross-modal resource segregation; rather, it appears to reflect a genuinely resource-tolerant mechanism of perceptual statistical learning.

It is important to note that our findings do not imply that learning, in the traditional sense of storing and retrieving information to guide behavior, is independent of attention. Our measure of learning (i.e., neural entrainment to statistical regularities) indexes the online perceptual tracking of structure rather than its encoding. While frequency tagging provides a precise and bias-free index of perceptual sensitivity to regularities, it remains unclear whether such entrainment translates into explicit or behaviorally usable knowledge (Ruzza & Crepaldi, 2026). These considerations are in line with the view that the online component of statistical learning (i.e., neural entrainment) is dissociable from the memory storage or retrieval component of statistical learning (as measured by offline statistical learning tasks). Indeed, prior work has often reported weak or absent correlations between neural entrainment and behavioral measures (Batterink et al., 2024; Smalle et al., 2022). Thus, our results should be interpreted as evidence that the early perceptual registration of structure seems to be robust to attentional load, not that the memory encoding of these regularities is attention-independent.

The transition from perceptual detection to stable memory representations goes beyond the purpose of our study and may still critically depend on attentional engagement (e.g., Turk-Browne et al., 2005).

Finally, it is worth noting a secondary finding, which, however, reveals another interesting aspect of the relationship between statistical learning and attention. Both Study 1 and Study 2 showed stronger neural entrainment at the structure frequency (1.2 Hz) for words than for pseudofonts. Interestingly, this neural enhancement did not translate into greater interference with the concurrent task: accuracy and RTs did not differ between word and pseudofont trials in either study. The word advantage may reflect facilitation from pre-existing orthographic and lexical representations, which could support more precise predictions and thereby enhance neural sensitivity to frequency-based structure. Future research should build on this possibility to determine whether -and if so, under which conditions -different item categories modulate neural sensitivity to statistical regularities.

Overall, our results offer a general view of statistical learning and attention as interdependent, but separable systems. Statistical learning appears to operate through an automatic, resource-tolerant perceptual mechanism that flexibly interacts with attention: it attracts it when structure is present, and it persists even when attentional resources are limited. More generally, this pattern suggests that the brain continuously tracks statistical structure through low-level mechanisms of sensory synchronization, while higher-level attentional processes might still determine whether that structure becomes encoded into memory or guides behavior.
