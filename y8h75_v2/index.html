To improve Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 23 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Navigating an uncertain world, we constantly face the explore-exploit dilemma: should we stick with what we know, or explore options that may or may not prove better? An influential theory proposes that humans solve this dilemma through a division of labor in time: explore in childhood, exploit in old age. Our work adds critical empirical nuance to this theory. Exploration can manifest as deliberate information-seeking ("directed exploration") or choice randomness ("random exploration"), and these strategies are employed differently for gain-seeking and loss-avoidance. Here, we ask how these modes of exploration relate to one another and jointly shape decision-making across the lifespan. A cross-sectional sample of 254 participants aged 8-79 performed a bandit task dissociating directed and random exploration in gain-seeking and lossavoidance contexts. Factor analysis revealed three factors driving behavior: two domain-specific factors reflecting information-seeking for gains and loss-avoidance, respectively, and a domain-general factor reflecting choice randomness. Directed exploration for gains increased steadily and well into young adulthood before declining in older adulthood, whereas directed exploration for loss-avoidance changed little across the lifespan. Children and older adults, relative to young adults, were characterized by greater choice randomness across contexts, even when exploration was disadvantageous. Though working memory and processing speed accounted for some of these effects, independent effects of age remained. Our findings demonstrate that exploration is not a unitary phenomenon: it encompasses dissociable subprocesses -some domain-specific and others domain-general -with distinct developmental timelines that are shaped by cognitive maturation and decline as well as factors beyond cognitive ability.

When navigating an uncertain world, we face a fundamental dilemma: should we stick with what we know, or explore options that may or may not prove better? The response may be different depending on our stage of life and whether we are motivated by gainseeking or loss-avoidance. In participants aged 8 to 79, deliberate information-seeking to maximize gains increased steadily from childhood into young adulthood before declining in old age, whereas information-seeking to avoid losses remained relatively stable throughout life. Across contexts, children and older adults were also less strategic, choosing at random even when exploring unfamiliar options was disadvantageous. Our findings show that exploration encompasses domain-specific and domain-general subprocesses that develop according to their own timelines.

1 Introduction

The explore-exploit-dilemma is a fundamental challenge of adaptive decision-making at every stage of the human lifespan. A prominent theory by Gopnik and colleagues (1) proposes that we partially solve this dilemma through a "division of labor in time", i.e., by exploring during childhood and exploiting what we have learned during adulthood and older age. Though compelling, this account is complicated by two intersecting strands of research.

The first shows that not all exploration is created equal. Among others, there is directed exploration, where choices are deliberately biased towards informative options, and random exploration, where random choice behavior facilitates information gain by chance (2). Likewise, exploration can be employed non-strategically or strategically -if and when the environment offers sufficient opportunity to capitalize on acquired information (2). It can be driven by factors such as novelty (3, 4), uncertainty (5), boredom (6), and the "richness" of the environment (i.e., availability of reward) (7, 8). Also, context is key: how much people explore seems to be modulated by whether they are trying to minimize losses or maximize gains (7).

The second strand shows that these different kinds of exploration have divergent developmental trajectories. Thus, in line with Gopnik and colleagues' theory, several studies report that children and adolescents have an increased tendency to seek out uncertainty (4, 9, 10) and to engage in random or value-free exploration (3-5), while older adults have been shown to display diminished uncertainty seeking and random exploration (11, 12). However, novelty seeking seems to remain stable at least across early-life development (3, 4), and strategic directed exploration has been demonstrated to expand rather than recede during adolescence (13), consistent with increasingly sophisticated cognitive abilities. Understanding the development of exploration therefore does not mean tracking a unidimensional behavior from childhood to old age.

Rather, it means identifying independent and interacting components that result in (different) explorative behavior(s) in different contexts, and investigating their impact across the lifespan.

In this study, we contribute to a better understanding of exploratory behavior from a lifespan perspective by studying several facets of exploration in a large in-lab sample spanning childhood and adolescence as well as young adulthood and old age. We investigate exploration along three dimensions and their intersections -strategic vs non-strategic, random vs directed, and motivated by gains or loss-avoidance -using the Horizon task, a bandit task designed to independently manipulate the value and informativeness of different options, the future value of information, and the motivational context (2, 7).

In line with previous findings, we hypothesize that in the gains domain, strategic exploration, especially strategic directed exploration, would expand from childhood to adulthood (13), while randomness would decline (5), with both reaching a plateau in young adulthood. We further hypothesize that older age would be associated with less directed and strategic random exploration (11, 12). As the lifespan development of exploration for loss-avoidance, and its relationship to exploration for gains, has not yet been systematically studied, we do not have strong directional hypotheses for these questions, and treat them as open empirical questions.

2 Methods

2.1 Participants

We pooled data from N=222 participants between 8 and 40 years (51% female) and N=32 older adults between 65 and 79 years (53% female) (Fig. 1-A.) from two larger laboratory studies investigating lifespan development of learning and decision-making (14) (15) (16). Volunteers were recruited from participant databases of the TU Dresden and the Max-Planck-Institute for Human Cognitive and Brain Sciences, and via local advertising in the Leipzig and Dresden areas. Participants were excluded if they reported any current neurological or psychiatric condition, took psychotropic medication, or scored below 23 during onsite Montreal Cognitive Assessment (17) testing (for older adults). For additional exclusion criteria in the Leipzig sample, see supplement. All participants (and caregivers where applicable) provided written informed consent and were compensated for their time. For detailed participant characteristics, see Supplementary Table 1.

2.2 Neuropsychological testing

All participants performed the Digit Symbol Substitution and Digit Span (backwards) tests (18, 19), assessing processing speed and working memory capacity, respectively (Fig. 1-B).

2.3 Horizon task

Participants performed a modified bandit task designed to probe strategic and nonstrategic directed and random exploration (the Horizon task) (2) in gain-seeking and loss-avoidance contexts (7). As illustrated in Fig. 1-B, the task consists of two blocks of 80 games each, a gains and a loss block. In each game, participants choose between two slot machines (one-armed bandits) with either 5 or 10 slots (trials). Each choice is followed by feedback (point gains in gains block, point losses in loss block).

Participants are instructed to try to earn as many and lose as few points as possible.

Payoffs for each bandit are drawn from normal distributions with variable means and a fixed standard deviation (SD=8). The task has several key manipulations to independently vary the value and informativeness of the bandits, as well as the strategic utility of exploration.

First, the task systematically manipulates the value difference between bandits. That is, in each game, the mean payoff of one bandit (chosen at random) is set to either 40 or 60 (-40 or -60 in the loss block), and the mean of the other bandit is offset relative to that value by ±30, ±20, ±12, ±8, or ±4.

Second, the task manipulates the informativeness of the bandits independent of their values. The first four trials of each game are forced-choice trials in which participants are directed to play specific bandits: sometimes in a [2, 2] choice pattern, so that each bandit is encountered equally often and are thus equally informative, sometimes in a [1, 3] or [3, 1] choice pattern, so that one bandit is encountered less often and is thus more informative than the other.

Third, the task manipulates the strategic utility of exploration for future choices by varying the number of free choice trials after the first four (forced-choice) trials (the horizon). Exploration on the first free-choice trial is useful only if that information can be used in the future: in games with 5 trials in total (short horizon), the first free choice trial is also the last. In this situation, a rational strategic agent should always exploit (i.e., choose the bandit with the higher observed mean reward value). In games with 10 trials in total, the first free choice trial is one out of six (long horizon), so that it may make sense to explore. As Wilson et al. have previously shown (2), humans do this by preferentially choosing more informative bandits in the long horizon (strategic directed exploration) but also by choosing more randomly (less driven by value) , which promotes information gain through more balanced sampling of bandits (strategic random exploration).

Note that a subset of participants (N=16) performed 160 games per block. Due to time constraints, this was reduced to 80 games per block in subsequent participants; all games are retained in the analysis.

2.4 Analysis

2.4.1 Behavioral analysis

To analyze behavior on the first free choice trial of each game, we employed a logistic mixed-effects model with maximal random effects structure (20) estimated using fitglme in MATLAB 2023a: The coefficient for Information difference (β Δinfo ) captures the tendency to choose the bandit with higher informativeness, i.e., directed exploration. The coefficient for Value difference (β Δvalue ) captures the tendency to exploit the higher mean bandit, i.e., random exploration, reverse coded. Their respective interactions with horizon capture the strategic deployment of information-seeking and random decision-making in long horizon games (strategic directed and random exploration, respectively).

3.1 Task effects

Strategic directed exploration.

Consistent with previous reports, we found that on average, participants strategically sought out informative options when the utility of information was high (strategic directed exploration) (e.g., 2, 7, 12, 13). Thus, there was a significant interaction of information difference x horizon (β=.27, z=8.43, p<.0001), indicating higher sensitivity to the informativeness of the bandits in long horizon games. This was reflected in a higher probability of choosing the more informative bandit in long horizon games (p(high info); Figure 2-A).

Further in line with prior work (7), participants were overall more likely to choose the more informative bandit in the losses compared to the gains condition (Figure 2-A), as reflected in an interaction between information difference and motivational context (β=-.68, z=-10.55, p<.0001).

Interestingly, the motivational context (gains vs. loss-avoidance) also affected the extent to which directed exploration was deployed strategically. Thus, we found a three-way interaction between information difference x horizon x motivational context (β=.29, z=8.06, p<.0001), such that the strategic modulation of directed exploration was more pronounced in the gains compared to the losses condition (Figure 2-A). To explore the interaction further, we performed simple effects analyses. These show that in the gains condition, participants overcame a bias towards exploitation in the gains condition when the utility of information was high. Thus, there was a negative effect of informativeness in short horizon games (β=-1.08, z=-9.67, p<.0001) -participants sought out more familiar options -, and no significant effect of informativeness in either direction in long horizon games (β=.03, z=.37, p=.71). Contrastingly, in the losses condition, there was a strong and approximately equal positive effect of informativeness in short and long horizon games (β=.84, z=9.15, p<.0001; β=.82, z=10.41, p<.0001), indicating that participants did not deploy directed exploration strategically in the losses condition.

Strategic random exploration.

Consistent with previous reports (e.g., 2, 7, 12, 13), participants showed more random exploration when the utility of information was high.

Thus, we found a significant interaction of value difference x horizon (β=-1.5, z=-19.85, p<.0001), indicating a decrease of exploitation (an increase in random decisionmaking) in long horizon games. This was reflected in a higher probability of choosing the lower mean bandit (p(low mean)) in long vs short horizon games (Figure 2-B). In addition, there was a three-way interaction between value difference, horizon, and motivational context (β=-.13, z=-2.56, p=.01), such that the strategic modulation of random exploration by horizon was more pronounced in the gains vs the losses condition (β=.08, z=3.6, p<.001; Figure 2-B). Though not significant, the same qualitative effect has previously been shown in a smaller sample (7).

Interaction between directed and random exploration.

There was no significant interaction between the effects of information and value difference (β=-.08, z=-1.76, p=.07), or between strategic directed and random exploration (information difference x value difference x horizon: β=-.02, z=-.54, p=.59).

3.2 Factor analysis

To further investigate how directed and random exploration and exploitation in gainseeking and loss-avoidance contexts relate to one another, we performed exploratory factor analysis on the subject-specific random slopes of information and value difference (β Δinfo and β Δvalue ) in short and long horizon games in each motivational context (Fig. 2-C). Visual inspection of the raw correlation matrix derived from M1 (Fig.

2-C) foreshadows the three-factor structure reported below: the four β Δvalue slopes were strongly and uniformly intercorrelated (r's = .76-.91) regardless of horizon or motivational context, while the β Δinfo slopes clustered separately by motivational context, with strong short-long horizon correlations within gains (r = .65) and within losses (r = .74), but weak-to-negative correlations across contexts (r's = -.38 to .34).

Factor 1 (Exploitation) was dominated by strong contributions of the effect of value difference independent of horizon and motivational context, with small to moderate negative contributions of the preference for the more informative bandit in short horizon games. 4 Discussion

Exploration is not a unitary phenomenon (2, 4, 6, 7). A central distinction is between directed exploration, where people explicitly seek out unfamiliar options, and random exploration, where they inject randomness into their response pattern to "passively" increase sampling of unfamiliar options (2). The motivational context -whether the focus is on maximizing gains or avoiding losses -has been shown to modulate how we employ these strategies (7). In this study, a large sample of participants with an age range spanning from childhood to old age performed a gain-and loss-framed variant of the Horizon task (7), allowing us to characterize how strategies of exploration for gain and loss-avoidance change across the lifespan.

First, we replicate previous findings, confirming that people strategically use random exploration in both gain-seeking and loss-avoidance contexts (7). We also confirm that in loss-avoidance contexts, participants tend to seek out unfamiliar options even when information has no strategic value (7). Extending prior work, we show that in gainseeking contexts, participants tended to avoid unfamiliar options when information had no strategic value but overcame this bias when information was useful, demonstrating strategic directed exploration (2, 7). There was no evidence of a strategic deployment of directed exploration in loss-avoidance contexts when the whole sample was considered, although exploratory analyses in young adults replicated Krueger et al.-'s (7) finding of strategic directed exploration of loss avoidance (see Supplement for details).

4.1 Factor structure of exploration for gains and loss-avoidance

We further demonstrate that individual tendencies towards directed and random exploration across motivational contexts were largely independent of each other. This was further underscored by divergent and distinct associations with age and cognitive abilities. First, exploratory factor analyses leveraging the variance-covariance structure of our behavioral model identified three mostly independent factors: one dominated by value sensitivity across horizons and conditions (Exploitation); and two factors reflecting directed exploration in the gains and loss-avoidance conditions, respectively.

The three factors were only weakly correlated. Exploitation had a small negative association with Information seeking for gains and none with Information seeking for loss-avoidance. Thus, a person's inclination towards exploitation -the mirror image of their inclination towards random exploration -reveals almost nothing about their inclination toward directed exploration, consistent with previous reports of different neural substrates (23). Similarly, Information seeking for gains and loss-avoidance were effectively orthogonal. The latter is consistent with prior investigations indicating that individual differences in uncertainty processing are mostly independent across motivational contexts (24, 25). Distinct developmental timelines of directed exploration for gains and loss-avoidance

In line with the factor analysis, our data suggest that directed exploration for gains and losses, and random exploration develop according to different age trajectories. Thus, directed exploration for gains and loss-avoidance showed noticeably divergent relationships with age. Consistent with previous work (13, 26), strategic directed exploration for gains increased steadily, substantially, and well into young adulthood.

Strategic directed exploration for loss-avoidance also expanded, though much less precipitously. The pattern reversed in older adults, who, in line with prior work (11, 12), showed a decline of strategic directed exploration in the gains and the losses condition relative to younger adults. The divergence across conditions is reminiscent of prior reports that another behavior involving uncertainty processing -risk-taking -, shows more pronounced age-related changes in the gains than the losses domain (27, 28).

To understand what drives the divergence, it helps to disentangle different mechanistic contributions. Recall that the gain-loss divergence from childhood to young adulthood reflected two parallel processes. First, strategic directed exploration -the additional premium placed on information in long-horizon games -expanded with age across conditions; second, the overall tendency to choose the more informative bandit increased with age in the gains, but not the losses condition. These effects compounded, such that the rise of the premium on information was particularly sharp in long horizon games of the gains condition.

Interestingly, accounting for age-related improvements in working-memory completely absorbed the gain/loss, but not the horizon differences in the age-related expansion of directed exploration, which remained statistically significant. This suggests that the influences of age and cognitive improvements are partially independent from each other. In fact, mediation analyses showed that -ceteris paribus -working memory and age had opposing directional effects on directed exploration in the loss domain: highe r working memory was associated with weaker, and age with stronger preference for more informative bandits in the loss condition. This suggests that two opposing developmental processes might underlie the observed pattern of age effects on directed exploration: from childhood to young adulthood, age-related gains in cognitive abilities may diminish deviations from rationality, reducing a tendency toward familiarity-seeking in the gains condition and toward ambiguity-seeking in the loss condition. An independent maturational process could, at the same time, lead to an overall expansion in strategic information seeking. Speculatively, this could reflect the increasing real-world strategic utility of information during young or "emerging" adulthood: the time period between age 18 and 29, defined by consequential, sometimes irreversible decisions about career commitments, relationships, family planning and so on (29). It is arguably during this period that gathering information before committing has the greatest value. Whether this manifests when informationseeking is tested in the lab is speculative, but the alignment in developmental timing is suggestive.

Older adults showed diminished sensitivity to information compared to younger adults and adolescents in the gains condition. Interestingly, accounting for neuropsychological metrics did not abolish this effect, indicating that in the older age group, there might be a "genuinely" age-related difference in directed exploration in different motivational contexts. A speculative interpretation might invoke Carstensen's socioemotional sensitivity theory (30), which posits that as perceived future time horizon shrinks in old age, motivation shifts toward more present and consumption focused goals, such that future-relevant information is discounted in favor of more certain, proximate gains.

4.2 Developmental timeline of random exploration

In contrast to what we saw for directed exploration and in line with the factor analysis, there was no difference in the development of random exploration for gains and lossavoidance. Strategic random exploration expanded rapidly during childhood and reached a plateau in late adolescence, where the difference in choice randomness across horizons reached its maximum. Critically, this was largely driven by increasing sensitivity to value differences between bandits in short horizon games -which makes people more likely to exploit when exploration is strategically inadvisable -rather than decreasing value sensitivity in long horizon games. This finding dovetails with a welldocumented overall decrease in choice randomness in adolescence (5, 14, 16, 31, 32). Congruently, the age-related increase in value sensitivity was mediated by processing speed, a measure of general cognitive efficiency. The mirror image of this pattern emerged in later life: older adults showed less strategic random exploration than younger adults, again mostly due to maladaptively high randomness in shorthorizon games rather than lower long horizon randomness. As in the younger cohort, the difference in value sensitivity disappeared when processing speed was controlled for. Though contrary to previous reports emphasizing reduced strategic random exploration in aging (11, 12), these findings chime with studies showing an overall increase in choice randomness (33, 34) and fluid intelligence (35) in older age.

Together, our data suggest that the development of random exploration is primarily driven by the acquisition and subsequent erosion of value sensitivity and general cognitive efficiency, rather than by bona fide changes of the disposition to explore. An intriguing possibility is that "random exploration" may in fact be better construed as "strategic exploitation", where the cognitive resources needed for precise value-driven decision-making are deployed when it is useful to do so -if and when those resources are available. Future work should investigate whether this is related to developmental changes in (striatal) dopamine and noradrenaline signaling, both of which have been implicated in general cognitive functioning across the lifespan (36) (37) (38), choice randomness in exploration contexts (39) (40) (41) (42) (43), and indeed, the willingness to exert cognitive effort (44, 45).

4.3 Limitations

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. The design is crosssectional, which means developmental trajectories are inferred from age differences rather than individual change, and cohort effects cannot be ruled out. In addition, our older adult sample is relatively modest in size, and the gap in coverage of middle age means the transition from young adulthood to older adulthood is not well characterized.

Longitudinal work with more uniform age sampling would be valuable to support and complement the findings of the current study.

4.4 Conclusions and future directions

These limitations notwithstanding, our results clearly demonstrate that exploration is neither a uniform behavior nor one with a unified developmental timeline. How exploration changes with age -and what drives that change -critically depends on which component of exploration one examines, and in what motivational context people explore. Our findings raise interesting questions that should be addressed in future research: Is random exploration really strategic exploitation? Does high working memory (or fluid intelligence generally) protect against biases in uncertainty processing? And might lifespan changes in strategic exploration be tied to lifespan changes in the value of information for current life goals? There is plenty yet to explore.

There was a main effect of block (β=-.05, z=-2.31, p=.02), such that participants chose the left side less in their second block.

8.5 Replication of Krueger et al., 2017 (young adults)

Constricting the sample to young adults (18-30), we replicate findings by Krueger et al. 2017, who observed strategic random exploration in both gains and loss-avoidance contexts. Here, we find a three-way interaction between informativeness, horizon, and motivational context (β=.23, z=3.54, p<.001), with a weaker, though significant informativeness x horizon interaction in the loss-avoidance than the gains condition (β=.28, z=2.96, p=.003; β=.75, z=7.71, p<.0001).
