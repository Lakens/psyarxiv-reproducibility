Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Selecting random-effects specifications in multilevel models is challenging when several plausible alternatives exist. Bayesian model averaging (BMA), Bayesian stacking (BS), and Bayesian hierarchical stacking (BHS) combine candidate predictions using weights constrained to sum to one, limiting their ability to accommodate systematic patterns beyond the candidate set. We apply Varying Coefficient Bayesian Additive Regression Trees (VCBART; Deshpande, Bai, Balocchi, Starling, & Weiss, 2026) with an adaptive intercept to address candidate-model inadequacy. VCBART estimates covariate-dependent ensemble weights without a sum-to-one constraint, allowing predictions to vary across student and school characteristics. We compare VCBART with conventional model averaging, model selection, and alternative prediction methods through simulations calibrated to PISA 2018 U.S. mathematics data and an empirical application. Under realistic outcome variability and moderate-to-high between-school heterogeneity, VCBART achieves the lowest out-of-sample RMSE among non-oracle methods, although its narrower prediction intervals exhibit modest undercoverage. In the PISA application, VCBART also achieves the highest predictive accuracy, while its identified adaptive intercept reveals systematic patterns not captured by the candidate models.

Bayesian Predictive Ensembles for Random Effects Specification Uncertainty: Capturing Individual Heterogeneity in Large-Scale Assessments Introduction Motivation

In educational research, the relationship between student socioeconomic status (SES) and mathematics achievement often varies across schools, as may the effects of teacher interest, learning adaptivity, or immigration background (Goldstein, 2011; Karakus, Courtney, & Aydin, 2023; Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002). Each of these varying relationships corresponds to a different random effects specification, and choosing among them is a recurring problem in multilevel modeling. With P predictors eligible for random slopes, there are 2 P possible random effects structures, since each predictor can either carry a random slope or not. Prior work suggests that theory alone may be insufficient for selecting among plausible combinations of random effects, especially when fitting all candidate specifications simultaneously is computationally costly (Barr, Levy, Scheepers, & Tily, 2013). Current standard practice selects one "best" specification and proceeds as though it were the true model, discarding information in the alternatives and understating the uncertainty in subsequent inferences (Barr et al., 2013; Draper, 1995; Hoeting, Madigan, Raftery, & Volinsky, 1999; Huang & Anderson, 2021).

How much this matters depends on the inferential target. When the goal is inference about fixed effects, valid standard errors can often be obtained from a fixed-effects or population-average model with cluster-robust standard errors, and the random-effects structure need not be specified at all (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002). The situation is different when the goal is to predict the outcome of an individual student together with its uncertainty. The random-effects structure then determines how a school's information is pooled with the population and which student characteristics modify that pooling. A misspecified structure changes both point and interval predictions for that student. This paper is about the second goal. Individual-level prediction is a routine task in educational practice. Early-warning systems flag students whose predicted achievement falls below a proficiency threshold so that support can be targeted (Bowers, Sprott, & Taff, 2013). Placement and tiered intervention decisions rely on expected scores given a student's background and school. School accountability indicators compare observed with predicted achievement at the student level before aggregating to schools (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002). In each case the prediction is made for a known student in a known school, and both the point prediction and its interval matter. Current practice usually fits one multilevel specification, chosen by subject-matter theories or by statistical model comparisons, and treats its predictions as if that model specification were correct.

The substantive implications extend beyond statistical inference. Different random-effects specifications can lead to different conclusions about between-school heterogeneity. For instance, a model with a random SES slope describes how much the SES-achievement relationship varies across schools, whereas a model with a random teacher-interest slope captures a different dimension of instructional variability. Each specification is theoretically motivated but provides only a partial representation of school-level processes. At the individual level, a student's predicted outcome and its associated uncertainty depend on which specification is selected, and misspecification can affect both the prediction and the inference for that student. Combining information across competing specifications while accounting for model-selection uncertainty is therefore more appropriate than selecting one and setting the others aside, particularly when predictions need to adapt to each student's own characteristics. Bayesian model averaging (BMA; Raftery, Madigan, & Hoeting, 1997), Bayesian stacking (BS; Yao, Vehtari, Simpson, & Gelman, 2018), and Bayesian hierarchical stacking (BHS; Yao, Pirš, Vehtari, & Gelman, 2022) address model uncertainty through weighted combinations of candidate model predictions. A nonparametric extension using the bracket the residual variation observed in that data, anchoring the evaluation in conditions representative of large-scale educational assessments.

Under this formulation, the weight assigned to each candidate model is estimated nonparametrically from student-and school-level characteristics, and the posterior distributions of these weights describe which specification contributes most to prediction for a given student and with what uncertainty. The adaptive intercept absorbs outcome variance not captured by any candidate, removing the sum-to-one constraint imposed by BMA, BS, and BHS and allowing the ensemble to correct for the collective shortfall of partially specified candidates. Among the methods compared, VCBART comes closest to the oracle in out-of-sample accuracy when within-school noise is comparable to real assessment data and between-school heterogeneity is moderate or high. Its prediction intervals are the narrowest, at a coverage slightly below the nominal level.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. The Background section reviews the ensemble methods and the varying coefficient model that motivates the proposed approach. The Method section describes the ensemble formulation. The simulation and empirical studies present the evidence, and the Discussion section closes with implications for practice.

Background

The M-Closed, M-Complete, and M-Open Settings Bernardo and Smith (2000) described three settings that characterize the relationship between a candidate model set M = {M 1 , . . . , M K } and the true data-generating process M 0 . In the M-closed setting, M 0 ∈ M, meaning one candidate is the true model, the case for which BMA was designed. In the M-complete setting, M 0 / ∈ M but the true predictive distribution can be approximated by combining the candidates. In the M-open setting, M 0 / ∈ M and the exact form of the true process is unknown to the analyst (Yao et al., 2018). The random effects specification problem examined here falls in the M-open setting. For instance, when the true model requires five simultaneous random slopes and each candidate includes only one, the true data-generating process lies outside the candidate set. Each partial specification still carries some information about the between-cluster structure, but constrained ensemble methods have limited capacity to correct for structural components not fully represented by any candidate model. The adaptive intercept in VCBART is intended to address this gap.

Bayesian Ensemble Methods

BMA (Raftery et al., 1997) forms the posterior predictive distribution as a weighted average over the candidates, where ∆ is any quantity of interest (in this paper, the predicted outcome ŷij for student i in school j), y is the observed data, and the weights p(M k | y) are posterior model probabilities derived from marginal likelihoods.

By treating model uncertainty probabilistically, BMA propagates that uncertainty into prediction interval widths, which is the reason why it achieves near-nominal coverage in the M-closed setting (Madigan & Raftery, 1994). When no candidate is correct, however, marginal likelihoods lose their probabilistic interpretation, and prior sensitivity becomes a concern (Clyde & George, 2004).

Because BMA's coherence depends on the M-closed assumption, an alternative approach is to treat model combination as an optimization problem rather than a probabilistic one. BS (Yao et al., 2018) selects weights on the unit simplex , where w = (w 1 , . . . , w K ) ⊤ is the weight vector, by maximizing the leave-one-out log predictive density summed over the n j students in each of the J clusters, ŵstack = argmax is the predictive density of student i in cluster j under model k when that student is left out of the fit.

The objective rewards the combination that predicts best on average over students, and the predictive distribution it defines is the same mixture of the candidates as in BMA, with different weights. The limitation is that ŵstack is a single vector for all observations, so it cannot express individual-or cluster-level variation in which candidate is relevant.

BHS (Yao et al., 2022) addresses this limitation by replacing fixed weights with covariate-dependent functions. For each model k and observation (i, j), the weights are obtained through a softmax transformation, where is the log-odds for model k, α 0k is the model-specific intercept, and α ℓk scales the contribution of basis function f ℓ (x ij ).

Hierarchical priors on α = {α ℓk } pool information across models. The softmax enforces positivity and the sum-to-one constraint, and the linear predictor inside it restricts which weight surfaces BHS can represent. For example, BHS may not detect that a random slope specification becomes relevant only above a threshold of school SES composition, or that two predictors jointly determine which specification fits best. Both constraints carry over as limitations when the candidate set is collectively incomplete. Related work has used BART directly to estimate input-dependent mixing weights for computer simulators (Yannotty, Santner, Furnstahl, & Pratola, 2024).

The varying coefficient model, reviewed next, provides the theoretical basis for individual-specific weights in the ensemble setting and a nonparametric estimator that overcomes these restrictions.

Varying Coefficient Models (VCM) and BART

The varying coefficient model (Hastie & Tibshirani, 1993) generalizes the conventional linear regression model by allowing coefficients to be functions of moderating variables: where β k (z i ) is the coefficient for predictor k as a function of the moderating variable z i , x ki is the value of predictor k for observation i, and ε i is a zero-mean error term.

Each β k varies with z i rather than being fixed across observations. This model applies in settings where predictor effects differ across subpopulations (Fan & Zhang, 2008). In the model ensemble context, the weight w k (x ij ) plays this role. It is the coefficient on model k's prediction in the ensemble regression, and it varies with individual-and cluster-level characteristics. What BHS attempts through its linear parametric weights is a restricted version of this idea. The restriction to linear forms and the softmax constraint arise from the parametric specification, not from the inferential goal.

Benchmark estimators for varying coefficient models, such as local polynomial regression (Fan & Zhang, 2008) and smoothing splines (Hastie & Tibshirani, 1993), require specifying a single scalar moderating variable and a smoothing bandwidth, making them difficult to apply when multiple student and school characteristics jointly determine model relevance. BART (Chipman, George, & McCulloch, 2010) circumvents both by representing an unknown function f (x) as a sum of H shallow regression trees, where each tree T h partitions the covariate space through binary splits, each leaf is assigned a scalar parameter in µ h , and g(x; T h , µ h ) returns the leaf value associated with x.

Shrinkage priors on the leaf parameters and a preference for shallow trees regularize the fit while allowing the posterior to capture nonlinear patterns across the full predictor vector. Deshpande et al. (2026) extended this to the varying coefficient setting by estimating each β k (z) as a separate BART via Bayesian backfitting, a strategy that scales to high-dimensional moderators without bandwidth selection. This extension makes VCBART applicable to the random effects specification problem, where moderating variables are individual-and cluster-level covariates and the weight functions need to adapt to their joint effects without requiring the analyst to prespecify which combinations matter. BART has also been used to estimate heterogeneous learning trajectories in large-scale assessment data (Huang, 2024), further supporting its suitability here.

Proposed Method for Incorporating VCBART into Model Ensemble

General Picture

As discussed in the Background section, the ensemble weight w k (x ij ) can be viewed as a varying coefficient. It is the coefficient on the prediction from model k in the ensemble regression, and it varies with individual-level and cluster-level characteristics. We use VCBART (Deshpande et al., 2026) to estimate these weights, following the ensemble approach introduced by Huang and Lyu (2025). The problem formulation here differs from prior ensemble work. The candidate models share the same fixed effects and differ only in which predictors carry random slopes. As a result, the weight functions indicate which random-effects specifications are relevant for which students, while the adaptive intercept helps correct the shared prediction error that remains across the candidate models.

Beyond Convex Combinations

The main difference from existing ensemble methods is the adaptive intercept, which avoids imposing a sum-to-one constraint. We write the ensemble prediction for where ŷijk is the point prediction from candidate model k, w k (x ij ) is the weight assigned to model k for covariate profile x ij , and w 0 (x ij ) is the adaptive intercept.

The weights w 1 (x ij ), . . . , w K (x ij ) are not required to sum to one.

The intercept w 0 (x ij ) plays two roles. When the candidate models collectively underrepresent the true random effects structure, their predictions carry a systematic bias.

The intercept reduces that bias by shifting ensemble predictions away from the span of candidate outputs. When w 0 (x ij ) ≈ 0 and k w k (x ij ) ≈ 1, the ensemble closely resembles BHS, signaling that the candidate set is adequate for individuals in that region of covariate space. The raw intercept is not by itself the best diagnostic, however, because in this setting the candidates predict similar values. Suppose every candidate predicts a score of 480 for a given student and let S = k w k (x ij ) denote the weight sum. The ensemble prediction is then w 0 + 480 S. If the ensemble predicts 495, every pair with w0 = 495 -480 S fits the data equally well: w0 = 15 with S = 1, w0 = 255 with S = 0.5, or w0 = 435 with S = 0.125. The data cannot tell these pairs apart, so the posterior of w 0 settles on whichever the prior happens to favour, and a large w 0 does not by itself mean that the candidates are inadequate. What the data do determine is the 15 points by which the ensemble departs from the candidates. The quantity recovers this departure, where m(x ij ) is a reference prediction that lies among the candidate predictions.

In the example, a = (495 -480 S) + (S -1) ×480 = 15 for every pair, so a is invariant to the split between w 0 and the weights.

The second term in (2) removes from w 0 the part that only compensates for a weight sum different from one. We call a(x ij ) the identified adaptive intercept. Any combination of the candidates whose weights sum to one can serve as the reference, such as a single candidate, BS, or BHS; the weights need not be nonnegative, but a combination whose weights sum to c ̸ = 1 predicts c times the candidates' common value, which no candidate predicts, and would shift a by (S -1)(c -1) m. Changing the reference from one admissible m to another m ′ changes a by (S -1)(m -m ′ ), which is small when the candidates agree. We use the BMA prediction because it is the constant-weight baseline against which the ensemble is compared throughout. The true data-generating model is not a valid reference, since its prediction lies outside the candidate span and a would then mix the candidates' shortfall with the ensemble's own error. When the candidates predict nearly the same value, a(x ij ) is simply the ensemble prediction minus the candidates' prediction, in score points. Values near zero across covariate space mean that the candidates are adequate there. Consistently positive values indicate that the ensemble predicts higher outcomes than the candidates for students with that profile, whereas consistently negative values indicate the opposite.

Either pattern marks a region where no candidate adequately captures the data and the candidate model set may need to be reconsidered. The posterior weight w k (x ij ) describes how much model specification k contributes to the prediction for an individual with profile x ij , and the posterior uncertainty around that weight reflects how confidently that assessment can be made.

Heterogeneity therefore enters the ensemble through two different objects. The school random effects enter only through the candidates: each candidate carries between-school heterogeneity in the form of its random slopes, and the candidates differ in which predictor is allowed to vary across schools. The weights enter through the weight functions, which depend on the student's own characteristics, the characteristics of the student's school, and the school identifier, which is included among the inputs to the weight functions. Within a school, the weights can differ between students whose own characteristics differ, so two students in the same school can be predicted by different mixtures of specifications. Across schools, the weights can differ through school characteristics and identity even for students whose own characteristics are alike. A convex combination with constant weights cannot represent either kind of variation, and BHS represents them only through the parametric form chosen for the weights. The identified intercept adds a third layer by capturing how the candidate set as a whole falls short for each profile.

Nonparametric Weight Function Estimation

With the ensemble structure defined, it remains to specify how each weight function is estimated. Each of the K + 1 weight components is modeled as a separate BART.

The input x includes all individual-and cluster-level covariates, and the tree ensemble learns from data how variables and their interactions moderate model relevance without requiring prespecified functional forms. In educational settings, VCBART can detect that a random slope for SES matters more in high-poverty schools, or that immigration-related heterogeneity concentrates in particular school contexts, without any prespecification.

Following Chipman et al. (2010), each tree is kept shallow through a splitting probability of 0.95(1 + d) -2 at depth d, which prevents any single tree from dominating the ensemble. Terminal node parameters µ kh receive independent N (0, σ 2 µ /H) priors, where σ 2 µ controls the overall scale of the weight functions and dividing by H stabilizes their total variance as the number of trees grows. We use H = 200 trees per weight function throughout, the default recommended by Chipman et al. (2010). The error variance σ 2 receives an inverse-gamma prior with shape parameter ν/2 and scale parameter νλ/2, where ν = 5 and λ is set so that the prior places 95% of its mass below the residual standard deviation of a linear model of the outcome on the candidate predictions and the covariates, fitted to the training data. Together these choices regularize the ensemble against overfitting while keeping the weight functions flexible enough to capture heterogeneous patterns of model relevance.

Bayesian Backfitting

All K + 1 weight functions are estimated jointly through Bayesian backfitting (Hastie, Tibshirani, & Friedman, 2009), a Gibbs-like procedure in which each component is updated in turn while all others are held fixed. Joint estimation is essential because the intercept and the model weights both depend on the outcome y ij , and estimating them separately would confound the mean correction captured by w 0 with the relative contributions of the candidate models. The partial residuals R ijk used to update w k disentangle these effects, where 1(•) denotes the indicator function.

For the intercept update (k = 0), all candidate model contributions are removed so that w 0 captures only what the candidates miss collectively. For model weight updates (k ≥ 1), both the intercept and all other model contributions are removed, isolating each specification's marginal contribution from the outcome. Within each MCMC iteration, tree structures are updated via Metropolis-Hastings, leaf parameters via Gibbs sampling, and σ 2 from its conjugate inverse-gamma posterior after all weight functions are updated. We run V = 2,000 iterations with a burn-in of V burn = 1,000 iterations, and retain all post-burn-in samples for inference.

These posterior distributions are the primary inferential output. They describe, for each individual and cluster profile, which random effects specification contributes most to prediction and with what degree of uncertainty. Predictions for new observations are obtained by evaluating Equation (1) at the posterior mean weight functions, and prediction intervals are obtained from the posterior predictive draws, that is, the draws of the ensemble mean plus Gaussian noise with the corresponding draw of σ.

The candidate set in both studies consists of six multilevel models, M1 to M6, which share identical fixed effects but differ in random effects structure. Eight methods are evaluated. Four ensemble methods (VCBART, BHS, BS, BMA) combine predictions from the six candidate models M1-M6. The fifth method, Selected, uses the single candidate with the highest leave-one-out expected log predictive density (ELPD-LOO), which is often how a model specification is chosen in current practice. In the simulation it has the same structure as the true DGM and serves as an oracle; in the empirical study it is the most elaborate specification considered. It is not a candidate model for any ensemble and provides a reference in both studies. All multilevel models are fitted using rstan (Stan Development Team, 2024). BMA weights are pseudo-BMA weights obtained by applying softmax normalization to the summed ELPD-LOO scores across candidate models, computed via the loo package (Vehtari, Gelman, & Gabry, 2017).

BS weights are obtained using loo_model_weights() from the same package with method = "stacking". VCBART uses flexBART (Deshpande, 2025) and soloBART uses BART (Sparapani, Spanbauer, & McCulloch, 2021). Full implementation details are provided in the Appendix, and reproducible code is available in the supplementary GitHub repository.

Two criteria are used to evaluate the methods. Predictive accuracy is measured by the root mean square error (RMSE) for both in-sample (training) and out-of-sample (held-out test) observations, where N = j n j is the total number of observations.

Uncertainty calibration is measured by the empirical coverage of 95% prediction intervals, defined as the proportion of observations whose true values fall within [L ij , U ij ], the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the posterior predictive distribution. For every method the interval is a posterior predictive interval, so that intervals are comparable across methods. A method whose intervals miss the 95% target may not support valid inference regardless of its point prediction accuracy.

Differences between VCBART and M7 are tested with a paired-samples t test and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test across the ten shared folds. For the PISA data, the t interval and p value are computed from the fold means and standard deviations. Computational cost is reported in minutes of wall-clock time per fold. All fits ran as separate jobs in a Docker container on the high-throughput computing pool, with four CPU cores for each method.

Uncertainty Calibration

Tables 5 and 6 report coverage of 95% prediction intervals. Every method except VCBART achieves near-nominal out-of-sample coverage (94-95%) in every condition. For BMA, BS, and BHS this holds because their predictive distributions are mixtures of the candidates' posterior predictive distributions, and each candidate is itself well calibrated for this outcome. The mixture must be sampled as such: averaging the candidates' posterior draws with the same weights gives the same point prediction but shrinks the predictive variance whenever the weights are spread over several candidates, and the resulting intervals undercover.

With mixture sampling the three model-averaging methods are indistinguishable in calibration. VCBART overcovers at σ = 0.1, where its intervals are far wider than the tiny residual noise, and undercovers mildly at σ ≥ 10, with out-of-sample coverage of 91-93% that declines slowly as the ICC rises. Its in-sample coverage is within 1.5 points of nominal in those conditions, so the shortfall out of sample reflects an ensemble mean fitted closely to the training students rather than a residual variance that is too small. The intervals VCBART produces are also the narrowest, by 4-9% at ICC 0.10 and by 15-18% at ICC 0.30, which is relevant when a decision depends on the interval and not only on the point prediction, a point we return to in the empirical study.

Empirical Study on PISA 2018 Mathematics A Prediction Problem from Educational Practice

The PISA 2018 mathematics scale places students on proficiency levels, and Level 2 (a score of 420.07 or above) is the OECD's baseline for the skills needed to participate in society (OECD, 2019). To target support to students at risk of falling below this baseline, a school system needs each student's predicted score and probability of falling below the cutoff, based on their background and the school they attend. The prediction is made for a known student in a known school, so the school's estimated effect is part of the forecast.

Which random-effects specification is used changes how the school effect is pooled with the population, which student characteristics modify that pooling, and therefore which students are flagged. We use this scenario to compare the methods on the PISA data. The quantities of interest are the predicted score, its 95% prediction interval, and the posterior probability that the student scores below Level 2, all evaluated on held-out students.

Data, Candidate Models, and Design

We apply all eight methods to the U.S. mathematics data from PISA 2018 (OECD, 2019). PISA is a triennial international assessment of 15-year-old students coordinated by the OECD and provides one of the few large-scale datasets with sufficient within-school sample sizes to support multilevel analyses with random slopes. The U.S. sample contains 4,838 students in 164 schools. Variable selection using lasso, random forest, and XGBoost consistently identified the same nine important predictors. These are individual socioeconomic status (ESCS), school-level socioeconomic composition (SCHOOLESCS), school bullying climate (SCHOOLBULLYING), school immigration composition (SCHOOLIMMIG), teacher interest (TEACHINT), learning adaptivity (ADAPTIVITY), experience of being bullied (BEINGBULLIED), staff shortage (STAFFSHORT), and immigration status (IMMIG). These span individual, family, classroom, and institutional influences on mathematics achievement (Karakus et al., 2023), and overlap with predictors examined in prior Bayesian ensemble analyses of PISA data (Huang & Kaplan, 2025; Huang & Lyu, 2025). Listwise deletion on these nine predictors, driven mainly by missing responses on the bullying and immigration items, leaves 3,647 students in 134 schools, the sample used in both studies. The outcome is the first plausible value of the mathematics scale.

The six candidate models have identical fixed effects (all nine predictors) but differ in random effects structure, mirroring the simulation design. The six candidates are close in leave-one-out predictive density on these data, with mean ELPD-LOO ranging from -3,750 (M5) to -3,747 (M2 and M3), and their out-of-sample RMSEs lie between 77.1 (M2) and 77.3 (M4 and M5). The specification with the highest ELPD-LOO, which the Selected method uses, changes across folds: M2 in five folds, M4 in three, and M1 and M3 in one each. No single random-slope specification is clearly preferred by the data, which is the situation in which averaging over specifications is meant to help. Table 9 presents parameter estimates for M1-M7 from fits to the full sample. Fixed effects are stable across models while the random slope's standard deviation ranges from 4.5 (M5, BEINGBULLIED) to 9.2 (M2, ESCS) and the random intercept-slope correlation ranges from -0.48 to 0.27. In M7 the five slope standard deviations are of the same order (4.0 to 9.6), and the residual standard deviation falls from 76.4 in M1 to 75.2, so the slopes together explain only a small part of the within-school variance. The between-school share of variance in these data depends on what is conditioned on. An intercept-only model gives an ICC of 0.18, within the range reported for U.S. achievement data (Hedges & Hedberg, 2007). The nine predictors include two school composition measures, and after conditioning on them the residual ICC implied by M1, ω 2 0 /(ω 2 0 + σ 2 ), is 0.06, below the lowest design ICC of the simulation. The design ICC is likewise defined net of the fixed effects, so the comparison is like for like, but the five random slopes in M7 add between-school variation that an intercept-only ICC does not count. Both quantities vary across PISA countries, subjects, and predictor sets, so we treat the PISA data as one point in the space of between-school variability rather than as a fixed level. This variation confirms that each predictor generates a qualitatively different pattern of between-school heterogeneity, so each candidate carries different information that the ensemble can use to go beyond what any single specification provides. M7 (all five random slopes simultaneously) serves as the fully specified benchmark. We evaluate all methods using 10-fold cross-validation with students split 80%/20% within each school, ensuring all schools appear in both training and test sets, and held-out students are predicted with the posterior random effects of their own school as in the simulation.

The relatively small differences among the candidate-based methods suggest that selecting or combining the available candidates yields limited improvements in predictive accuracy, whereas VCBART provides additional flexibility beyond these specifications. Turning to uncertainty quantification, all methods except VCBART achieve near-nominal out-of-sample coverage (94.6-95.4%), with interval widths ranging from 294 to 307 points. VCBART produces the narrowest intervals (283 points), approximately 6.3% narrower than M7, but its out-of-sample coverage is lower at 93.3%. Its in-sample coverage of 96.3% is comparable to the other methods. Thus, VCBART's improved predictive accuracy and narrower intervals come with a modest reduction in out-of-sample coverage.

This pattern is consistent with the simulation findings at σ = 50 and 100, where the candidate set is collectively incomplete and the adaptive intercept provides additional flexibility.

Table 7 and Figure 2 further illustrate this flexibility. On the PISA data, the weight sum is 1.37, and the identified adaptive intercept has a mean absolute value of approximately 15 points, larger than in any simulation condition and about 0.15 of the outcome standard deviation. Its relationship with ESCS is relatively flat over most of the range but becomes negative above an ESCS of approximately 2, where the candidates overpredict the few highest-SES students by about 20-30 points. Such nonlinear SES-achievement relationships have been documented in the school effectiveness literature (Willms, 2010). Because this pattern is not represented by the candidate specifications, the adaptive intercept provides a correction.

The weight functions also reveal heterogeneity across student groups. Figure 3 displays the posterior mean weights ŵ1 to ŵ6 for the 728 held-out students in fold 1, grouped by immigration status. The weights are nearly constant within each group but differ substantially between groups. No other predictor has a correlation exceeding 0.6 with any weight, and most correlations are below 0.3. These results suggest that immigration status is an important covariate along which the relative contributions of the candidate models vary.

However, because the weights are not constrained to sum to one and are not separately identified from w 0 when candidate predictions are similar, they should not be interpreted as model shares. The weight sums also differ between immigrant and non-immigrant students (1.12 and 1.27, respectively). Overall, the ensemble tends to increase predictions for students in more advantaged schools and decrease them for students in less advantaged schools, capturing a between-school pattern not represented by the individual candidate specifications. Discussion

Findings and Summary

The simulation results illustrate how the relative performance of the methods depends on the completeness of the candidate model set. When the outcome noise is negligible, the candidates approximate the data-generating process closely, corresponding to a practically M-closed setting. In this regime, BMA, BS, BHS, and model selection perform comparably to the oracle, while VCBART introduces unnecessary flexibility and estimation variability. As within-school noise and between-school heterogeneity increase, the candidate set becomes increasingly inadequate, approaching an M-open setting. Under these conditions, VCBART generally achieves the best out-of-sample predictive accuracy among the non-oracle methods, with improvements that become more pronounced as the ICC and residual variability increase. Its adaptive intercept allows the ensemble to capture systematic patterns not represented by the candidates. However, these improvements come with somewhat narrower prediction intervals and lower coverage. In contrast, BMA, BS, and BHS maintain near-nominal coverage when their predictive distributions are constructed as mixtures of candidate predictions. These findings suggest that VCBART is most beneficial when the available specifications are collectively incomplete, whereas conventional model averaging and selection remain competitive when the candidate models adequately represent the outcome structure.

The empirical analysis of PISA 2018 mathematics achievement supports these findings. VCBART achieves the lowest in-sample and out-of-sample RMSE, outperforming both the candidate-based methods and soloBART. It also produces the narrowest prediction intervals, although its out-of-sample coverage is slightly below the nominal level.

In contrast, BMA, BS, BHS, and model selection exhibit similar predictive performance and near-nominal coverage. This similarity reflects the substantial overlap among the candidate specifications, which share the same fixed effects and random intercept and differ primarily in their random slopes. When the candidates generate similar predictions, selecting or averaging among them offers limited improvements. VCBART provides additional flexibility by capturing systematic variation beyond the candidate specifications, including nonlinear relationships with socioeconomic status and differences across student and school characteristics.

An important contribution of VCBART is its ability to accommodate individual-level heterogeneity through covariate-dependent weights and an adaptive intercept.

Unlike conventional model averaging, which assigns a common set of weights to all observations, VCBART allows the contributions of candidate models to vary across student profiles. In the empirical application, immigration status is strongly associated with the estimated weight functions, indicating that the ensemble combines the candidate specifications differently across student groups. Meanwhile, the identified adaptive intercept reveals systematic discrepancies between the candidate predictions and observed outcome patterns, particularly along student and school socioeconomic dimensions. These findings illustrate how VCBART can capture subgroup-specific patterns and nonlinear relationships that are not explicitly represented in the candidate models. However, because individual weights are not separately identified when candidate predictions are similar, they should be interpreted jointly with the identified adaptive intercept rather than as independent measures of model importance. Together, the simulation and empirical findings highlight a trade-off between predictive flexibility and uncertainty calibration. VCBART offers improved predictive accuracy when the candidate set is incomplete, but its narrower prediction intervals may result in modest undercoverage. Conventional model averaging and selection provide more stable uncertainty quantification when the candidates perform similarly. Importantly, these differences can affect substantive decisions. In the PISA application, VCBART and the multilevel-based methods disagree on approximately 8% of student classifications relative to the proficiency threshold. Thus, even modest improvements in predictive accuracy can lead to meaningful differences in individual-level decisions.

Applications and Implications

The proposed framework is particularly relevant to educational and psychological research, where heterogeneous relationships are common and the appropriate model specification is often uncertain. In school effectiveness research, for example, the association between student characteristics and achievement may vary across schools (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002; Willms, 2010). Conventional approaches typically select a particular random-effects structure or average predictions using fixed model weights.

VCBART instead allows the relative contributions of candidate specifications to vary with student and school characteristics, while the adaptive intercept accounts for systematic patterns not captured by the candidates. This flexibility is useful when researchers cannot confidently identify a single specification that adequately represents the underlying heterogeneity.

The empirical application illustrates this potential through socioeconomic status and immigration background. The estimated weight functions vary substantially by immigration status, while the adaptive intercept captures nonlinear socioeconomic patterns and differences associated with school composition. These findings suggest that the relevance of candidate specifications and the inadequacy of their combined predictions may arise along different dimensions of the covariate space. Importantly, the weights are not constrained to be positive or sum to one, and their individual magnitudes may not be identifiable when candidate predictions are highly correlated. Consequently, interpretation should focus on the overall ensemble predictions, the identified adaptive intercept, and systematic patterns in the weight functions rather than treating individual weights as model probabilities.

The broader methodological contribution extends beyond educational assessment.

Similar challenges arise in precision medicine, psychology, and other fields where heterogeneous populations are represented by competing models or latent subgroups. Hard model selection or subgroup assignment may overlook uncertainty about which specification is appropriate for a particular individual, while conventional model averaging assumes that the relative contributions of candidate models remain constant across observations. VCBART provides a flexible alternative by combining covariate-dependent weighting with an adaptive correction for shared model inadequacy. This approach builds on the varying coefficient framework (Hastie & Tibshirani, 1993) and offers opportunities for extending Bayesian nonparametric methods to more complex educational and psychological measurement settings (Huang & Ghosh, 2025).

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations warrant further investigation. First, VCBART may introduce unnecessary estimation variability when the candidate models already provide an adequate approximation to the data-generating process. In such settings, conventional model averaging or selection can achieve better predictive accuracy and calibration with lower computational cost. More adaptive regularization, such as shrinkage priors on the weight functions and adaptive intercept, could help VCBART reduce its complexity when the data provide little evidence of candidate-model inadequacy.

Second, improvements in predictive accuracy are accompanied by some loss of uncertainty calibration. Both the simulations and empirical application show that VCBART tends to produce narrower prediction intervals with below-nominal out-of-sample coverage. Stronger regularization of the tree ensemble or alternative procedures for calibrating predictive uncertainty may help address this trade-off. In addition, the weight functions and the raw adaptive intercept are not separately identified when candidate predictions are highly correlated. Although the identified adaptive intercept provides an interpretable summary of the correction, alternative parameterizations that explicitly separate baseline predictions from candidate-specific deviations may improve identifiability and interpretation.

Third, the simulation design considers a limited range of within-school and between-school variability under Gaussian errors and a two-level hierarchical structure.

Future research should examine more complex data-generating processes, including nonlinear covariate effects, cross-level interactions, non-Gaussian random effects, and higher levels of between-school heterogeneity. Extensions to binary, ordinal, and count outcomes would also broaden the applicability of VCBART to educational and psychological research.

Finally, the empirical application has limitations related to the PISA data and the prediction setting. PISA plausible values reflect measurement uncertainty arising from the assessment design and scaling model (OECD, 2019; Rutkowski, Gonzalez, Joncas, & von Davier, 2010; von Davier, Gonzalez, & Mislevy, 2009). The present analysis uses a single plausible value rather than combining results across all ten, so the reported uncertainty does not fully account for this measurement component. Moreover, the cross-validation procedure evaluates predictions for held-out students within schools represented in the training data, rather than generalization to entirely new schools. Future studies should incorporate multiple plausible values, complex sampling features, and school-level cross-validation to assess the robustness and generalizability of the proposed approach.

Further investigation of the adaptive intercept as a formal diagnostic of candidate-model inadequacy, in connection with existing Bayesian model-checking approaches (Vehtari et al., 2017), would also strengthen its inferential utility.

Candidate Model Fitting

All six candidate multilevel models are fitted using rstan (Stan Development Team, 2024) with 3,000 MCMC iterations (2,000 warmup), 4 chains, adapt_delta = 0.85, and max_treedepth = 11. M1 uses a random intercept only, and M2-M6 each add one random slope (for x 1 , x 5 , x 6 , x 7 , and x 9 respectively) with an LKJ(1) correlation prior. The outcome is standardized with training-set moments inside the Stan program and predictions are returned on the original scale. Held-out students are predicted with the posterior random effects of their own school. Pointwise leave-one-out log predictive densities are computed from the training-set log-likelihood of each candidate with the loo package (Vehtari et al., 2017). Convergence is assessed by R < 1.01 and effective sample size. M7 uses the same settings with adapt_delta = 0.90 and max_treedepth = 12, and includes all five random slopes simultaneously with a 6 × 6 LKJ(1) correlation prior.
