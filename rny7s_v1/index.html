To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

We found 1 imprecise p value out of 22 detected p values.

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p < .05 Means within a row that do not share a common subscript differ significantly at p < .05 (Tukey HSD). ***p < .001.

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Can a lack of racial diversity in training data produce human-like forms of AI face classification bias? We examined the causal effect of racially-imbalanced training data on face classification model disparities and whether these disparities resemble three well-documented human biases-the cross-race effect, hypodescent, and the gendered-race effect. We further examined whether balanced training data mitigate these disparities. In three experiments, we trained deep Vision Transformer models for face classification on 16,000 face images, experimentally manipulating racial composition while holding other dataset features and training procedures constant. Study 1 showed that models trained on low-diversity (majority-White) datasets classified White faces more accurately than Black, East Asian, and Indian faces, resembling the human cross-race effect. In Study 2, using parametrically-morphed Black-White biracial face images to estimate race-classification thresholds, lower-diversity models required more Black than White content to reach this threshold, yet represented biracial faces with more Black content. Lastly, Study 3 revealed a gendered-race effect: regardless of training set imbalance, models classified Black men more accurately than Black women, but East Asian women more accurately than East Asian men. The use of racially-balanced training data nearly eliminated disparities in monoracial classification, reduced bias in biracial classification, but it did not alter the gendered-race effect. These findings show that training data composition causally shapes algorithmic performance in ways that both mimic and diverge from human biases. We discuss implications for AI-based discrimination and both the promise and limitations of data balancing as a strategy to mitigate algorithmic bias.

[div-01]

Artificial intelligence (AI) systems are increasingly used to classify socially meaningful human categories from facial images, with applications ranging from identity verification and surveillance to policing, security, and marketing (1) (2) (3)). Yet face classification systems have been found to exhibit systematic racial disparities, including lower accuracy for women and people with darker skin tones (4, 5). Such disparities can be especially harmful when AI outputs inform high-stakes decisions, such as in policing (6) (7) (8), and particularly when they align with existing patterns of human racial bias (9, 10). To date, however, the causal impact of training data imbalance on racial disparities in face classification, and their resemblance to human social perception, has not been investigated. Here, we experimentally tested whether racial imbalances in training data produce racial disparities in face classification model performance and, if so, whether these disparities mimic well-known human biases in face categorization.

Patterns of racial bias in AI face categorization systems

Demographic characteristics of faces-including race and gender-are known to influence the performance of AI systems for face detection, recognition, and classification, producing systematic disparities in model output (11) (12) (13) (14) (15). These disparities have been attributed, in part, to racial imbalances in the datasets used to train classification models (4, 5, (16) (17) (18) (19). For example, many existing benchmark datasets (e.g., LFW+, IJB-A, Adience, CelebA) have been reported to contain high proportions of White faces (e.g., 80% or more) and uneven representations of other racial/ethnic groups, in addition to imbalances on other demographic characteristics (4, 5, 20, 21). Consistent with this account, models trained on existing majority-White datasets have been found to produce racial disparities in gender classification-that is, lower accuracy for people of color, particularly for women (4, 5). By contrast, when models were trained on more racially-balanced datasets, these disparities were reduced. However, while informative, the existing evidence is limited because it has not established whether these disparities arise from racial composition specifically or from correlated properties of the datasets being compared. This is because the datasets used in prior work differ on dimensions besides race or ethnicity, such as image quality, sample size, annotation procedures, sampling context, preprocessing, and other demographic characteristics (e.g., age). As a result, differences associated with such datasets cannot be causally attributed to racial imbalance alone. Rather, a causal attribution would require an experimental approach, in which racial balance is manipulated while other dataset features are held constant. Furthermore, most studies on the effects of dataset imbalance have, to date, focused on gender classification (4, 5). Although some research has observed performance decrements in race classification for minority faces trained on White-dominant data (22), the effects of dataset imbalance on race classification-and whether these effects resemble human patterns of racial bias-have not been systematically investigated.

Face classification biases in humans and AI

If racially imbalanced training data produce disparities in model performance, to what extent do these disparities resemble common human biases in race perception? Psychology research on human face categorization has identified three well-documented patterns of racial bias: the cross-race effect, perceptual hypodescent, and the gendered-race effect. Each captures a distinct pattern of categorization and therefore provides a social psychological benchmark for evaluating the effects of training data composition on AI face classification.

Cross-race effect. The cross-race effect (or other-race effect) describes the tendency for people to recognize ingroup faces more accurately than outgroup faces (23, 24)-an effect attributed to both perceptual expertise and preferences for ingroup members (25, 26). In AI systems, a cross-race pattern has been observed for facial recognition: models developed in Western countries performed more accurately for White faces than East Asian faces, whereas the opposite pattern was found for models developed in East Asian countries (27). In the context of race classification, existing studies suggest a pattern analogous to the cross-race effect (22), such that the use of majority-White training data would lead models to classify White faces more accurately than faces from underrepresented racial groups.

Perceptual hypodescent.

Human racial categorization also exhibits a pattern of hypodescent: the tendency to categorize people of mixed racial ancestry according to their lower-status racial group (28). For example, White Americans tend to perceive Black-White biracial faces as Black rather than White (29-31)-a pattern linked to intergroup motives for power and resources (32, 33). Social psychological research has identified two expressions of hypodescent in humans: a classification bias, in which White participants are more likely to classify a biracial face as Black than as White (29, 33), and a representation bias, whereby participants' mental image of a Black-White biracial face is more similar to a Black than White face (34, 35). In the context of AI, research using a visual semantic model (Contrastive Language Image Pretraining; CLIP) to assign racial labels to face images found that biracial faces tended to be labeled according to their lower-status identity (36). In terms of face classification, a computational analogue would be a systematic classification of biracial faces as belonging to the lower-status racial group. Testing this possibility requires moving beyond monoracial classification to the classification of biracial faces that vary systematically in racial composition.

Gendered-race effect.

Race also shapes human gender categorization through the genderedrace effect-an intersectional expression of racial stereotypes in gender perception (37). For example, consistent with US stereotypes of Black people as aggressive and dominant and East Asian people as docile and submissive (38, 39), White American perceivers are more likely to categorize gender-ambiguous Black faces as male and gender-ambiguous East Asian faces as female (37, 40). In AI face classification, a corresponding pattern would be reflected in an interaction between race and gender in gender classification accuracy: specifically, greater accuracy for Black men than Black women, but greater accuracy for Asian women than Asian men. This pattern would expand on previously-reported performance decrements for women of color (4) to place it within a broader pattern of racial stereotype effects on gender classification.

Together, these three bias benchmarks from psychological research capture distinct dimensions of social categorization: differences in monoracial classification accuracy across racial groups, the categorization of biracial identities, and interactive effects of race and gender on gender classification. Although AI classification systems might not reproduce these human biases exactly, an assessment of where their performance mimics or diverges from patterns of human social perception can help to identify specific disparity patterns and inform their potential influence on human users.

Results

The present research tested whether racial composition in training data causally shapes face classification performance and whether the resulting disparities converge with or diverge from established patterns of human social bias. In each of three studies, we manipulated the racial composition of the training data for White faces relative to Black, East Asian, and Indian faces, using 27,040 randomly-drawn images and corresponding labels from FairFace (5). From this sample, we created four separate training datasets, each comprising 16,000 images, which varied systematically in racial composition while holding gender balance and proportion of minority faces constant within each condition (Figure 1). This design allowed us to compare an extreme White-dominance condition (94% White)-designed to approximate publicly-available datasets shown previously to produce biased classification (4, 5)-to conditions with progressively increasing racial balance (75%, 50%, and 25% White, i.e., fully-balanced). To build classification models, we used a Vision Transformer (ViT) pretrained on ImageNet-21k and fine-tuned it end-to-end on each manipulated training dataset, replacing the final classification layer with a task-specific linear head predicting either race (Studies 1-2) or gender (Study 3). To account for variability due to sampling and stochastic optimization, each condition was trained with five independent random seeds. For each seed, a new training dataset was generated according to the condition-specific demographic constraints (balanced versus imbalanced race distributions), ensuring that differences between conditions reflected controlled changes in data composition rather than idiosyncrasies of individual images. All models were evaluated on a balanced held-out test set containing 6,024 face images with equal numbers of images for each Race × Gender combination, such that any differences in accuracy could not be attributed to subgroup frequencies in the test data (see Methods).

Study 1

In Study 1, we tested the effect of dataset diversity on race classification accuracy (proportion of correct race predictions out of all race predictions), using the experimental manipulation and procedures for model training and evaluation described above. A seed-blocked factorial ANOVA produced significant main effects of both race, F(3, 60) = 109.67, p < .001, and training balance condition, F(3, 60) = 62.63, p < .001, indicating higher overall accuracy for White faces and for models trained on balanced datasets. However, these effects were qualified by the Condition × Race interaction, F(9, 60) = 21.17, p < .001 (Figure 2), indicating that race classification disparities depended on dataset balance. To characterize this interaction, we tested simple effects of race within each condition (Table 1). The analyses revealed a large classification accuracy disparity in the extreme imbalance condition, which incrementally decreased as datasets became more balanced. In the fullybalanced condition, this disparity was fully mitigated among White, Black, and East Asian faces, with a relatively small disparity remaining for Indian faces. Notably, classification accuracy was consistently lowest for Indian faces, potentially suggesting greater variability in the images or annotator labels, among other possible factors.

In addition to reducing mean classification accuracy for underrepresented racial groups, training data imbalance was associated with greater variability across independently trained models. In the balanced condition, variability was small and comparable across racial groups (White: SD = .011; Black: SD = .007; East Asian: SD = .008; Indian: SD = .011). Under extreme White dominance, variability remained low for White faces (SD = .005) but increased substantially for Black (SD = .067), East Asian (SD = .084), and Indian faces (SD = .096). The intermediate conditions showed a graded pattern: SDs for underrepresented groups ranged from .013 to .018 under moderate White dominance and from .016 to .034 under strong White dominance. This greater variability may have reflected both the smaller number of available training images and variability in the images randomly selected for each seed. This pattern suggests an effect of imbalance on model stability, in addition to classification accuracy, particularly when imbalance creates categories with relatively scarce data.

These results provide experimental evidence that training data composition causally shapes racial disparities in face classification. Models trained on extreme White-dominant datasets classified White faces more accurately than faces from underrepresented racial groups, analogous to the human cross-race effect. These disparities decreased as training datasets became more racially balanced and were largely eliminated.

For each condition, across test images, classification probabilities for Black and White categories were obtained and used to compute each model's point of subjective equality (PSE)-the point along the morph spectrum at which a face is equally likely to be classified as Black or White. Results indicated that race-classification thresholds differed by training set imbalance. A repeated-measures ANOVA of per-spectrum PSE scores revealed a significant main effect of training balance condition, F(3, 1137) = 1102.29, p < .001. Although the balanced model's threshold was closer to .50 than other conditions, it was nonetheless significantly above the point of compositional parity. These results demonstrate that models trained on datasets with greater White dominance can result in a Black-White race classification threshold that requires greater Black than White racial composition. This pattern contrasts with the hypodescent classification effect observed in humans and suggests that AI systems would be more likely to identify biracial faces as Black than as White-a pattern of bias that may have different implications for discrimination than in humans.

It is notable, however, that hypodescent in humans is also expressed in mental representations of biracial faces as being more Black than White (34, 35). An implication of the model classification pattern is that it suggests that White-dominant models-particularly the extreme model-represent biracial faces as more Black in composition than biracial faces identified by the balanced model.

All four conditions yielded PSEs substantially above .5, indicating a classification boundary requiring greater Black than White content-a pattern that contrasts with human hypodescent effects in race categorization. However, this threshold was higher for models trained on imbalanced than balanced datasets. Additionally, as a consequence of these model threshold differences, White-dominant models appeared to represent biracial faces with greater Black than White content than the balanced model-a pattern that converges with hypodescent in humans' mental representations of biracial faces.

Study 3

In Study 3, we asked whether a racial imbalance in training data affects gender classificationthat is, whether models exhibit a gendered-race effect, such that gender classification performance varies by race in a manner consistent with racial stereotypes. The same data and model architecture were used as in Study 1, except that we trained classification models to predict binary gender (female, male).

A factorial ANOVA with condition, race, and gender as fixed factors, and seed included as a blocking factor, produced significant main effects of condition, F(3, 124) = 7.96, p < .001, race, F(3, 124) = 355.98, p < .001, and gender, F(1, 124) = 7.89, p = .006 (Table S1 in SI). Of these, the main effect of condition indicated that overall gender accuracy improved as models became more balanced. Importantly, these main effects were qualified by a significant Race × Gender interaction, F(3, 124) = 66.02, p < .001, such that gender classification performance varied as a function of race. A significant three-way interaction, F(9, 124) = 2.14, p = .030, further suggested that the effect of race on gender classification varied by the degree of dataset imbalance (Figure 6; see Table S2 in SI for effects within condition).

To conduct a more focused test of the gendered-race effect, we reran the ANOVA while including only Black and East Asian faces. This analysis revealed a significant Race × Gender interaction, F(1, 60) = 119.25, p < .001. Simple-effect analyses indicated the gendered-race pattern: for Black faces, males (M = .911, SD = .013) were classified more accurately than females (M = .869, SD = .009), t(60) = 5.31, p < .001, whereas the reverse was found for East Asian faces, such that females (M = .944, SD = .009) were classified more accurately than males (M = .921, SD = .009), t(60) = 2.91, p = .005. However, this gendered-race pattern was not significantly moderated by dataset imbalance (three-way interaction: F(3, 60) = 2.16, p = .102). Study 3 therefore revealed a prominent gendered-race effect across all face classification models. Furthermore, gender classification models did not just perform worse for underrepresented racial groups or for a specific intersectional category (e.g., Black women); rather, gender classification performance showed a pattern of bias consistent with US racial stereotypes that associate Black people with more masculine traits and East Asian people with more feminine traits. Unlike the race classification effects in Studies 1 and 2, the use of raciallybalanced training data did not significantly reduce the gendered-race effect in face classification performance.

Discussion

We investigated whether racial composition in AI training data shapes face classification patterns and whether such patterns resemble known human biases in social categorization. By manipulating dataset racial balance while controlling for image source, training set size, gender balance, and model parameters, we examined computational analogues of three welldocumented patterns of human racial bias in face categorization-the cross-race, hypodescent, and gendered-race effects (42). We found that training data composition can causally alter the structure of classification behavior, producing patterns that both converge and diverge with common biases in human social perception.

Study 1 showed that models trained on datasets with disproportionately White faces, like many publicly-available datasets, performed much more accurately for White faces than Black, East Asian, and Indian faces-a pattern akin to the human cross-race effect. However, these disparities decreased progressively with greater racial balance and were largely eliminated in the fully-balanced condition. These results provide experimental evidence that racial imbalances in training data can causally produce disparities in race classification.

Study 2 extended this approach to the classification of racially ambiguous faces. Dataset imbalance again impacted performance, shifting the decision boundary for racial classification, but in a direction opposite to the bias observed in White participants. That is, whereas human perceivers tend to classify Black-White biracial faces as Black, the models here tended to classify biracial faces as White-an effect that was strongest for the extreme White-dominant model. However, as a consequence of these classification thresholds, imbalanced models appeared to represent biracial faces as having greater Black content (i.e., appeared more Black) than the balanced model-a pattern mimicking a different human expression of hypodescent whereby mental representations of biracial faces are more Black than White (34, 35). These findings suggest that depending on how an AI face classification system is used, its results could parallel or contrast with human hypodescent bias.

Study 3 examined the effect of racial imbalance on gender classification. Across conditions, models more accurately classified the gender of Black male faces than Black female faces, and of East Asian female faces than East Asian male faces-a specific interaction pattern resembling the gendered-race effect in humans in which racial stereotypes influence gender categorization (37, 40). This finding suggests that prior observations of diminished gender classification accuracy for Black women may reflect a broader intersectional pattern involving multiple racial groups (4). Although racial balancing improved overall gender classification accuracy, it did not significantly alter the specific gendered-race pattern observed for Black and East Asian faces. Thus, the origins of this pattern remain unclear, but may be due to annotator bias in the labeling of training images, systematic features of the images themselves, or other aspects of model training.

Together, these studies reveal both the value and limits of using human social cognition as a framework for understanding algorithmic bias. The cross-race and gendered-race patterns in our models resembled established human biases, whereas the biracial classification thresholds diverged from human perceptual hypodescent. These similarities and differences suggest that examining AI outputs through the lens of human psychology can reveal distinct forms of disparity that may be overlooked by aggregate measures of model accuracy. At the same time, similarities between human and AI classification patterns should not be taken as evidence of shared underlying mechanisms. Human racial categorization is shaped by factors such as perceptual experience, stereotypes, and intergroup motivation, whereas AI classification reflects statistical regularities in training data and the optimization processes used to fit model parameters.

These findings also raise questions about how algorithmic disparities might interact with human judgment. Existing psychological research has demonstrated that people are more receptive to information that confirms their existing expectations and stereotypes, influencing consequential judgments such as eyewitness evaluations and assessments of job candidates (24, 43, 44). Research on humans' use of AI finds similar patterns of confirmation bias (9, 45, 46). Thus, if AI systems produce outputs that align with stereotypical perceptions and expectancies, users may be less likely to detect or correct for these biases. Though not the aim of the present work, identifying the effect of these bias patterns on human decision making is an important next step toward determining its impact on AI-based discrimination.

Regarding bias mitigation, our studies suggest that balanced training data can reduce some forms of algorithmic bias but not others. For AI systems designed for single-category race classification, as in Study 1, balanced race representation may effectively mitigate bias. But when the relationship between racial composition of training data and the classification objective was less direct-such as when classifying mixed-race identities or gender in Studies 2 and 3-dataset balance did not eliminate disparities. As such, additional approaches warrant investigation, including those that target model training procedures, such as efforts to prevent hidden correlations between social categories represented in training data, as well as psychology-based interventions, such as procedures to improve accuracy and reduce bias in human annotation (6, (47) (48) (49) (50).

Our findings highlight a broader risk of systems that impose rigid categorical identities on individuals. Classifiers that assign people to a single race or gender can misclassify those who do not fit neatly into one category (51). Such systems may also reproduce and reinforce the categorical frameworks through which social differences are constructed and reinforced, potentially contributing to discrimination and inequality (52). Future research should therefore examine not only how to improve classification within existing frameworks, but also whether systems that accommodate multidimensional or uncertain identities can reduce the harms associated with rigid categorization.

Our conclusions should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, we examined Whitemajority training datasets, given the prevalence of White-dominant datasets and their relevance to racial disparities in AI. However, bias-related effects may differ under other configurations of demographic imbalance. Second, the gendered-race pattern we observed concerns Black and East Asian faces and their associated stereotypes in a US cultural context, yet different patterns may emerge in other cultural settings. Third, while our experimental design isolated effects of training data composition within our model training pipeline, other factors-such as FairFace labels, ImageNet pretraining, image preprocessing, sampling procedures, and model architecture-could also affect classification outcomes. Lastly, our design does not disentangle the effects of racial group proportions from the absolute number of minority-group images, because these features vary together as the composition of a fixedsize dataset changes. Future research could manipulate these factors independently to identify their respective contributions.

More broadly, our findings illustrate the value of an integrated computational-psychological approach to algorithmic bias, treating AI systems as products of machine learning processes embedded in human social contexts (53) (54) (55). Psychological theory provides a framework for identifying and interpreting patterns of algorithmic behavior that may not be captured by aggregate performance metrics alone (56). By examining where AI classification patterns converge with and diverge from human social biases, this approach can clarify how algorithmic disparities arise and contribute to discrimination, identify the limits of existing mitigation strategies, and guide future research on the societal impacts of AI.

Materials and Methods

Image Dataset. 27,040 unique faces were sampled from FairFace (5). Sampling was based on FairFace image labels, annotated by Amazon Mechanical Turk workers who, in 2020, were USbased and primarily White (57). From this pool, we constructed four training datasets of 16,000 images each, comprising unique White-, Black-, East Asian-, and Indian-labeled faces across four conditions that varied in their proportion of White faces (94%, 75%, 50%, 25%), with equal proportions of the three non-White racial groups and balanced gender within race. To include a sufficient number of images for each condition, East Asian and Southeast Asian categories were combined, and Middle Eastern, Latino, and "Other" were excluded. This sample size (16,000) was chosen because it was the largest possible sample that permitted controlled manipulations of racial imbalance while preserving gender balance within race.

Classification Models.

Vision Transformer (ViT-Base/16) models (58), pretrained on ImageNet-21k, were fine-tuned end-to-end on the manipulated training datasets. The original classification layer was replaced with a newly initialized linear classification head predicting race (Studies 1 and 2) or gender (Study 3). All images were converted to RGB and processed using the pretrained model's image processor, which resized images to 224 × 224 pixels and applied the corresponding rescaling and normalization procedures. No data augmentation was used. To account for variability due to data sampling and stochastic optimisation, five models were trained independently within each experimental condition, each using a separately sampled training dataset drawn from the FairFace training pool according to the conditionspecific demographic constraints.

Models were trained for up to 10 epochs using the AdamW optimizer, a learning rate of 6 × 10⁻⁵, a batch size of 64, and cross-entropy loss, with a cosine learning-rate schedule and 10% warmup. The checkpoint achieving the highest macro-F1 score on the balanced validation set was retained for subsequent analyses. Model training was conducted on the Snellius supercomputer using one NVIDIA A100 GPU, eight CPU cores, and 48 GB of system memory per training run.

Evaluation of Classification Accuracy on a Balanced Test Set (Studies 1 and 3).

All models were evaluated on the same held-out test set (N = 6,024) which contained equal numbers of images for each race × gender subgroup, ensuring that differences in classification performance were not attributable to differences in the demographic composition of the evaluation data. We assessed overall classification accuracy as well as subgroup accuracy for racial (Study 1) and gender groups (Study 3). Classification accuracy was defined as the proportion of test images for which the model's predicted category matched the corresponding human annotation. In addition to overall accuracy, we calculated race-specific accuracy for Study 1 (White, Black, East Asian, and Indian) and accuracy separately for each race × gender subgroup in Study 3. Accuracy was first calculated separately for each model and subgroup and subsequently averaged across the five random seeds within each training condition.

Evaluation of Classification Threshold by Estimating Category Boundaries (Study 2). Study 2 examined classification thresholds for the racial classification of Black-White biracial faces.

To create the test images that varied parametrically in Black-White racial composition, 20 faces of individuals who both appeared and self-identified monoracially as Black (10 male, 10 female) and 20 faces from individuals who both appeared and self-identified monoracially as White (10 male, 10 female) faces were selected from two popular face databases: the Chicago Face Database (59) and the Face Research Lab London Set (60). Faces were posed with a neutral expression and direct gaze and cropped. Next, 760 unique "parent" faces were created by morphing every combination of same-gender White and Black face images (190 each for Black male, White male, Black female, and White female). Mixed-race morphs were then created from each Black-White combination of parent faces within gender at 10% increments, each producing 11 face images that varied in racial composition from 100% Black to 100% White. This process produced 190 unique male and 190 unique female spectra, comprising 4180 unique test faces. Each face morph image appeared on a gray background, cropped, resized, and edited, such that the final image excluded hairstyles, necks, and ears.

Using this test set, we estimated category boundaries along controlled Black-White face-morph spectra for each Study 1 training condition. Each of the five random-seed models within a condition classified each image along each morph spectrum. Classification probabilities were then averaged across the five models, and a PSE was estimated for each morph spectrum (33). The PSE represents the point along the morph continuum at which the averaged model predictions indicate equal probability of classification as Black or White.

Differences in PSE were assessed using a repeated-measures ANOVA, with training condition as the within-spectrum factor and morph spectrum as the repeated unit. To estimate conditionlevel PSEs and their uncertainty, morph spectra were resampled with replacement over 1,000 bootstrap iterations. For each iteration, the average classification curve and corresponding PSE were recalculated. Mean bootstrap PSEs and percentile-based 95% confidence intervals were then obtained from the resulting distributions.
