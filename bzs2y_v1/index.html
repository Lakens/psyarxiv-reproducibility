Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Autistic youth are a prevalent population in the U.S. facing greater risks for depression and adverse mental health outcomes than the general population of youth. The psychometric properties of most measures remain untested with autistic youth even though autism is characterized by significant heterogeneity including unique cognitive, affective, behavioral and sensory needs. The present study tested the internal consistency, confirmatory factor analysis, convergent and discriminant validity, and cross-informant agreement between 101 autistic adolescents (14-17 years old) and their parents for several depression and social-emotional functioning measures (e.g., social motivation, anhedonia/apathy). Most measures showed acceptable-to-good internal consistency across informants, while confirmatory factor analyses yielded mixed evidence of structural validity.

Convergent validity was moderate and adolescent and parent reports agreed poorly to moderately, which emphasizes the value of collecting both perspectives. Findings inform clinical practice by supporting the continued use of several measures in autistic adolescents, while suggesting caution in interpreting others.

Introduction

Autistic youth are a prevalent population in the U.S. (1 in 31 8-year-old children diagnosed with autism 1 ) facing significantly greater risks for depression 2 , anxiety 3 , and suicidal thoughts and behaviors 4 compared to the general population of youth. Despite these alarming statistics, few assessments and treatments exist to prevent and address adverse mental health outcomes in autistic youth. 2, 5, 6 Reliable and valid assessments are not only essential to screening and prevention efforts and diagnostic conceptualizations, but also to clinical trial readiness and accurate treatment planning. 7 Most assessments of clinical outcomes (e.g., depression, anxiety) and risk/protective factors (e.g., reward responsivity, social relationships) were developed for the general population, often excluding autistic youth. 8, 9 In fact, many assessments have not been validated for autistic youth and may not account for their unique neurodevelopmental differences and needs. Efforts to establish the psychometric properties of assessments with autistic youth are necessary to understand and treat adverse mental health outcomes in this population.

Autism is characterized by significant heterogeneity 10 including unique cognitive (e.g., concrete thinking 11 ), affective (e.g., alexithymia 12 ), behavioral (e.g., routines, repetitive behaviors 13 ), and sensory needs, 14 which underscores the importance of establishing the psychometric properties of assessments with this population. A key challenge facing mental health professionals is a limited evidence base on the rigor of assessments for autistic people, including: (a) whether generic assessments are reliable and valid, (b) if autism-adapted versions of assessments are more appropriate than generic ones, and/or (c) whether new assessments created for and with autistic people outperform generic assessments. Initial findings indicate that some assessments developed for the general population (e.g., Revised Children's Anxiety and Depression Scale; RCADS 15 ) are reliable and valid for use with autistic people. 16, 17 However, other findings suggest that autism-adapted versions of generic assessments 18, 19 increase their utility (e.g., Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale; C-SSRS 20 ) for the autistic community. Similarly, some researchers advocate for creating new assessments (e.g., Suicidal Behaviours Questionnaire-Autism Spectrum Conditions; SBC-ASC 21 ) through community-based participatory research methods that not only capture the unique experiences of autistic people, but also cater to their neurodevelopmental needs. As autistic people continue to experience adverse mental health challenges with little assessment and treatment options, efforts to increase the rigor of assessments are a critical starting point.

Semi-structured clinical interviews and questionnaires are common assessment approaches in research and clinical settings for the general population, with a limited evidence base for the autistic community. For autistic people with social communication and interaction differences 14 , interviews may not be an optimal approach as they are perceived as confusing, stressful, ambiguous, and/or frustrating. 22 In one study, an adapted semistructured interview that accounted for the unique neurodevelopmental needs of autistic people (i.e., PARS-ASD 19 ) was more reliable and valid in assessing for anxiety symptoms and severity in this population than the generic version (i.e., PARS). In another study, a generic interview (e.g., C-SSRS) reliably detected neurotypical youth at-risk for suicide, yet failed to detect 1 in 5 autistic youth at risk. 18 Importantly, the addition of a self-report questionnaire to the suicide interview increased the rigor of these screening efforts for autistic youth. 18 Initial findings suggest that some generic interviews may fail to capture the needs of autistic people, even though these interviews are widely used in clinical and research settings. In another example, the Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia (K-SADS 23 ) is a common interview of psychiatric symptoms and severity in youth that has had limited psychometric evaluation among autistic youth, representing a gap in assessment knowledge. Parent report is another common assessment approach for identifying and treating mental health needs in youth. 24, 25 Parent-child rating discrepancies are common, 26 yet more salient for autistic youth and their parents than neurotypical youth and their parents. 27, 28 In one study, parent-child rating discrepancies were more significant among autistic child-parent dyads than neurotypical child-parent dyads on a common, generic questionnaire of depression (i.e., Children's Depression Inventory; CDI 29 ). 27 Depression is an internalizing condition that may be more challenging for parents to identify and report than externalizing conditions for which parent-child discrepancies are fewer 30, 31 ; additional research in this area is needed to clarify potential rating patterns. Some investigators question the validity of generic self-report questionnaires among autistic youth 32 and advocate for stronger consideration of parent/collateral report, while other investigators highlight the importance of both youth self-report and parent report as raters may capture different, but equally important, information. 33, 34 Additional research on parent-child rating patterns is essential for improving the rigor of assessments for the autistic community as parents play a central role in youth access and engagement in treatment.

The present study aimed to test the psychometric properties of several generic interviews and questionnaires of depression, social motivation, and anhedonia/apathy with autistic adolescents and their parents. The study aims were to test the psychometric properties of generic interviews and questionnaires including reliability, validity, and level of agreement between raters. Given previous research, it was hypothesized that some assessments (e.g., RCADS 15 ) would be reliable and valid for autistic adolescents; for other assessments (e.g., K-SADS), reliability and validity would be tested for the first time in this population. As rating discrepancies are more common for autistic parent-child dyads than neurotypical dyads, it was hypothesized that rating discrepancies would emerge across most assessments, to varying degrees.

Methods

Participants

Autistic adolescents (14-17 years old) without intellectual disability and one of their parents were recruited from the community through flyering and social media advertising. Inclusion criteria included adolescents with: (a) an autism diagnosis, confirmed by record review of autism evaluations within the past 24 months [i.e., Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition (ADOS-2 35 ) was administered] or ADOS-2 administration in lab, (b) without intellectual disability (IQ≥70), confirmed by record review of cognitive evaluations within the past 24 months (e.g., scores provided from Wechsler Scales of Intelligence) or administration of the Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence, Second Edition (WASI-II 36 ) in lab.

Exclusion criteria included adolescents with: (a) severe aggression to self/others that compromise safety during study visits, (b) neurological disorders, (c) uncontrolled seizure disorder, and/or (d) intellectual disability (IQ<70).

Additional details regarding participant recruitment, screening, and eligibility are provided in our published study protocol [masked for review].

Procedures

In this longitudinal study, families participated in four study visits over a one-year period; in the present study, data collected at the first study visit were analyzed. In the first study visit, families participated in informed consent/assent, clinical interviews, neuropsychological testing, and surveys with adolescents and parents interviewed separately. Families were financially compensated for their participation in study visits and could request a free psychological assessment report. Informed consent and assent were collected in writing and all study procedures were approved by the [masked for review] Institutional Review Board (Protocol #22-00278) in accordance with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. Details regarding specific study procedures are provided in our published study protocol [masked for review].

Measures

Clinical interviews and questionnaires were completed by adolescents and parents separately.

Adolescents and parents completed the same interviews and questionnaires, apart from the Anticipatory and Consummatory Interpersonal Pleasure Scale (ACIPS 37 ) and Anhedonia Scale for Adolescents (ASA 38 ) which were only completed by adolescents.

Adaptive functioning and autistic traits. Adaptive functioning was assessed by parent ratings on the Adaptive Behavior Assessment System, Third Edition (ABAS-3) 39 . Autistic traits were assessed by parent-report in early development [Social Communication Questionnaire (SCQ) 40 ] and the past six months [Social Responsiveness Scale (SRS-2) 41 ], and from adolescent perspectives [Comprehensive Autistic Trait Inventory (CATI) 42 ].

Social Motivation.

Adolescent social motivation was assessed using multiple units of analysis including an interview, the Social Motivation Interview (SMI) 43 , and a questionnaire, the Anticipatory and Consummatory Interpersonal Pleasure Scale (ACIPS) 37 . The SMI is a semi-structured interview measuring levels of social motivation (i.e., interest/desire to engage with others and accompanying behaviors) in autistic youth (8-17 years old). The SMI contains 23 items assessing youth social interest/desire and behaviors, each rated on a 4-point Likert-type scale with higher scores indicating higher social motivation. Items comprise two subscales of social wanting and social behavior, which are summed into a total social motivation score. Previous pilot testing of the SMI with 18 autistic youth suggested its feasibility of administration, user satisfaction, and promising psychometric properties 43 ; however, the authors noted a need for future examination of the SMI in larger samples. The original 23 items of the SMI were administered in the present study, with additional questions added by our research team to more thoroughly examine and delineate social desire, behaviors, and outcome, with author permission. This expanded SMI resulted in 18 items across three categories of social motivation (54 items total): (a) social desire/interest (e.g., degree of importance of a social situation, interaction, or relationship to the youth), (b) social behaviors (e.g., frequency and type of social behavior done by the youth), and (c) social outcome (e.g., degree of perceived success of a social behavior). In the present study, items in each of the three categories were summed and compared in analyses as individual subscales without a total score.

The ACIPS is a 17-item questionnaire assessing reward capacity for social and interpersonal pleasure.

Items are rated on a 6-point Likert-type scale and summed into a total score, with higher scores indicating higher social motivation and reward. In the adolescent validation study, the ACIPS was tested in 449 community adolescents aged 13-19 and showed excellent internal consistency (ordinal α = 0.95). 44 ACIPS scores correlated positively with TEPS anticipatory (r = 0.44) and consummatory (r = 0.30) subscales, supporting convergent validity, and were not significantly associated with general psychological distress (GHQ-12), supporting discriminant validity. 44 There is limited data on the psychometric properties of the ACIPS with autistic people. In a sample of 49 autistic adults, the ACIPS total score demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach's α = .95) 45 ; however, its psychometric properties have not been tested with autistic adolescents.

Anhedonia/Apathy.

Adolescent anhedonia/apathy was assessed using multiple units of analysis including an interview, the Lille Apathy Rating Scale (LARS) 46 , and a questionnaire, the Anhedonia Scale for Adolescents (ASA) 38 . The LARS is a semi-structured interview measuring the presence and severity of apathy with items rated on a dichotomous scale. The LARS is comprised of 33 items divided into nine domains (i.e., everyday productivity, interests, taking initiative, novelty seeking, motivation -voluntary actions, emotional responses, concern, social life, and self-awareness) that are summed into a total score of apathy, with higher scores indicating higher apathy. In the founding paper, the LARS was tested in 159 patients with Parkinson's disease and 58 healthy controls, and showed high internal consistency (between-items α = 0.80, between-subscales α = 0.74) and strong test-retest reliability (r = 0.95), along with excellent inter-rater reliability (ICC = 0.98). 47 LARS scores correlated strongly with the Marin Apathy Scale (r = 0.87), supporting convergent validity, and reliably distinguished apathy from depression using an empirically derived cutoff (accuracy = 0.91, sensitivity = 0.89, specificity = 0.92). 47 The LARS has not been evaluated for use with autistic youth, which is a secondary aim of the present study.

The ASA is a 14-item questionnaire assessing general anhedonia in adolescents aged 11-18 years old.

Items are rated on a 4-point Likert-type scale and divided into three subscales (i.e., enjoyment, excitement, and emotional flattening; enthusiasm, connection, and purpose; and effort, motivation, and drive), which are summed into a total anhedonia score with higher scores indicating higher anhedonia. The ASA was developed through qualitative interviews, then tested for structural validity using exploratory factor analysis in 1,057 adolescents and confirmatory factor analysis in an independent sample of 1,041 adolescents; all adolescents were community/general-population adolescents. 38 The scale showed high internal consistency (total α = .93-.94 across samples) and good test-retest reliability over 7-11 weeks (ICC = 0.73). 38 ASA scores correlated strongly with depression (Mood and Feelings Questionnaire, r = .78) and negative symptoms of schizophrenia (Self-Evaluation of Negative Symptoms scale, r = .71), and more weakly with anxiety (RCADS subscales, r = .48-.52) and trait-level measures of behavioral approach/inhibition (r = .13-.40), supporting convergent and discriminant validity. 38 The ASA has not been evaluated for use with autistic youth, which is a secondary aim of the present study.

Depression. Adolescent depression was assessed using multiple units of analysis including an interview, the modified depression module of the Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia (K-SADS) 23 , and a questionnaire, the Revised Children's Anxiety and Depression Scale (RCADS-47) 15 . The modified depression module of the K-SADS is a semi-structured interview used to assess the presence and severity of depressive symptoms in youth including depressed mood, irritability and anger, negative self-image, fatigue or lack of energy, concentration difficulties, psychomotor agitation, insomnia, anorexia, weight loss, and suicidal thoughts and behaviors. Depression symptoms are rated on a 6-point Likert-type scale on the K-SADS, with higher scores indicating higher symptom severity. The K-SADS demonstrated strong reliability in its initial validation study among non-autistic children and adolescents ages 7-17, including 55 psychiatric outpatients and 11 healthy controls. Interrater agreement ranged from 93% to 100%, while test-retest reliability was strong across diagnoses, with κ values ranging from 0.63 to 1.00. 23 As noted, the K-SADS has limited psychometric evaluation with autistic youth, which is a primary aim of the present study.

The RCADS 15 is a 47-item rating scale of internalizing symptoms in youth completed by youth and their parents. All items are summed into a total score of internalizing symptoms, which is comprised of the following subscales: separation anxiety, generalized anxiety, panic, social anxiety, obsessive-compulsive traits, and depression. In de Ross et al.'s (2002) psychometric investigation with 405 community Australian youth aged 8-18, the RCADS showed excellent internal consistency for the total scale (α = 0.96) and adequate-to-good consistency across subscales (α = 0.79-0.88), and confirmatory factor analysis supported the original six-factor structure (CFI = 0.83, RMSEA = 0.06). 48 Convergent validity was supported by moderate-to-strong correlations with the Revised Children's Manifest Anxiety Scale (r = 0.62-0.75) and with the Children's Depression Inventory for the Major Depressive Disorder subscale (r = 0.80). 48 The RCADS has been previously validated for use with autistic youth. 16, 17 In Kim and Lecavalier's (2024) study of 55 autistic youth aged 8-17 presenting with depressive or anxiety symptoms, the RCADS Total score showed excellent internal consistency at both administrations (α = 0.93 and 0.94), with major depressive disorder (MDD) subscale alphas ranging from 0.75 to 0.78. 49 Test-retest reliability over a two-week interval was good for most subscales (ICC = 0.74-0.87), with the MDD (ICC = 0.81) subscale showing high stability. 49 In the present study, only the RCADS depression subscale is presented and evaluated (a 10-item rating scale on a 4-point Likert-type scale).

Statistical Analysis

Demographics and baseline adaptive functioning and autism-trait scores were summarized as median (Q1, Q3) for continuous variables and as n (%) for categorical variables. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.6.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), using the psych, lavaan, semTools, and correlation packages.

Two levels of analysis were used throughout: item-level analyses (internal consistency, confirmatory factor analysis) were fit directly on raw item responses, while composite-score analyses (convergent/discriminant validity, cross-informant agreement) used the subscale/total scores described above. Missing data were handled using instrument-specific rules for composite-level analyses. For the ASA, following the guidance in the published scoring materials, the subscale scores and total score were prorated when 25% or fewer of items were missing; otherwise, scores were set to missing. 38 For the LARS and SMI, as no official guidance on missing items exists in the published scoring materials, a 25% missingness threshold was applied, consistent with the ASA. For the LARS, the total score was in turn set to missing if any subscale was missing. The K-SADS-PL likewise has no official guidance on missing items; given the absence of an established convention and only nine items in the instrument, any missing item resulted in the total score being set to missing. The ACIPS was scored following the procedure used in its original validation study, in which surveys missing more than 10% of items were excluded from analysis and the remainder prorated. 44 The RCADS-47 was scored according to its manual scoring guidance, which recommends that subscales be prorated (summing completed items, dividing by the number completed, and multiplying by the total items in the subscale) when two or fewer items are missing, and left unscored otherwise; in our dataset, only the major depression subscale from this procedure was retained for analysis. 15 As a result, item-level and composite-level analyses can retain different effective samples, as detailed below.

Internal consistency was estimated using Cronbach's alpha and McDonald's omega, computed on raw item-level responses using pairwise-complete observations. 50, 51 Omega-hierarchical (variance attributable to the general factor) and omega-total (overall reliability including group/specific factors) are reported for every scale and subscale; the two converge when a scale is effectively unidimensional. 52 For the LARS, reliability was evaluated at the 33-item total-score level only instead of within each domain, consistent with prior adult LARS validation work (as the 9 domains have too few items to support a stable subscale-level alpha estimate). 53 Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to evaluate the factor structure of each measure, treating items as ordered-categorical indicators and using the WLSMV estimator with factor variances fixed to 1 (std.lv = TRUE). Model fit was evaluated against the benchmarks of Hu and Bentler (1999) 54 : CFI/TLI ≥ .95 (good), RMSEA ≤ .06 (good), and SRMR ≤ .08, with the more lenient conventional thresholds of CFI/TLI ≥ .90 and RMSEA ≤ .08 treated as acceptable, interpreted alongside substantive judgment; because the WLSMV χ² test is highly sensitive to sample size, model fit is summarized using these fit indices rather than the χ² test itself. Cases with any missing item within a given model were excluded listwise. For measures with a subscale structure, firstorder factors were specified for each subscale, with items as indicators. For LARS and ASA, subscale factors were further specified to load onto a single second-order general factor, consistent with the total scores computed for these instruments. SMI subscales (behavior, desire, outcome) were modeled as correlated first-order factors without a superordinate general factor, as no SMI total score is calculated. ACIPS, and the depression subscales of the KSADS and RCADS, were each specified as unidimensional single-factor models, consistent with their scoring as a single total.

Convergent and discriminant validity were evaluated by correlating (Spearman's ρ) composite scores across all six measures within each rater (adolescent, parent), based on an a priori set of same-construct ("convergent") pairs -SMI subscales with ACIPS, LARS with ASA, and K-SADS with RCADS depressionagainst all remaining cross-construct ("discriminant") pairs. Pairs between two subscales of the same instrument (i.e., the three pairings among the SMI Behavior/Desire/Outcome subscales as SMI has no single total score) are neither convergent nor genuinely cross-construct and were therefore excluded from both groups.

Agreement between adolescent self-report and parent-report was evaluated, for every scale/subscale with both rater versions, using the two-way random-effects, absolute-agreement, single-rater intraclass correlation ICC(2,1) 55 , the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (paired, non-parametric) to test for systematic mean differences between raters, and Bland-Altman 56 plots to visualize the pattern of agreement across the range of scores. Analyses used the same prorated composite scores as the convergent/discriminant validity analyses above (i.e., the same data, re-paired by rater rather than by construct), restricted to complete pairs -participants with a non-missing score from both raters on the given scale.

Results

Participant Characteristics

The sample included 101 adolescents (median age = 15.0 years, Q1, Q3 [14.0, 16.0]; 68.3% male, 31.7% female) and their parents (median age = 48.0 years, Q1, Q3 [45.0, 51.5]; 84.2% female). The sample was predominantly White (64.5% of adolescents, 72.2% of parents), with 26.0% of adolescents and 30.5% of parents identifying as Hispanic/Latino. Most parents held a college degree or higher (73.7%), and family income was distributed across the <$75,000 (22.4%), $75,000-$150,000 (41.2%), and $150,000+ (36.5%) brackets. The sample showed adaptive functioning in the below-average range (ABAS-3 General Adaptive Composite median = 80.0, Q1, Q3 [73.0, 87.0]) and elevated autistic traits consistent with clinical/research autistic samples (SRS-2 Total T-score median = 73.0, Q1, Q3 [66.0, 81.0]; CATI Total median = 138.0, Q1, Q3 [110.0, 155.0]). Full sample characteristics are reported in Table 1.

Internal Consistency

Internal consistency estimates by informant are reported in Table 2. Across most scales, Cronbach's α ranged from acceptable to good (.71-.92), with generally comparable reliability between adolescent-and parentreport versions of the same measure. The SMI subscales showed acceptable-to-good reliability for both raters (adolescent α = .76-.80; parent α = .80-.87), and omega-hierarchical and omega-total values were fairly close within each subscale, consistent with each SMI subscale functioning as an essentially unidimensional construct.

The ACIPS (α = .89) and the ASA subscales (α = .75-.89) showed acceptable-to-good adolescent-report reliability; parent-report versions are not available. The LARS total score showed acceptable reliability for both raters (adolescent α = .76; parent α = .79); however, ω-hierarchical and ω-total diverged more substantially than for the other multidimensional totals, most notably for parent-report LARS, where ω-hierarchical (.38) was far lower than ω-total (.87), indicating that most of the reliable variance in parent-rated LARS total scores is attributable to the nine domain-level factors rather than a single general apathy factor. A similar, but smaller divergence was present for adolescent-report LARS (ω-hierarchical = .40 vs. ω-total = .85). The ASA total score showed a much smaller gap between the two (ω-hierarchical = .79 vs. ω-total = .94), suggesting its general factor accounts for more of the total score's reliable variance than LARS's does. KSADS-Depression (α = .78 adolescent, .71 parent) and RCADS-Depression (α = .86 adolescent, .84 parent) both showed acceptable-togood reliability across adolescents and parents.

Confirmatory Factor Analysis

CFA model fit by informant is reported in Table 3. SMI fit poorly on every index for both raters (adolescents and parents), with CFI as low as .45 for adolescents. ACIPS (adolescent-report only) showed acceptable CFI/TLI (~.90), but RMSEA (.11) and SRMR (.10) both landed just outside the acceptable range; however, RMSEA can artificially inflate in models with small sample sizes. LARS showed the opposite split: RMSEA looked good for both raters (.06), but CFI, TLI, and SRMR (SRMR = .17, more than double the acceptable cutoff) all pointed to poor fit. ASA (adolescent-report only) was the one model with no disagreement among indices -fit was good to excellent across the board, including a perfect CFI/TLI of 1.00. KSADS-Depression fit well for adolescents but poorly across every index for parents. RCADS-Depression showed the same pattern as ACIPS: solid CFI/TLI for both raters, but RMSEA (~.11) and SRMR (~.09-.10) both outside the acceptable range for both raters.

Convergent and Discriminant Validity

Convergent and discriminant validity, evaluated via Spearman correlations among composite scores within each rater, are shown in Figure 1. Among adolescent report, the five a priori convergent pairs showed a higher mean correlation (mean ρ = .41) than the 20 cross-construct discriminant pairs (mean ρ = .25; three sameinstrument SMI subscale pairs were excluded from both groups). Individually, all three SMI subscales correlated positively with ACIPS-Total as hypothesized (ρ = .33-.48), and KSADS-Depression correlated with RCADS-Depression (ρ = .62). The LARS-ASA convergent pair showed a smaller positive correlation (ρ = .25) than the other convergent pairs. Among the discriminant pairs, LARS-Total correlated negatively with ACIPS-Total (ρ = -.60); ASA-Total correlated positively with both RCADS-Depression (ρ = .72) and KSADS-Depression (ρ = .51).

Among parent report, where ACIPS and ASA are unavailable, leaving only the KSADS-RCADS pair as a convergent comparison, the single convergent correlation (ρ = .61) exceeded the mean of the 11 discriminant pairs (mean ρ = .23), though this comparison rests on a single correlation and should be interpreted accordingly.

Cross-Informant Agreement

Agreement between adolescent self-report and parent report is reported in Table 4, with corresponding Bland-Altman plots in Figure 2. ICC(2,1) values ranged widely, from poor (LARS -Interests, ICC = -.11 [-.31, .09]; LARS -Productive, ICC = .05 [-.15, .25]) to moderate (SMI -Behavior, ICC = .56 [.40, .69]), with most scales falling in the poor-to-moderate range. 57 Wilcoxon signed-rank tests indicated statistically significant systematic differences between raters for 9 of the 15 scale/subscale comparisons. Adolescents rated themselves higher than parents rated them on three scales: SMI -Outcome (mean diff = 4.22, p < .001), LARS -Emotion (mean diff = 0.53, p = .022), and RCADS-Depression raw score (mean diff = 1.17, p = .042). Conversely, parents rated adolescents higher than adolescents rated themselves on five LARS domains -Initiative, Novelty, Motivation, Concern, and Awareness (mean diffs = -0.64 to -1.76, all p ≤ .007) -as well as the LARS total score (mean diff = -5.13, p < .001). The remaining comparisons -LARS -Productive, Interests, and Social, along with SMI -Behavior, SMI -Desire/Motivation, and KSADS-Depression -showed no significant mean difference between raters (all p ≥ .118). Spearman correlations, which index rank-order agreement independent of systematic mean differences, were generally weak-to-moderate and broadly tracked the ICC pattern (ρ = -.11 to .56), with the strongest rank agreement for SMI -Behavior (ρ = .56, p < .001) and the weakest for LARS -Interests (ρ = -.11, p = .302). Bland-Altman plots (Figure 2) showed reasonably consistent scatter across the range of scores for most scales without strong evidence of proportional bias, though limits of agreement were comparatively wide relative to scale range for LARS -Total, consistent with the larger mean discrepancies observed for that score.

Discussion

The present study is among the first to test the psychometric properties of several interviews and questionnaires of mental health outcomes and risk/protective factors (e.g., depression, social relationships) in a modest sample of autistic adolescents and their parents. Internal consistency estimates ranged from acceptable to good across most assessments, with generally comparable reliability between adolescent-and parent-report versions of the same assessment; findings provide preliminary support for the reliability of these assessments with autistic adolescents. Importantly, a common interview of adolescent depression (K-SADS) showed acceptable-to-good internal consistency across autistic adolescents and their parents, providing initial support for the use of this interview to assess the presence (and severity) of depression in autistic adolescents.

Confirmatory factor analyses demonstrated that adolescent-reported anhedonia on the ASA was the only model with a good fit across indices (i.e., without disagreement) whereas other models showed mixed or poor fit, which may reflect model misspecification, the modest sample size, or limited prior psychometric data. In terms of validity, all three SMI subscales correlated positively with the ACIPS and the KSADS-Depression correlated with RCADS-Depression, as hypothesized, while the LARS-ASA convergent pair showed a smaller positive correlation than the other convergent pairs. Agreement between adolescent self-report and parent report ranged widely with most assessments falling in the poor-to-moderate range; findings align with previous research on neurotypical adolescent/parent disagreement on internalizing and motivation-related constructs.

Confirmatory factor analyses showed good fit for the ASA and adolescent-report KSADS-Depression, mixed fit for the ACIPS and RCADS-Depression, and poor fit for the SMI, LARS, and parent-report KSADS-Depression. The ASA's perfect CFI/TLI (1.00) may reflect few degrees of freedom and warrants replication.

Overall, the findings provide preliminary support for the ASA, RCADS-Depression, and adolescent-report KSADS-Depression; tentative support for the ACIPS; and a basis for caution with the SMI, LARS total score, and parent-report KSADS-Depression. These results support use of the better-supported measures in current and future mental health research with autistic adolescents and their families, with further validation needed for the others. Our findings align with some previous research supporting the use of generic mental health assessments with autistic adolescents 18 . However, the extent to which autism-specific adaptations to these assessments may enhance internal consistency and other psychometric properties remains untested. As noted, internal consistency results for the parent-report KSADS were lower than other assessments and may be attributed to the internalizing nature of depressive symptoms that might not be readily observed by parents. It is also possible that alexithymia or other social communication differences common among autistic adolescents 12 prevent full disclosure of their internalizing states to parents. Though the LARS reliability overall was acceptable, a closer investigation shows that most of that reliability is derived from the nine separate sub-domains and not from one unified "apathy" factor. Thus, the LARS total score may not provide meaningful information on its own (i.e., one coherent apathy measure) for autistic adolescents and their parents and instead, the nine sub-domains may provide more useful information.

In terms of convergent/discriminant validity, findings suggest modest convergent validity between measures of similar constructs. Among adolescent self-reports, hypothesized convergent pairs correlated more strongly than cross-construct pairs, which is the basic pattern expected, though the gap was modest. The SMI and ACIPS behaved as expected, with all SMI subscales correlating positively with the ACIPS-Total, and the two depression measures (KSADS and RCADS) showed a moderate-to-strong association. One notable exception was the LARS-ASA pair, which we expected to overlap given the conceptual relationship between apathy and anhedonia but correlated only weakly. This may suggest that the LARS does not map neatly onto adolescent anhedonia as captured by the ASA, though this interpretation is tentative. Notably, the LARS-Total correlated more strongly with the ACIPS-Total, indicating that apathy overlaps substantially with reduced pleasure. The ASA also correlated highly with both depression measures. Finally, the parent-report validity evidence is limited, as only one convergent pair could be evaluated as the ACIPS and ASA have no parent versions. Although this correlation exceeded the mean of the discriminant pairs, it rests on a single comparison and should be interpreted cautiously.

Cross-informant agreement between adolescents and their parents ranged from poor to moderate for all assessments, with the strongest agreement observed for adolescent social behaviors on the SMI. Findings align with previous work showcasing significant cross-informant disagreement between neurotypical adolescents and their parents 31 as well as autistic youth and their parents. 27 In our study, adolescents reported stronger skills than those reported by their parents in the areas of social success (SMI) and emotional reactivity (LARS), yet adolescents endorsed a higher severity of depressive symptoms. It is possible that adolescents' perceived success in social situations with peers, internal emotional states, and internalizing depressive symptoms may not be readily observable to parents, contributing to discrepant ratings.

Of note, depression scores were discrepant across raters when assessed by a questionnaire (RCADS) with adolescents endorsing more severe symptoms than those reported by their parents, yet depression severity was not significantly different across raters on an interview (K-SADS). It is possible that adolescents disclose more of their distress on questionnaires as this assessment method lacks the social exchange of an interview, which may be experienced as stressful. 18, 22 These findings are consistent with previous work suggesting that self-report questionnaires paired with interviews may better detect autistic youth at risk for mental health distress. 18 Clinically, findings emphasize the importance of understanding both adolescent and parent perspectives in detecting and addressing mental health distress in autistic adolescents. While some previous work questions the reliability of autistic youth self-report 32 , our work supports the use of self-report interview and questionnaire assessments with autistic adolescents to identify youth at risk.

Limitations

The present study was among the first to assess the psychometric properties of several interviews and questionnaires of mental health outcomes and risk/protective factors in a moderate sample of autistic adolescents and their parents, with findings that can guide future research in this area. At the same time, there are several limitations to the present study that warrant discussion. First, the sample size was modest, which limited our ability to robustly test multi-factor models; the confirmatory factor analyses for the LARS and SMI in particular should be interpreted cautiously, as fit indices may be unstable. Second, though missing data is common in clinical research, it limited our ability to include assessments from all participants. Third, assessments were administered at a single timepoint, so we could not evaluate test-retest reliability, and we did not assess inter-rater reliability for the KSADS. Fourth, tests of convergent validity were limited for some measures (e.g., the LARS had a single hypothesized comparator, the ASA).

Conclusion

This study provides preliminary evidence on the psychometric properties of several mental health measures in autistic adolescents and their parents. Most measures showed acceptable-to-good internal consistency across informants, while confirmatory factor analyses yielded mixed evidence of structural validity, with only the ASA showing good fit across all indices. Convergent validity was moderate: related measures generally correlated more strongly than unrelated ones, though the LARS showed weaker-than-expected overlap with the ASA, and its reliability appeared to depend largely on its nine domain-level factors rather than a single general apathy factor. Adolescent and parent reports agreed poorly to moderately, which emphasizes the value of collecting both perspectives. Taken together, these findings support the continued use of several of these measures in autistic adolescents, particularly the ASA, ACIPS, KSADS adolescent report, and RCADS, while suggesting caution in interpreting the LARS total score and SMI structure. Replication in larger samples, with longitudinal designs and autism-specific adaptations, is needed to confirm these findings and clarify how well these measures capture mental health in autistic youth.
