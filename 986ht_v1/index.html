How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Does Daily Meditation Increase Self-Compassion? A Bayesian Multilevel Diary Study

Self-compassion is the disposition to treat oneself with kindness when things go badly, to see one's difficulties as part of shared human experience, and to hold painful thoughts in balanced awareness (Neff, 2003). Self-compassionate people tend to rely heavily on positive cognitive restructuring when they cope with stressful events (Allen & Leary, 2010, p. 107). Selfcompassion is also trainable. A structured program raised self-compassion and reduced distress in a randomized trial (Neff & Germer, 2013). Brief online formats make such training feasible for employees during a working week.

One such format was evaluated with a 28-day diary (Li et al., 2021). Sixty-five employees completed a control week, two weeks of guided online meditation (a body scan in the first week and self-compassionate breathing in the second), and a follow-up week, and they reported daily self-compassion, perceived stress, and emotional exhaustion each evening. The analysis compared outcome levels and time trends across the three periods with hierarchical linear models and treated trait self-compassion as a person-level moderator. That design tests whether outcomes differ between the weeks of the program.

A phase comparison does not test whether a person's reported outcomes differ between the days they actually practice and the days they do not. Participants in the original study did not meditate every day (Li et al., 2021). Each morning of the intervention weeks they reported whether they had meditated in the previous 24 hours, and in the original report this item was used to describe compliance, not as a predictor. No analysis in the original report compares meditation days with nonmeditation days, models adherence or dose, or examines a lagged nextday association of meditation with exhaustion; its one-day lagged analyses relate selfcompassion to next-day stress and exhaustion (Li et al., 2021).

There are substantive reasons to expect a same-day association. Guided self-compassion meditation focuses attention on kind and accepting self-talk, so the state the practice targets should be more available on a day of practice. A next-day association with emotional exhaustion is plausible for a related reason. Exhaustion persists from one day to the next, and selfcompassion is linked to how people cope with stressful events (Allen & Leary, 2010, p. 107).

Because this persistence is expected, any next-day test has to account for the previous day's exhaustion before attributing change to practice. The day-to-day persistence of affective states, often called inertia, is itself a feature of emotional functioning (Kuppens et al., 2010; Kuppens & Verduyn, 2017), so it is modeled rather than left in the residual.

The adherence question also raises a methodological problem. A diary lets each person serve as their own comparison. Centering a day-level predictor on each person's mean leaves no between-person variation in the predictor and yields a pure estimate of the within-person relationship (Enders & Tofighi, 2007, p. 128). The standard way to obtain that contrast is to subtract each person's observed mean of the predictor from the daily value. When a person contributes only a few days, however, the observed mean may be an unreliable measure of the person-level predictor, which biases the between-person estimate (Lüdtke et al., 2008). With a lagged outcome in the model, observed-mean centering also biases the autoregressive term (Hamaker & Grasman, 2015). Dynamic structural equation modeling addresses this by treating the person mean as a latent variable estimated jointly with the rest of the model (Asparouhov et al., 2018; Hamaker et al., 2018).

This problem is particularly important in the archival diary (Li et al., 2021). Meditation was common in the intervention period. The archival data file records 725 meditation reports, the original authors had already excluded participants who meditated fewer than seven times.

Many participants therefore have few or no nonmeditation days, so their mean of the dichotomous meditation indicator is close to 1 and depends on a few informative days. A latentcentered model fits these data and makes the precision of the within-person estimate explicit.

In a secondary analysis of the 14 intervention days of the archival diary (Li et al., 2021), I estimate day-level associations between meditation and outcomes, using a latent-centered Bayesian model specified and time-stamped before any outcome was modeled. The research question was not addressed in the original study, and the prior use of the data is disclosed. The analysis adds a day-level adherence test to the original phase evaluation, and its estimates are within-person associations.

Design and Procedure

The design was nonexperimental and longitudinal: a secondary analysis of the daily diary from a single-group intervention study with no control group. The diary began on the Monday after baseline measures and ran for 28 consecutive days in three periods: a 7-day control period with the diary only, a 14-day intervention period, and a 7-day follow-up period with the diary only. Each evening, participants completed the diary on a survey website. In the intervention period they first read materials introducing self-compassion and then followed a guided meditation, a body scan in the first intervention week and self-compassionate breathing in the second, both adapted from guided meditations by Kristin Neff. Each morning of the intervention period they reported whether they had performed the meditation during the past 24 hours (yes or no). The original report does not describe masking of participants or researchers, or any adverse events (Li et al., 2021).

Measures

Daily self-compassion was measured with the six-item Daily Self-Compassion Scale (Li et al., 2021), rated with reference to how participants treated themselves during difficult times that day, from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always). In the archival dataset, the composite is the sum of the six items, with Items 2, 4, and 6 reverse-scored, as the dataset's variable labels document (codebook; Li et al., 2021); reverse-scoring of negatively worded items follows the scoring of the Self-Compassion Scale (Neff, 2003, p. 234). The composite has a possible range of 6 to 30. Daily emotional exhaustion was measured with the five-item Emotional Exhaustion subscale of the Maslach Burnout Inventory-General Survey (Schaufeli et al., 1996 , as cited in Li et al., 2021), rated for that day from 0 (completely disagree) to 6 (completely agree). The composite was computed in the archival dataset as the sum of the five items and has a possible range of 0 to 30. The original report does not state which Chinese-language versions of the scales were administered (Li et al., 2021). The original composites were used as provided; how partially missing items entered them follows the original scoring, which is not documented. Daily perceived stress was measured with the four-item Perceived Stress Scale (Leung et al., 2010), rated from 0 (never) to 4 (very often); it is a prespecified additional outcome whose timing and sign were set post hoc. Meditation was the dichotomous morning report. Trait selfcompassion was also measured at baseline with the 26-item Self-Compassion Scale (Li et al., 2021; Neff, 2003); it does not enter the present models.

Reliability was estimated separately within and between persons with two-level confirmatory factor analysis of self-compassion and exhaustion over the 14 intervention days (Days 7-20 of the diary). Omega at each level was computed by applying Equation 3 of Geldhof et al. (2014) to the level-specific estimates of a one-factor model per level, estimated in lavaan, Version 0.7-3 (Rosseel et al., 2026). Within-person omega was .60 for self-compassion and .89 for exhaustion; between-person omega was .75 and .97 (Table 1). Following Lai (2021), these between-person omegas describe latent person-level constructs and may overstate the reliability of observed person means of these scales, which were not analyzed. Over all 28 days, the selfcompassion solution was inadmissible (see Supplemental Material), so omega is reported for the intervention days only. The original study reported within-person omegas of .886 for selfcompassion, .849 for exhaustion, and .794 for perceived stress (Li et al., 2021), compared with the within-person omega of .60 for self-compassion here.

Analytic Sample and Alignment of Meditation Reports

The analysis used the 14 intervention days (Days 7-20; diary days are numbered 0-27) of all 65 participants (910 person-days); the control and follow-up weeks did not enter the models, because meditation was prompted and reported only during the intervention. Because the meditation item was asked in the morning about the previous 24 hours, each morning report was assigned to the preceding diary day, so that meditation for day t was read from the report given on the morning of day t + 1. Under this rule the last intervention day would take its value from the first follow-up morning, when no report was collected, so that day's meditation status was treated as missing rather than as a "no." Reading the report as describing the same day was tested as a sensitivity analysis. No participant was excluded; participants with no nonmeditation days remained in the model, where they informed the person-level and autoregressive parameters, and their number is reported in Results. The original exclusion of participants who meditated fewer than seven times (Li et al., 2021) was already applied in the archival sample; a further refit applying that rule to the aligned data is sensitivity analysis S5. Analytic counts after alignment are reported in Results and Table 1.

Statistical Model

A two-level model of days within persons was fitted in Stan (Carpenter et al., 2017) via CmdStan 2.40.0, using the R packages cmdstanr 0.9.0 and posterior 1.6.1 in R 4.5.0 (versions as recorded in the project repository). The model was written for this study following dynamic structural equation modeling (Asparouhov et al., 2018; Hamaker et al., 2018). Each outcome was standardized over the observed values in each model's analysis period (Days 8-20 for Hypothesis 2 and the next-day perceived-stress model) so that coefficients are in units of the total (pooled) SD of the outcome. Slopes rescaled to within-person residual-SD units are given in the Table 2 note.

The model has two linked parts. In the first, each person's daily meditation report is modeled as a dichotomous (Bernoulli) outcome with a person-specific latent propensity, the probability that this person meditates on a given day. In the second, the outcome on a given day is predicted from (a) the within-person meditation term, the difference between that day's report and the person's latent propensity; (b) the latent propensity itself, which represents betweenperson differences in how often people meditate; (c) a weekend indicator; (d) day in the program, centered at the middle of the study; and (e) the person's outcome on the previous day, expressed as a deviation from the person's own level, which captures day-to-day persistence with a common autoregressive coefficient. Each person has a random intercept and a random withinperson meditation slope, with a correlation between them. For participants without nonmeditation days, the person-specific slope is informed only by the population distribution; they contribute to the intercept, propensity, and autoregressive parameters. The primary model retains these participants; no sensitivity fit excluding them was run. The fixed (average) withinperson meditation slope is the estimand for both hypotheses.

For Hypothesis 1 the outcome is same-day self-compassion. For Hypothesis 2 the outcome is emotional exhaustion on day t + 1, the predictor is meditation on day t, and the autoregressive term is exhaustion on day t, so the meditation coefficient is adjusted for the previous day's exhaustion; on the first outcome day (Day 8), the first day of the analysis period, the autoregressive term is omitted, so that day is not adjusted for Day 7 exhaustion. Meditation reports were missing on 100 of the 910 person-days, 65 of them on Day 20 by design (Table 1).

In the primary model, missing reports are flagged rather than coded as nonmeditation: Those days contribute to the outcome model but provide no within-person meditation information and are excluded from the meditation-propensity likelihood, which assumes that the reports are missing at random. The brms comparison model (S6) drops those days listwise. A sensitivity analysis for missing meditation reports was added after the data release (see Deviations From the Protocol). Missing outcome values are estimated within the joint model, which is valid if they are missing at random given the variables in the model (Gelman et al., 2013, Section 8.2).

Prior Distributions and Estimation

Priors were weakly informative and centered on no association. The fixed slopes, including the within-person meditation slope, received normal priors centered at zero with a standard deviation of half an outcome standard deviation. The intercept, the autoregressive coefficient (bounded between -1 and 1), the standard deviations of the random intercept, random slope, and residual, and the mean log-odds of meditation received comparably weak normal or half-normal priors (on priors for standard deviations, see Gelman, 2006). The correlation between the random intercept and the random slope received a Lewandowski-Kurowicka-Joe (LKJ) prior that mildly favors small correlations. Exact prior specifications are in the protocol in the project repository, and prior and posterior densities of key parameters are in Supplemental Figure S2. Prior choice was informed by the When to Worry and How to Avoid the Misuse of Bayesian Statistics (WAMBS) checklist (Depaoli & van de Schoot, 2017). The posterior was sampled with the No-U-Turn sampler (Hoffman & Gelman, 2014) in four chains of 1,000 warmup and 1,000 sampling iterations, with a target acceptance rate of .95 and maximum tree depth of 12. Convergence required a rank-normalized R ̂ below 1.01 (Vehtari et al., 2021, p. 671) and bulk and tail effective sample sizes above 400 (Vehtari et al., 2021, p. 672). Divergent transitions, which signal problems in the sampler's exploration of the posterior (Carpenter et al., 2017, Section 2.4; Gabry et al., 2019, Section 4), were also required to be absent, and any divergence was specified to trigger a refit with a target acceptance rate of .99 (Bürkner, 2017, p. 10).

Posterior predictive checks compared the observed mean, standard deviation, and lag-1 autocorrelation of each outcome with those of data replicated from the posterior (Gabry et al., 2019; Gelman et al., 2013, Section 6.3; Figure 1). A rerun of the analysis code reproduced the saved results exactly. An independent reimplementation of the Hypothesis 1 and 2 models agreed with the primary fit within Monte Carlo error (details in the project repository).

Estimation and Precision

Hypothesis 1 is primary; Hypothesis 2 is secondary and is reported on its own. The directional posterior probability in the prespecified direction, P(b > 0) for Hypothesis 1 and P(b < 0) for Hypothesis 2, is reported descriptively (Gelman et al., 2013, pp. 77, 95; Marsman & Wagenmakers, 2017, pp. 529-531). Here P(•) denotes a posterior probability; for example, P(b < 0) is the posterior probability that the within-person slope is negative. The additional analyses are likewise reported descriptively (Rubin, 2021). For each estimate, the posterior median and the 95% equal-tailed credible interval (CrI) are also reported, following Bayesian reporting guidelines (Kruschke, 2021). Because nonmeditation days were few, precision is made explicit by reporting the posterior standard deviation and the width of the 95% CrI. I dropped the protocol's prespecified ±0.2 SD band because this report uses no effect-size cutoff; the bandbased quantities remain in the released aggregates.

Sensitivity Analyses

Six sensitivity analyses (S1-S6) were specified in protocol Version 1 §9; Version 2 revised the S4 wording and implemented S6, and the protocol was locked before the first data release. Each was evaluated in the direction of the hypothesis it concerns, to check whether the results held under alternative assumptions (Cro et al., 2020 (Cro et al., , p. 2816;; Little et al., 2012; Morris et al., 2014; Thabane et al., 2013); because S5 proved identical to the primary Hypothesis 1 model, five of the six are independent checks. S1 refits Hypothesis 1 with the prior SD of the slope coefficients (within-person slope, propensity, weekend, time, and lagged-covariate coefficients) doubled (1.0) and halved (0.25). S2 reads each meditation report as describing the same day. S3 relaxes the missing-at-random assumption with a delta-adjusted pattern-mixture model in which missing self-compassion values are shifted 0.25 and 0.50 SD below the model prediction, following the general delta-adjustment approach to sensitivity analysis for missing data (Hayati Rezvan et al., 2018). S4 repeats Hypothesis 2 under the S1 priors, the S2 alignment, and missing exhaustion values shifted 0.50 SD above the prediction. S5 refits Hypothesis 1 after excluding participants with fewer than seven meditation days, the original rule (Li et al., 2021), applied to the aligned data. S6 replaces latent centering with conventional observed person-mean centering in brms 2.22.0 (Bürkner, 2017 (Bürkner, , 2018)), regressing standardized self-compassion on the withinperson and person-mean meditation terms, weekend, and centered time, with a random intercept and random meditation slope, first-order autoregressive residuals, and the same weakly informative priors on all four fixed coefficients. S6 is reported to show how much the centering choice matters; it never replaces the primary model.

The protocol went through three time-stamped versions, all dated October 24, 2026, and was specified a priori (record in the project repository), except the timing and sign of the perceived-stress analyses, which were set after the Hypothesis 1 and 2 results were known. I also dropped the ±0.2 SD inconclusive band, prespecified in Version 1 and confirmed in Version 2, and the band-based quantities, because no effect-size cutoff is used; their values remain in the released aggregates. Version 2 raised the cutoff of the posterior-probability decision rule, and Version 3 corrected how the sign of each hypothesis is applied to that rule. I later dropped this prespecified posterior-probability decision rule, and the results are reported descriptively. The originally locked reliability computation omitted the factor variance term, giving within-and between-person omegas of .986 and .912 for self-compassion and .967 and .973 for exhaustion; after the data release, I corrected the computation to implement Equation 3 of Geldhof et al. Results

Data Description and Compliance

After alignment, the 65 participants contributed 910 intervention person-days. Meditation was reported for 678 days, nonmeditation for 132 days, and the meditation report was missing for 100 days (Table 1). These counts differ from the same-row counts in the archival data file (725, 147, and 38) only because of the alignment: The three missing reports from the first intervention morning fall outside the analysis period, and the last intervention day, which has no following morning report, adds 65 missing values (38 -3 +65 = 100). Twelve participants had no nonmeditation day and were retained; all 65 had at least seven meditation days. Selfcompassion and emotional exhaustion were each missing on 50 person-days. On the first intervention day (Day 7), self-compassion was missing for 2 of 65 participants and exhaustion for 2 of 65, and the Day 8 morning report that supplies Day 7's meditation status was missing for fewer than 10 of 65 participants. On the last intervention day (Day 20), meditation status was missing for all 65 participants by construction, and self-compassion was missing for 3 of 65 participants and exhaustion for 3 of 65. Convergence and Model Checks

The 14 Stan fits comprised the two primary models, all sensitivity models except S6 (S5 among them, although it duplicates the primary Hypothesis 1 model), and the two perceivedstress models (a prespecified additional outcome; timing and sign set post hoc). Across those 14 fits, computed over the key model parameters, every R ̂ was below the 1.01 criterion (maximum 1.01 when rounded to two decimals), the minimum bulk effective sample size was 602, and there were no divergent transitions, so the convergence criteria were met and no refit was needed (Supplemental Table S2, Supplemental Figure S1). For the 12 primary and sensitivity fits, the minimum tail effective sample size was 618; for the two perceived-stress fits, the minimum bulk effective sample size was 615. For the brms comparison model (S6), the maximum R ̂ saved with the original fit was 1.00; diagnostics extracted post hoc from a seeded rerun of the locked analysis code, whose results were byte-identical to the original run, gave a minimum bulk effective sample size of 1,310 (criterion 400). In posterior predictive checks with 400 replicated datasets per model, both primary models matched the mean and standard deviation of their outcome well (posterior predictive p values [Gelman et al., 1996], reported descriptively: .48 and .50 for Hypothesis 1, .52 and .55 for Hypothesis 2) but underestimated lag-1 autocorrelation. The replicated value reached the observed value in 0 of 400 replications: For Hypothesis 1 the observed value was .88 versus a replicated median of .80, 95% interval [.76, .83], and for Hypothesis 2 it was .84 versus .73, 95% interval [.69, .77] (Figure 1).

Discussion

For same-day self-compassion (Hypothesis 1), the within-person meditation slope had a median of 0.00 SD, P(b > 0) = .48, and the next-day exhaustion slope (Hypothesis 2) had P(b < 0) = .65. Both within-person slopes had 95% CrIs spanning zero, [-0.09, 0.09] and [-0.16, 0.11].

These estimates come from a latent-centered model, chosen because nonmeditation days were few, and address whether, within the two weeks of an online self-compassion program, the days on which employees meditated differed from the days on which they did not.

Relation to the Original Phase Analysis

The original report describes changes in daily outcomes across the program phases (Li et al., 2021). Within the intervention period, there is little evidence that those changes were concentrated on the days on which participants actually meditated. The within-person difference in self-compassion between meditation and nonmeditation days (Hypothesis 1) was centered on zero, with an interval spanning both signs. One interpretation is that the changes during such a program reflect being in the program (reading the materials, expecting benefit, monitoring one's state, or the passage of time) rather than a given day's practice. Another is that any benefit of practice is cumulative over days and persists on days without practice, which a same-day contrast cannot detect; the autoregressive term accounts for some day-to-day persistence but does not model cumulative dose. The single-group design cannot exclude time and monitoring effects (Li et al., 2021), but a day-level contrast within the intervention period holds them constant across meditation and nonmeditation days.

Methodological Implications

The latent-centered estimate (0.00) and the observed-centering estimate (0.02) differed by about 0.02 SD, and both 95% CrIs included zero, although 12 participants contributed no nonmeditation day. The largest shift came from the alignment of the morning report: Reading it as describing the same day moved the next-day exhaustion estimate (Hypothesis 2) from -0.03 to -0.07, so the mapping of an adherence item onto diary days should be stated and tested.

Archival intervention diaries often contain adherence items that the original phase analysis did not use; such items can be analyzed as day-level predictors, provided the morning-report alignment, missing reports, and sparse nonadherence days are handled explicitly.

Limitations

Self-chosen timing means unmeasured demands could confound the association. The seven-meditation-day minimum left few nonmeditation days. Missing meditation reports were not imputed. Composites were computed in the archival dataset. Race and ethnicity were not reported in the original study or in the shared data. The model pools the two weeks' different meditations. Results from snowball-sampled Chinese employees may not generalize to other populations or formats. Underpredicted lag-1 autocorrelation (Figure 1) means slope intervals may be too narrow.

Future Directions

Because participants chose when to practice, designs that randomize practice days, such as microrandomized trials (Klasnja et al., 2015), would be needed to identify day-level effects of practice. The same-day perceived-stress slope, P(b < 0) = .90, is a candidate for a confirmatory test. Measuring daily demands would address time-varying confounding, and longer programs or samples without an adherence-based exclusion would yield more nonmeditation days per person and more precise within-person estimates. A prior predictive check and sensitivity analyses for the LKJ prior on the random-intercept-slope correlation and for the priors other than the fixedslope priors (varied in S1 and S4) were not conducted.

Conclusion

In a secondary analysis of an archival 28-day diary, the days on which employees practiced self-compassion meditation did not differ clearly from their nonmeditation days in same-day self-compassion or next-day emotional exhaustion: Both within-person slopes had 95% CrIs spanning zero, and the Hypothesis 2 slope had a posterior median of -0.03 SD, with P(b < 0) = .65. Archival intervention diaries can thus answer adherence questions that their original analyses left open; here, the estimates gave no indication that the program's changes were concentrated on the days on which employees practiced. Data Structure, Missing Data, and Source Notes

Missing meditation reports were not imputed, which assumes they were missing at random with respect to daily self-compassion. The model uses a complete person-day grid (65 ×14 = 910 person-days, 845 one-day lag pairs), so every lag is exactly 1 day. On the 100 days with a missing meditation report in the Hypothesis 1 model, the report was flagged as missing rather than coded as nonmeditation and excluded from the meditation-propensity likelihood, and those days added no within-person meditation information, at some cost in precision. The 50 missing self-compassion values were estimated within the model, so 1-day lags that span missing outcome days depend on that estimation; the delta-shift sensitivity analyses (S3 and S4) test the missing-data assumption for self-compassion and exhaustion values only. Dropping missing days instead would make 4.28% of intervals (34 of 795) longer than 1 day. Four further limitations concern the source data and code. First, the self-compassion and exhaustion composites were computed in the archival dataset, and how partially missing items were handled follows the original scoring, which is not documented. Second, the Day 8 outcome in the Hypothesis 2 model is not adjusted for Day 7 exhaustion, because the autoregressive term is omitted on the first day of each person's model window (Days 8-20). Third, some priors were taken from the analysis code rather than from the time-stamped protocol. Fourth, the source does not document the label for the time of each report or the coding of the weekday/weekend indicator. Meditation was reported the next morning; the same-day alignment is tested as a sensitivity analysis ( S2 and S4). Because the original sample retained only participants who meditated at least seven times, the results may not generalize to less adherent employees.
