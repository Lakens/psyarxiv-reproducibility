Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

Both sleep and stress are important predictors of health and functioning. Most people would probably agree that sleep and stress are linked: during periods of high stress, one sleeps worse, and after a bad night, one is more easily stressed. In line with this common perception, previous research has repeatedly shown that stress, particularly in the evening, is linked with poorer sleep, both in quality and quantity [1, 2]. Similarly, poor sleep has been linked with increased stress during the next day [2, 3]. However, these previous studies had poor temporal resolution regarding both evening and daytime stress, leaving open the question of whether stress at different times of day is differentially associated with sleep. Furthermore, despite age differences in sleep [4, 5] and stress processes [6, 7], age has thus far received little attention as a moderator of associations between sleep and stress in daily life.

Sleep Predicting Stress

Following the classical transactional model of stress and coping [8] stress arises from an interaction of person and environment through situation-specific appraisals. That is, a situation does not become "stressful" unless someone perceives their own current resources as insufficient to deal with a potential threat. Resulting from these appraisals, people experience immediate affective and physiological responses. As such, studying stress in (near) real time, in people's daily lives using socalled ambulatory assessment designs [9] promises insights into relevant predictors and consequences of momentary stress.

One important such predictor is sleep: Insufficient sleep may reduce (perceived) resources and thus increase perceived stress. In line with this, ambulatory assessment research has found that both sleeping worse (self-report) and sleeping shorter (self-report and actigraphy) than usual for oneself were linked with higher next-morning expectations of stressors and stressfulness [10].

Additionally, insufficient sleep may act as a physiological stressor itself [11, 12] and thereby impact psychological stress as well. Supporting this, findings also extend to physiological and experienced stress. One study found effects of sleep on self-reported stress and physiological stress markers: Shorter self-reported sleep duration and worse sleep quality were associated with a higher heart rate and higher stress levels that same morning; worse sleep quality was additionally associated with higher blood pressure [13]. Additionally, several self-reported and actigraphically measured sleep parameters like shorter sleep and lower sleep efficiency were linked with higher subsequent daytime stress in one study [2]. This is in contrast to another study in which only self-reported wake after sleep onset, an indicator of sleep fragmentation, was associated with higher severity of reported stressors the next day, whereas EEG-or actigraphy-based sleep parameters were not [3]. Both self-reported higher sleep quality and longer sleep duration predicted lower odds of reporting stressors the next day [14].

Stress Predicting Sleep

Previous research has also found that previous stress can predict subsequent sleep in daily life. One of the earliest daily life studies in this context found that stress and worries at bedtime were the main predictors of sleep quality during the following night; daytime stress only predicted sleep quality in a univariate model, but no longer when bedtime stress and worries were controlled for [1].

Similarly, higher perceived stress as reported in the evening predicted shorter sleep in another study; however, this research did not consider potential effects of stress earlier in the day [2]. One study found that daily stressor occurrence, but not severity, predicted shorter actigraphically measured sleep duration [3], whereas stressor occurrence predicted neither self-reported sleep quality nor duration in another one [14].

Regarding the mechanisms linking stress to subsequent sleep, research identified pre-sleep arousal as an important within-person mediator of the association between stress and subsequent sleep quality [15, 16]. Taken together, it seems that stress (remaining) in the evening should be the main predictor of reduced sleep quality and duration, which aligns with research pointing towards the relevance of emotion regulation for sleep, for example, in the context of insomnia [17].

Temporal Resolution: Stress at Different Times of Day

One important gap in previous research is the poor temporal resolution: With only one to three daily measurements of stress, research has not yet determined whether sleep differentially predicts stress at different times of day. When looking at other affective outcomes, research has typically found that the effects of sleep on affect are stronger in the morning [18, 19]; thus, it would seem plausible that this could be the case for stress as well.

The same is true for the opposite direction: While there is substantial evidence that stress and arousal in the evening play an important role for subsequent sleep, little research has considered stress at earlier times throughout the day and earlier or later in the evening.

Age Differences in Associations of Sleep and Stress

Based on results from laboratory research [20] and general age differences in sleep and affective functioning [21, 5], it is reasonable to assume people of different ages might differ in effects of sleep on stress and vice versa. However, age differences in daily life association between sleep and stress have not received much attention in previous research.

Regarding the effects of sleep on subsequent stress, laboratory research has found that older adults were less vulnerable to cognitive and affective effects of experimentally induced sleep loss [20, 22]. However, younger and older adults did not differ in reactivity to a standardized laboratorybased stressor following sleep deprivation [12]. In daily life, younger compared with older adults experienced significantly stronger associations of sleep quality with morning self-rated stress, heart rate, and blood pressure and of sleep duration with heart rate and blood pressure [13]. Taken together, previous research thus suggests that older adults may be less vulnerable to insufficient sleep and likely react with less of an increase in stress compared with younger adults.

Older adults might experience this reduced vulnerability because they are more used to shorter and low quality sleep [23]. In line with this, research has also suggested that older adults may experience a lower homeostatic sleep drive after sleep loss [24]. Additionally, older adults have been suggested to be better able to regulate their emotions [21], which could make potentially threatening situations seem easier to handle. At the same time, one of the strengths of older adults' emotion regulation is their ability to avoid potentially stressful situations in the first place [21], that is, they may also particularly avoid stressors after nights with poorer sleep (for a similar argument see [19]). Regarding age differences in effects of stress on sleep, there is comparatively less research to build on. It has been suggested that older adults' sleep may be more vulnerable to the effects of external influences (e.g., caffeine, stress) because they have weaker circadian rhythms, which may be more easily disturbed [25, 26]. Similarly, older adults may have difficulty down-regulating the physiological arousal associated with increased stress [21, 27] which may also lead to stronger effects of stress on subsequent sleep. However, previous research indicated a smaller role of somatic compared with cognitive arousal in explaining the associations of stress with worse subsequent sleep [15, 16] and older adults may be better able to reduce cognitive arousal [21] which could make them less vulnerable. Overall, it seems reasonable to assume that younger and older adults may differ in associations of stress with subsequent sleep, but it is difficult to predict the direction of these differences.

Further Relevant Variables

As pre-registered, we considered several further variables that may have substantial associations with sleep, stress, and/or their associations. First, we considered whether a given day and night occurred on a weekday vs. a weekend or public holiday as people may both experience less stress and better/longer sleep on weekends or holidays [28]. Second and third, we considered the effect of someone taking a nap as well as their subjective health on a given day on sleep the following night, as these have sometimes been linked with sleep outcomes [29, 30]. Fourth, higher BMI has been linked with sleep [31]. And lastly, being in full-time retirement may substantially impact both stress levels and sleep [28] and was thus controlled for.

The Current Study

In the current study, we analyze the bidirectional, time-lagged associations of sleep quality and sleep duration with stress assessed up to eight times per day across a 21-day ambulatory assessment study. Because of the more fine-grained temporal resolution in the measurement of stress, we can overcome limitations of previous research and analyze how sleep is associated with stress at different times of day. The sample consists of younger and older adults, thus allowing us to consider age differences in the bidirectional associations between sleep and stress.

We hypothesize that poorer sleep, operationalized as shorter sleep duration (actigraphy) and poorer sleep quality (self-rated), is associated with higher self-rated stress the next morning (H1a) and that these associations are less pronounced in older compared with younger adults (H1b).

Similarly, we expect that higher self-rated stress in the evening is associated with poorer subsequent sleep operationalized as shorter actigraphically measured sleep duration and poorer self-rated sleep quality (H2a) and that these associations differ for younger compared with older adults (H2b). We exploratorily consider sleep efficiency and sleep onset as further sleep parameters. Hypotheses and analyses were preregistered (https://osf.io/xj497/overview?view_only=a2b63e3b64874e7980e52c69b381719a).

Methods

Participants and Procedure

Participants included in this study were 161 younger adults (18 -30 years; Mage = 23.8, SD = 3.0, 49.1% male) and 170 older adults (55 -75 years; Mage = 63.6, SD = 5.69, 41.2% male). Participants were recruited from the larger Stockholm area online forums and social media, as well as paper flyers. They needed to speak Swedish fluently, own and be able to use a smartphone compatible with the study app PsyMate™, and show no signs of cognitive impairment. Exclusion criteria included regular shift work and health conditions that may interfere with daily activity, sleep, well-being, and/or cognitive performance (e.g., insomnia, depression, or dementia; for a more detailed description, please refer to the published study protocol [32]).

Interested participants first answered an online screening survey to determine their eligibility. Eligible participants then filled out a more extensive online background survey, including questionnaires on, for example, their sleep, health, and well-being. In the following in-person briefing, participants were screened for cognitive impairment, received further information on the study procedures, and gave written informed consent. Participants also learned to use the study app PsyMate™ by filling out an exemplary questionnaire, including all questions and the cognitive test.

They further received an actigraph (Motionwatch 8 by CamNtech, Cambridge, United Kingdom) to wear on their non-dominant wrist.

The 21-day ambulatory assessment period started the day after the briefing meeting. Every participant was called by phone 2-3 days into this phase to ensure everything was working; they also had access to phone support throughout their participation should they encounter any problems.

During the ambulatory assessment period, participants answered up to 10 short questionnaires per day: One morning, one evening and eight daytime questionnaires. In the following, we focus on the questions relevant to the current research and refer the reader to the published study protocol for an overview of further assessed variables [32]. Concretely, we use data from the sleep reports in the morning questionnaire, stress reports from the daytime questionnaires, and reports of subjective health and naps taken on a given day from the evening questionnaire. The design of the ambulatory assessment study, including the relevant variables, is visualized in Figure 1.

The morning and evening questionnaires were interval and event contingent: The morning questionnaire was available for participants from 5:00 to 13:00 and they were asked to fill it out soon after waking up; the evening questionnaire was available for participants from 19:00 to 4:00 and they were asked to fill it out shortly before going to sleep. The eight daytime questionnaires (in the following also referred to as beeps) were prompted semi-randomly within 2-hour time windows starting at 7:00 in the morning (Beep 1: 7:00-9:00, Beep 2: 9:00-11:00, Beep 3: 11:00-13:00, Beep 4: 13:00-15:00, Beep 5: 15:00-17:00, Beep 6: 17:00-19:00, Beep 7: 19:00-21:00, Beep 8: 21:00-23:00).

Beeps were scheduled to be at minimum 30 min apart and timed out after 20 min. Participants received monetary compensation for their study participation, which was commensurate with their compliance with the protocol. When they consistently answered at least seven of the daily beeps across each day, they could receive up to 2,940 SEK (ca. 300 $). The study was approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (approval number: Dnr 2021-03941, amendments: Dnr 2022-02642-02, Dnr 2022-04683-02, Dnr 2024-08391-02).

Measures

Stress

In each of the up to eight daily questionnaires, participants reported how stressed they currently felt using a 9-point scale from 1 (very relaxed) to 9 (extremely stressed), with verbal anchors provided at every second scale level. This scale has shown to been sensitive to experimental stress induction in younger and older adults [12].

Sleep

Self-reported sleep quality. Sleep quality was assessed each morning using four items from the Karolinska Sleep Diary [33]. Participants reported on problems falling asleep, how calm vs. restless their sleep was, issues with early awakening, and overall perception of sleep quality on a scale of 1 -5, with higher numbers originally indicating worse sleep. The average across the four items is used as the sleep quality score. To ease interpretation, the score was recoded so higher numbers indicate better sleep quality. Internal consistency was ω w = 0.71 on the within-person level and ω b = 0.70 on the between-person level.

Actigraphy.

Participants wore an actigraph (Motionwatch 8 by CamNtech, Cambridge, United Kingdom) on their non-dominant wrist which collected data on movement along three axes every 30 seconds. Using the MotionWare AutoSleep function, in combination with information provided on sleep timing in the morning questionnaire, event button presses at lights-off and wake-up where available, and visual checks, sleep onset time, wake-up time, total sleep time, and sleep efficiency were determined. (For more details, see [34].) Sleep onset was coded to hours (i.e., 22:30 became 22.5), and we added 24 to any sleep onset times after midnight (i.e., 01:15 became 25.25) to correctly reflect the later sleep onset time. Sleep efficiency was calculated as the share of sleep time of the time between sleep onset and wake-up time.

Analytic Strategy

Modelling

To address our hypotheses and concurrently analyze bidirectional associations between sleep and stress, we used a two-level dynamic structural equation modelling approach in Mplus. The approach is based on a model that was developed for variables assessed at different time scales, such as sleep and stress in our case [35]. It allows us to separately model associations of sleep with stress at different times of day while considering autoregressive dynamics within each of the variables.

Dynamic structural equation models in Mplus use the Bayes estimator with Markov Chain

Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods. We used the default uninformative priors in Mplus [36]. We determined convergence using the potential scale reduction factor [37] set to 1.01 and visual checks of parameter traceplots.

To apply this model, we transformed the data from the typical long-format (one row per answered beep per participant) to a semi-wide format with one row per day per participant and stress reports from the different beeps organized in separate variables (e.g., stress from the first beep in the variable str1, stress from the second beep in the variable str2 and so on; see Figure 1).

The model thus considers two levels: Days (Level 1) nested within persons (Level 2). We used separate models of the same form for each of the sleep variables; in the following we describe the model generally and simply refer to "sleep".

Repeatedly measured variables (sleep, stress) are automatically decomposed into their within-and between-person components (see left part of Figure 2). On Level 1, we obtain deviations from people's usual level of sleep and stress. On Level 2, we obtain a random intercept estimate of the sleep component. Due to the format of the data, we estimate the random intercept of stress by letting all eight momentary stress variables load onto a common stress factor with a factor loading of 1 to obtain one person-level estimate of stress.

On Level 1, we modelled associations of the previous night's sleep with stress at beeps 1 to 4foot_0 and associations of stress at beeps 4 to 8 with sleep in the next night. All these associations are modelled as random slopes, that is, they can differ between participants. We furthermore included an autoregressive component for sleep (regressing sleep on the previous night's sleep) to account for stability and/or compensatory effects, as well as separate within-day and overnight autoregressive components for stress, because these may be governed by different processes [38, 39]. These autoregressive components were modelled as random slopes as well. Similar to the procedure for the random intercept for stress, we estimated one day-time estimate of autoregression per person with all within-day autoregressions loading onto a common factor (day_ar) with a loading of 1 (not depicted in Figure 2 for simplicity). Because of issues with convergence, we did not include random residual variances, following the pre-registered procedure for this case.

To determine age differences in associations, we regressed all random effects (intercepts, slopes, residual variances) on a binary age-group variable coding young adults as -0.5 and older adults as 0.5. This coding scheme has the advantage that model estimates refer to the average across both age groups. Following the original model [35], we included correlations between all random effects on the between-person level.

We report the within-person standardized estimates for associations on Level 1 and standardized estimates for associations on Level 2 (i.e., age effects). We consider an effect as significant when the 95% credible interval does not include 0. Whenever we report p-values, these are Bayesian p-values referring to the percentage of the posterior distribution of opposite sign to the point estimate. Note. Decision Rules and Deviations from Preregistration

As preregistered, a significant association of previous sleep with stress at beep 1 and/or beep 2 would support hypothesis H1a regarding insufficient sleep predicting stronger stress in the morning. Deviating from the preregistration, we decided to include associations of sleep with subsequent stress not only at the first two but rather at the first four beeps as a more severe test of our hypothesis that sleep should predict stress specifically in the morning. Thus, sleep predicting stress at beep 3 or 4 (as well) would speak against H1a. This procedure mirrors the preregistered analysis and decision rules for hypothesis H2a regarding stress in the evening predicting subsequent sleep. Here, a significant association of stress at beeps 7 and/or 8 with subsequent sleep would support H2a whereas any significant associations of stress at earlier beeps would speak against it.

Significant age group differences in associations of sleep with stress or stress with subsequent sleep would support H1b and H2b respectively.

The results showed that better sleep quality predicted lower stress at all included beeps (i.e., beeps 1 -4, throughout the first half of day). Longer sleep duration in the previous night predicted lower stress reported at beeps 1 and 3. Effects of Stress on Sleep

For the opposite direction, higher stress at beep 8 (answered 21:00-23:00 in the evening) predicted lower sleep quality.

For sleep duration, higher stress at beep 8 unexpectedly predicted longer sleep duration the subsequent night, whereas higher stress at beep 7 predicted shorter sleep duration as expected; additionally, higher stress at beep 4 predicted shorter sleep duration (see Table 3).

The main results remained the same in direction and significance when controlling for weekends/holidays, naps, subjective health, BMI and full-time retirement (see Table 4). When combining stress reported at beeps 7 and 8, higher stress reported at people's last beep before going to bed predicted lower sleep quality, but not shorter or longer sleep duration. However, higher stress at beep 6 predicted shorter sleep duration.

To situate the unexpected association of higher stress at beep 8 with longer sleep duration in the main model, we ran further analyses. First, to assess multicollinearity as one potential explanation, we calculated a variance inflation factor (VIF) for the within-person parts of stress at beeps 4 to 8 (i.e., the predictors of sleep the following night). With VIF = 1.13, multicollinearity does not seem to be a major issue. Another explanation could be suppression effects, that is, effects of one predictor variable changing because of shared variance with another predictor being "suppressed". To see results without this potential suppression, we repeated the analyses using only stress at beep 8 or only stress at beep 7 as predictors. In these analyses, stress at beep 8 no longer predicted longer sleep duration, and stress at beep 7 predicted shorter sleep as before. Both lower stress at beep 8 and stress at beep 7 predicted better sleep quality in these models. The relevant parameters from these analyses are reported in the supplement (Table S4).

There was only one significant age difference in associations of stress with subsequent sleep:

The association of stress at beep 6 with sleep quality was significantly more positive for older compared with younger adults. Simple slope analyses showed that older adults experienced better sleep quality with higher stress at beep 6 (p = .019), whereas younger adults did not (p = .266). This effect was not significant in the model where we combined the stress at beep 7 and beep 8 to only include the last report before going to bed.

Further Results

Sleep Efficiency and Sleep Onset

We additionally analyzed associations of sleep efficiency and sleep onset time with subsequent stress. Whereas sleep efficiency did not predict stress the next day, later sleep onset was associated with lower stress throughout the first half of the day. In models controlling for weekends/holidays, naps, subjective health, BMI and full-time retirement, higher sleep efficiency predicted lower stress at beep 1 and beep 4 in these models, which it did not previously. In contrast, later sleep onset was no longer associated with lower stress when controlling for these variables.

However, a significant age difference emerged: The association of later sleep onset with stress at beep 2 was significantly more positive in older compared with younger adults. Simple slopes analyses indicated that later sleep onset was associated with higher stress at beep two for older (p = .010) but not for younger adults (p = .067).

For the opposite direction, higher stress at beep 8 predicted higher sleep efficiency, whereas higher stress at beep 6 predicted lower sleep efficiency. Higher stress at beep 5 again predicted higher sleep efficiency. Stress did not predict sleep onset time. These results remained in the models, including control variables. Results for these models are reported in the Supplement (Table S5).

In the models, we see age-related differences in stress, with older adults reporting lower stress on average. Regarding sleep, we see a difference in sleep onset, with older adults sleeping earlier compared to younger adults. There were no significant age-group differences in sleep quality, sleep duration, or sleep efficiency in the unadjusted models. In the adjusted models, controlling for weekends/holidays, naps, subjective health, BMI and full-time retirement, we additionally observed an age difference in sleep quality, with lower sleep quality for older adults.

Discussion

Taken together, when people reported lower quality sleep than usual for them, they experienced higher stress throughout the first half of the following day. With shorter sleep duration, people mainly experienced higher stress early in the morning but also around midday. Both of these findings are in contrast to the hypothesis (H1a) that sleep quality should mainly be linked specifically with stress in the morning. For the opposite direction, when people experienced higher than usual stress at beep 8 (time interval 21:00-23:00) or the last beep they answered in the evening (combined beep 7/8, time interval 19:00-23:00) they reported lower sleep quality for the following night, supporting hypothesis H2a for sleep quality. Findings regarding associations of stress with sleep duration, sleep efficiency, and sleep onset differed depending on modelling choices. In contrast to hypotheses H1b and H2b, there were hardly any age differences in associations between sleep and stress.

Sleep Predicts Morning and Daytime Stress

Generally aligning with previous research, we observed effects of previous higher quality and longer sleep with lower stress throughout the first half of the day (7:00-15:00), not only during morning hours. For sleep quality, we found this effect consistently across all included beeps 1 to 4; for sleep duration, some effects were more stable than others: Shorter sleep duration only consistently predicted higher stress at beeps 1 and 3. Given that effects of sleep on stress persisted past the morning into the early afternoon, future research my consider analysing whether effects further extend into the late afternoon and evening as well.

The findings generally support the theoretical supposition and results from sleep deprivation studies showing that lower quality and shorter duration sleep may increase stress, for example through reduced available resources and increased activity in physiological stress systems [12, 40].

Similar to previous findings regarding affect [19, 18], these associations appeared numerically strongest in the morning and weaker for stress later in the day, although we did not test whether this difference was statistically significant. In contrast to most previous research, we included autoregressive paths for stress, thus an effect of sleep on stress at beep 2 reflects the association with stress at beep 2 above and beyond higher levels of stress carrying-over from beep 1. Accordingly, the effects of sleep quality on subsequent stress seem to play out across several hours, not just in the (early) morning.

Other research has indicated that lower quality sleep may further be associated with increased affective inertia [39]. We observed substantial inertia in feeling stressed, and it is possible that sleep quality, duration, or other sleep parameters might be linked with stress inertia, i.e., reduced recovery from stress. This association cannot be tested with the current model but would be an interesting question for future research to consider.

Surprisingly, later sleep onset was associated with lower stress at beeps 1 -4, however these associations were no longer significant in the models including control variables such as weekend.

One plausible explanation for this finding would thus be that people both went to bed later and experienced less stress on the weekend compared with weekdays, which aligns with the observed zero-order correlations.

In this study, we only observed significant associations of sleep efficiency with subsequent stress in the model with control variables. Given that sleep efficiency was linked with subsequent stress in previous research [2], future research may explore potential boundary conditions for this association.

Differential Associations of Stress with Subsequent Sleep

In contrast to our hypotheses, we did not consistently observe effects of stress in the (late) evening on subsequent sleep across different sleep parameters. Rather, effects were more nuanced.

Whereas theoretically predicted findings regarding increased stress before going to bed predicting impaired subsequent sleep quality largely held and stress earlier in the day did not play a role here, this was not true for sleep duration. More stress at beep 8 instead predicted longer sleep duration, but higher stress earlier in the day mostly predicted shorter sleep (beep 7, 19:00-21:00, and beep 4, 13:00-15:00).

The results for sleep quality support our hypothesis and are in line with previous research showing that higher stress in the evening is linked with lower subsequent sleep quality [1]. The findings partially showing links of higher stress with longer sleep are rather puzzling, as most research so far has observed that, if anything, higher stress in the evening predicted shorter subsequent sleep [2, 3]. However, none of the previous research had the same level of temporal resolution and considered stress across the afternoon and evening as separate predictors of sleep. To further situate this unexpected finding, we thus consider results from additional analyses. Firstly, in the pre-registered sensitivity analyses combining stress reports from beep 7 and beep 8, the last stress report before people went to bed was no longer associated with longer or more efficient sleep. In additional analyses, only including stress at beep 8 or stress at beep 7 and not any other beeps, higher stress was not associated with longer subsequent sleep duration either, rather, higher stress at beep 7 predicted shorter sleep. Jointly considering these findings suggests that there may have been a suppressor effect at play. That is, the observed effects of stress at beep 8 may have resulted from shared variance with stress at another time (most likely beep 7) being "suppressed".

Alternatively, it is also possible that this effect could simply be the result of a type-1 error, considering the overall number of tests as well as how close the lower end of the reported CI is to zero. Accordingly, we refrain from overinterpreting this effect here.

Regarding the expected but not observed effects of increased stress on shorter sleep duration, one further aspect to consider is delayed sleep onset as a potential mechanism. However, in this study, stress across the afternoon and evening was not associated with delayed sleep onset either.

In the following, we consider some characteristics of the stress measurements that could help in interpreting the overall findings. First of all, participants in this study reported low levels of stress on average (between 3.27-3.80 on a 9-point scale, i.e., well below the scale mid-point of5 = neither relaxed nor stressed), with the lowest average stress ratings occurring in the evening.

Furthermore, the effects of stress on subsequent sleep were at least partially mediated through cognitive pre-sleep arousal in previous research [41, 15, 16] and an increased rating on the applied stress-scale, especially on the lower end of the scale, may not necessarily imply increased cognitive arousal. Instead of linear effects of within-person deviations, it is possible that stress only impacts subsequent sleep once it crosses a certain threshold. However, in the current study high stress ratings were rare: only 5.9% of cases used the ratings 7 (=stressed) or higher on the 9-point scale.

These findings thus highlight the need to more closely consider and model the potentially non-linear mechanisms through which stress at different times of day may differentially impair different aspects of sleep at night.

Younger and Older Adults' Sleep and Stress are Associated Similarly

In contrast to our hypotheses, younger and older adults did not generally differ in associations between sleep and stress. Normal variations in sleep, as they occur in daily life, were overall not differentially linked with self-reported stress for younger and older adults. This is in contrast to lab-based findings on affective functioning, which found that older adults were less vulnerable to affective consequences of sleep loss [20] and initial findings from daily life more strongly linking sleep quality with self-reported and physiological stress in the morning for younger compared with older adults [13]. However, the findings align with previous research showing similar stress responses to a standardized stressor in the laboratory following sleep loss (TSST) [12].

Interestingly, shorter sleep duration predicted higher stress at beep 2 specifically for older but not younger adults. Because this effect disappeared in the adjusted model, it is possible that it may have been driven by the fully retired older adults, however, this is merely speculative. No comparable effects were found in the models combining stress at beep 1 and 2 to reflect the first stress report in the morning. For the opposite direction, more stress at beep 6 was linked with better sleep quality the next night for older but not for younger adults, both in models with and without control variables. Considering the generally low levels of stress reported in the study, even more so by older adults, slightly increased stress in the late afternoon may imply increased activity more than actual stress, which might be linked with better sleep quality. Previous research has also found quadratic associations of stress reactivity with health and well-being [42], suggesting that some level of reacting to stress may generally be adaptive.

In light of age differences within old age in previous research (e.g., [19]), it is important to consider that this study includes a rather heterogeneous older sample: Strictly speaking, part of the sample would still be considered middle-aged, and some people were still working, whereas others were fully retired. Accordingly, grouping together this heterogeneous group may have overshadowed potential effects. Furthermore, very old adults in the (> 80 years) who generally experience stronger declines in functioning may differ from late middle-aged and "young-old" adults [43] and might become more vulnerable to stress-effects on sleep, which should be considered in future research.

Strengths and Limitations

This research has several important strengths, namely, a large age-heterogeneous sample (N = 331), a long assessment period with many data points (21 days with an average of 116 data points per person compared to the average of 12.4 days with 51 data points per person in a meta-analysis of ambulatory assessment studies [44]), and high temporal resolution with up to eight daily reports of stress. To make best use of this high temporal resolution, we applied a model specifically designed to study dynamics of variables assessed at different time scales [38], which allowed us to assess differential associations of sleep with stress at different times of the day.

This measurement and modelling approach is also an important point to consider when comparing results of the current study with those of previous studies. Including several correlated measures of stress across the second part of the day as predictors of subsequent sleep, we may have inadvertently reduced power for each separate one of those effects. Future research may consider testing how different "configurations" of stress throughout the afternoon predict subsequent sleep, for example, average stress, the amount and/or timing of the strongest experience of stress, recovery trajectories or similar.

Furthermore, the meaning of "feeling stressed" may not be invariant across persons or time:

In line with previous research, we assessed stress rather broadly by asking people how stressed they currently felt. As such, we do not know how people defined feeling relaxed vs. stressed at different times. It is possible that something that would not have increased their stress ratings during waking hours could be counted as more stressful late at night. Supporting this general idea, recent qualitative research showed that people's interpretation of ambulatory assessment items varies over time and that they consider different aspects of their recent experiences when answering these items [45]. More qualitative assessments of stress would enable new insights into people's reasons for feeling stressed (e.g., the type of stressor) and could be included in future research on links between sleep and stress. Similarly, when we consider stress as a process, we can differentiate phases such as anticipation, reactivity, or recovery [46], which might be differently associated with sleep [19, 47].

This paper focused on within-person associations between sleep and stress -however, it is possible that between-person differences in stress or sleep may impact within-person associations.

For example, one could imagine people who are chronically stressed also sleep poorly in general but no longer react to (minor) changes in stress from day to day.
