Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Human navigation has mostly been studied on flat ground, leaving open how elevation changes affect path integration. In two experiments, blindfolded participants reproduced the horizontal distance home after traversing flat paths, a bridge or stairs. Experiment 1 showed no detectable mean error after a straight flat path, but a substantial undershoot after the bridge. In Experiment 2, bridge and stair paths produced shorter returns relative to a straight-flat baseline than did a flat path matching the bridge's profile; crawling over the bridge increased this undershoot. Participants overestimated bridge inclination and height. In Experiment 1, a horizontal projection based on perceived slopes descriptively covered approximately 74% of the group mean undershoot, without a detectable individual association. In Experiment 2, an exploratory perception-behaviour correlation did not survive correction for multiple comparisons, and homing errors showed no detectable inclination effect despite different perceptual predictions. Slope overestimation may therefore contribute to the undershoot, but its causal role remains unresolved. One interpretation is that humans integrate vertical movements metrically using biased inputs. The findings do not distinguish a 3D representation from projection onto a 2D representation.

Introduction

Navigation studies are essential because understanding how animals, including humans, navigate in their environment helps us to understand how the brain works 1, 2 . The discovery of brain cells that map the spatial position of an animal earned John O'Keefe, May-Britt Moser and Edvard I. Moser the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The study of navigation has mainly focused on performance on flat, even terrain, but the last two decades have seen the emergence of studies investigating the ability to navigate on uneven terrain, that is, terrain with elevation changes such as slopes and stairs. The main question is whether human spatial processing is isotropic, with identical properties in the horizontal and vertical dimensions, or anisotropic, with properties that differ systematically between them 3 . The story begins with the observation that humans prefer the horizontal plane. Intuitively, most of us would think that we do not need a 3D representation of space, as we travel mostly in 2D. Indeed, cities have mainly been built in valleys rather than on mountain tops, stairs are more frequent than slopes, and our morphology is not made for climbing steep slopes: even trained mountain runners cannot keep their balance on a treadmill inclined at more than about 40° 4 . Accordingly, humans perform better on even than on uneven surfaces, the so-called horizontal advantage. Interestingly, this advantage has been reported in very different types of tasks, such as the passive reproduction of travelled distance from memory 5 , real navigation in a multilevel building 6, 7 , and spatial learning 8 . Although prevalent, the horizontal advantage is not systematic: some studies report a behavioural difference between the two planes without an advantage for either of them [9] [10] [11] , and one study found no behavioural difference but still suggested separate neural pathways for the two planes 12 . Taken together, the literature indicates that humans' ability to navigate is influenced by the topography of the world, sometimes revealing a horizontal advantage. These results are in line with the currently prevailing hypothesis that flying and swimming species process space in 3D, whereas walking species process it in 2D [13] [14] [15] [16] . This hypothesis has been largely confirmed in ethology across several species: for example, rats and dogs are more accurate in the horizontal than in the vertical plane 17- 20 , whereas birds and fish perform equally well in all directions 18, 21 . Interestingly, ants, which are terrestrial animals but can walk on vertical and upside-down surfaces, perform equally well on even and uneven terrain [22] [23] [24] [25] . In addition to these behavioural differences, neuroethological studies support the assumption that the locomotory style influences spatial processing. In rodents, neural responses change depending on the dimension, horizontal vs. vertical 26, 27 . In contrast, studies on freely flying bats report 3D place cells 28 and 3D head-direction cells 29 . Crucially, bats differ from rats in at least one important aspect: bats fly, whereas rats walk. Altogether, the literature suggests that spatial processing is constrained by the means living beings have for exploration. If we follow this line of thinking, humans, as walking terrestrial animals, should have a sharp representation of 2D space and only a coarse representation of 3D space. However, the story does not seem to stop here: recent studies challenge this hypothesis. An fMRI study found 3D place information in the human hippocampus 30 and reported similar memory accuracy for the horizontal and the vertical planes, as did another study 31 . In rodents as well, a recent study reported head-direction cells tuned in 3D 32 . Although still scarce, these new results question the assumption that spatial processing is constrained only by the locomotory style. In this context, we asked how vertical movements contribute to human path integration, the estimation of the way home from self-motion information [33] [34] [35] . Path integration is usually modelled as the continuous summation of self-motion vectors 33, 36.

Previous human studies have examined spatial memory and learning 8, 31 , virtual navigation 5, 12, 30, 31 , multilevel buildings 6, 8, 11 and motion simulators 9, 10 . Real movement involving elevation changes has also been studied: Zhou and colleagues tested spatial updating after stepladder descent in darkness and found preferential horizontal updating 37 . Building on this work, we measured the horizontal distance reproduced on a flat return path after an outbound ascent and descent over a bridge or stairs. A flat path matching the bridge's profile provided a control. Our contribution therefore extends existing research on elevation and path integration to this homing task. To investigate the nature of humans' spatial processing, we compared navigational performance on even and uneven terrain. Specifically, we tested humans' ability to go back home (homebound) on a straight, flat path after an initial (outbound) path that was flat or inclined. This design mirrors experiments in desert ants, which, after walking over hills, return home over the horizontally projected (ground) distance rather than the distance actually walked 22, 24, 25 . As in these experiments, home lay at the same height as the end of the outbound path. Our task therefore tests whether vertical movements are taken into account accurately when computing the horizontal distance home; it does not test whether elevation itself is represented, that is, whether space is represented in 2D or in 3D. If the isotropic hypothesis is true, the inclined terrain should be processed as accurately as the flat terrain, and participants should go home accurately in all conditions. However, if the anisotropic hypothesis is true, the two terrains are processed differently, and performance should differ. A horizontal advantage predicts better performance on flat surfaces. On inclined surfaces, an overshoot is expected if the vertical component is not taken into account, as the distance travelled over inclined terrain is longer than the projected ground distance. Additionally, we measured how participants perceived the inclined terrain, as slopes are known to be overestimated [38] [39] [40] [41] [42] . In Experiment 1, participants walked back home after a flat, straight outbound path or after walking over a bridge with 24.5° slopes, and they estimated the inclination and height of the bridge. Mean homing error did not differ significantly from zero after the flat path, whereas participants undershot the distance after the bridge. We examined slope overestimation as a possible contributor: if perceived slopes were used to project the travelled path onto the horizontal plane, steeper perceived slopes would predict a shorter distance home. This comparison evaluates the compatibility of the group means with a perceptual account; it does not establish that slope overestimation caused the undershoot. Experiment 2 tested this explanation further with four manipulations. First, we compared two inclinations, 12° and 24°: if the undershoot results from slope overestimation, it should be larger for the steeper slope. Second, we added stairs, which involve an elevation change without an inclined surface underfoot, to test whether the undershoot is tied to the slope or to elevation per se. Third, we added a flat control in which the profile of the bridge was laid flat on the floor, so that the outbound path had the same shape as the path over the bridge but remained in the horizontal plane. Fourth, because it has often been claimed that the nature and properties of the inputs predict the output behaviour 43 , we compared two types of locomotion: walking vs. crawling. We expected crawling to exaggerate the effect compared with walking, as crawling is less natural.

Experiment 1 Methods

Twenty-seven participants (17 women, 10 men; aged 18-27 years, M = 22.0, SD = 2.3) took part after giving written informed consent. They were recruited through the university's participant pool and received course credit or monetary compensation; no participant was excluded. They reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no hearing, vestibular or balance impairments. The study was approved by the ethics committee of Ulm University and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. A sensitivity analysis showed that, with 27 participants, a paired t-test had 90% power to detect within-subject effects of dz ≥ 0.65 (α = .05, two-tailed).

Experimental Design & Apparatus

Participants performed a homing task on two different terrains: flat-straight vs. bridge. They were instructed to walk an imposed distance (outbound), to stop and, once turned around, to walk back home, that is, to stop walking when they estimated that they had reached their starting position (homebound; see Figure 1). We measured the distance actually travelled back home. The outbound path ended when the experimenter said "stop" as participants' feet crossed the end line; participants were then turned by 180°, repositioned on the flat track, and walked back. The distance travelled was measured with a laser distance meter (Bosch PLR 40 C). To ensure that participants relied only on the integration of their own movements (path integration 33 ), no visual, auditory or tactile cues were provided: participants wore a blindfold and soundproof headphones. We built a dedicated handrail to prevent participants from veering 44 and from gaining tactile information about the distance. It consisted of two rails on which a carriage could move, like a toy train on its track. In all conditions, participants used this handrail. Each condition was repeated 12 times, leading to a total of 24 trials per participant. To prevent fatigue, participants took breaks. The order of conditions (flat-straight vs. bridge) was randomized and counterbalanced across participants. Participants received no feedback during the experiment and saw the bridge only at the very end. Before the homing task, we measured how participants perceived the bridge from body-based cues alone. They were instructed to walk up the bridge blindfolded and to estimate the inclination of the slope out loud, in degrees. Once they reached the plateau, they estimated the height of the bridge in centimetres. Finally, on their way down, they estimated the inclination of the slope again. The method is based on an earlier study 38 . Each participant performed 7 trials, each including 3 estimates (inclination up, height, inclination down). The flat-straight path measured 387 cm; the path over the bridge measured 420 cm (projected distance: 387 cm). The inclination of the bridge was 24.5°, the plateau measured 51 cm, each slope 184.5 cm, and the height was 76.5 cm. 1 for the control and bridge conditions. In both cases, the return distance was 387 cm. In the control condition, participants walked on a flat surface. In the bridge condition, the outbound path went over the bridge (24.5° slopes, 420 cm path length), while the homebound path was on flat ground.

Results

Behaviour.

A repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted to assess the effect of terrain type (flat-straight vs. bridge) on homing errors. The analysis revealed a significant main effect, F(1, 26) = 96.93, p < .001, partial η² = .79 (flat-straight: 95% CI [-12, 17]; bridge: 95% CI [-110, -67]). Participants stopped well before home in the bridge condition compared with the flat-straight condition (Figure 2): in the bridge condition, they stopped on average 89 cm before home (SE = 10.36), an undershoot of 22.9% of the 387-cm homebound distance. This finding suggests that the inclined terrain influenced participants' homing behaviour. Additionally, one-sample t-tests were conducted to test whether the mean homing errors differed from zero in each condition. The bridge condition was significantly below zero, indicating a substantial undershoot (t(26) = -8.56, p < .001), whereas the flat-straight condition did not differ significantly from zero (t(26) = 0.31, p = .76). On average, participants largely overestimated the size of the bridge. The height of the bridge, 76.5 cm, was perceived as 104 cm (SD = 34). Experiment 2 Methods

Sixteen new participants (7 women, 9 men; aged 19-34 years, M = 25.1, SD = 3.7) took part after giving written informed consent; none of them had taken part in Experiment 1. They were recruited through the university's participant pool and personal contacts, reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no hearing, vestibular or balance impairments, and received course credit or monetary compensation; no participant was excluded. The study was approved by the ethics committee of Ulm University and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Based on the bridge effect in Experiment 1 (dz = 1.89; paired t-test, α = .05, two-tailed), 6 participants would have provided 90% power; with 16 participants, the design had 90% power to detect within-subject effects of dz ≥ 0.87.

Experimental Design & Apparatus

Experiment 2 consisted of three sessions, each lasting approximately two hours. Participants performed a homing task similar to the one in Experiment 1 (see Figure 1). In Experiment 2, we manipulated the inclination of the slopes (12° vs. 24°), the type of movement (walking vs. crawling), and the type of terrain (flat vs. stairs vs. bridge). We measured the distance actually travelled back home: the outbound path ended when the experimenter said "stop" as participants' feet crossed the end line; participants were then turned by 180° and walked or crawled back, and the distance travelled was measured with a laser distance meter (Bosch PLR 40 C). Participants were then led back to the start along a detour. At the end of the two sessions that included the bridge (12° and 24°), participants were asked to draw the bridge on paper, once for each type of movement (walking and crawling), without visual access to it, and to estimate its height; they also estimated the height of the stairs. We computed the perceived angles from these drawings. As in Experiment 1, participants received no feedback and saw the bridge only at the very end of the experiment. Each session contained four conditions, balanced for movement type (walking vs. crawling) to mitigate fatigue: the 12° bridge (walking and crawling), the 12° stairs and the 24° flat control (crawling) in one session; the corresponding 24° conditions and the 12° flat control (crawling) in another; and the straight-flat paths (walking and crawling) and the 12° and 24° flat controls (walking) in the third. Participants performed a straight-flat path, which served as their individual baseline condition, both walking and crawling. They also performed angled paths on flat ground corresponding to the flat control condition for 12° and 24°, as well as the stairs conditions for both inclinations (12° and 24°), and finally the bridge conditions for 12° and 24°. All conditions were performed while walking, and all except the stairs were also performed while crawling. This resulted in 12 conditions, each repeated 12 times, for a total of 144 trials per participant. To prevent habituation effects and limit the time spent blindfolded, each session was divided into two blocks, with six trials per condition. The order of sessions and conditions was randomized and counterbalanced across participants. To add stairs and manipulate the inclination of the slopes (12° vs. 24°), the setup differed from Experiment 1. The handrail was removed; to ensure a straight return when walking, participants pushed a trolley with fixed, straight-line wheels, and when crawling, they were aligned in the right direction and asked to move as straight as possible. For the bridge conditions, the setup was built against a wall that participants used as a handrail, and they wore a thick glove to prevent any tactile information.

The straight-flat path (baseline condition) was 278 cm long. For the flat control paths, the profile of the bridge was laid flat onto the floor, so that the outbound path formed the same angles (12° and 24°) in the horizontal plane. These paths were marked with chalk on the floor of a separate room, and the experimenter turned participants by the shoulders at each turn. For the stairs, two staircases were installed back-to-back to simulate an ascent to a plateau followed by a descent; two conditions were tested, corresponding to 12° and 24°. For the bridge, wooden boards were fitted over the staircases to create inclined surfaces of 12° and 24°. Each staircase was 150 cm wide and 96 cm deep, with four steps, each 24 cm deep. The ramps of the bridges were 150 cm wide and 117 cm (12°) or 126 cm (24°) long. The total path length over the bridge was 283 cm (12°) and 300 cm (24°). In crawling trials (back and forth), participants moved on hands and knees while wearing protective knee pads and goalkeeper gloves to minimize discomfort and prevent tactile feedback. In all trials, participants wore a blindfold and soundproof headphones.

Analysis

For each participant and condition, homing errors were averaged over the 12 trials. Because crawling trials started with the hands at the starting line whereas the return distance was measured behind the feet, each participant's body span was subtracted from the crawled distances; the homebound distance was 278 cm when walking. Except for the comparison between Experiments 1 and 2 and the comparison with the homebound distances predicted from the perceived slopes, all analyses were conducted after subtracting each participant's performance in the corresponding baseline condition, the straight-flat path (walking or crawling). Performance in these baseline conditions represents the raw estimation accuracy and accounts for individual differences. Baseline-corrected error was defined as the participant's raw homing error in the condition minus that participant's raw homing error in the corresponding straightflat baseline. Thus, zero after correction denotes performance equal to the baseline, rather than necessarily accurate return to the starting location. No participants or trials were excluded, and no participant's baseline-corrected mean deviated by more than 3 SD from the condition mean. Repeated-measures ANOVAs were Greenhouse-Geisser corrected when Mauchly's test indicated a violation of sphericity, and post hoc comparisons were Bonferroni-corrected paired t-tests. Analyses were run in MATLAB R2024a (MathWorks). The six Experiment 2 perception-behaviour correlations were treated as an exploratory family and Bonferroni-corrected together; unadjusted pvalues and pointwise confidence intervals are also reported where relevant. The two follow-up perception contrasts comparing inclinations within each locomotion mode were Bonferroni-corrected as a separate family. Nonsignificant tests were interpreted as an absence of a detected difference; no equivalence tests were performed. To compare Experiments 1 and 2, we also analysed the raw homing errors in the baseline condition and in the 24° bridge condition, expressed as a percentage of the homebound distance. To test for practice effects, we compared the first and second halves of the trials in each condition (the two blocks in Experiment 2).

Results

Behaviour.

A two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted to assess the effects of condition (3D Bridge Walking, 3D Bridge Crawling, 3D Stairs Walking) and slope inclination (12° vs. 24°) on homing errors. The analysis revealed a significant main effect of condition, F(1.42, 21.23) = 93.61, p < .001, partial η² = .86, Greenhouse-Geisser corrected, indicating that homing errors differed depending on how participants moved through the environment. Bonferronicorrected pairwise comparisons showed that crawling over the bridge led to a significantly larger undershoot than walking over the bridge (mean difference = -95.66 cm, SE = 8.23, p < .001, 95% CI [-117.83, -73.50]) and walking over the stairs (mean difference = -84.48 cm, SE = 9.24, p < .001, 95% CI [-109.36, -59.60]). Participants thus systematically undershot the distance to home more when crawling than when walking. The main effect of inclination was not significant, F(1, 15) = 0.79, p = .39, partial η² = .05. The interaction between condition and inclination was not significant either, F(1.36, 20.47) = 0.59, p = .50, partial η² = .04. Discussion

The current study investigated the nature of humans' spatial processing by comparing navigational performance on horizontal terrain and on terrain with vertical components. In Experiment 1, no significant mean bias was detected after a straight flat path, whereas participants undershot after walking over the bridge. In Experiment 2, walking along the flat angled paths produced no detectable difference from the straight-flat walking baseline. Bridge and stair paths produced shorter baseline-corrected returns, and the undershoot relative to baseline was larger when crawling over the bridge. Slope overestimation is one possible contributor: projecting a travelled path using steeper perceived slopes predicts a shorter horizontal distance. However, the present results do not establish that this perceptual bias caused the observed homing errors. The behavioural anisotropy could arise from the representation of the vertical dimension, for instance the coarse, non-metric representation proposed by the bicoded map hypothesis 3, 45 , or from biased inputs used in metric integration. Stairs constrain these accounts: participants' feet remained on horizontal treads, yet they undershot relative to baseline. No significant difference was detected between bridge and stair walking, although this does not establish equivalence. Thus, undershooting was not confined to an inclined surface underfoot. Stair height was overestimated by about 20 cm, compared with about 30 cm for the bridge, but individual height overestimation was not significantly associated with stair homing errors. A simple perceptual account is insufficient to explain the full pattern. One interpretation is that humans take vertical movements into account metrically using biased inputs. This would offer an alternative to a coarse, non-metric vertical representation 3, 45 and is compatible with findings of 3D spatial coding in humans and mice 30, 32 . In Experiment 1, projecting the bridge using perceived slopes predicted approximately 74% of the group mean undershoot. However, this descriptive agreement does not demonstrate the underlying processing mechanism, and the exploratory Experiment 2 correlation did not survive correction. Metric integration remains one interpretation to be distinguished from alternative accounts of distance estimation and integration. Taking slopes into account also does not require a full 3D representation: desert ants compute the ground distance of hilly paths, yet their path integrator appears to operate in the horizontal plane 23, 25 . With home and the outbound endpoint both at floor level, our task cannot distinguish a 3D representation from projection onto a 2D representation. A home vector with a vertical component would be needed. This distinction connects our findings with preferential horizontal updating after stepladder descent 37 , while our task measures return distance after both ascent and descent. Slope overestimation is therefore a plausible contributor to the group mean undershoot, but whether more accurate slope perception would improve homing requires an experiment that manipulates or independently tests that perceptual input. Several findings limit this account. Individual perceived inclination was not significantly associated with homing errors in Experiment 1, and no Experiment 2 perceptionbehaviour correlation survived correction. Perceived slopes predicted a larger undershoot at 24° than at 12°, without a detected inclination effect in homing. Undershooting also occurred on stairs and increased when crawling, without a significant movement-type effect on slope judgments. Measuring perception immediately after each trial, including an appropriate measure for stairs, would help test these relationships. Slope overestimation is well established for both proprioceptive and visual judgments [38] [39] [40] 42 . Its origin remains debated, but it has often been argued to have limited consequences for behaviour 38, 39, 42, 46 . Our findings motivate testing whether it contributes to navigation errors. This possibility may also help interpret earlier work: travelled distances are reproduced more accurately upright than when pitched backward by 30° 5 , potentially reflecting biased body-orientation estimates. Some virtual-reality studies, in contrast, detected no horizontal-vertical behavioural difference 12, 30 . Although visual slopes can be overestimated 39 , stationary virtual-navigation tasks lack the same body-based slope signals as physical locomotion. These comparisons suggest possible roles for perceptual inputs, but do not establish that slope overestimation causes homing errors. We also found a clear effect of movement type on homing errors: participants undershot the distance home much more when crawling over the bridge than when walking over it. No significant movement-type effect on perceived slope was detected, so the larger homing error was not accompanied by a demonstrated change in the slope judgments. Because each locomotion mode was corrected by its own straightflat baseline, it cannot reflect a general miscalibration of crawled distance either. Crawling also produced an undershoot on the flat angled paths, but a much smaller one than over the bridge, so most of the additional error is tied to crawling over the bridge. Additionally, variability in responses was greater in the crawling condition, likely due to the unfamiliarity of this locomotion mode. As walking is our primary means of navigation, participants were accustomed to it, leading to more stable and accurate performance. One possible explanation for the larger errors when crawling is body orientation: when walking over the bridge, the trunk remains close to upright, whereas when crawling, it is pitched along the slope, and the horizontal-vertical anisotropy in the representation of travelled distance is body-relative 5 . Another possibility is that crawling over a slope takes more time and effort than walking: walked distance is estimated by combining several body-based cues 47, 48 , and path integration can be leaky 49 , so part of the outbound path might be lost, leading participants to stop earlier on their way back. Interestingly, despite the differences in homing errors, no significant movement-type difference was detected in participants' perception of slope steepness; this does not establish equivalence. We initially expected to observe this difference. However, participants drew the bridge at the end of the session, after having crossed it both walking and crawling. They may therefore have recognized it as the same bridge and adjusted their perception retrospectively. Informal observations suggest that participants found crawling considerably more challenging, and many reported that the bridge felt steeper when crawling than when walking. These reports are in line with the claim that the nature and properties of the inputs predict the output behaviour 43 , but this would need to be tested with a perception measure taken immediately after each trial. Finally, we compared two different inclinations (12° and 24°), expecting the effects to be more pronounced at the steeper inclination of 24°. However, no significant inclination effect on homing errors was detected. Descriptively, the undershoot in the 3D conditions was even slightly smaller at 24° than at 12°, and only crawling on the flat angled paths led to a larger undershoot at 24°. Perception descriptively aligned with our hypothesis in the walking conditions: steeper slopes were overestimated more in absolute terms (by about 16° for the 24° slopes vs. 12° for the 12° slopes), so that the perceived inclination predicted an undershoot twice as large at 24° (about 38 vs. 18 cm). The walking perception contrast did not survive correction, so this descriptive pattern should not be treated as a confirmed inclination effect. The discrepancy between perceptual predictions and behaviour remains an open question; it is the main challenge for a purely perceptual account and warrants further investigation.

The bridge-minus-flat difference in homing error was smaller in Experiment 2 than in Experiment 1 (14.3% vs. 23.5% of the homebound distance). These percentages describe the terrain effect relative to the flat condition, rather than the absolute bridge error. A likely reason is the much greater exposure to elevation changes in Experiment 2: 72 of its 144 trials included vertical displacement (two bridges, walked and crawled, and two staircases), compared with 12 of 24 trials in Experiment 1 (plus the seven crossings of the perception task). In line with this, the undershoot in Experiment 2 decreased from the first to the second block in the 3D conditions, but not in the flat conditions. This fits the idea that the horizontal advantage partly reflects our limited experience with elevation changes: with more exposure to 3D paths, participants undershot less. Repeating the same bridge was not sufficient, however: the undershoot did not decrease over the 12 bridge trials of Experiment 1. Other differences between the experiments may also have contributed. In Experiment 1, participants estimated the inclination and height of the bridge before the homing task, which may have made its vertical extent salient and encouraged an explicit strategy; in Experiment 2, participants drew the bridge only after the homing trials. The setups also differed in the height of the bridge (76.5 vs. 51 cm), the homing distance (387 vs. 278 cm) and the guidance (handrail vs. wall). When participants finally saw the bridge, at the very end of the experiment, all of them were surprised that it was smaller than they had thought, which is consistent with their overestimation of its inclination and height. This surprise was most pronounced in Experiment 1. In Experiment 2, it may have been attenuated by the 12° condition and by the much longer exposure to the bridge, which gave participants time to get used to its inclination. To conclude, elevation changes altered the horizontal distance participants reproduced on returning home. Slope overestimation may contribute to this effect, but its causal role remains unresolved: the individual-level associations did not survive correction, and perceptual predictions did not capture the full pattern across inclinations, stairs and locomotion modes. One interpretation is that humans integrate vertical movements metrically, but from biased inputs. The present experiments are compatible with this interpretation without uniquely establishing it. They also leave open whether vertical movements are integrated within a 3D representation of space or projected onto a 2D representation, as desert ants appear to do 23, 25.
