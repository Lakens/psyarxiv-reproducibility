Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used in psychologically sensitive conversations, where repeated affirmation may reinforce delusion-related beliefs and associated risk. Inference-time self-guidance, in which a model generates conversation-specific guidance for subsequent responses without parameter updates, may offer an approach to conversational safety, but its utility in delusion-related interactions remains unclear.

Objective: To develop and evaluate conversation-specific inference-time self-guidance for conversational safety in delusion-related interactions.

Methods: In this preregistered study, Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-5, and DeepSeek V4 Flash completed 16 Psychosis-Bench scenarios under Baseline and three self-guidance conditions: response rules, contrastive examples, and short-horizon consequence simulation. The fully crossed design yielded 192 conversations and 2,304 assistant turns. A separate automated judge scored Delusion Confirmation (DCS), Harm Enablement (HES), and Safety Intervention (SIS) using published Psychosis-Bench rubrics. Analyses compared benchmark-defined Explicit and Implicit risk-cue scenarios and examined whether self-guidance reduced this gap.

Results: Across models, all three self-guidance conditions significantly reduced delusion confirmation and harm enablement and increased safety intervention relative to Baseline (all nine PFDR<.001; dz = 1.10-2.17). At Baseline, explicit risk cues elicited significantly greater SIS than matched implicit cues, whereas cue-type differences in DCS and HES were not significant. Selfguidance did not significantly attenuate the explicit-implicit safety gap after correction for multiple comparisons.

Conclusions: Model-generated inference-time self-guidance improved benchmark-defined safety behavior during standardized scripted escalation without parameter updates. These findings support further evaluation of inference-time self-guidance for delusion-related conversational safety, particularly when risk is communicated indirectly.

Introduction

A 26-year-old woman told ChatGPT that she believed her deceased brother had left behind a digital version of himself that she could find and communicate with. Although GPT-4o initially expressed caution, it subsequently engaged with and reinforced the premise, at one point telling her, "You're not crazy" [1]. The exchange took place through ChatGPT, a general-purpose platform with more than 1 billion weekly active users by August 2026 [2]. The woman was subsequently hospitalized with agitated psychosis. The case report documented sleep deprivation, prescription stimulant use, and other risk factors, so the episode cannot be attributed to chatbot use alone [1]. The exchange nonetheless illustrates a delusional spiral: repeated model affirmation accompanies a user's growing conviction in an implausible belief across turns [3]. By April 2026, the Human Line Project had collected self-reported accounts from 410 individuals describing comparable trajectories, including reports involving hospitalization or death [4]. In July 2026, a California man also sued OpenAI, alleging that ChatGPT validated delusional beliefs during a manic episode and failed to redirect him toward real-world support before a subsequent suicide attempt [5]. These case reports, self-reported accounts, and legal allegations cannot establish incidence, diagnosis, or causality. They expose the safety failure examined here: conversational AI can continue reinforcing delusion-related content as risk escalates.

This safety failure matters beyond individual cases because people already use generalpurpose GenAI for psychologically sensitive support. In a nationally diverse sample of 584 GenAI users, 29.3% reported using these systems for emotional support or coping advice [6]. Among U.S. adults with ongoing mental health conditions who had used an LLM during the previous year, 48.7% reported using one for psychological support [7]. General-purpose systems such as ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini consequently function as de facto mental health resources, although they were not designed or clinically validated for therapeutic care [8]. Their widespread use therefore places nonclinical models in conversations in which seemingly supportive responses may carry clinically meaningful consequences [9, 10, 11] One mechanism underlying this risk is sycophancy, the tendency of LLMs to align with a user's stated view even when a more accurate or corrective response is warranted [12]. In psychologically sensitive conversations, sycophancy may blur the distinction between validating a user's emotions and endorsing an implausible belief. This creates a difficult safety problem: users may trust and prefer the very responses that reinforce their perspective, even when those responses strengthen their conviction that they are right and reduce their willingness to repair interpersonal conflict [13]. Experimental evidence also indicates that training models to respond more warmly can increase sycophancy and reduce accuracy, particularly when users express sadness [14]. The risk may also escalate over time: affirmations generated early in a conversation become part of the context guiding subsequent responses, potentially creating a cycle of increasingly reinforcing statements [3, 15]. Thus, a response that initially appears supportive may become unsafe through repeated confirmation across conversational turns.

When repeated reinforcement contributes to the emergence, elaboration, or worsening of delusion-related beliefs, public and clinical discussions increasingly refer to such conversational trajectories as "AI psychosis" [4, 16]. Although this term is not a recognized psychiatric diagnosis and does not establish that chatbot use independently causes psychosis, it captures a distinct conversational risk [4, 16]. Conversational AI participates in an unfolding exchange and may validate or elaborate a user's belief across successive turns [17]. Empirical findings support concern about this pattern. Across three versions of ChatGPT, responses to psychosis-related prompts were less often judged appropriate than responses to matched control prompts [18].

Psychosis-Bench conceptualized the model behavior underlying this risk as psychogenicity, defined as confirming delusion-related beliefs, enabling associated harm, or failing to provide an appropriate safety intervention as the conversation unfolds [19]. The benchmark identified all three forms of failure across multiple models, particularly when risk cues were implicit. Together, these findings establish a measurable conversational safety failure. However, they leave an important question unanswered: whether a model can adapt its subsequent behavior once such a risk emerges.

Existing efforts to prevent these conversational failures have largely relied on safety guidance defined before the interaction begins [20, 21, 22]. Approaches such as targeted finetuning, written behavioral principles, and prompting strategies can reduce unsafe responses in anticipated situations. For example, targeted prompting and fine-tuning reduced sycophantic agreement with false medical premises [23]. However, these approaches share an important limitation: researchers or developers specify the relevant safety guidance in advance, and the model's role is primarily to follow it [20, 23]. These approaches leave open whether models can adapt when conversational risk emerges during an ongoing interaction. Such adaptation would require the model to identify the risk, formulate context-specific procedural guidance, and apply that guidance to its subsequent response. This creates a bounded form of inference-time adaptation in which the model generates and applies context-specific guidance during the interaction within a researcher-defined procedure [24, 25]. Agentic AI makes this form of adaptation increasingly plausible. Multi-stage architectures can use intermediate model outputs to shape later steps, allowing a model to generate procedural guidance during an ongoing task [24, 25]. This flexibility creates an important opportunity for safety: guidance can be generated in response to the specific risk emerging in the conversation instead of being fully specified in advance. At the same time, it creates a new safety challenge. If the model generates inappropriate guidance, the same mechanism could reinforce the behavior it was intended to correct. Skills-based architectures provide one implementation of this idea by packaging procedural guidance into units that can be activated when relevant to the task [26, 27].

Applied to conversational safety, this suggests a detect-generate-apply process in which the model identifies an emerging risk, generates context-specific guidance, and uses that guidance to shape its next response. Because this process occurs without parameter updates, it represents a bounded form of inference-time self-guidance within a researcher-defined procedure [24, 25].

Evidence for this inference-time mechanism comes from studies showing that models can improve their subsequent behavior without updating their weights [28, 29]. In psychologically sensitive contexts, prompt-level interventions have similarly been shown to modify model behavior without parameter updates [30]. Models can critique and revise their own outputs, generate demonstrations that guide later responses, and derive procedural rules from intermediate reasoning [25, 31, 32]. At the agent level, SkillWeaver provides a concrete demonstration: web agents discovered, practiced, and refined reusable skills through interaction, then applied those skills to subsequent tasks without parameter updates [25]. Together, these findings show how experience generated during inference can be converted into temporary procedural guidance that changes later behavior. However, prior work has used this capability primarily to improve task performance. Whether this general approach can be applied to identify an emerging conversational risk and generate context-specific guidance for the subsequent response within an unfolding, psychologically high-risk conversation remains unknown. The present study developed and evaluated an inference-time self-guidance procedure for conversational safety in delusion-related interactions. Three LLMs completed the original Psychosis-Bench scenarios under an unmodified Baseline and three self-guidance conditions: behavioral rules, contrastive examples, and short-horizon consequence simulation [19]. In each condition, a researcher-defined procedure prompted the model to generate conversation-specific safety guidance that shaped its next response, without updating model weights.

Methods

Study Design

The study used a fully crossed design comprising three models, four conditions, and 16 Psychosis-Bench scenarios, yielding 192 conversations and 2,304 assistant turns. Each model completed every scenario once per condition, enabling matched comparisons across conditions.

Benchmark and Conversation Task

Psychosis-Bench comprises eight benchmark-defined Explicit-Implicit scenario pairs (16 scenarios) spanning several delusion-related themes, including grandiose or messianic beliefs, erotic attachment, and referential beliefs [19]. Within each pair, the Explicit version communicates delusion-related content or harmful intent directly, whereas the Implicit version conveys comparable risk through less direct cues. All source scenarios were obtained from the original Psychosis-Bench GitHub repository [33] and retained without modification.

Each scenario followed a fixed 12-turn scripted escalation from initial engagement (turns 1-3), through delusion formation (turns 4-6) and consolidation (turns 7-9), to possible behavioral enactment (turns 10-12). Subsequent user messages were predetermined and did not change in response to the model's preceding replies, thereby standardizing the user trajectory across models and conditions.

Models and Generation Settings

Three LLMs were evaluated: GPT-5 (OpenAI), Gemini 2.5 Pro (Google), and DeepSeek V4 Flash [19] (see Table 1 and Supplementary Appendix, Section A.1). In the three intervention conditions, each model generated its own guidance before producing its final response to the user. Thus, no external model generated guidance for the model being evaluated.

A separate fixed judge, GPT-4o-mini, was used only for outcome scoring. This model was retained because it was used as the judge in the source Psychosis-Bench study, preserving continuity with the benchmark evaluation procedure [19] (see Table 1). The judge did not participate in conversation generation, risk appraisal, activation, or self-guidance. For GPT-5, the temperature parameter was omitted for both user-facing response generation and self-guidance calls, leaving sampling behavior provider-controlled (see Table S2 in Supplementary Appendix).

The automated judge, gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18, was run at temperature 0.0. Because the source Psychosis-Bench study specified GPT-4o-mini as its judge but did not report a judge temperature, the present study fixed this parameter to reduce sampling-induced variability in rubric-based scoring and to maintain a consistent evaluation configuration across models and conditions [19, 34] (see Supplementary Appendix, Section B.1).

Experimental Architecture and Conditions

Following the generation structure of the source Psychosis-Bench evaluation, each target model completed one 12-turn conversation for each of the 16 benchmark scenarios under Baseline and Treatments A, B, and C. Each scenario consisted of 12 predetermined user messages representing a progressively developing delusion-related interaction. A separate conversation was initiated for every model × scenario × condition combination, and all condition-specific state was cleared before the next scenario.

Conversation generation proceeded sequentially through the scripted user turns. At each turn, the next predetermined user message was appended to the accumulated conversation history.

The target model then generated a user-facing response, which was added to that history and became part of the context available on the following turn. The scripted user trajectory itself did not change in response to the model. Thus, within a given scenario, every model and condition encountered the same sequence of user messages while retaining its own preceding responses as conversational context.

Figure 1 summarizes the experimental architecture and the position of self-guidance within the conversation loop. In Baseline, the target model received the accumulated conversation history through the current user message and generated its user-facing response directly. In Treatments A-C, the same target LLM was additionally invoked in distinct functional roles before producing that response. These roles were implemented as separate model calls within a sequential agentic self-guidance architecture and did not represent independent autonomous agents.

Researcher-authored prompts defined the operation performed at each stage, whereas the target model under evaluation generated the conversation-specific intermediate outputs and final response. A separate automated judge operated only after the user-facing response had been generated and never participated in appraisal, activation, guidance generation, or response production (see Supplementary Appendix, Section A.2 and Table S3 in Supplementary Appendix). If the activation stage determined that guidance was not needed, the guidance-generation stage was skipped and the response followed the Baselineequivalent generation path. In Treatments A and B, existing guidance could be reused without new guidance generation. The automated judge operated only after the user-facing response had been generated and did not participate in the self-guidance pipeline.

Outcome Evaluation

Outcome evaluation combined benchmark-based automated scoring with a complementary blinded clinical evaluation. The automated evaluation provided standardized measurement across all conversations using the three Psychosis-Bench outcomes and preserved continuity with the source benchmark. The clinical evaluation examined whether differences in these benchmarkdefined safety outcomes were also reflected in broader clinically relevant features of conversational conduct that were not fully captured by the automated rubrics.

The three Psychosis-Bench outcomes capture distinct response behaviors and were not combined into a global score [19]. DCS scoring began at turn 4, when delusion-related content first emerged, whereas HES and SIS scoring began at turn 7, when potential harm and opportunities for safety intervention became relevant (see Table 2). A separate fixed automated judge scored each eligible assistant response using the published Psychosis-Bench rubrics. The judge received the conversation through the current assistant reply and, for HES, the benchmarkdefined harm category; it did not receive the target-model identity, condition label, or intervention artifact (see Supplementary Appendix, Section B.1).

Statistical Analysis

The analyses followed the research questions, directional hypotheses, and matched comparisons specified in the registration document, available on OSF [35].

The model-averaging procedures, statistical tests, and multiplicity families described below operationalized that plan. Across-model estimates used equally weighted scores from the three evaluated systems. The models were treated as a fixed set of evaluated systems; inferential tests therefore quantify variation across scenario profiles or cue pairs and should not be interpreted as Secondary direct comparisons among Treatments A, B, and C were conducted separately within each model and outcome using two-sided paired t tests across the 16 matched scenarios.

The 27 comparisons formed one FDR family. Mean safety-oriented differences, Cohen dz, and unadjusted 95% confidence intervals were reported (see Table S10 in Supplementary Appendix). An additional post-registration exploratory descriptive analysis examined whether Stage-2 guidance activation differed between Explicit and Implicit scenarios in Treatments A-C. An active turn was defined by the recorded intervention indicator (intervened = 1), including creation, reuse, or refinement of guidance. Activation rates were calculated across all 12 turns, consistent with the treatment-exposure analysis, and separately across turns 7-12, corresponding to the HES and SIS scoring windows. Rates were computed within each model, treatment, scenario pair, and cue type, then averaged equally across models to characterize differences across the eight matched Explicit-Implicit pairs. No inferential tests were conducted. Stage-1 severity values were unavailable in the exported analysis dataset and were not analyzed (see Supplementary Appendix, Section B.2).

Ethical Considerations

The study used fixed synthetic user scripts and model-generated replies. No participants were recruited, and the study used no patient records or interaction with people in crisis. The complementary clinical evaluation comprised expert ratings of synthetic conversations by two psychologists with clinical training; it involved no clinical assessment or treatment of research participants. Institutional review board approval and participant informed consent were not applicable to this simulation-based study. The outcomes therefore estimate benchmark-assessed conversational behavior; they do not show that a model can diagnose, treat, or manage psychosis.

Results

Completion and Intervention Exposure

All 192 planned conversations were available for analysis, with complete ratings in the outcomespecific scoring windows. Guidance was active on 93.8% of Treatment A turns, 81.9% of Treatment B turns, and 84.2% of Treatment C turns. No Stage-3 generation failure was recorded in the treatment exposure data (see Table S4 in Supplementary Appendix). In the additional descriptive analysis, activation across all 12 turns was higher in Explicit than Implicit scenarios under Treatment A (95.8% vs. 91.7%), Treatment B (86.5% vs. 77.4%), and Treatment C (88.2% vs. 80.2%). During turns 7-12, activation reached 100% for Explicit scenarios in all three treatments, compared with 98.6%, 91.0%, and 95.8% for Implicit scenarios under Treatments A, B, and C, respectively (Table S5 in Supplementary Appendix).

Clinical Evaluation Interrater Reliability

Interrater agreement was estimated using linear weighted Cohen's κ for each of the four ordinal items. κ values were .55 for referral, .74 for uncritical agreement, .58 for effect on harm risk, and .61 for timing of concern. Exact agreement ranged from 50.0% to 66.7% across items. Confidence intervals around the κ estimates were wide, consistent with the small number of rated conversations (see Table S26 in Supplementary Appendix).

Blinded clinical-evaluation ratings showed a condition pattern consistent with more safetyoriented clinical content under self-guidance (Table 3). Discussion

Principal Findings

The present study examined whether model-generated inference-time self-guidance improved Psychosis-Bench safety outcomes during standardized scripted escalation in delusion-related conversations [19]. Across the three evaluated models, all intervention conditions reduced delusion confirmation and harm enablement and increased safety intervention relative to Baseline at the pooled level. Cue-related effects were more selective: explicit risk cues elicited greater safety intervention than matched implicit cues at Baseline, and the interventions did not establish a reliable reduction in the explicit-implicit safety gap after correction for multiple comparisons.

Overall, self-guidance improved benchmark-defined safety behavior while indirectly communicated risk remained an important boundary.

The blinded clinical evaluation provided complementary evidence. Across the selected conversations, all three intervention conditions received more favorable ratings than Baseline for referral, uncritical agreement, apparent effect on harm risk, and timing of concern. These dimensions extended beyond the behaviors captured by the automated benchmark outcomes, indicating that condition differences were also visible under a broader assessment of conversational conduct. However, the clinical evaluation used a small, mechanically selected within-case sample and was intended for descriptive triangulation, not to establish clinical effectiveness or validate the automated measures.

The overall treatment effect is consistent with the importance of conversational context in multiturn safety. Earlier responses become part of the context used to generate later replies, allowing reinforcing or unsafe patterns to accumulate across turns [3, 12, 15]. Recent experimental work similarly found that accumulated conversation history altered responses to delusional beliefs in model-dependent ways, with poorer safety responses in some models but stronger safety interventions in others [37]. In the present procedure, risk appraisal and activation preceded condition-specific model-generated guidance that was incorporated into final response generation.

Related work has shown that model-generated feedback and demonstrations can modify subsequent outputs during inference without parameter updates [31, 32]. The present results extend this principle to delusion-related conversational safety. However, because appraisal, activation, and guidance generation were implemented as a combined researcher-defined procedure, the observed effects cannot be attributed uniquely to self-generated guidance.

Differences among guidance formats were limited. Although the three interventions varied in complexity and structure, none showed a consistent advantage across models and outcomes.

The isolated advantage of contrastive examples over response rules for Gemini 2.5 Pro did not generalize. The findings therefore support the broader value of structured inference-time safety processing more strongly than they support any particular guidance format. More complex procedures, including consequence simulation and selection, would need to demonstrate incremental benefits sufficient to justify their additional latency, token use, and computational cost.

Variation in treatment effects was more apparent across models. DeepSeek V4 Flash and Gemini 2.5 Pro improved on every evaluated outcome under each intervention, whereas GPT-5 showed a more selective pattern. One plausible contributor is limited baseline headroom: GPT-5 began with harm enablement near the safer end of the scale and comparatively high safety intervention, leaving less measurable room for improvement. Fixed benchmarks can become less discriminative as performance approaches the safer end of their scales [38], and prior work has documented substantial variation in psychosis-related safety behavior across LLMs [19]. However, because the study did not test model-by-treatment interactions, these differences should be interpreted as heterogeneity in observed treatment patterns, not evidence of intrinsic differences in responsiveness to self-guidance.

Indirectly communicated risk remained a notable limitation of the intervention approach.

At Baseline, explicit scenarios elicited substantially greater safety intervention than matched implicit scenarios, consistent with prior Psychosis-Bench findings showing poorer safety performance when risk was communicated indirectly [19]. Although the SIS gap was descriptively smaller under all three interventions, no treatment produced a statistically significant attenuation of the explicit-implicit gap. In the post-registration analyses, the explicit SIS advantage remained detectable under contrastive examples and short-horizon consequence simulation in the primary parametric analysis. The rank-based robustness analysis supported this finding for short-horizon consequence simulation, whereas the contrastive-examples contrast was not significant, with only five nonzero matched-pair differences contributing to that test. The absence of a significant within-condition contrast under response rules does not establish elimination of the disparity, because the direct gap-reduction analysis did not show significant attenuation for any treatment. Taken together, improved overall safety did not reliably eliminate sensitivity to how risk was communicated.

Implicit communication requires integration of contextual information beyond what is

stated explicitly, and LLM performance varies across pragmatic-inference tasks [39]. Similar difficulties have been reported for implicit suicidal ideation in psychologically rich dialogue [40]. The post-registration descriptive analysis showed lower Stage-2 activation for Implicit than

Explicit scenarios under all three treatments. However, activation approached ceiling during the HES and SIS scoring windows, and most missed activations in these later turns were concentrated in a small number of scenario pairs. These findings indicate that activation differed by cue type but do not establish whether those differences caused the downstream safety disparity. Because Stage-1 severity values were unavailable in the exported dataset, potential cue-related differences in initial risk appraisal remain untested.

Finally, the results should be interpreted within the scope of the Psychosis-Bench outcomes. DCS, HES, and SIS quantify specific safety-relevant response behaviors but do not capture overall clinical appropriateness [19]. The DCS rubric assigns its lowest score to both direct challenges and grounding responses, although these approaches may have different clinical implications. Likewise, SIS captures whether an explicit recommendation to seek support is offered, without evaluating its necessity or potential repetition. Thus, more favorable benchmark scores cannot rule out over-correction through direct confrontation or unnecessary referral. The blinded clinical evaluation included a needs-based referral item but did not directly quantify either form of over-correction. Clinician-rated work has treated appropriate responses to psychotic content as multidimensional, including recognition of symptoms, avoidance of reinforcement, acknowledgment of urgency, and provision of appropriate guidance and resources [18]. Accordingly, the present results support improved benchmark-defined safety behavior but do not establish therapeutic quality or clinically optimal communication.

Limitations and Future Research

Several limitations constrain the interpretation of these findings. First, the intervention conditions combined risk appraisal, activation, and model-generated guidance before final response generation. The design therefore cannot isolate the contribution of each component or determine whether self-generated guidance provided a unique benefit. In particular, the study did not include a fixed safety-prompt condition. Thus, improvements over the unmodified Baseline cannot establish whether the full self-guidance procedures provide additional benefit beyond a static safety prompt. Dismantling studies should manipulate these stages separately and compare modelgenerated guidance with a fixed, researcher-authored safety prompt and other externally generated or generic guidance.

Second, conversations followed fixed scripted trajectories, so subsequent user messages did not change in response to the model. Although this enabled controlled comparisons across conditions, it does not establish whether safer responses would alter the course of an interaction or whether direct confrontation and repeated referrals would have adverse downstream consequences.

Future evaluations should incorporate adaptive simulated users and examine interactional outcomes such as help-seeking and changes in harmful beliefs. Such designs could also examine whether ambiguous risk cues warrant a direct, context-appropriate clarifying question, drawing on the clinical principle of direct inquiry in risk assessment [41].

Third, although the exploratory process analysis characterized Stage-2 activation by cue type, it did not establish where cue-related differences originated within the intervention pipeline.

Stage-1 severity values were unavailable in the exported dataset, late-turn activation differences were concentrated in a small number of scenario pairs, and only eight matched Explicit-Implicit pairs were available. Future studies should retain appraisal outputs and jointly examine appraisal, activation, guidance content, and final responses to determine which stages contribute to cuerelated differences in conversational safety.

Fourth, the primary outcomes were scored by a single automated judge. The blinded clinical evaluation provided complementary human assessment but covered only 12 mechanically selected conversations and was intended for descriptive triangulation. It did not directly validate the judge's turn-level ratings against human scoring or determine whether SIS improvements partly reflected increased use of explicit help-seeking language. Broader preregistered clinical evaluations with larger blinded-rater samples are needed to assess whether the observed patterns extend across the full scenario set.

Fifth, the scenarios were drawn from a publicly available benchmark and used without modification. Prior exposure to benchmark content cannot be excluded for commercial models with undisclosed training and post-training data, limiting certainty that performance generalizes to unseen scenarios [42]. Replication with newly developed held-out scenarios is therefore needed.

Finally, the study included a limited number of scenarios and cue pairs, one generation per model × condition × scenario cell, a single model-sampling period, and English-language scenarios covering a restricted range of delusion-related themes. Each conversation was limited to 12 scripted turns, whereas real-world chat logs document interactions extending across hundreds of turns and, in some cases, weeks [3]. The present design therefore cannot establish whether the observed benefits of conversation-local self-guidance persist or drift over longer interaction histories. The original Psychosis-Bench study reported descriptive variation across delusion themes and harm types, but the present study did not test whether the effectiveness of the three self-guidance strategies varied across these scenario characteristics. Future work should therefore use larger and more diverse scenario sets to examine intervention heterogeneity across delusion themes and harm contexts, while also evaluating longer conversations, repeated generations, additional model versions and sampling periods, languages, and cultural contexts.

Conclusions

The present study shows that conditions incorporating model-generated inference-time selfguidance can improve benchmark-defined responses during standardized scripted escalation in delusion-related conversations without updating model parameters. These improvements were observed across three forms of guidance, although their magnitude and consistency varied across models and outcomes. Indirectly communicated risk remained an important boundary, and the exploratory analyses did not establish that any intervention reduced the explicit-implicit disparity.

Taken together, the findings support further evaluation of conversation-specific self-guidance as an inference-time approach for modifying unsafe response behavior while underscoring the need to determine which components of the procedure are necessary and how safety-oriented processing can respond more reliably when psychologically significant risk is communicated indirectly.
