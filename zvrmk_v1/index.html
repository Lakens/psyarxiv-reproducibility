Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Ideation-to-action theories explain how suicidal desire develops and how some people come to act on it, but they give little guidance on interventions for the physiological state that accompanies suicidal crises. This article proposes Simulated Survival, a theoretical framework for using controlled, demanding experiences in immersive virtual reality (VR) as an adjunct to suicide-specific care. Drawing on the Three-Step Theory, the Integrated Motivational-Volitional model, and the Interpersonal Theory of Suicide, together with research on autonomic regulation, interoception, attention under pressure, challenge appraisal, adventure-based interventions, and VR, the framework specifies three hypothesized mechanisms: interruption of suicide-specific rumination through attention captured by demanding, embodied tasks; repeated stress-recovery cycles that train autonomic and interoceptive regulation; and the pairing of high arousal with successful protective action, intended to strengthen self-preservation rather than tolerance of death-related fear. Expectation violation is proposed as the learning process that turns short-term effects into lasting change. Because repeated exposure to danger could raise fearlessness about death, the framework sets design rules (no suicide-method cues, no virtual death states, graded difficulty) and treats fearlessness about death as a primary safety outcome. Eight testable propositions and a staged research path are set out. Simulated Survival offers a testable basis for body-based digital interventions in suicide prevention, and its value depends on experimental work that tests each mechanism against active VR control conditions before any clinical trial.

Simulated Survival: A Theoretical Framework for Using Immersive Virtual Reality Challenge to Target Ideation-to-Action Mechanisms of Suicide Risk

An estimated 727,000 people died by suicide in 2021, and most of these deaths occurred in low-and middle-income countries (World Health Organization [WHO], 2025). Fifty years of research on risk factors has produced weak prediction, with most factors performing only slightly better than chance (Franklin et al., 2017), and machine-learning approaches have not yet solved this problem (Burke et al., 2019; Nordin et al., 2022). One response has been to study the processes that move a person toward suicidal behavior, rather than to add more distal risk markers.

The ideation-to-action framework is the clearest product of that shift. It holds that the development of suicidal ideation and the progression from ideation to suicide attempts are separate processes with partly different causes (Klonsky & May, 2014; Klonsky et al., 2018).

Three theories lead the framework: the Interpersonal Theory of Suicide (IPTS; Joiner, 2005; Van Orden et al., 2010), the Integrated Motivational-Volitional model (IMV; O'Connor & Kirtley, 2018), and the Three-Step Theory (3ST; Klonsky & May, 2015; Klonsky et al., 2021).

Consistent with this distinction, depression, hopelessness, and most psychiatric diagnoses are associated with ideation but separate people who attempt suicide from those who only think about it poorly (May & Klonsky, 2016).

These theories have improved the understanding of risk, but they say less about how to change the state a person is in during a suicidal crisis. That state has a bodily side. A metaanalysis of 22 studies found reduced heart rate variability (HRV) in adults with suicidal thoughts and behaviors, although the pooled effect was small and the association with electrodermal activity was not significant (Chowdhury et al., 2026). Interoceptive deficits distinguish suicide groups in a military sample (Smith et al., 2020), and autonomic measures have been proposed as point-of-care aids to risk assessment (McCall et al., 2022). People with suicidal thoughts and behaviors also show difficulties in decision-making and cognitive control (Gifuni et al., 2021) and an attentional bias toward suicide-related information (Richard-Devantoy et al., 2016).

Interventions that rely mainly on verbal reasoning may therefore be hardest to use when risk is highest.

Body-based strategies already exist. Dialectical behavior therapy teaches crisis-survival skills that change physiology directly, including cold-water immersion of the face, brief intense exercise, and paced breathing (Linehan, 2015). Heart rate variability biofeedback trains slow breathing at a resonance frequency (Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014), and a meta-analysis found that it reduces self-reported stress and anxiety (Goessl et al., 2017). An integrative review of mindbody interventions concluded that those working bottom-up, through body movement and emotional expression, remain under-studied compared with meditation-based approaches (Lazzarelli et al., 2024). The gap, then, is not an absence of body-based tools. It is the absence of a framework that ties such tools to the constructs of ideation-to-action theory and states which suicide-related processes they should change, by what route, and with what risks.

This article proposes such a framework, which we call Simulated Survival. Its central claim is that demanding but controllable challenge, followed by guided physiological recovery and delivered in immersive virtual reality (VR), may act on several mechanisms named by ideation-to-action theories. We do not claim that this intervention works. We set out what it would have to do, how each step could be tested, and what could go wrong.

Approach

We used a narrative theoretical synthesis. The starting point was the three ideation-toaction theories. For each construct those theories treat as central (psychological pain, hopelessness, defeat, entrapment, connectedness, and capability), we looked for evidence on its bodily, attentional, and neural correlates and for interventions that could plausibly change it. We then drew on research on autonomic regulation, interoception, optimal performance states, challenge and threat appraisal, adventure and nature-based interventions, and VR in mental health care and in suicide research. This is a conceptual paper, not a systematic review. Evidence was selected for its relevance to each proposed link, with priority given to meta-analyses, systematic reviews, and primary studies that tested a link directly, so the studies cited illustrate each link rather than exhaust it. The framework was written to meet three requirements: each mechanism had to map onto a named theoretical construct, each had to yield a prediction that could fail, and each had to state its main risk. No new data were collected for this article. A generative AI tool (Claude, Opus 5.5 version; Anthropic, 2026) was used to assist with language editing and with checking reference details against bibliographic records; the authors reviewed all output and take full responsibility for the content.

Targets in the Ideation-to-Action Pathway Pain, Hopelessness, and Rigid Expectations

The 3ST proposes that suicidal ideation begins when psychological pain is combined with hopelessness about that pain improving (Klonsky & May, 2015). Shneidman (1993) called this unbearable pain psychache. Predictive processing accounts of depression suggest why hopelessness can persist despite contrary experience: negative expectations about the self and the future are held with high confidence, and information that contradicts them tends to be discounted rather than used to revise them (Kube et al., 2020). If this account applies to suicidal hopelessness, an intervention would need to produce experiences that clearly violate these expectations and are hard to dismiss.

Defeat, Entrapment, and Suicide-Specific Rumination

In the IMV model, ideation emerges when defeat or humiliation is followed by entrapment, the sense that escape from one's circumstances, or from one's own thoughts and feelings, is impossible (O'Connor & Kirtley, 2018). Gilbert and Allan (1998) described entrapment as arrested flight, a blocked urge to escape. In a large online sample, defeat and entrapment both predicted suicide risk, and in cross-lagged analysis defeat predicted later entrapment but not the reverse (Comtois et al., 2025). Internal and external entrapment are both associated with ideation and suicidal behavior in clinical samples (Aliño-Dies et al., 2026; Rasmussen et al., 2023), and internal entrapment mediates the association between posttraumatic stress and suicidal planning (Oakey-Frost et al., 2022). Suicide-specific rumination, repeated thinking about one's suicidal thoughts and plans, has been linked to the persistence of ideation over time (Hensel & Teismann, 2026).

Neuroimaging findings fit this picture but need careful reading. Reviews describe altered function in the default mode network (DMN), the salience network, and frontoparietal control networks in people with suicidal thoughts and behaviors (Dobbertin et al., 2023), and restingstate data link active ideation to DMN dysfunction in young people (Chin Fatt et al., 2024). The DMN supports self-referential thought and has been associated with depressive rumination (Hamilton et al., 2015; Menon, 2023). These findings are correlational, often come from small samples, and do not show that changing DMN activity changes ideation. We treat them as a source of hypotheses, not as an established mechanism.

Connectedness and Interpersonal Needs

In the 3ST, connectedness to people, roles, or a sense of purpose is the main factor that keeps ideation from becoming strong when pain is high (Klonsky & May, 2015). The IPTS places thwarted belongingness and perceived burdensomeness at the center of suicidal desire (Van Orden et al., 2010). Meta-analytic support is stronger for perceived burdensomeness and for the interaction of the two constructs than for thwarted belongingness alone (Chu et al., 2017).

An intervention that ignores these interpersonal needs can address only part of the motivational phase.

Capability and Volitional Factors

The step from ideation to attempt requires capability. The IPTS describes acquired capability as reduced fear of death together with raised pain tolerance, built up through repeated painful and provocative events (Van Orden et al., 2010). The 3ST widens the construct to dispositional, acquired, and practical capability, the last including knowledge of and access to lethal means (Klonsky & May, 2015). The IMV model adds other volitional factors, among them fearlessness about death, impulsivity, access to means, and mental imagery about death (O'Connor & Kirtley, 2018). Imagery matters here because vivid, future-oriented images of suicide are common among depressed people with suicidal ideation (Holmes et al., 2007).

Capability is usually measured as fearlessness about death (Ribeiro et al., 2014), although its factor structure is still debated (Thomas et al., 2025).

The Acute State

Fluid vulnerability theory separates a person's baseline, chronic risk from acute suicidal episodes, which are time-limited and involve linked changes in cognition, emotion, physiology, and behavior (Rudd, 2006). Simulated Survival is aimed at processes that are active in and around these episodes: suicide-specific rumination, internal entrapment, and poorly regulated arousal. It is not designed to treat chronic vulnerability on its own.

Existing Approaches and the Remaining Gap

Two bodies of work suggest that challenge-based experience can help. The first concerns adventure and nature. A review of 61 reviews classified wilderness and adventure therapy as one of 13 types of nature-based intervention and identified social contact and physical activity among the factors that moderate their effects (Kaleta et al., 2025). In a meta-analysis of group programs for adolescents, quasi-experimental studies showed a large pooled effect on mental health, and multi-day programs led by clinically trained or specialist facilitators tended to do better (Campbell et al., 2026). In an early intervention service for psychosis, most participants in a four-day adventure therapy trip reported gains in confidence and connectedness, although questionnaire measures of recovery did not improve at follow-up (Collin et al., 2025). Interview studies of extreme sport participants describe fear as something used and managed rather than simply endured (Brymer & Schweitzer, 2013), and an ecological account locates the value of adventure in the fit between person, task, and setting rather than in risk itself (Brymer et al., 2024). None of this work was designed for suicide prevention, and real high-risk activities cannot ethically be prescribed to people at risk of suicide.

The second body of work concerns VR. Immersive VR produces presence, the sense of being in the virtual place, so that perceptual and bodily responses resemble responses to real events (Slater & Sanchez-Vives, 2016). An umbrella review reported benefits of VR and gamebased interventions for anxiety, depression, and stress, while noting that few randomized trials exist and that methods are often weak (Coelho & Abreu, 2026). In a large randomized trial, automated VR therapy reduced agoraphobic avoidance and distress in patients with psychosis (Freeman et al., 2022). Two quasi-experimental studies from one research group reported reductions in depression, anxiety, and stress after VR extreme-sports sessions in people with social anxiety disorder (Wang et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2026); these results await independent replication. In suicide research, VR has been used to create laboratory approximations of suicidal behavior (Franklin et al., 2019), to test dissociation as a facilitator of risk (Caulfield et al., 2022), and to examine who chooses to jump in a virtual suicide scenario (Buerke et al., 2025).

Longitudinal follow-up of participants in these paradigms did not find increases in suicidal thoughts or behaviors (Huang et al., 2021).

The remaining gap is therefore specific. Body-based crisis skills are taught as coping strategies but are not tied to ideation-to-action constructs. Adventure programs act on these constructs only incidentally. VR suicide paradigms study risk rather than reduce it. No framework yet specifies how controlled challenge could act on suicide-specific rumination, entrapment, autonomic regulation, and capability, or how it could be made safe for people with suicidal ideation.

The Simulated Survival Framework

Simulated Survival is defined as a series of sessions in which a person faces a demanding but controllable challenge in immersive VR, succeeds through their own actions, and then completes guided physiological recovery. Each session therefore has three phases: challenge, success, and recovery. Mechanism 1: Interrupting Suicide-Specific Rumination

Demanding, embodied tasks compete for the attention that rumination needs. Studies of climbers distinguish two optimal states: flow, an effortless absorption in the task, and clutch, a deliberate, effortful focus under pressure (Boudreau et al., 2022; Swann et al., 2017). Flow is reliably associated with better performance (Harris et al., 2023). When a task requires continuous perception and action, little capacity is left for repetitive self-focused thought. We hypothesize that state rumination and the intensity of suicidal thoughts fall during and shortly after such tasks, and that this effect is larger than that of an equally engaging task with low arousal.

The proposed neural correlate is a shift away from DMN-dominant activity toward the salience and control networks that support externally directed attention (Menon, 2023). A competing account, transient hypofrontality, holds that intense physical activity temporarily reduces prefrontal activity and, with it, effortful thought such as rumination (Dietrich, 2006). The two accounts predict different patterns of executive performance during the task, so they can be compared directly. For people in a suicidal state, clutch may be easier to reach than flow, because flow depends on a balance between skill and challenge that may not yet exist. This is one reason the framework requires graded difficulty.

On its own, this mechanism would amount to distraction. Distraction can reduce rumination in the moment, but short-term relief is not lasting change. The framework therefore treats Mechanism 1 as the condition that opens a period in which the other mechanisms can work, not as the therapeutic effect itself.

Mechanism 2: Training Autonomic and Interoceptive Regulation

The neurovisceral integration model links vagally mediated HRV to flexible, prefrontally guided regulation of emotion and attention (Thayer & Lane, 2000). Reduced HRV in adults with suicidal thoughts and behaviors (Chowdhury et al., 2026) points to reduced flexibility in this system. Simulated Survival proposes that repeated cycles of sympathetic activation during challenge, followed by guided parasympathetic recovery, can train that flexibility. The recovery phase uses slow, paced breathing of the kind used in HRV biofeedback, which has a welldescribed physiological basis (Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014) and evidence of benefit for stress and anxiety (Goessl et al., 2017). Real-time feedback during recovery lets the person see their heart rate fall as a result of what they are doing.

VR suits this mechanism because it can produce measurable stress responses. In military police recruits, a VR scenario raised heart rate by about 26% above baseline, compared with about 34% for the same scenario performed in real life (Brinkmann et al., 2026). VR therefore induces real but somewhat weaker arousal, which is acceptable, and possibly preferable, for a clinical population. Interoceptive accuracy may also relate to the sense of owning a virtual body, although this evidence is preliminary (Savickaite et al., 2026). We hypothesize that the speed of HRV recovery after challenge improves across sessions, and that this improvement predicts later reductions in internal entrapment, because the person repeatedly experiences high arousal as something they can bring down.

Mechanism 3: Strengthening Self-Preservation Under Arousal

This mechanism carries the most risk and must be stated with care. Acquired capability is, by definition, reduced fear of death built through repeated exposure to pain and fear (Van Orden et al., 2010). An intervention that repeatedly placed people in vivid near-death scenes could lower that fear through habituation, the very process the IPTS describes as dangerous.

Evidence from VR suicide research makes the concern concrete. Some of these paradigms use stepping from a virtual height as the analogue of a suicidal act, and suicidal ideation predicts choosing to jump (Buerke et al., 2025; Franklin et al., 2019). The IMV model also counts imagery about death as a volitional risk factor (O'Connor & Kirtley, 2018).

Simulated Survival therefore does not aim to raise tolerance of death-related fear. Its aim runs in the opposite direction. Each challenge requires the person to take protective action, such as reaching shelter, steadying their footing, or guiding another person to safety, and to succeed.

High arousal is repeatedly paired with self-protective behavior and its success. In social cognitive terms, these are mastery experiences, which Bandura (1977) identified as the most dependable source of self-efficacy expectations. In the biopsychosocial model of challenge and threat, the aim is to shift the appraisal of high-demand situations from threat toward challenge (Blascovich, 2008), a state associated with better performance (Hase et al., 2025). The prediction is that self-efficacy rises while fearlessness about death does not. If fearlessness about death rises, the mechanism has failed and the design must change.

Expectation Violation and Consolidation

The three mechanisms describe what happens within sessions. Lasting change requires learning, and we propose that the main learning process is expectation violation. A person who expects "I cannot escape" or "I cannot cope when my body feels like this" meets repeated, concrete evidence to the contrary: they act, they succeed, and their arousal comes down. In predictive processing terms, these outcomes are prediction errors that could revise rigid negative expectations, provided they are salient and hard to discount (Kube et al., 2020). Riva et al. (2019) argued that VR is well placed to produce such errors because, like the brain, it works by simulating the expected sensory consequences of action. Exposure research points the same way.

In the inhibitory learning account, the gap between what a person expects and what happens drives new learning, and that learning holds better when it takes place across varied contexts (Craske et al., 2014). This implies that scenarios should vary across sessions.

Transfer to daily life cannot be assumed. Each session should end with a short debrief that links what happened in VR to the person's own warning signs and coping steps, and the paced-breathing skill should be written into the person's safety plan (Stanley & Brown, 2012).

Without this step, gains may stay inside the headset.

Interpersonal Elements

A solo VR task cannot meet the need to belong. Two design features may partly address the IPTS constructs. Scenarios can include a virtual companion whom the person helps to safety, which gives a direct experience of being useful to someone else and bears on perceived burdensomeness. Sessions can also be delivered with a clinician present who joins the debrief.

Both features are hypotheses to be tested, not established effects.

Design Principles and Safety

Target Population and Setting

Simulated Survival is proposed as an adjunct to suicide-specific care, not as a stand-alone treatment. The intended population is adults with recent suicidal ideation who are not at imminent risk and who are already receiving clinical care, for example in outpatient services or after discharge from inpatient care. It is not intended for use during an active crisis, when a person needs immediate assessment and safety management. Sessions should take place in a supervised clinical setting, with a clinician able to stop a session at any point.

Scenario Design Rules

Four rules follow from the risks described above. First, scenarios must contain no suicide-method cues: no heights or falls, no firearms, no drowning, and no fire, the last being relevant in regions where self-immolation occurs. Second, there are no virtual death states. If the person fails a step, the scenario pauses and offers an easier route, so that failure cannot reinforce defeat. Third, difficulty is graded and adjusts to performance and to physiological response, keeping arousal within a preset range. Fourth, success always depends on the person's own protective actions, never on being rescued.

As an illustration, consider a scenario set on a canyon floor during a rockfall. The person must time their movements to cross open ground, help an injured hiker to a shelter, and reach safety. All hazards are at ground level. Once both reach the shelter, the scene calms, and a virtual coach leads two to three minutes of paced breathing while a display shows the person's heart rate. The clinician then leads a brief debrief. Scenario content, session length, and dose are open questions for development research.

Exclusions, Monitoring, and Adverse Effects

Exclusion criteria should include current psychosis, severe dissociation, uncontrolled epilepsy or photosensitivity, and unmanaged cardiovascular disease. People whose painful and provocative experiences overlap with a scenario's content, such as combat, need individual assessment and may need trauma-informed adaptation. A systematic review of VR and augmented reality interventions in psychiatry found that 45 of 73 studies did not mention adverse effects at all, and that seven reported worsening symptoms or an increased risk of falls (Lundin et al., 2023). Trials of Simulated Survival should therefore record cybersickness, distress, dissociation, and suicidal ideation before and after every session, and should treat fearlessness about death as a primary safety outcome with a predefined stopping rule.

Limitations and Future Directions

The framework has clear limits. Most of the evidence behind each mechanism comes from people who were not suicidal, such as climbers, military recruits, and people with social anxiety. The neural account rests on correlational imaging data. The VR extreme-sports studies most similar to the proposed intervention come from one research group and have not been replicated. VR also produces weaker arousal than real-world challenge (Brinkmann et al., 2026), and whether that arousal is enough to drive the proposed learning is unknown. Finally, persistent and recurrent suicidal ideation are not yet consistently defined (Spangenberg et al., 2025), which complicates the choice of outcome measures.

Access is a further concern. Most suicides occur in low-and middle-income countries (WHO, 2025), where VR hardware and supervised delivery may be hard to provide. Standalone headsets have reduced costs, but clinical supervision remains essential. The meaning of adventure and challenge also differs across cultures, so scenario content will need local adaptation and testing.

We suggest a staged path based on the ORBIT model for developing behavioral treatments (Czajkowski et al., 2015). The first stage would test each mechanism in laboratory studies, beginning with healthy volunteers and then with people who have recent ideation, against the control conditions described above. The second stage would test feasibility, acceptability, and safety, with fearlessness about death as a primary outcome. Only if both stages succeed would a randomized trial against an active VR control, with suicidal ideation and behavior as outcomes, be justified.

Conclusion

Simulated Survival proposes that demanding but controllable challenge, delivered safely in VR and followed by guided recovery, may interrupt suicide-specific rumination, train autonomic regulation, and strengthen self-preserving action under arousal. It extends ideation-to-action theory toward intervention rather than replacing it. Its most important feature may be its constraints: it excludes suicide-method cues, avoids virtual death, and treats any rise in fearlessness about death as a sign of failure. Each proposition can be tested, and each could prove wrong. That is the standard against which the framework should be judged.
