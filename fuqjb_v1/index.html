To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 9 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 51 papers with 87 statements, this module correctly identified 38 statements (true positives) and incorrectly flagged 22 statements (false positives). It failed to detect 27 statements. Thus, among all statements flagged by the module, 63% were genuine cases (positive predictive value). However, the module missed 42% of all true statements (false negative rate).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is effective for anxiety disorders, depression and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), but the neurobiological mechanisms of change remain unclear. This systematic review synthesized neuroimaging evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to examine whether CBT can be considered a neurobiological intervention. Following PRISMA guidelines (PROSPERO: CRD420251239465), PubMed, Scopus and PsycInfo were searched for RCTs of CBT in adults with anxiety disorders, depression or OCD-spectrum disorders reporting preand post-treatment neuroimaging. Risk of bias was assessed with RoB 2, and findings were synthesized narratively. Seventeen RCTs were included (OCD = 5, anxiety disorders = 6, depression = 6), using task-based fMRI (n = 8), resting-state fMRI (n = 6), structural MRI (n = 2) or PET (n = 1). Changes after CBT involved emotional reactivity regions, prefrontal and frontoparietal control systems and their connectivity, with disorder-specific patterns: reduced threat reactivity in anxiety disorders, altered cognitive control networks in OCD, and altered mood-related, self-referential and reward connectivity in depression. Neural and clinical change did not always coincide, and comparisons with pharmacotherapy were mixed. Five studies were at high risk of bias. These findings provide preliminary support for CBT as a neurobiological intervention, although small samples and heterogeneous protocols limit conclusions.

Introduction

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is one of the most empirically supported psychotherapeutic approaches for anxiety disorders, depression and obsessivecompulsive disorder (OCD) (Cuijpers et al., 2023; Hassan Kariri & Almubaddel, 2024), and is recommended as the first-line treatment by the guidelines of international organizations (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, 2024; World Health Organization, 2023). Despite the strong evidence for its effectiveness, the mechanisms through which therapeutic change is achieved remain less well understood. As early as the late 1990s, it was argued that psychotherapy works through learning and, consequently, through changes in the brain (Kandel, 1998).

The cognitive models of anxiety and depression describe psychopathology as an imbalance between the increased bottom-up processing of the threat stimulus and the inefficient top-down cognitive control, with CBT acting to reinforce the latter (Clark & Beck, 2010). In terms of neurobiology, bottom-up processing involves brain regions related to emotional reactivity such as the amygdala, the insula, and the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), while the top-down control relies on prefrontal regions that regulate their activity (Etkin et al., 2011; Ochsner & Gross, 2005). According to these models, effective CBT would be expected to reduce emotional reactivity, increase activation in prefrontal regions, and alter the connectivity between them.

Different models of learning suggest different mechanisms through which this change is achieved, mostly through exposure to the threat stimulus. For example, according to the emotional processing theory, fear activation by exposure and its reduction during the sessions are prerequisites for therapeutic change (Foa & Kozak, 1986). In contrast, the inhibitory learning model suggests that this exposure does not modify the original fear memory but creates new, competing learning and the outcome largely depends on the ability to recall this in future circumstances (Craske et al., 2014).

Neuroimaging data could contribute in distinguishing between the two models, by examining which neurobiological changes are actually associated with clinical improvement.

Recently, an increasing number of studies has investigated the neurobiological changes that follow CBT, and some systematic reviews have attempted to synthesize their findings. However, the majority of these reviews focus either on a specific disorder or a specific neuroimaging technique, included studies with no comparing arm or excluded studies with no statistically significant results (König et al., 2025; Picó-Pérez et al., 2023; Poli et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2022). A review restricted to randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that examines different disorders and imaging techniques together can identify which findings are shared across disorders and which are disorderspecific. It can also examine whether these changes are associated with clinical improvement.

The aim of the present review was to examine whether CBT could be considered as a neurobiological intervention by synthesizing findings from RCTs in adults with anxiety disorders, depression or disorders within the obsessive-compulsive spectrum.

More specifically, the review examined a) which neurobiological changes follow CBT intervention and whether these are different among different disorders, b) if these changes are related to the patients' clinical improvement, c) whether the changes are different than those following pharmaceutical treatment or other types of psychotherapy, and d) if pre-intervention neurobiological indices could predict CBT response.

Methods

The review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (Page et al., 2021). The review process included the following steps; formulating the research question, conducting a thorough search of the existing literature, extracting and evaluating relevant data, and, lastly, synthesizing and presenting the findings. The review was pre-registered in the international registry PROSPERO under the registration number CRD420251239465.

Eligibility criteria

Each study had to meet specific eligibility criteria. These were defined a priori, and no modifications were made following the pre-registration in PROSPERO. A prior search of the registry identified no ongoing or completed reviews addressing the same question. Only RCTs that investigated the effect of CBT on neurobiological and clinical parameters were included. Study participants had to be adults (≥ 18 years of age) with a clinically confirmed diagnosis of an anxiety disorder, depression, or a disorder that fell under the obsessive-compulsive spectrum. The diagnosis had to be established based on DSM or ICD criteria, or via a standardized clinical interview (e.g., SCID, MINI) conducted by a qualified clinician. No criteria were set regarding the demographic characteristics of the studies' samples (e.g. gender or ethnicity) or the severity of the diagnosis. The therapeutic intervention had to be any form of CBT (individualized or group delivery), and to describe the intervention's protocol (number of sessions, duration, core techniques). Regarding neurobiological data, studies had to utilize specific neuroimaging methods. These methods are presented in Table 1. Studies had to be published in the English language by peer-reviewed journals, irrespective of the time of publication.

Regarding the criteria of exclusion, these were the following: a) samples including individuals under the age of 18; b) participants without a primary diagnosis of an anxiety disorder, a depressive disorder, or a disorder within the obsessivecompulsive spectrum; c) samples with a primary diagnosis outside the scope of this review (e.g., psychotic or neurocognitive disorders, autism spectrum disorders); d) studies where the intervention's objective was outside the scope of this review (e.g., stress management); e) studies in which CBT could not be isolated as an independent variable (e.g., combined with other interventions). Search strategy

The PubMed, Scopus, and PsycInfo databases were thoroughly searched between 1 and 27 August 2026, in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. The terms used were "Cognitive behavioral therapy" OR "CBT" AND "neural mechanisms" OR "neuroplasticity" OR "brain function" OR "functional neuroimaging" OR "fMRI" OR "rs-fMRI" OR "structural MRI" OR "PET" OR "DTI" AND "anxiety disorders" OR "generalized anxiety disorder" OR "panic disorder" OR "social anxiety" OR "mood disorders" OR "major depressive disorder" OR "depression" OR "specific phobias" OR "obsessive-compulsive disorder" OR "OCD" OR "OCD spectrum". AND "randomized controlled trial" OR "RCT". A similar conceptual structure was used in all databases, adapted to the syntax and available filters of each. All retrieved records were imported into Zotero, where duplicates were identified and removed using automatic and manual checks. Titles and abstracts were screened by two reviewers. Full texts of potentially eligible studies were assessed independently by two reviewers and disagreements were resolved through discussion. Additionally, a backward search (hand search of reference lists) of included papers was conducted to identify additional studies relevant to the topic.

Data extraction

The data extraction was conducted in a standardized manner to ensure the accuracy and reproducibility of the results, and was performed by two researchers. A pre-defined extraction form, developed specifically for the purposes of this review, was used for data collection. The data extracted by each study included the year of publication, sample size and demographic characteristics (where available), as well as the primary diagnosis and the diagnostic method employed. Regarding the intervention, details were collected on the method that CBT was delivered, the therapeutic protocol applied (number and frequency of the sessions), and the nature of the comparator arm.

Information was extracted regarding the performed neuroimaging techniques, the psychometric assessments (if any) conducted, and the primary findings of each study.

Quality assessment of the included studies

Methodological quality was assessed with the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for RCTs (RoB 2) (Sterne et al., 2019), which evaluates the following five domains; a) bias arising from the randomization process, b) bias due to deviations from intended interventions, c) bias due to missing outcome data, d) bias in the measurements of the outcome, and e) bias in the selection of the reported result. Each domain can be rated as low risk, with some concerns, or high risk of bias using signaling questions and the tool's algorithm. These domain-level judgements determine the overall risk-of-bias rating for each study. Risk of bias was assessed by one and checked by a second reviewer; any uncertainties were resolved through discussion.

Main characteristics of the included studies

The 17 included studies were published in peer-reviewed journals between 2006 and 2025. Five studies included participants with a diagnosis within the OCD spectrum, six included participants with an anxiety disorder (specific phobias, social anxiety disorder and panic disorder with or without agoraphobia), and six focused on depression. In total, the study groups comprised 492 participants and the comparator arms 782 participants. The weighted mean age of the participants in the CBT interventions was 33.9 years, with one study reporting only the age range (41-44 years).

The mean proportion of females was 57.6%, with one study not reporting the sex distribution of its sample. In four studies, a proportion of participants in the CBT group were receiving concurrent medication (mean across these studies 60.5%).

Six studies compared CBT with pharmacotherapy (fluoxetine, escitalopram, escitalopram/duloxetine, or unspecified SSRIs/SNRIs). Regarding the neuroimaging techniques, eight studies used task-based fMRI, six used resting-state fMRI, two used structural MRI, and one used PET. With respect to comparison conditions, nine studies used a waitlist control, six used active pharmacotherapy, and two used supportive psychotherapy. Eight studies employed a three-group design, comprising a CBT group, a clinical control group (waitlist or active treatment), and a healthy control group for comparison of neuroimaging data. Regarding the CBT protocol, three studies delivered group CBT and 14 delivered individual CBT. One study used computer-assisted CBT, combining five individual sessions with a nine-module online self-help program. Three studies were explicitly based on an exposure and response prevention (ERP) protocol.

The number of sessions ranged from one to 20. Lastly, session duration was approximately one hour for eight of the included studies, whereas the remaining nine used longer sessions, ranging from 90 minutes to 4-5 hours. Main findings based on the research question

Findings were grouped by brain region or network and diagnostic category (Table 3). Post-CBT changes clustered in three systems; regions of emotional reactivity (amygdala, insula, ACC), prefrontal cognitive control systems and the default mode network (DMN) and reward circuitry. The direction of prefrontal changes depended on the performed task. Activation increased during active emotion regulation (Goldin et al., 2014 (Goldin et al., , 2021) ) and decreased during emotion maintenance (Reinecke et al., 2018). In seven studies, at least one neurobiological change correlated with clinical improvement (Goldin et al., 2014; Jacoby et al., 2025; Katayama et al., 2023; Liebscher et al., 2016; Moody et al., 2017; Schienle et al., 2007; Sheline et al., 2025), whereas in two the most prominent changes did not (Jacoby et al., 2025; Katayama et al., 2022). Four studies identified pre-treatment predictors of outcome (Dunlop et al., 2017; Hoexter et al., 2013; McGrath et al., 2014; Reggente et al., 2018), two of which differentiated response to CBT from pharmacotherapy (Dunlop et al., 2017; Hoexter et al., 2013).

Obsessive compulsive disorder

CBT did not alter grey matter volume, although symptom severity decreased by 34.5% (p = .001) for both treatment groups, and putamen volume increased only with fluoxetine (Hoexter et al., 2012). Larger pre-treatment right medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) volume predicted response to CBT, whereas smaller lateral orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) volume predicted response to fluoxetine (Hoexter et al., 2013). Increased amygdala, hippocampal and ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (vlPFC) activation after CBT did not correlate with symptom change, whereas reduced left Brodmann area (BA) 40 activation during fear extinction recall did (Z = 2.7, p = .046) (Jacoby et al., 2025).

Increased right frontal-left parietal connectivity correlated with improved resistance to compulsions (r = .51, p < .001) (Moody et al., 2017), and pre-treatment DMN-visual network connectivity explained up to 67% of the variance in post-treatment severity (Reggente et al., 2018).

Anxiety disorders

Reduced amygdala and insula activity after CBT was the most consistent finding, reported in four of the six studies (Liebscher et al., 2016; Reinecke et al., 2018; Schienle et al., 2007; Straube et al., 2006). It correlated with improvements in anxiety (Liebscher et al., 2016) and somatic symptoms of anxiety (Schienle et al., 2007), and was accompanied by normalization of insula and ACC hyperactivity in spider phobia (Straube et al., 2006). In panic disorder, amygdala activation decreased after both therapist-guided and self-directed exposure (Liebscher et al., 2016), and 71% of patients recovered after four sessions of CBT, compared with 7% on the waitlist (p < .001) (Reinecke et al., 2018). In social anxiety disorder, changes during social criticism predicted 24% of the variance in symptom reduction (Goldin et al., 2014), whereas, when compared with mindfulness-based stress reduction, CBT resulted in similar increases in cognitive control activation, with no between-group differences in emotion (p = .10-.71) or neural activity (p > .28) (Goldin et al., 2021).

Depression

Higher pre-treatment glucose metabolism in the subgenual cingulate cortex (SCC) (d = 1.15) and right superior temporal sulcus (d = 1.76) predicted non-response to both CBT and escitalopram (McGrath et al., 2014). SCC connectivity predicted outcome in opposite directions for CBT and medication (Dunlop et al., 2017). After CBT, change in frontoparietal network-subgenual ACC connectivity correlated with BDI-II reduction (p = .01) (Sheline et al., 2025), and increased frontal-nucleus accumbens connectivity predicted fewer negative automatic thoughts and lower depression severity at 6-and 12-month post-intervention measurements (Katayama et al., 2023). Reduced BA10 activation correlated with positive automatic thoughts but not with depression severity (Katayama et al., 2022). Remission irrespective of treatment was associated with decreased cingulate-motor cortex connectivity, whereas CBT specifically increased executive control-parietal connectivity, exceeding healthy control levels at week-12 of the intervention (Dunlop et al., 2023).

Discussion

This review examined whether CBT can be considered a neurobiological intervention. Across 17 RCTs, clinical improvement after CBT was accompanied by measurable changes in brain function and connectivity, providing preliminary support for this view. This is consistent with the long-standing view that psychotherapy, insofar as it is effective, acts through learning and therefore through changes in the brain (Kandel, 1998). The present findings extend this view by mapping where such changes occur across disorders. However, the evidence base is small, heterogeneous and largely correlational, and the findings should be interpreted with necessary caution.

A common pattern with disorder-specific expressions

Despite heterogeneity in diagnoses, protocols and imaging methods, most studies reported changes in regions involved in emotional reactivity (amygdala, insula, ACC), in prefrontal and frontoparietal control systems, or in the connectivity between them. This pattern is broadly consistent with cognitive models of anxiety and depression, which posit an imbalance between increased bottom-up threat processing and insufficient top-down cognitive control (Clark & Beck, 2010), and with the neuroscience of emotion regulation, in which cognitive reappraisal relies on prefrontal modulation of the amygdala (Ochsner & Gross, 2005). However, the direction of prefrontal changes varied with the task paradigm, so the data do not support a simple model in which CBT uniformly strengthens prefrontal control.

The pattern was expressed differently in each disorder. In anxiety disorders, the most consistent finding was reduced reactivity to threat-related stimuli in regions that detect threat and generate bodily arousal (Liebscher et al., 2016; Reinecke et al., 2018; Schienle et al., 2007; Straube et al., 2006). In OCD, changes involved networks rather than single regions, including circuits supporting motor and habit control and networks supporting cognitive control. Notably, the change in cognitive control connectivity was associated with improved resistance to compulsions (Moody et al., 2017), the core target of ERP. This suggests that the neural changes accompanying ERP may relate less to the reduction of obsessions than to the capacity to tolerate the urge without acting on it, the component directly trained by response prevention. In depression, changes involved the relationship between regions linked to negative mood, the network supporting self-focused thought and the cognitive control network (Sheline et al., 2025), as well as connectivity between frontal regions and the reward system (Katayama et al., 2023). Taken together, these findings suggest that the neural changes accompanying CBT may involve circuits implicated in the maintenance of each disorder, rather than a nonspecific reduction in brain activity. As this conclusion derives from small samples, it cannot inform how treatment should be individualized.

What the findings suggest about how CBT works

The clearest mechanistic signal concerned exposure. Emotional processing theory proposes that fear activation during exposure is a prerequisite for change (Foa & Kozak, 1986). In OCD, however, how strongly the brain's threat-detection system responded during fear learning was unrelated to symptom improvement. Instead, improvement was associated with lower activity in a parietal region involved in executive control when patients later recalled what they had learned during extinction (Jacoby et al., 2025). This finding is consistent with the inhibitory learning model, according to which exposure does not erase the original fear memory but creates a competing safety memory, and outcome depends on whether this new learning is retrieved when the feared stimulus is encountered again (Craske et al., 2014). Although based on a single small study using a laboratory task, it is in line with the clinical shift from emphasizing fear reduction within sessions to promoting the retrieval of new learning across different contexts. In practice, this framework favors strategies such as designing exposures to violate patients' specific expectancies, conducting exposure across varied stimuli and contexts, including between-session practice without the therapist as a safety signal, and evaluating progress by what patients learned rather than by how much their distress decreased within a session (Craske et al., 2014).

In social anxiety disorder, CBT increased activity in regions involved in attention and emotion regulation and decreased activity in regions involved in processing social cues, such as facial expressions and voices, during exposure to social criticism. These changes explained a substantial proportion of symptom improvement (Goldin et al., 2014), suggesting that the effects of cognitive restructuring extend beyond how patients verbally reinterpret a situation to how the brain processes social threat. In depression, CBT strengthened communication between frontal control regions and a key hub of the reward system, and this was associated with fewer negative automatic thoughts and lower depression severity up to a year after treatment (Katayama et al., 2023). Because anhedonia was not assessed, whether CBT acts on reward processing remains a hypothesis for future research.

Specific techniques or common factors?

A central question is whether the observed changes reflect CBT-specific techniques or factors common to other effective psychotherapies. In the only study comparing CBT with an equally structured active intervention, group CBT and mindfulness-based cognitive therapy resulted in equivalent clinical improvement and increases in cognitive control activation (Goldin et al., 2021). Two interventions with different rationales, one targeting the content of thoughts and the other the person's relationship to them, thus converged on the same neural outcome. This may indicate a common pathway of therapeutic change that can be reached by different routes.

Alternatively, part of the changes attributed to CBT may reflect common factors such as the therapeutic alliance, expectations and psychotherapy structure, which account for a substantial proportion of outcome variance (Wampold, 2015). By contrast, when CBT was compared with unstructured supportive therapy, it produced activation and connectivity changes that were not observed in the control group, suggesting that at least some of these changes may be attributable to CBT-specific techniques rather than to common factors (Katayama et al., 2022 (Katayama et al., , 2023)). Similarly, in panic disorder, amygdala activation decreased comparably whether exposure exercises were guided by the therapist or carried out by patients alone (Liebscher et al., 2016), suggesting that this change was linked to exposure itself rather than to the therapist's presence during it. The present evidence cannot fully distinguish between these accounts, as most studies used waitlist controls, and none measured therapeutic alliance, therapist competence or patient engagement.

CBT versus pharmacotherapy

Studies comparing CBT with medication can show whether CBT has a distinct neural signature or whether its effects simply accompany symptom reduction. The evidence is mixed. In OCD, the volume of the putamen, a structure involved in habit formation, increased only with fluoxetine, despite comparable clinical improvement (Hoexter et al., 2012). In depression, CBT was specifically associated with stronger connectivity within networks that support cognitive control, whereas medication was associated with more widespread reductions in communication between brain networks (Dunlop et al., 2023). These findings are compatible with the proposal that psychotherapy acts predominantly top-down, through prefrontal control regions, and pharmacotherapy bottom-up, through deeper emotional regions (DeRubeis et al., 2008; Goldapple et al., 2004). Other findings, however, pointed to shared mechanisms.

Remission was associated with reduced connectivity between regions involved in emotional and attentional processing and motor regions, irrespective of treatment (Dunlop et al., 2023), and higher pre-treatment metabolism in regions linked to negative mood and in regions involved in social perception predicted non-response to both CBT and escitalopram (McGrath et al., 2014). Given the small number of comparative studies, it would be premature to conclude that CBT has a distinct neurobiological signature. A more cautious conclusion would be that both psychotherapy and medication alter brain function, possibly through partly different routes.

Pre-treatment predictors of response

Baseline brain measures predicted treatment response in several studies, and in two cases differentially predicted response to CBT versus medication (Dunlop et al., 2017; Hoexter et al., 2013; Reggente et al., 2018). Such findings are relevant to precision psychiatry, which aims to match patients to treatments on the basis of individual characteristics (DeRubeis et al., 2014; Williams & Whitfield Gabrieli, 2025).

However, these were exploratory analyses in small samples, and they do not yet support clinical decision-making, nor do they imply that treatment outcome is biologically predetermined.

Neurobiological and clinical change are not equivalent

Neural and clinical change did not always coincide. Neural changes occurred without between-group differences in depression severity (Katayama et al., 2022), symptoms improved without structural change (Hoexter et al., 2012), and the most prominent activation changes were not those associated with symptom improvement (Jacoby et al., 2025). Neuroimaging should therefore not be treated as an objective validation of psychotherapy, as if clinical improvement were meaningful only when visible on a scan. Reducing psychological difficulties to neurobiology risks overlooking the psychological and interpersonal processes that are often the most clinically important (Miller, 2010). For clinicians, this implies that the patient's experience and functioning remain the primary indicators of therapeutic change, with neurobiological findings serving to inform, rather than validate, how that change occurs. However, as none of the included studies tested mediation, the direction of the relationship between neural and clinical change remains unknown.

Strengths and limitations

This review has several strengths. It was pre-registered in PROSPERO, with eligibility criteria and methods defined before the search was conducted, which reduces the risk of selective inclusion and reporting. It was also restricted to RCTs, reducing the risk that neural changes reflect the passage of time, repeated measurement or nonspecific effects. By including anxiety disorders, depression and OCD, and synthesizing task-based, resting-state, structural and metabolic data, it allowed findings that are shared across disorders to be distinguished from those that are disorder-specific.

Beyond identifying which regions change, it examined whether these changes were associated with clinical improvement and how they compared with pharmacotherapy and other psychotherapies. Finally, findings were interpreted in relation to the clinical processes targeted in CBT, such as exposure, response prevention and cognitive restructuring, linking neuroimaging evidence to questions directly relevant to psychotherapeutic practice.

Some limitations that concern both the primary studies and this review should also be addressed. The term CBT covered heterogeneous protocols, from a single 4hour exposure session to 20 sessions, which precludes identifying the components responsible for the observed changes. Samples were small and largely free of comorbidity, limiting generalizability to clinical practice, and a few studies appear to derive from overlapping samples, so the number of independent samples is smaller than the number of studies. In four studies, participants received concurrent medication, complicating attribution of changes to CBT. Few studies included follow-up imaging, and task-based paradigms may not capture the processes that occur during exposure or behavioral experiments within therapy. Lastly, five studies were at high risk of bias, and almost all lacked a pre-specified analysis plan, a notable concern given the analytic flexibility of neuroimaging data. Limitations of the review itself include the restriction to peer-reviewed English-language publications, which may have introduced language and publication bias. The heterogeneity of paradigms and outcomes precluded metaanalysis, and the narrative synthesis relied partly on counting studies with significant findings, which does not account for differences in sample size or effect magnitude.

Grouping findings by region and network involved interpretive judgment and was not pre-specified. Furthermore, RoB2 was designed for clinical outcomes and does not capture imaging-specific sources of bias, such as region-of-interest (ROI) selection or correction for multiple comparisons, and the certainty of evidence was not formally graded.

Conclusions

This systematic review suggests that CBT could be regarded as a neurobiological intervention. Clinical improvement in OCD, anxiety disorders and depression was accompanied by measurable changes in brain function and connectivity.

Across disorders, changes involved emotional reactivity regions, prefrontal and frontoparietal control systems and their interactions, with disorder-specific patterns that may relate to the mechanisms maintaining each condition. Evidence for a neural signature distinct from pharmacotherapy and for clinically useful predictors of response remains preliminary. These conclusions are limited by small samples, heterogeneous protocols and the risk of bias in several studies. Future research should shift from asking whether the brain changes with CBT to asking which therapeutic processes change which circuits, and in whom. This requires dismantling designs that isolate specific components (e.g., exposure versus cognitive restructuring), active control conditions, repeated measurements during treatment to capture when and how change occurs (Kazdin, 2007), formal mediation analyses, and systematic assessment of the therapeutic relationship.
