Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 16 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

While social anxiety disorder is defined by fear of scrutiny from others (American Psychiatric Association, 2022), a strained relationship with the self may lie at its heart (Cox et al., 2004; Gregory & Peters, 2017). Cognitive models delineate that people with heightened social anxiety often deem aspects of themselves as deficient, flawed, or contrary to societal standards (Moscovitch, 2009). This negative self-perception renders social interactions as threats-chances for flaws to be exposed and negative evaluation, failure, or rejection to ensue (Afram & Kashdan, 2015; Buckner et al., 2010). Viewing social situations through a self-critical lens, people with social anxiety may avoid social encounters, ruminate on perceived missteps, inhibit authentic self-expression, and discount positive feedback (Blackie & Kocovski, 2018; Hofmann, 2007; Plasencia et al., 2016). In this way, harsh selfcriticism may underpin several core cognitive and behavioral processes that perpetuate social anxiety.

In contrast to harsh criticism, compassion involves an awareness of or sensitivity to suffering, alongside a commitment or motivation to alleviate it (Gilbert, 2017; Strauss et al., 2016). Self-compassion is often conceptualized as extending this orientation inward, toward oneself, through treating oneself with kindness rather than judgment, mindfully observing one's suffering rather than overidentifying with it, and connecting to a shared sense of humanity rather than self-isolation (Neff, 2003). Overall, a stance of 'befriending' oneself through self-soothing, warmth and reassurance appears beneficial for mental health and wellbeing across an array of mental health challenges, including high levels of self-criticism and social anxiety (Athanasakou et al., 2020; Slivjak et al., 2022; Wakelin et al., 2022a).

Thus, enhancing self-compassion appears to be a promising target for interventions aimed at alleviating harsh self-criticism (Wakelin et al., 2022b) and social anxiety (Ferrari et al., 2019).

MDMA-Assisted Therapy (MDMA-AT) and Self-Compassion

3,4-Methylenedioxymethamphetamine (MDMA) is a psychoactive compound that has broad-acting effects, increasing the release of serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine, alongside circulating levels of oxytocin and vasopressin. These neurochemical effects contribute to a pharmacological profile characterized by positive mood, and 'empathogenic' effects, including feelings of social connectedness, sociability, emotional empathy, openness, and prosocial behaviors (Bershad et al., 2016; Peled-Avron et al., 2025).

Though less extensively researched than its interpersonal effects, MDMA additionally appears to have beneficial intrapersonal effects. Two laboratory studies found that participant-provided "ecstasy" (chemically verified as MDMA in one of these studies) increased state self-compassion and reduced self-criticism (Kamboj et al., 2015 (Kamboj et al., , 2018)).

Within the context of MDMA-AT, both therapists and clients have observed and reported increased self-compassion during MDMA-AT (Barone et al., 2019; Passie, 2018). In accordance, a randomized, placebo-controlled trial of MDMA-AT for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) found that self-compassion increased during treatment (van der Kolk et al., 2024). Moreover, changes in self-compassion mediated the relationship between treatment (MDMA-AT vs. placebo plus therapy) and PTSD and anxiety symptoms 2-months posttreatment (Agin-Liebes et al., 2025).

Psychedelic-Assisted Therapy and Self-Compassion

In addition to the two studies on self-compassion's role in in the context of MDMA-AT (Agin-Liebes et al., 2025; Barone et al., 2019), other studies have explored selfcompassion in the context of classic psychedelic use (such as psilocybin, ayahuasca, and LSD), which act primarily on serotonergic systems. Because different classes of psychedelics can produce similar profiles of subjective experiences-particularly when contextual factors are controlled for (Chwyl et al., 2026)-the broader literature on psychedelic-assisted therapies may help elucidate potential MDMA-AT mechanisms.

In a prospective observational study of an ayahuasca ceremony (Domínguez-Clavé et al., 2022), medium to large sized improvements in self-compassion and self-criticism were observed. In a convenience sample of people naturalistically using psychedelics (most commonly LSD, psilocybin or ayahuasca), moderate to high doses were prospectively associated with heightened positive emotions, including love, and compassion towards self and others. Self-compassion was most robustly associated with wellbeing four weeks following psychedelic use; in models with other positive states (e.g., gratitude, ecstasy, awe, peace, love) and mystical experiences, only self-compassion remained a significant predictor of wellbeing (Zeifman et al., 2025).

Similarly, in a clinical trial comparing psilocybin with escitalopram in people with major depressive disorder, psilocybin experiences were characterized by heightened positive emotion, including compassion toward self and others. Of the positive emotional experiences assessed (e.g., awe, gratitude, love, ecstasy, peace), self-compassion was the only variable that mediated the relationship between treatment (psilocybin vs. escitalopram) and all posttreatment outcomes-well-being, anxiety, depressive severity, suicidal ideation. In models including either mystical experience or positive mood alongside self-compassion, selfcompassion remained the only significant mediator of outcomes (Zeifman et al., 2025).

Current Study

In sum, self-compassion may be a valuable therapeutic resource for addressing the maintenance factors of social anxiety disorder, and a small though growing body of work has evaluated psychological mechanisms of action within psychedelic-assisted therapy and suggests that self-compassion is a promising potential mechanism of change within psychedelic-assisted therapy and MDMA-AT. We propose that self-compassion serves as an important process of change in MDMA-AT for social anxiety disorder (Luoma & Lear, 2021), especially when the psychotherapy component of MDMA-AT is optimized to target underlying cognitive and behavioral processes theorized to maintain social anxiety disorder (e.g., harsh self-criticism), as in the current intervention (Luoma et al., in press). However, no prior projects have examined this hypothesis.

In this paper, we assess the role of self-compassion in a MDMA-AT iteratively developed to synergistically pair the effects of therapy with psychedelics, and target underlying social anxiety disorder processes. In the primary outcomes paper (Luoma et al., 2026)-a clinical trial of 20 adults with social anxiety disorder-we observed a large, statistically significant increase in trait self-compassion from the first preparation session to post-treatment (Hedges' g = 1.79, p < .001), but no significant association between changes in trait self-compassion and changes in social anxiety symptoms (rho = -.28, p = .24). The current paper builds upon this work by examining: (1) the trajectory of trait self-compassion (Luoma et al., 2026) over various phases of MDMA-AT; (2) whether state self-compassion during dosing sessions is associated with changes in trait self-compassion; and (3) whether both trait self-compassion changes over the course of treatment and acute self-compassion levels experienced during dosing sessions contributed to a range of clinical outcomes (social anxiety and depressive symptoms, functional impairment) and factors thought to underpin social anxiety disorder (internalized shame, and sense of belonging). We hope that our work serves as a springboard for future mechanistically-oriented psychedelic-assisted therapy trials.

Methods

This study represents a secondary data analysis of a randomized waitlist, delayedtreatment control trial of MDMA-AT for 20 adults with social anxiety disorder (NCT05138068; (Lear et al., 2023). Primary outcomes are reported elsewhere (Luoma et al., 2026). The current pre-registered analyses measurement schedule. Participants received two dosing sessions (each ~7-8 hours), typically four weeks apart. Preparation and integration sessions each lasted approximately 90minutes. The post-treatment assessment occurred around 1-week after the final integration session (16 weeks). compassion. SCS-SF = 12-item self-compassion scale short-form.

The full treatment and treatment development process is outlined in (Luoma et al., in press). Overall, throughout treatment, therapists aimed to foster strong therapeutic rapport, a sense of psychological safety, and supported clients in both receiving care from others and cultivating self-compassion by modeling warmth, authenticity and nonjudgment. The treatment initially followed an inner-directed framework, guided by the MDMA-AT manual for PTSD developed by Multidisciplinary Association for Psychedelics Studies (Mithoefer, 2017).

After six participants, the treatment was iteratively refined based on relevant cognitive behavioral theory and empirical research regarding how MDMA may benefit social anxiety: updating shame-based memory networks and decreasing behavioral avoidance through expectancy violation and memory reconsolidation (Luoma et al., 2022). The treatment is grounded in the conceptualization of social anxiety disorder as a shame-centered condition, in which self-protective behaviors are driven by a central belief that the authentic self is fundamentally flawed. These behaviors paradoxically maintain social anxiety and prevent the corrective interpersonal experiences necessary for change. As such, the therapy encourages behavioral changes that allow for expectancy violations and new learning, and compassionate reframing of shame-based schemas.

For instance, during preparation sessions, participants' histories of social experiences of shame and criticism were explored and reframed through a compassionate lens. Dosing sessions included structured tasks such as guided reprocessing of shame-related memories and "Authenticity Experiments" in which clients engaged in commonly avoided interpersonal behaviors with the therapist team. Integration sessions often incorporated a perspectivetaking exercise, the Empathic Reimagining Task, which guides clients to revisit shame-laden memories from a compassionate vantage point accessed during dosing. Between sessions, clients are supported in practicing values-consistent interpersonal behavior that may otherwise be avoided, with support from the therapist team on navigating these new experiences with self-compassion.

Initial doses were 80 mg and 120 mg, respectively, and a 40 mg supplemental dose in most cases. Further dosing information is available in (Lear et al., 2023).

Measures

Baseline and Post-treatment Measures

Outcome measures were collected at baseline, prior to session one, and posttreatment. As described in the treatment outcomes paper (Luoma et al., 2026), the primary outcome was social anxiety symptoms, assessed with the Leibowitz Social Anxiety Scale (LSAS; internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha (α) = .83-.90 in the current study) (Heimberg et al., 1999). Secondary outcomes were functional impairment, assessed with the Global Functional Impairment subscale of the Sheehan Disability Scale (SDS) (Sheehan et al., 2011; Sheehan & Sheehan, 2008), depressive symptoms, assessed with the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9; αs = .78-.86) (Kroenke et al., 2001), internalized shame, assessed with the Internalized Shame Scale (ISS; αs = .91-.95) (Cook, 1988; Delrosario & White, 2006), and belonging, assessed with the thwarted belongingness subscale of the Interpersonal Needs Questionnaire (INQ; αs = .86-.93) (Freedenthal et al., 2011).

Assessment of Self-Compassion

Trait self-compassion was assessed at eight timepoints-immediately prior to each of the preparation sessions, immediately prior to the second and third integration session following the MDMA dosing sessions, and at post-treatment (see Figure 1). Trait selfcompassion was assessed with the 12-item self-compassion scale short-form (SCS-SF) (Raes et al., 2010) which has been validated in clinical samples (Hayes et al., 2016). Participant report how they typically act towards themselves in difficult times with a Likert scale ranging from 1 (Almost never) to 5 (Almost always). In the current study, internal reliability was high across assessments (αs = .88-.95).

State self-compassion was assessed the morning after each MDMA session, immediately prior to the integration session. Participants were asked to report how much they felt sympathetic, soft-hearted, warm, compassionate and caring towards themselves during the prior MDMA session. They responded using a Likert scale ranging from 1 (Not at all) to 5 (Extremely). These items, originally used to assess compassion toward another person in need (Batson, 2023), were adapted in the current study by modifying the target to be selfreferent and replacing two items assessing feeling "tender" and "moved" with a single item assessing caring. Internal reliability in the current study was good across the two MDMA dosing sessions (αs = .93-.94)

Analyses were completed in R (Version 4.4.2; R Core Team, 2024).

Modeling Trait Self-Compassion Over Time

To evaluate the trajectory of trait self-compassion change over the course of MDMA-AT, we began by comparing the fit of: (a) a multilevel model with linear time; (b) a multilevel model with quadratic and linear time; and (c) multilevel models with time specified to reflect the observed pattern of change observed across the intervention phases (Figure 2). Specifically, for (c), we fit a 3-phase time model: time 1 ('preparation phase', i.e., the three preparatory sessions), time 2 ('post-dose 1 phase', i.e., the two integration sessions after MDMA dose 1), and time 3 ('post-dose 2 phase', i.e., the two integration sessions after dose 1 as well as the post-treatment assessment). Another tested model comprised two phases: time 1 (preparation phase), and time 2 (i.e., the remaining sessions following the first dosing session). Model (c) was selected (with time coded per three intervention phases) and used a random intercept and slope terms based on model fit statistics (AIC, BIC, log likelihood). Robust standard errors were used to account for heteroscedasticity.

Modeling the Association between State and Trait Self-Compassion

To examine whether state self-compassion experienced during each of the two MDMA sessions was associated with changes in trait self-compassion, we initially specified a multilevel model with state self-compassion predicting the change in trait self-compassion from before to after the dosing session, with MDMA dose (1 vs. 2) included as a withinperson factor, and a random intercept for participant. However, the model fit was singular, indicating that the random-effects structure was not supported by the data. Accordingly, we ran two separate simple linear regressions examining whether: (1) state self-compassion during dose 1 was associated with the change in trait self-compassion between phase 2 and phase 1; and (2) state self-compassion during dose 2 was associated with the change in trait self-compassion between phase 3 and phase 2. Model assumptions were evaluated via diagnostic plots, and robust standard errors were applied to address heteroskedasticity. No covariates were included in the models.

Discussion

Overall, these findings suggest that our MDMA-AT intervention tailored to target core cognitive and behavioral social anxiety disorder processes was associated with changes in self-compassion: the intervention fostered high self-compassion levels during the two dosing sessions and trait-level increases in self-compassion following the MDMA dosing sessions. Both greater state self-compassion during dosing sessions and greater overall trait self-compassion changes across the intervention appear to hold therapeutic value for certain outcomes within MDMA-AT for social anxiety disorder, especially internalized shame.

Trait Self-Compassion Trajectories

Trait self-compassion significantly increased over the course of MDMA-AT, with a large-sized effect, consistent with observed increases in self-compassion following psychedelic use in past research (Domínguez-Clavé et al., 2022; van der Kolk et al., 2024). A nonlinear pattern of change best fit the data, wherein self-compassion increased in a stepwise fashion immediately after each dosing session. The largest increase occurred from preparation to post-dose 1, with additional gains post-dose 2. However, individual selfcompassion change trajectories across MDMA-AT were highly variable, suggesting that this observed pattern of change was not uniformly experienced across participants and that there may be multiple pathways through which self-compassion may develop.

State Self-Compassion Experiences

Participants generally reported experiencing high levels of state self-compassion during MDMA dosing sessions, consistent with descriptions of heightened self-compassion in prior qualitative work of psychedelic-assisted therapy (Agin-Liebes et al., 2025; Barone et al., 2019). In conjunction with prior work finding additive effects of self-compassion interventions and MDMA (Kamboj et al., 2015 (Kamboj et al., , 2018)), these findings suggest that the psychotherapy component may play a key role in potentiating MDMA's effects on selfcompassion.

Associations between Self-Compassion and Outcomes

The most reliable associations overall were between self-compassion and internalized shame. We observed that greater self-compassion, whether state changes measured during dosing sessions or trait changes self-compassion in subsequent sessions, were associated with reductions in internalized shame. Other outcomes were associated with either state or trait self-compassion: greater state self-compassion was associated with depressive symptom reductions, while trait self-compassion increase was associated with reductions in feelings of thwarted belonging. Although sample size precluded formal mediation analysis and led to limited power to detect statistical significance, these findings contribute to a growing body of work suggesting that state self-compassion (Agin-Liebes et al., 2025) and trait selfcompassion (Zeifman et al., 2025) may serve as important mechanisms of change for certain core psychosocial outcomes within psychedelic-assisted therapy.

State Self-Compassion Associations

State self-compassion showed the largest association with internalized shame, supporting research identifying self-compassion as a helpful shame-regulation strategy for individuals with elevated social anxiety (Cȃndea & Szentágotai-Tătar, 2018). Speculatively, intense, acute experiences of self-compassion may be more impactful for internalized shame and depressive symptoms than for social anxiety, functional impairment, and sense of belonging because these outcomes may be more directly rooted in negative internal processes that could be interrupted by self-compassion experiences. In contrast, social anxiety and belonging may depend more heavily on social factors and interpersonal learning, in addition to negative internal processes, thus making the connection between self-compassion and these outcomes weaker and less direct.

Our findings with state self-compassion are partially consistent with past research. Zeifman et al., (2025) similarly found that state self-compassion mediated the relationship between treatment (psilocybin vs. placebo) and depressive severity and suicidal ideation for participants (n = 59) with major depressive disorder. However, they also found that state selfcompassion mediated the relationship between treatment and well-being and anxiety (Zeifman et al., 2025). The discrepancy between our results and this prior research may be due to population (social anxiety disorder vs. major depressive disorder), analysis (sample size precluded formal mediation analysis in the current study), substance type (MDMA vs.

psilocybin), therapy type (an evidence-based adaptation of psychotherapy for psychedelicassisted therapy vs. nondirective psychological support) or ceiling effects in the present study. For instance, state self-compassion was generally high during MDMA sessions, with few participants scoring below the scale's midpoint (none in session 1, and two in session 2); such ceiling effects may have limited variability and reduced the ability to detect associations with several outcomes, such as social anxiety, functioning, or belonging.

Inconsistent with our hypothesis, state self-compassion experienced during dosing sessions had small, nonsignificant associations with increases in trait self-compassion from before to after the dosing sessions. This suggests that the cultivation of trait-level selfcompassion may not depend solely on the intensity of in-session self-compassion experiences, but also on unfolding processes outside of peak in-session experiences (e.g., integration sessions, reflection, repeated practice in daily life). Alternatively, the noted ceiling effects for state self-compassion during dosing sessions may have limited their statistical associations.

Trait Self-Compassion Change Associations

Our results were partially consistent with our hypotheses: trait self-compassion changes had statistically significant associations with certain beneficial outcomes (reduced internalized shame and thwarted belonging), but not others (social anxiety symptoms, functional impairment and depressive symptoms). Speculatively, the constructs of internalized shame and belonging map most closely onto the constructs measured by the SCS-SF and were thus most directly influenced by changes in self-compassion over the course of MDMA-AT. For instance, a greater sense of common humanity (vs. isolation), may have contributed to less thwarted belonging, while greater self-kindness (vs. harsh selfcriticism), may have diminished self-shaming. In contrast, social anxiety, functional impairment and depressive symptoms may operate more distally from the construct of trait self-compassion. Changes in self-compassion may thus influence these outcomes less strongly, directly, or immediately. For instance, it may take a longer period for changes in self-compassion to translate into durable decreases in depressive symptoms, whereas the impacts of trait self-compassion on shame may be noticeable immediately.

Later increases in self-compassion, occurring after the second MDMA dosing session, were associated with benefits, whereas earlier increases, occurring after the first dosing session, were not. This pattern of findings could simply reflect a measurement artifact: variables assessed more proximally to outcomes may have stronger associations with outcomes.

Alternatively, this pattern may speak to clinically relevant temporal dynamics. For instance, the therapeutic effects of self-compassion may accumulate over the course of treatment, such that later or sustained increases confer greater benefit than initial trait selfcompassion changes.

If replicated, this would suggest the value of additional dosing sessions or self-compassion interventions to reinforce early gains in self-compassion changes. Another possibility is that changes in trait self-compassion require time to consolidate before they translate into broader psychological benefits. Early self-compassion increases may represent an emerging way of relating to oneself that takes time to apply to daily life before meaningful changes in shame and sense of belonging become evident. Our results are somewhat inconsistent with prior work-while we also found that trait self-compassion changes conferred benefit, Agin-Liebes et al., (2025) found support for the mediating role of trait self-compassion on PTSD and depressive symptoms in MDMA-AT for PTSD, whereas in our study, self-compassion changes were not significantly associated with mental health symptoms (depression or social anxiety symptoms). However, comparison of our findings with prior work is made difficult by several methodological differences, including analysis method, clinical population (social anxiety disorder vs. PTSD), therapy type (cognitive-behavioral theory-driven vs. inner-directed) and the modest sample size and power of the current study. Future research in larger samples is needed to examine what outcomes trait self-compassion changes may mediate, over what timescale, and in what samples.

Baseline Self-Compassion Associations

While we did not form a priori hypotheses around the role of baseline selfcompassion on outcomes, we included baseline levels of trait self-compassion in our models and observed a consistent pattern: greater self-compassion levels during the preparation period were associated with several improvements: less post-treatment social anxiety, functional impairment, internalized shame, and thwarted belonging. Potentially, greater baseline trait self-compassion may facilitate greater engagement with and thus benefit from treatment, such as through increasing openness to MDMA experiences, persistence with behavioral experiments, psychological flexibility, or ability to disengage from anticipatory anxiety or post-event rumination. This observed pattern suggests that initial self-compassion levels may serve as a moderator of certain outcomes in MDMA-AT for social anxiety disorder, though this should be directly tested in future larger trials. Further, if replicated, the generalizability of this pattern to other outcomes and clinical populations should be examined.

Limitations

Preliminary support for self-compassion's role in MDMA-AT in the current study may reflect features of the specific study design, rather than being generalizable to MDMA-AT in other contexts/for other disorders. Indeed, the psychotherapy component of the treatment was carefully and iteratively developed and included elements that aimed to enhance MDMA's effect on self-compassion. For instance, unlike more inner-directed psychedelic-assisted therapy approaches (Alpert et al., 2026), the treatment manual included an 'Empathic Reimagining Task' during either MDMA dosing or integration sessions, wherein participants were guided through bringing a compassionate, warm perspective to a memory characterized by high shame, self-criticism, or perceived social rejection. This intervention component, distinct from MDMA's effect, might explain the degree of change in self-compassion over the course of treatment, and some of the patterns of associations between self-compassion and outcome described here. Future work would benefit from examining whether the observed findings generalize to other types of MDMA-AT and psychedelic-assisted therapy, and whether adding therapeutic techniques specifically involving self-compassion enhance outcomes within MDMA-AT and psychedelic-assisted therapy. Furthermore, future work would benefit from replicating this work in larger more demographically heterogeneous samples.

Several additional limitations should be considered. First, because this was an openlabel trial, causality cannot be inferred. Second, the small sample size introduces instability in effect size estimates and measures of variability and significantly lowers statistical power; null findings in particular should be interpreted with caution. Third, our method of examining changes in trait self-compassion across treatment phases may oversimplify change; future studies with larger samples could employ more flexible longitudinal modeling approaches to better characterize nonlinear dynamics and examine if different trajectories predict different outcomes or if there are diverse trajectories that predict similar outcomes. Additionally, future work may benefit from identifying predictors of these trajectories, including baseline characteristics, in-session experiences, and integration processes.

Conclusion

MDMA-AT for social anxiety disorder appears capable of fostering both acute state self-compassion and enduring trait-level self-compassion changes. Both trait self-compassion changes over the course of MDMA-AT and state self-compassion experienced during MDMA dosing sessions contributed to certain beneficial psychosocial outcomes. While state self-compassion was most robustly associated with improvements in depressive symptoms and internalized shame, trait self-compassion changes were most robustly associated with improvements in internalized shame and sense of belonging. Both acute state self-compassion experiences and trait self-compassion changes over the course of treatment may serve as distinct mechanism of change within MDMA-AT; future work should examine whether these patterns generalize in larger mechanistically-oriented psychedelic-assisted therapy trials, with other types of psychotherapy, and whether findings are transdiagnostic or specific to certain clinical populations.
