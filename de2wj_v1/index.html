Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Mental disorders are typically approached as syndrome-defined states, and questions like why conditions outlast their triggers, why diagnoses overlap, why some resist treatment, how change occurs and what recovery should aim at are usually addressed separately. Here, we approach mental health conditions as processes within a person's lifelong development, putting forward four propositions: 1) maintenance and development compete for a limited capacity for change; 2) repeated activity becomes structure; 3) self-maintenance can turn into entrapment, which depletes finite reserves; 4) reorganization is often abrupt and lasts only if consolidated. From this perspective, mental health appears as the preserved capacity to keep developing, whereas many persistent conditions appear as active entrapment-a pattern reproduced by the person's own activity (avoidance, rumination). Therefore, entering a state, remaining in it, leaving it and stabilizing the outcome can depend on different conditions. For each of these phases we derive clinical implications and testable predictions.

Mental Health Conditions as Processes: Developmental Features and Clinical Implications 1. Development as a General Perspective on Mental Health Conditions

Mental disorders affect more than one billion people, anxiety and depressive disorders being the most common (World Health Organization, 2025). There is an observed tendency for the early onset of these conditions: in a meta-analysis of 192 epidemiological studies, Solmi and colleagues (2022) placed the onset before age 14 in about a third of affected individuals, before age 18-in almost half, and before age 25-in more than 60%. Despite this burden, treatment coverage remains low. In the World Mental Health Surveys (21 countries), only 1 in 5 people with major depressive disorder in high-income countries, and 1 in 27 in low-and lower-middleincome countries, received minimally adequate treatment (Thornicroft et al., 2017).

Where treatment is delivered, its effects are real, although limited. In an umbrella review of 102 meta-analyses (3,782 randomized controlled trials; ~650,000 adults with major mental disorders), Leichsenring and colleagues (2022) found pooled standardized mean differences of 0.34 for psychotherapies and 0.36 for pharmacotherapies against placebo or usual care, and concluded that these limited effects suggest a "ceiling effect" for treatment research as presently conducted. How effective treatments work is still poorly understood, and the Lancet Psychiatry Commission on psychological treatments research named the elucidation of their mechanisms a priority (Holmes et al., 2018).

Diagnosis raises difficulties of its own. Fried and Nesse (2015) found 1,030 different symptom profiles among 3,703 outpatients with major depressive disorder from the STAR*D study, the most common shared by fewer than 2% of patients, so that a single diagnosis grouped people whose symptom profiles rarely coincided. Of note, in a nationwide Danish cohort of almost 6 million people, every mental disorder was associated with an increased risk of every other for at least 15 years (Plana-Ripoll et al., 2019)-a pattern hardly expected if diagnoses corresponded to separate diseases.

Several strategies have been devised to move beyond this "ceiling": dimensional classifications built from how symptoms co-occur, such as HiTOP (Kotov et al., 2017); research frameworks organized around domains of functioning instead of diagnoses, such as the Research Domain Criteria (Insel et al., 2010); network models, in which a disorder is a self-sustaining state of mutually reinforcing symptoms (Borsboom, 2017); transdiagnostic treatments (Barlow et al., 2017); process-based therapy, which targets modifiable processes of change in place of syndromes (S. C. Hayes et al., 2020; Hofmann & Hayes, 2019); and person-specific research designs (Fisher et al., 2018). Each of these approaches has advanced the field, although they have largely addressed different parts of the problem, and it remains unclear how are connected the key questions: 1) why conditions persist, 2) why they overlap, 3) why some resist treatment, 4) how change happens and 5) what recovery should aim at.

In our opinion, three reasons make these questions difficult to answer. First, mental health conditions are processes that unfold over time, following the view set out by Nicholson and Dupré (2018) of living systems as constituted by processes instead of things. However, most of the field's evidence comes from cross-sectional snapshots and group averages, which often fail to describe any single person (Fisher et al., 2018). Second, a condition changes the person while the person lives with it and is treated for it, so the causes of its onset are not necessarily the causes of its persistence. Third, a capacity on which many psychological treatments rely (the person's ability to observe/redirect their own mental activity) can itself be drawn into the condition. Thus, the question should rather be how a developing system-the person-changes over time, and under which conditions it enters a state, remains in it and leaves it.

In the present work we propose a perspective that addresses these difficulties by treating each state as one phase of a process. It is closest to three existing approaches (network and other dynamical-systems models, transdiagnostic and process-based approaches, and the cognitivebehavioral models describing the cycles that maintain particular disorders), and connects findings usually examined separately-by diagnosis, treatment modality and level of analysis, from synaptic plasticity to epidemiology. To these approaches three distinctions are added: 1) entering a state, remaining in it, leaving it and stabilizing the new state are governed by different conditions; 2) resistance to treatment depends on which of the person's means of regulation (e.g., insight/motivation) the condition has captured, and on which reserves remain, indicating which interventions are likely to lose effect; and 3) change is more likely to last when, by the end of treatment, it is being built upon and transfers to other contexts, which offers a criterion for ending treatment and for anticipating relapse beyond symptom reduction. As a result, a limited set of plausible and empirically testable predictions is obtained.

In this review we put forward four propositions on how mental health conditions change over time, and approach the five key questions raised above through this lens. The four propositions and the reasons for choosing them are presented first (section 2). The evidence on the five questions is then interpreted through these propositions (section 3). Conditions outside the main pattern are considered separately (section 4), before the discussion of the clinical implications and tests of the proposal (section 5).

2. The Proposal: Mental Health as a Process of Development

In this section we delimit what we understand by development in terms general enough to apply to mental life without reference to any particular model of a disorder.

2.1 Development and Its Markers

By development of a system we understand its production of qualitatively new organization, which is further built upon, channeled through the constraints the system already has, conserved and integrated into a coherent whole. In mental life, what develops is the person's organization of experience and action, hereinafter referred to as mental organization: the ways of perceiving, interpreting, feeling, acting and relating, each built on earlier ones and integrated into a coherent self. This definition follows a long tradition in developmental science, from Heinz Werner (1890 Werner ( -1964)), for whom development combines differentiation with integration (Werner, 1957), to Jean Piaget (1896 -1980), for whom it combines the revision of existing structures with their preservation (Piaget, 1975 (Piaget, /1985). Werner's differentiation and integration correspond to the production of new organization and its integration into a whole, whereas Piaget's revision and preservation correspond to its channeling through, and conservation within, the structures that already exist.

Seen from outside, development of a person's mental organization can be recognized by four markers: 1) new ways of understanding or acting appear; 2) later changes build on earlier ones; 3) what is new transfers to other relevant contexts; and 4) it persists after the intervention that produced it is withdrawn, within the person's ordinary environment. These markers describe the structure of a change, and not its value or direction. The first three can be assessed when the change occurs, whereas the fourth can only be identified a posteriori. They correspond to criteria already used to distinguish cumulative from isolated change (marker 2; Masten & Cicchetti, 2010), transferable from context-bound change (marker 3; Barnett & Ceci, 2002) and -in the terms of Soderstrom and Bjork (2015)-durable learning from momentary performance (marker 4).

2.2 Four Development-Related Propositions to Assess Mental Health Conditions

Here we present four propositions that describe how a developing system changes over time (as a process), and apply them to mental health conditions.

Proposition 1. Processes of maintenance and development compete. A developing system must keep itself coherent and also produce new organization. Both processes draw on the same limited capacity to modify itself. If maintenance absorbs that capacity, the system persists without developing. Conversely, if generation outruns maintenance, so that change comes faster than it can be assimilated, the system changes but loses coherence.

Proposition 2. Activity internalizes, becoming structure. What a self-modifying system repeatedly does is laid down (is internalized) as structure that then shapes its further activity. The conditions under which a pattern arises are therefore not necessarily the conditions that keep it in place.

Proposition 3. Entrapment is the price of self-maintenance. A system that maintains itself through its own activity can become entrapped: the activity that maintains it reproduces the constraints that confine it, consuming resources without producing anything new. The reserves that allow such a system to sustain periods of entrapment are finite.

Proposition 4. Reorganization is discontinuous and must be consolidated. Change accumulates within an existing organization until the latter is reorganized, often abruptly. The new organization persists only if it is integrated with the rest of the system, and earlier organization is overlaid rather than erased.

Applied to a broad class of persistent conditions, the four propositions describe a single course: a pattern is laid down by the person's own activity (Proposition 2) into a "loop" that maintains itself while consuming reserves (Proposition 3) and the capacity for change required for development (Proposition 1), until a reorganization occurs that lasts only if it is consolidated (Proposition 4). The five key questions raised above (why a condition persists, why it takes similar forms across diagnoses, why it resists treatment, how it changes and what makes the change last) address different parts of this course.

The propositions above were chosen because they meet three criteria. The first is convergence: each is consistent with findings from at least two research traditions that use different methods and developed largely independently of one another (Table 1). The second is necessity: each is needed to answer at least one of the questions raised above, and none restates another. The third is joint productivity: together they yield predictions that none of them yields alone. We intentionally left out propositions about specific mechanisms, whether genetic, neural or cognitive, as the propositions presented here concern how change is organized over time and are, thus, compatible with mechanistic accounts at any level.

2.3 A Development-Based Definition of Mental Health

Taken together, Propositions 2 and 4 lead to a corollary that runs through the rest of this article. Just as the conditions that bring a state about are not necessarily those that keep it in place (Proposition 2), the conditions that allow a person to leave a state are not necessarily those that make what replaces it last (Proposition 4). Entering a state, remaining in it, leaving it and stabilizing its successor are, thus, four phases, each governed by its own conditions (Figure 1). In major depressive disorder, clinical research already distinguishes phases such as remission, recovery, relapse and recurrence (Frank et al., 1991), and clinical staging models place a person along the progression of an illness so that interventions can be matched to the stage reached (McGorry et al., 2006). Both distinctions already treat the course of a condition as a sequence of phases, although these are defined mainly by clinical features (symptom level, duration and recurrence), and not by what governs each of them.

With development understood in this way, we propose to regard mental health as the preserved capacity to keep developing while remaining coherent (Proposition 1). Mental health is then neither the absence of disorder nor a state to be restored, but a capacity that can be preserved in some areas of a person's life (work, relationships, etc.) while being lost in others.

3. Five Open Questions

In this section we interpret the available evidence on each of the five questions through the propositions (Table 1), summarizing what is known, proposing an interpretation, considering its clinical implications and indicating which parts remain hypothetical.

3.1 Question 1: Why Do Conditions Persist After Their Triggers Have Passed?

Many mental health conditions persist long after the events that triggered them have gone. Recurrences can be less tied to external events than first episodes, as Kendler and coworkers (2000) found in a large cohort of women, in whom the association between stressful life events and the onset of major depressive disorder weakened as previous episodes accumulated, up to a threshold. In other words, with each episode the condition depended less on what triggered it and more on its own history. For the sake of fairness, it should be noted that this "kindling" pattern is still debated (Monroe & Harkness, 2005).

Etiological research has often focused primarily on causes of onset, encouraging the assumption that the conditions that started a disorder also sustain it. Cognitive-behavioral research, by contrast, has identified maintaining processes, of which safety behaviors are probably the clearest example: they prevent feared outcomes from ever being disconfirmed (Clark, 1999), so that a person with panic disorder who holds on to something whenever they feel faint never learns that they would not have collapsed. Importantly, in an experiment by Salkovskis and collaborators (1999), dropping such behaviors during exposure produced larger reductions in anxiety and in threat beliefs-an indication that the behavior itself kept the fear in place. Repetitive negative thinking, such as worry and rumination (Watkins, 2008), and stress generation, in which the condition itself produces new stressful events (Hammen, 2006), play similar maintaining roles, and network models likewise describe disorders as self-sustaining states (Borsboom, 2017).

Put in the terms of Propositions 2 and 3, these findings share one structure: the person is not passive. The mind remains active (checking, worrying, ruminating, avoiding, planning around the problem), but each cycle lays down the associations, habits and avoidances -a "selfconstructed niche"-that make the next cycle more likely. By "activity" we understand here the ongoing operation of the person's mental organization, which implies neither choice nor fault. This configuration, which we denominate active entrapment, is that of a mind that keeps working, but whose work rebuilds the pattern instead of moving beyond it. Two features usually accompany it: the pattern is local, so other areas of life may still be developing, and the person often keeps a position from which the pattern is recognized as unwanted. Activity alone, however, does not decide the outcome. As Watkins (2008) concluded, repetitive thinking aids recovery when it is concrete and oriented to solving problems, but can maintain depression and anxiety when it is abstract and evaluative. The same activity can, thus, either move the person beyond the pattern or rebuild it.

We refer to entrapment when the pattern is held mainly by what the person's own activity has laid down. Nevertheless, such a loop can extend beyond the affected person when others take part in it, as in family accommodation of obsessive-compulsive symptoms (relatives taking part in rituals or adjusting family routines to them), which is associated with greater symptom severity (Wu et al., 2016). Conversely, if a pattern is held mainly by current external conditions (ongoing threat, coercion, poverty, chronic pain and others), its persistence is possibly an adequate response to them, and not entrapment. Thus, case formulation should address what maintains the condition at present, and not only what started it, including what the condition itself has laid down (=its "niche"): avoided places/people, abandoned roles/routines, disturbed sleep and newly generated stressors (Clark, 1999; Hammen, 2006; Salkovskis et al., 1999; Watkins, 2008). It should also distinguish the two sources of persistence, since where current external conditions hold the pattern, they become the primary target of intervention, whereas once a pattern turns self-maintained, the resolution of the original stressor can hardly be expected to end it, in agreement with the kindling pattern (Kendler et al., 2000; Monroe & Harkness, 2005). Treatment can then build on the two features described earlier-the "observing position" that the person often preserves and the areas of life that continue to develop. These correspond, respectively, to the capacity to shift perspective that Kashdan and Rottenberg (2010) place within psychological flexibility and to the connectedness, identity and meaning that people describe in personal recovery (Leamy et al., 2011).

The processes described above show that anxiety and depressive disorders can be maintained by the person's own activity (Clark, 1999; Hammen, 2006; Salkovskis et al., 1999; Watkins, 2008). Comparable cycles have been described, each in its own terms, in other diagnostic groups. In posttraumatic stress disorder, thought suppression, rumination and safety behaviors prevent change in the trauma memory and in its appraisals, so that a sense of current threat persists (Ehlers & Clark, 2000). In eating disorders, whatever the specific diagnosis, the over-evaluation of shape and weight drives strict dieting, whose breakdowns into binge eating intensify the same concerns (Fairburn et al., 2003). In addiction, drug seeking apparently shifts from goal-directed action to habit and compulsion, and persists despite its adverse consequences (Everitt & Robbins, 2016) How far this structure extends, however, remains to be determined. We propose that active entrapment is a common maintaining structure of a broad class of persistent conditions, including anxiety disorders; obsessive-compulsive and related disorders; depressive disorders; trauma-and stressor-related disorders; substance-related and addictive disorders; and feeding and eating disorders-and not only of the processes in which it has been demonstrated. If so, it can be expected that interventions chosen from a formulation of the maintaining loop, independently of diagnosis, outperform those chosen by diagnosis.

3.2 Question 2: Why Do Diagnoses Overlap, and Why Do Shared Treatments Work?

If diagnoses corresponded to separate diseases, the pervasive comorbidity between them (Plana-Ripoll et al., 2019) and the extreme heterogeneity within a single diagnosis (Fried & Nesse, 2015) would be anomalies. One of the reasons lies in the prevailing classifications (the DSM and the ICD), which are primarily syndromic and define disorders by patterns of symptoms and signs, together with requirements for duration, distress or impairment and exclusions, and not by the processes that maintain them (Insel et al., 2010).

Dimensional and transdiagnostic approaches have addressed this difficulty. In a randomized trial, the Unified Protocol -a single transdiagnostic treatment-proved equivalent to disorder-specific protocols for several anxiety disorders, with fewer patients dropping out (Barlow et al., 2017). Similarly, Problem Management Plus, a brief intervention combining problem solving, behavioral activation, social support and stress management and delivered by lay health workers, reduced anxiety and depressive symptoms in a conflict-affected area of Pakistan (Rahman et al., 2016). In both cases, a treatment not tailored to any single diagnosis was effective across several, as transdiagnostic treatments were for anxiety and depression in the meta-analysis of Newby and colleagues (2015), which suggests that what these treatments address is shared across diagnoses.

Active entrapment is a structure, and a structure can carry many contents, as the same kind of loop can run on bodily sensations, threats, intrusive thoughts, memories, substances, food or self-image (roughly, the contents of panic, anxiety, obsessive-compulsive, posttraumatic, addictive and eating disorders), and at different levels of organization (bodily, affective, cognitive, relational or identity-related). Within this class of conditions, much of what distinguishes one diagnosis from another possibly lies in the content and the level on which the loop runs, although diagnostic categories also differ in time course, impairment, associated features and exclusion criteria. In other words, different diagnoses can represent different realizations (=instances) of a shared structure. A shared structure, thus, offers one possible explanation of why conditions co-occur, why a person may move between diagnoses over time (in a birth cohort followed for four decades, mental disorder life histories shifted among different successive disorders; Caspi et al., 2020), and why treatments aimed at the structure work across diagnoses.

We should note that this explanation is compatible with others, such as a liability shared across disorders, the so-called p factor (Caspi et al., 2014), partly genetic in origin (Cross-Disorder Group of the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium, 2019), and direct interactions between symptoms (Borsboom, 2017). In this account, culture shapes the content of distress and the idiom in which it is expressed more than the structure itself. Somatic symptoms, for example, can function as "idioms of distress" in many cultural groups, as documented by Kirmayer (2001), so that distress that elsewhere would be reported as sadness or worry is expressed through bodily complaints. Moreover, in a meta-analysis of 45 studies, cultural concepts of distress were consistently associated with general psychological distress, whereas their associations with specific diagnoses (anxiety, depression, posttraumatic stress disorder, somatization) varied widely (Kohrt et al., 2014), as expected if such concepts name a distress shared across diagnoses instead of corresponding to particular ones. The structural interpretation also loosens the categorical-versus-dimensional debate: which loop runs, and at which level, is a qualitative question, whereas how strong the loop is and how long it lasts are quantitative ones.

Clinically, it seems useful to record the structure of a condition alongside its diagnosis: which loop runs, on what content and at which level, what maintains it, and which reserves remain. A person may, thus, need the same "structural work" under successive diagnoses-a continuity that the sequence of diagnostic labels does not reveal. Culturally-specific presentations can likewise be read as idioms that may carry a familiar structure, so that treatment can be delivered through the person's own interpretive categories and social resources.

That shared treatments can work across diagnoses is established, at least for anxiety and depression (Barlow et al., 2017; Newby et al., 2015; Rahman et al., 2016). Whether "structural assessment" can be transferred across cultures is less certain. If culture shapes the content of a condition more than its structure, structural assessment could prove more transferable than syndrome-based diagnosis, a possibility that, to our knowledge, has not yet been tested.

3.3 Question 3: Why Are Some Conditions Much Harder to Treat?

Some conditions respond within weeks, whereas others resist years of treatment. Illness severity and duration account for part of this difference, but cannot explain why a person with specific phobia typically experiences the symptoms as unwanted and seeks relief, whereas some people with anorexia nervosa consider aspects of the illness as valued-an ego-syntonic quality that can reduce their motivation for treatment and their engagement in it (Gregertsen et al., 2017). Then, what makes a condition resist treatment beyond its severity?

Insight, motivation and readiness for change are assessed clinically, and readiness, at least, predicts outcome: in a meta-analysis by Krebs and colleagues (2018), patients who started psychotherapy further along the stages of change improved more. Similarly, loss of interest, reduced activity and indecisiveness predicted a poorer response to antidepressants in two large samples, independently of overall severity (Uher et al., 2012). What matters, thus, is not only how severe a condition is, but also which functions it has affected. Early intervention, in turn, seeks to shorten the duration of untreated illness, which is associated with poorer outcomes in several conditions. The evidence is firmest for the duration of untreated psychosis (DUP), for which an umbrella review found that a DUP of four weeks predicted more than 20% more severe symptoms at follow-up than a DUP of one week (Howes et al., 2021). Meanwhile, in depression the evidence is more limited (Ghio et al., 2014).

Important for the subject of this section, the route through which psychotherapy produces change also differs between patients. In 608 outpatients, Gómez Penedo and colleagues (2023) estimated how strongly each patient's improvement followed sessions that brought mastery experiences (coping better with the problem) or clarification experiences (understanding it better): these links varied between patients and were only weakly predictable from baseline characteristics (r = .18 and .16, respectively). The same treatment, thus, works through different routes in different patients, and which route will work cannot be anticipated reliably.

Two factors, or a combination of both, could underlie these differences: 1) the loss of the person's independent means of regulating the maintaining process, here termed capture, and 2) the depletion of the person's reserves. Below, we analyze each of these possibilities. Many psychological interventions depend, to varying degrees, on the means of regulation, so that the more of them the loop "absorbs", the less effective those interventions probably become.

Capture is a profile rather than a single degree-as insight, motivation, attention, behavioral control and identity can each be captured separately, and these channels overlap with the psychological dimensions of the extended evolutionary meta-model used in process-based therapy (S. C. Hayes et al., 2020). Curiously, the channels can also diverge-a person may recognize a compulsion as senseless and still be unable to stop it, or lack insight and still respond to a behavioral intervention. Conditions differ in their typical profiles, from patterns experienced as alien (ego-dystonic) to patterns that have become part of identity (ego-syntonic).

The second factor is depletion (Proposition 3). Remaining entrapped consumes the person's reserves without replenishing them, in line with Hobfoll's (1989) view of stress as the loss of valued resources and with the cumulative physiological cost of stress known as allostatic load (McEwen, 1998)-two accounts in which the cost of sustained coping accumulates instead of being repaid. These reserves can be of several kinds: physiological (sleep, nutrition, health), cognitive (attention, memory), motivational (interest, hope), social (support, roles) and material (income, housing). Importantly, they are not interchangeable, so that a person with several independent reserves may sustain entrapment longer and leave it more easily, than one whose remaining functions depend on a single depleted resource.

For social reserves, an influence on the course of a condition is already documented: in a systematic review of 34 longitudinal studies, poorer perceived social support predicted more severe symptoms, poorer recovery and worse functioning in depression, with preliminary evidence for other conditions (Wang et al., 2018). A similar notion has been developed in neurology, where cognitive reserve explains why some people tolerate more age-related or Alzheimer's pathology than others before their functioning declines (Stern, 2012). Both factors can also act together, as apparently occurs in anorexia nervosa, where the valuing of the disorder combines with physiological depletion.

For the clinician, the profile of capture would then matter as much as overall severity, and it could be assessed channel by channel. Where a channel is captured, interventions that do not depend on it can be given more weight (including those acting through the body, relationships or the environment, and pharmacotherapy when indicated), while rebuilding the captured channel becomes a treatment goal in itself. Reserves can likewise be assessed by kind, and protected and restored from the start of treatment (Hobfoll, 1989; McEwen, 1998), which gives early intervention a further rationale. When entrapment extends to the sense that there is no way out, suicidal ideation may emerge or intensify, perceived entrapment being a central driver of suicidal ideation in the integrated motivational-volitional model of O'Connor and Kirtley (2018). Of note, perceived entrapment is not specific to suicidality: in a meta-analysis by Siddaway and colleagues (2015), perceptions of defeat and entrapment were strongly associated with depression, anxiety, posttraumatic stress disorder and suicidality alike-possibly the experienced side of the structure described here.

The observations on the duration of untreated illness and on the valuing of the disorder in anorexia nervosa are established (Ghio et al., 2014; Gregertsen et al., 2017; Howes et al., 2021), whereas the structure of capture and the role of reserves are still hypothetical. We propose that the channels of capture are separable, and that capture of a given channel reduces the response to interventions relying on that channel more than the response to interventions entering elsewhere.

A compelling test should compare both kinds of intervention in patients with and without capture of that channel. We also propose that reserves measured before onset predict how long an episode lasts more than whether it begins, and that the number of independent reserves predicts duration at least as well as their total.

3.4 Question 4: How Does Change Happen?

Very different treatments reach similar outcomes, and their active ingredients remain unclear (Holmes et al., 2018). Change, moreover, is often not gradual, and sudden gains (large improvements between consecutive sessions) are common and, according to the meta-analysis of Shalom and Aderka (2020), predict better outcomes at the end of treatment and at follow-up.

Component studies have tried to isolate the active ingredients, with a curious asymmetry: in the meta-analysis of Bell and colleagues (2013), removing components in dismantling designs did not, on average, worsen outcomes, whereas adding them in additive designs gave small benefits on the targeted problems, which grew at follow-up. In line with this asymmetry, behavioral activation alone (one of the components of cognitive therapy) was as effective as full cognitive therapy (Jacobson et al., 1996) and, in more severe depression-as antidepressant medication (Dimidjian et al., 2006).

As change is often abrupt, dynamical-systems research has looked, instead of active ingredients, for "early-warning signals" of transitions: changes in system fluctuation that announce an abrupt shift before it occurs, as described for ecosystems and climate (Scheffer et al., 2009). In psychotherapy, two such signals have been studied. The first is instability-a rise in the irregularity of the fluctuations in patients' daily self-ratings. In 23 patients with obsessivecompulsive disorder, change occurred as discontinuous transitions between patterns, prepared by such instabilities and requiring what the authors call stable boundary conditions, a positive atmosphere at the treatment facility and trusting therapeutic relationships (Schiepek et al., 2014).

In patients with mood disorders, fluctuations in daily self-ratings preceded both sudden gains and sudden losses (Olthof, Hasselman, Strunk, van Rooij, et al., 2020), and periods of such destabilization during treatment were associated with better outcomes (Olthof, Hasselman, Strunk, Aas, et al., 2020).

The second signal, and the most widely studied, is "critical slowing down": as a system approaches a tipping point, it recovers ever more slowly from small, everyday disturbances (Scheffer et al., 2009). In a person who rates their emotions several times a day, this would show as ratings that change sluggishly (each one resembling the previous one more closely), fluctuate more widely and rise and fall together. In a large sample of healthy individuals and patients, these three signs were indeed related to the probability of a later shift into or out of depression (van de Leemput et al., 2014). They are not yet reliable enough, however, to tell whether, and when, a particular patient will change: after reviewing the evidence, Helmich and coworkers (2024) found little support for their clinical use and urged researchers to "slow down and be critical" (a play on the very term). These findings fit the network destabilization and transition model of A. M. Hayes and Andrews (2020), in which change proceeds in two steps: the entrenched pattern that maintains a problem (in the terms of section 3.1, the self-maintaining loop) is first destabilized, which shows as increased fluctuation of symptoms and emotions, and a more functional pattern can then emerge and stabilize.

How, then, can interventions as different as exposure, medication or a change of environment lead to similar outcomes? We propose that any intervention can be described by three independent properties (Table 2). The first is where the intervention enters (its site): at the level of the entrapment pattern, at a lower (physiological) level, at a higher (interpersonal or social) level such as relationships and roles, or through the environment. The second is what it does to the pattern (its operation): it can loosen the pattern's constraints, as in exposure, where, according to the inhibitory learning account, new learning overrides the old fear associations without erasing them (Craske et al., 2014). It can change what the pattern's elements mean, as in cognitive restructuring; or it can change the repertoire of elements, adding activities, relationships and contexts, or removing those that sustain the pattern. The third is how the intervention is applied over time (its time regime): as a single acute intervention, as small changes that accumulate until the organization shifts (Proposition 4), or as a sustained condition.

As the "exit" is apparently not the easiest path from inside the pattern, entering from a site that is not itself entrapped may make change possible. For example, combining psychotherapy, which enters at the level of the pattern, with pharmacotherapy, which enters at a lower (physiological) level, outperforms either alone in adult depression (Cuijpers et al., 2020).

Changing the timing between trigger and response, as mindfulness-based cognitive therapy does, can be described as loosening their coupling, and this approach reduces the risk of depressive relapse (Kuyken et al., 2016). This interpretation has two consequences: destabilization opens possibilities but does not set their direction, as the fluctuations preceding both gains and losses already suggested (Olthof, Hasselman, Strunk, van Rooij, et al., 2020), and a reorganization persists only if it is consolidated. In a randomized study of internet-delivered treatment for social anxiety disorder, sudden gains that were explicitly processed (by considering their reasons, their meaning and the opportunities to build on them) were less likely to reverse, whereas unprocessed gains led to outcomes no better than having no gain at all (Shalom et al., 2025).

In treatment, these considerations favor combining interventions that differ on at least one of the three axes over relying on a single one, in line with the benefits of combined treatment and of added components (Bell et al., 2013; Cuijpers et al., 2020). When symptom dynamics become unstable during treatment, closer clinical monitoring would be warranted, since instability can precede condition deterioration as well as improvement (Olthof, Hasselman, Strunk, van Rooij, et al., 2020). Whether added support during such periods also steers (at least in part) the direction of change remains unclear, although this possibility is consistent with the stable boundary conditions that transitions required (Schiepek et al., 2014). After an improvement, consolidation would need to be deliberate, involving a review of what changed and why, the practice of the new pattern across contexts and its connection with daily life (Shalom et al., 2025). Relapse prevention could then be planned on the assumption, following Proposition 4, that the old pattern may remain available beneath the new one (Craske et al., 2014). By the same reasoning, approaches that loosen constraints transiently and broadly -such as psychedelic-assisted therapy, which the REBUS model of Carhart-Harris and Friston (2019) describes as a relaxation of high-level beliefs-would require an explicit consolidation phase.

Sudden gains and their prognostic value are established (Shalom & Aderka, 2020), whereas the benefit of processing gains rests on a single trial and awaits replication (Shalom et al., 2025). The three axes presented in Table 2 are a classification that organizes existing interventions without defining a new therapy. The effect of combining operations, however, remains a hypothesis. We propose that engaging several operations makes improvement more durable, but not necessarily larger, a possibility weakly supported by the growth of additive effects at follow-up (Bell et al., 2013). As a single protocol can engage several operations (behavioral activation, for example, also reduces avoidance), engagement has to be measured by process indicators instead of by counting protocol components.

3.5 Question 5: What Should Recovery Aim at, and How Should Treatment End?

Symptomatic remission and the recovery of a satisfying life often diverge. Definitions of health and mental health have moved from the absence of disorder toward a "dynamic state of internal equilibrium" (Galderisi et al., 2015, p. 231) and "the ability to adapt and to self manage" (Huber et al., 2011, p. 3), while people with lived experience of mental illness describe personal recovery in terms of connectedness, hope, identity, meaning and empowerment, summarized in the CHIME framework (Leamy et al., 2011). In both cases, health and recovery are described by what a person can do, and not by the absence of symptoms. Endings pose a specific problem, as, during antidepressant dose reduction or after discontinuation, worsening may reflect withdrawal symptoms, relapse, or both.

Psychological flexibility has been proposed as a core of health by Kashdan and Rottenberg (2010). For endings, options include maintenance pharmacotherapy, psychological relapse prevention and, when antidepressants are discontinued, gradual tapering, with hyperbolic tapering proposed by Horowitz and Taylor (2019) as one way to reduce withdrawal symptoms.

In the ANTLER trial in primary care, participants randomized to antidepressant discontinuation had a one-year relapse rate of 56%, compared with 39% among those randomized to maintenance treatment, and reported more withdrawal symptoms (Lewis et al., 2021).

Interestingly, prior cognitive therapy protected against relapse after treatment ended at least as well as continued medication (Hollon et al., 2005). Similarly, in an individual participant data meta-analysis of four trials, time to relapse did not differ between a psychological intervention given during or after antidepressant tapering and continued medication (Breedvelt et al., 2021).

In both cases, what was acquired in a time-limited psychological treatment apparently persisted after it ended, as the fourth marker of development describes (section 2.1).

As proposed in section 2.3, mental health is the preserved capacity to keep developing, and not a state to be restored. This proposal has three consequences. First, recovery runs forward: a developing system cannot return to a previous state, because what it has lived through has become part of its structure (Proposition 2). The realistic aim is, thus, a new organization that integrates the experience, with the markers of development serving as indicators of whether such an organization has formed. Second, activity is not development, as each of the functions that make up development can "run idle" (examples in Table 3). Third, two different processes can follow the reduction/end of treatment. If treatment suppressed the pattern without reorganizing it and the pattern returns when treatment is reduced or stopped-this corresponds to relapse. If the body has adapted physiologically to the medication, so that reducing or stopping it destabilizes the body whatever the course of the original condition-it corresponds to withdrawal. The two processes can co-occur, and withdrawal, being itself a destabilization, can precipitate relapse where no reorganization has taken place. In psychological treatment, the equivalent of reorganization is what remains when the therapist is no longer accompanying the patient.

Treatment goals could then be framed as "resumed development," that is, in terms of what the person can do, relate to, learn and become, and not only as symptom thresholds or as a return to "how I was" (Huber et al., 2011; Leamy et al., 2011). The patterns of idle functioning (Table 3) can help to assess whether effortful activity is producing change. Before treatment ends, it becomes relevant to evaluate whether reorganization has occurred (i.e., whether gains show the first three markers of development, the fourth being confirmable only later), and not only whether symptoms have diminished. The same interpretation gives a reason for tapering antidepressant medication gradually when discontinuation is planned (Horowitz & Taylor, 2019; Lewis et al., 2021).

Timing and symptom profile can help to distinguish withdrawal from relapse, as withdrawal commonly begins within days of a dose reduction/discontinuation, whereas relapse usually appears later, and symptoms that are new or qualitatively different from the original presentation favor withdrawal (Horowitz & Taylor, 2019). Nevertheless, withdrawal symptoms can resemble the original disorder.

The ideas presented in this section rest on different grounds: our account of mental health is introduced as a definition; the patterns of idle functioning in Table 3 are a classification that has to be validated, their signatures being candidate indicators; the distinction between relapse and withdrawal, as well as gradual tapering, is consistent with current literature (Horowitz & Taylor, 2019; Lewis et al., 2021). Hypothetical is the durability of improvement: we expect improvements that show the first three markers of development when treatment ends to be followed by less relapse than an equal symptom reduction without them, as the protection afforded by prior cognitive therapy (Breedvelt et al., 2021; Hollon et al., 2005) would suggest.

Taken together, this evidence allows us to propose a preliminary scheme of the course of persistent conditions (Figure 1): 1) the person's own activity lays down a pattern that closes into a self-maintaining loop (Propositions 2 and 3); 2) the loop consumes reserves and the capacity for change that development requires (Propositions 1 and 3); 3) the more of the person's means of regulation it captures, the harder the state becomes to leave; and 4) a reorganization, often abrupt, lasts only if consolidated (Proposition 4). These steps might be interdependent. In this scheme, the five questions share a common answer, but what remains uncertain is the trigger that sets the direction of reorganization once a state has been destabilized.

4. Conditions Outside the Pattern

Not all mental health conditions follow the pattern of active entrapment, and in some of them the difficulty apparently lies elsewhere than in a loop that keeps rebuilding itself. Below, we give examples of how such conditions might be described in terms of the propositions, and of how the aims of treatment would then differ. A detailed analysis of each of them is out of the scope of the present work.

In psychotic disorders, the problem would lie (at least in part) in the relations between levels of organization. A hallucination would then be less an excess of internally generated experience than a failure to keep what is generated answerable to sensory evidence, a view that coincides with accounts in which hallucinations arise when prior expectations outweigh sensory input, such as that of Corlett and colleagues (2019). Treatment would, thus, target the relations between levels instead of a self-maintaining loop. During a manic episode, generation would outpace integration-the second case described in Proposition 1. This view is consistent with accounts of mania as a dysregulation of the reward and goal system, in which goal attainment can trigger an escalating pursuit of new goals (Johnson et al., 2012). Therapy in this case should seek to restore integration, rather than loosening constraints.

Neurodevelopmental conditions, such as autism spectrum disorder or attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, would be regarded as developmental configurations in their own right, and not as entrapment, and support would then aim at the fit between the person and the environment-a view close to the neurodiversity perspective that is reshaping autism research (Pellicano & den Houting, 2022). In neurodegenerative disorders, finally, the capacity for self-modification itself may progressively (and perhaps irreversibly) decline, and care would center on retaining and protecting what still continues to develop. These configurations do not exclude entrapment, as comorbidity is common (Plana-Ripoll et al., 2019) and an entrapment loop can form on top of any of the mentioned conditions, remaining open to the means described above. Still, the implications drawn so far do not transfer automatically to these conditions, and formulation should first establish which configuration is present.

Here a natural question arises: how large is the share of cases that do not follow the proposed pattern? A rough estimate can be obtained from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2023, which did not count substance use disorders and dementias among mental disorders.

According to this study, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and neurodevelopmental conditions accounted for about one in five prevalent cases of mental disorders and a quarter of the disability-adjusted life years due to them, whereas the conditions proposed to follow the pattern (the main subject of this proposal-anxiety, depressive and eating disorders) accounted for about two-thirds of both (GBD 2023 Mental Disorder Collaborators, 2026).

5. Discussion: Clinical Implications and Testable Predictions

It seems reasonable to ask what would follow if the proposed interpretation were carried into clinical practice. As each of the four phases is governed by its own conditions, the implications drawn above can be arranged by phase (Table 4). Like the three axes of intervention, they do not amount to a new therapy, and what would change is how existing interventions are chosen, combined, timed and evaluated. First, assessment would add to the diagnosis a formulation of what maintains the condition (the loop, its content and level, the channels it has captured and the reserves that remain). Second, treatment would be selected and combined from that formulation, leading with interventions that do not depend on a captured channel, and gains consolidated before treatment ends. Third, relapse risk would be judged not only by residual symptoms, but also by whether gains show the first three markers of development, which the proposal expects to predict relapse beyond symptom reduction (Table 5, prediction 8).

Of the 19 implications listed in Table 4, seven coincide with practices that already rest on independent evidence (early intervention, the formulation of maintaining processes, gradual tapering of antidepressant medication and relapse prevention, among others), for which the interpretation supplies a common reason and not a new recommendation. Seven extend that evidence, as in combining interventions that differ on the three axes or monitoring more closely during periods of symptom instability. The remaining five are specific to the proposal and are so far untested. These five implications concern mainly what would be assessed: the structure of the maintaining loop, capture channel by channel, reserves by kind and, before treatment ends, the first three markers of development. Even the established elements owe their place in this sequence to the proposal, so that the implications are better regarded as candidate clinical actions for empirical evaluation than as recommendations.

Whether such actions improve care, and whether the propositions behind them hold, are empirical questions. The proposal stands or falls with the predictions gathered in Table 5, each paired with the observation that would allow its refutation. For example, capturing a channel such as insight should lower the response to interventions that rely on insight more than to those that bypass it, and the prediction fails if capture lowers all responses alike, or none (Table 5, prediction 10). Similarly, relapse after antidepressant discontinuation should concentrate among people whose improvement lacked the first three markers of development, and the prediction fails if relapse is independent of those markers (Table 5, prediction 13). Most predictions can be tested with established designs (randomized comparisons, prospective cohorts, intensive longitudinal monitoring), and those on reserves and population differences (Table 5, predictions 7 and 9) could be examined using existing epidemiological data.

The present work has three main limitations. First, the evidence reviewed here was selected to examine the propositions instead of being gathered systematically, and is mostly positive. We conducted a brief, non-systematic search of the published literature for evidence that would meet any of the refutation criteria presented and found none. The studies that come closest were not designed around the constructs proposed here, so the presented predictions remain open to test. Second, the constructs introduced in the present work (active entrapment, capture, reserves and others) have not yet been operationalized. Third, as the present work is theoretical, no participants were recruited and no demographic data (racial or ethnic identification, cultural or geographic background, sex or gender, and income, education or socioeconomic status) were collected. The evidence we draw on comes from samples of different backgrounds, including surveys in 21 countries of different income levels (Thornicroft et al., 2017), a trial in a conflict-affected area of Pakistan (Rahman et al., 2016), studies of how distress is expressed in different cultural groups (Kirmayer, 2001; Kohrt et al., 2014) and a cohort of women (Kendler et al., 2000). We did not analyze this evidence by demographic group. Of these factors, culture is addressed explicitly (section 3.2), and its influence is open to test (Table 5, predictions 1 and 9). For a theoretical proposal built on the evidence available, such differences between samples are of minor importance. However, they may become important for more detailed studies, once data collected using the constructs proposed here become available.

6. Conclusions

Mental health conditions are currently defined mostly by syndromes, studied crosssectionally and treated with symptom reduction as the main criterion of success. The main difference of our proposal is that it approaches them as phases of a developmental process, addressing the three difficulties identified at the outset: the four phases treat conditions as processes (Figure 1), Proposition 2 accounts for the way a condition changes the person who has it, and capture describes how a condition absorbs a capacity on which many psychological treatments rely.

Why should a developing mind be prone to entrapment at all? Within the proposal, the answer is given by Proposition 3: a system that maintains itself through its own activity cannot exclude that this activity reproduces the constraints that confine it: entrapment can become the other face of self-maintenance. Recovery, in turn, runs forward toward a new organization and not back to a previous state (Proposition 2).

For practice, attention moves from the diagnostic label to what currently maintains a condition, from symptom reduction alone to whether change has become part of the person's organization, and from the end of an episode to the stabilization of what replaces it (Table 4). As most conditions begin early, and leaving a state may become harder the longer it lasts (Table 5, prediction 11), attention to the entry phase adds to the rationale for early intervention. If the structure is shared across diagnoses, structural assessment may also be shared across settings (including those where specialists are scarce), although individualized case formulation is harder to learn than manualized treatment (Emmelkamp, 2021). Its reliability across settings is, thus, the first aspect to test (Table 5, prediction 1).

For research, the proposal directs study to four phases governed by different conditions and turns the apparent "ceiling" of current treatments into testable questions, such as whether matching interventions to structure and capture, and consolidating gains, improves outcomes (Table 5). Formulated independently of any single diagnosis or treatment tradition, the propositions offer psychiatry, clinical psychology and non-specialist services a common language for formulating and testing change, complementing the one proposed by S. C. Hayes and colleagues (2020) for processes of change. Process-based approaches ask which processes to target and how to change them (Hofmann & Hayes, 2019), whereas the phases, capture, reserves and consolidation described here specify when a change can occur and whether it lasts.

Several issues were only touched upon or not covered here: specific genetic, neural and cognitive mechanisms (intentionally left out; see section 2.2), conditions outside the entrapment pattern (only outlined in section 4) and the operationalization of the constructs. We started from five questions usually approached separately. Within the proposal they appear as parts of a single course, whose correspondence to actual patient trajectories remains to be tested.
