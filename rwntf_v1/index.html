Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Puberty is a period of major neural reorganization, accompanied by changing demands to navigate increasingly complex social environments and the emergence of many mental health disorders. Although pubertal processes are becoming well-documented in neurotypical youth, the field has only begun to interrogate these processes in youth with autism. In this mini-review, we evaluate evidence regarding altered pubertal development in autism based on studies examining physical development milestones and neuroendocrine axes pertinent to adrenarche and gonadarche. We review studies linking pubertal factors with mental health symptoms in autistic youth to assess potential puberty-related links to risk for co-morbid psychopathology. We bring together multiple literatures across human and animal models to assess evidence of consensus across fields. Findings suggest that aspects of puberty may be accelerated in autism, and that they may be at elevated risk for co-morbid mental health symptoms. We conclude with recommendations for future research, including the need for integration across pubertal, neural, and mental health measures to delineate how trajectories of risk may unfold in youth with autism.

1. Introduction

Existing literature has demonstrated the profound influence of pubertal development on mental health in ostensibly neurotypical (NT) populations (Dehestani et al., 2023; Pfeifer & Allen, 2021), with evidence for marked increases in psychopathology symptoms in this adolescent developmental window (Blakemore, 2019). However, relatively few studies have focused on how puberty-related changes affect adolescents with developmental disorders, particularly those with autism spectrum disorders (ASD). 1Despite evidence that neurodevelopmental and behavioral trajectories may diverge between autistic and NT youth specifically during adolescence (Picci & Scherf, 2015; Uddin et al., 2013), research is only beginning to address how basic endocrine factors contributing to pubertal development differ in individuals with ASD compared to NTs. There is an emerging body of literature showing alterations in key markers, including timing of puberty onset and pubertal hormone levels in youth with ASD (Tian et al., 2026). Given that individuals with ASD may experience puberty in ways that diverge from their NT peers, it is crucial to examine how these changes impact their mental health, social interactions and relationships, cognition, as well as their overall well-being. This conceptual mini-review aims to evaluate the extant literature on the neuroendocrine factors that contribute to pubertal development in individuals with ASD, shedding light on the unique challenges faced by this population during adolescence. In what follows, we evaluate the current state of knowledge regarding various neuroendocrine axes and their development in ASD, including the hypothalamicpituitary-adrenal (HPA), hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG), and the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO [in females assigned at birth]) axes, and pubertal development patterns in youth with ASD. To fully evaluate the existing body of evidence, this review bridges human and animal studies (where applicable) and the myriad of indices that characterize pubertal development (e.g., hormone levels, physical exams, questionnaires). Given the known sensitive period that the adolescent window poses for the onset of most major mental health disorders (Solmi et al., 2022), we also weigh evidence for links between pubertal indices and co-morbid mental health issues in ASD. We conclude with considerations and recommendations for future research to push the field forward toward designing studies to capture pubertal development and all its sequalae in autism.

2. Endocrine Development in Autism

>Endocrine Development in Autism

2.1. Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal (HPA) Axis, Adrenarche

The HPA axis regulates the body's homeostasis, responses to stress, and aspects of pubertal development (i.e., adrenarche). Despite there being a longstanding literature on cortisol rhythms in ASD, there are mixed patterns regarding how and whether autistic individuals exhibit cortisol dysregulation compared to NT individuals. Literature examining diurnal and acute cortisol responses in ASD has been systematically reviewed elsewhere (Taylor & Corbett, 2014; van der Linden et al., 2022). Thus, we do not duplicate efforts here and instead focus our review on studies investigating the HPA-axis in the context of puberty and links made to symptomology in autistic youth (see Section 4.1). In addition, we evaluate the extent to which animal models corroborate findings from human studies.

DHEA/DHEA-S. Adrenarche, the first major pubertal event, is spurred by rising production of androgens from the zona reticularis of the adrenal glands, including dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA), its sulfate (DHEA-S) and androstenedione, under regulation of the HPA axis. DHEA/DHEA-S levels begin to rise around 6-8 years of age (Rosenfield, 2021), with physical markers including pubic and axillary hair, as well as skin changes and body odor.

There is relatively limited knowledge about how adrenarche and its associated hormones are related to adolescent mental health (Byrne et al., 2017), particularly in autism. However, in NT samples, there is growing evidence for aspects of adrenarche influencing brain development (Barendse et al., 2018; Penhale et al., 2022) and risk for psychopathology during the pubertal window (Murray et al., 2016; Picci et al., 2025; Whittle et al., 2015). Crucially, insights into adrenarche offer foundational knowledge about how the earliest pubertal event(s) occurring before the major physical changes associated with central pubertal (i.e., gonadarche) can forecast behavior and mental health during adolescence. This may be particularly pertinent to the development of autism symptomology and broader functioning, as adrenarche is hypothesized to support synaptic plasticity in higher-order cortices supporting complex social-affective behaviors affected in autism (Campbell, 2011; Cumberland et al., 2021). In addition to serving as peripheral biomarkers of pubertal maturation, DHEA and DHEA-S function as neuroactive steroids that cross the blood brain barrier and modulate GABAergic and glutamatergic signaling (Compagnone & Mellon, 2000; Maninger et al., 2009a). These processes are central to cortical excitation-inhibition balance and experience dependent plasticity seen during adolescence, whereby neural circuits are being refined and reorganized in response to environmental inputs, including social experiences and learning (Ho, 2026; Parr et al., 2025; Perica et al., 2022). Given longstanding evidence for altered excitationinhibition balance and differences in social affective circuitry in autism (Pelphrey et al., 2011; Rubenstein & Merzenich, 2003; Sohal & Rubenstein, 2019), variability in adrenarcheal timing and adrenal androgen production may provide unique insight into neurodevelopmental changes in core autism features as well as the emergence of co-occurring mental health conditions such as anxiety and depression, which are highly prevalent in autistic youth (Kirsch et al., 2020).

Studies to date in autism samples have begun to show initial links between hormonal indices of adrenarche and diagnostic status (see Table 1 for overview). Specifically, in a sample of prepubertal children (i.e., Tanner 1), it was found that autistic boys had significantly higher serum DHEA levels than NT boys (Al-Zaid et al., 2021). Similarly, in another prepubertal sample, boys and girls with ASD had higher salivary androgens (e.g., DHEA) compared to NT children, with stronger effects in older boys (Majewska et al., 2014). In terms links to autism symptomology, robust associations between elevated androgen levels and autism symptom severity was reported in girls only (Majewska et al., 2014). Though, in a recent meta-analysis examining androgen levels in case-control studies of ASD, it was found that males with ASD in particular have elevated levels of DHEA compared to NTs, with insufficient data to make conclusions about females (Wang et al., 2024). In a recent study with a large sample of girls with and without ASD, there were no diagnostic differences in DHEA-S levels (Corbett, Calvosa, et al., 2025). Thus, as with most studies of autism, future work would benefit from greater inclusion of girls with ASD to shed light on how adrenarcheal hormones and processes may differ between girls with and without ASD, as well as between boys and girls with ASD. These findings provide some initial support for potentially elevated androgen levels in autistic youth during the pubertal window. Broadly, more systematic investigation of adrenarcheal hormones in girls and boys with ASD is crucial, given the growing evidence of adrenarche as a neurodevelopmental driver that is unique from gonadarche, and a developmental process linked to risk for psychopathology that are overrepresented in girls more broadly (e.g., internalizing symptoms) (Szakal & Crespi, 2025).

Animal Models.

Adrenarche is largely unique to humans and some non-human primates (Cutler et al., 1978), making literature using cross-species models far more limited. Traditional laboratory rodent models (i.e., the house mouse and rat) lack a true adrenarche equivalent due to an underdeveloped zona reticularis. The gradually increasing levels of adrenal androgen and DHEA that define adrenarche are difficult and time-consuming to simulate; however, more acute doses of these hormones have been used to model aspects of adrenarcheal neurodevelopment and stress/HPA axis interactions (Karishma & Herbert, 2002; Maninger et al., 2009b) as well as reproductive disorders like PCOS (Motta, 2025).

2.2. Hypothalamic Pituitary Gonadal (HPG) axis -Gonadarche

The HPG axis regulates the synthesis and release of sex steroid hormones, including testosterone and estradiol, across males and females. Gonadarche, also referred to as central puberty, begins with the reactivation of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) signaling, which stimulates the production and major influx of sex steroid hormones involved in key physical and reproductive changes during puberty (e.g., growth spurts, facial hair growth, breast development, genital maturation, menarche). A small but growing literature has examined whether autistic youth differ from neurotypical (NT) youth in circulating sex hormone levels, which may provide insight into differences in pubertal maturation and broader neuroendocrine functioning in ASD. In what follows, we review literature on both testosterone and estrogen concentrations in youth with ASD (see Table 1 for overview).

Testosterone. Studies examining differences in testosterone levels between those with ASD and NTs during development have largely yielded mixed results. In early cross-sectional studies of testosterone levels collected from serum and saliva, youth with ASD had significantly lower testosterone levels than NT youth pre-and post-pubertal onset (Croonenberghs et al., 2010; Krajmer et al., 2011). Similarly, recent longitudinal work has suggested that that boys with ASD may have blunted testosterone during adolescence compared to NTs (Corbett, McGonigle, et al., 2025; Sharpley et al., 2017). In contrast, a few studies report elevated testosterone levels (via urine, and serum) in boys and girls with ASD compared to NT youth (Gasser et al., 2020; Hassan et al., 2019), as well as those with other psychiatric conditions (de Giambattista et al., 2025). However, a number of studies also report no diagnostic differences in salivary or plasma testosterone levels in girls or boys cross-sectionally (Bakker-Huvenaars et al., 2020; Corbett, Calvosa, et al., 2025; Tordjman et al., 1995). Taken together, at a minimum, this work challenges assumptions made by the "extreme male brain" hypothesis of ASD (Baron-Cohen, 2002), given that evidence for elevated testosterone in autism, at least during adolescence, is inconclusive. Findings appear to be indeterminate as to whether there are systematic differences in testosterone levels between those with and without ASD, and caution should be warranted when interpreting earlier studies, as they typically have much smaller samples and do not control for factors known to co-vary with testosterone concentrations (e.g., age, time of day). Thus, future work would benefit from more rigorous investigation of how and whether testosterone levels may vary during gonadarche as a function of diagnosis, and whether this has any bearing on ASD symptom severity, neurodevelopment, and adaptive functioning.

Estrogen. To date, very little work has focused on variability in estradiol levels during adolescence in autistic youth. Estradiol is a key sex steroid hormone involved in gonadal maturation and pubertal development in both boys and girls and is also synthesized through the aromatization of testosterone. Importantly, though it is often viewed as the primary sex hormone for females, estrogen does play a main regulatory role in physical changes in both boys and girls, including as an important driver of the pubertal growth spurt (Singh et al., 2010). With respect to estradiol levels in ASD, multiple studies have reported no differences between autistic versus NT youth nor individuals with other psychiatric diagnoses (Al-Zaid et al., 2021; Corbett, Calvosa, et al., 2025; de Giambattista et al., 2025). In contrast, one study reported lower estradiol levels in boys with ASD compared to NT youth (Hassan et al., 2019). In addition, postmortem tissue work suggests reduced aromatase mRNA expression in the frontal cortex of youth with ASD compared to NT youth (Sarachana et al., 2011), which may be indicative of autism-related alterations in estradiol and testosterone regulation. More broadly, other work has similarly implicated altered estrogen-related signaling in ASD, including reduced serum levels of the G protein-coupled estrogen receptor in autistic youth (Altun et al., 2017). Similar to testosterone, initial investigations of estradiol levels during adolescence remain largely inconclusive, with most studies reporting no group differences. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as this literature is primarily crosssectional and often limited by methodological concerns related to sample size and hormone modeling approaches (e.g., covariate selection and treatment of hormone data). Future work would benefit from larger, prospectively followed cohorts that are carefully characterized with respect to pubertal maturation in order to better capture developmental changes in hormone dynamics across adolescence and determine whether estradiol trajectories systematically differ in ASD.

Importantly, inconsistent findings in circulating estradiol concentrations in ASD do not necessarily exclude a role for estrogen signaling in ASD neurobiology. Experimental studies have demonstrated that postnatal administration of 17β-estradiol to Pvalb heterozygous mice increased parvalbumin expression within cortical interneurons and significantly improved deficits in social interaction and repetitive behaviors (Filice et al., 2018). These findings suggest that altered tissue responsiveness to sex steroids, rather than differences in circulating hormone concentrations alone, may contribute to neurodevelopmental phenotypes and highlight the importance of considering both endocrine and cellspecific mechanisms when investigating gonadal steroid signaling in ASD.

Animal Models. Compared with extensive behavioral, synaptic, and circuit-level characterization, reproductive neuroendocrine phenotyping has received relatively little attention in rodent models of ASD. Among existing models, Mecp2 deletion-associated with Rett Syndrome-has undergone the most comprehensive endocrine characterization. A recent study of Rett syndrome mice demonstrated delayed pubertal maturation accompanied by reduced circulating testosterone, decreased luteinizing hormone (LH), and impaired GnRH signaling despite preservation of GnRH neuron number (Martín-Sánchez et al., 2026), suggesting that MECP2 deficiency disrupts functional maturation of hypothalamic reproductive circuits rather than their developmental specification. Consistent with this interpretation, androgenresponsive vasopressin circuitry was similarly reduced, indicating that altered testosterone signaling extends beyond reproductive tissues to influence sexually dimorphic neural networks. Conversely, mouse models of MECP2 duplication syndrome exhibit elevated circulating testosterone with relatively preserved estradiol concentrations (Wang et al., 2021), demonstrating that both increased and decreased MECP2 dosage disrupt androgen homeostasis through distinct, likely epigenetically mediated mechanisms.

Evidence for HPG axis dysfunction has also emerged from Fmr1 mouse models of Fragile X syndrome, although the literature is less integrated. Independent studies have reported alterations in testosterone, estradiol, gonadotropin secretion, gonadal maturation, and fertility, consistent with impaired reproductive endocrine regulation (Elmazoudy et al., 2026). However, these investigations have largely arisen from studies of reproductive physiology rather than neurodevelopment, and relatively few have examined hypothalamic GnRH circuitry or pubertal progression alongside behavioral phenotypes.

Together, animal studies suggest that disruption of genes implicated in neurodevelopment can influence pubertal maturation and reproductive endocrine function, supporting the concept that altered HPG axis development may represent an intrinsic component of at least a subset of ASD. At the same time, they highlight substantial gaps in the field which are largely echoed among the human literature.

3. Physical Markers of Pubertal Development in ASD

>Physical Markers of Pubertal Development in ASD

3.1. Pubertal Development -Physical Exam Staging & Perceived Status

A quickly growing literature evaluating physical development markers of pubertal development in youth with ASD has yielded important insights regarding the timing and tempo of puberty in autism (see Table 2). In what follows, we review two separate but interrelated lines of research here, including general trends of pubertal timing, stage, tempo, and patterns of precocious puberty in autism.

3.2 Pubertal Development Trends in Autism

In studies of pubertal timing, there has been some evidence for earlier onset of puberty in adolescents with ASD compared to NT peers (Corbett et al., 2020 (Corbett et al., , 2022;; Groenman et al., 2024) with mixed results regarding age at menarche for girls. In terms of earlier pubertal onset, cross-sectional work in 10-13year-olds has shown that autistic girls, compared to autistic boys and NT girls, show gonadarche-related changes earlier (i.e., breast development and menarche onset) (Corbett et al., 2020). In fact, autistic girls tended to experience menarche 9.5 months earlier than NT girls. No within-sex diagnostic group-level differences in adrenarcheal processes (i.e., pubic hair development) were observed. Another recent study revealed that girls with and without autism did not differ in age of menarche, but that overall, youth with ASD started gonadarche, but not adrenarche, earlier than TD youth (Groenman et al., 2024). In one of very few longitudinal studies to date (Corbett et al., 2022), across three annual assessments, youth with ASD had earlier gonadarche and adrenarche timing compared to NT youth. Autistic youth tended to start gonadarche and adrenarche approximately 5 and 4 months earlier, respectively, than their NT peers. Given the longitudinal nature of this study, they also examined the tempo or pace of pubertal development. However, no differences were found between youth with and without autism for either gonadarche or adrenarche tempo. Similar to the cross-sectional Corbett et al., 2020 study, girls with ASD started gonadarche earlier than NT girls but did not show any timing differences for adrenarche. There were also no significant diagnostic differences for boys in their timing and tempo of gonadarche or adrenarche. Conversely, with an additional year of data (i.e., 4 time points), Corbett and colleagues report no differences in breast development timing and tempo between ASD and NT girls, though other physical markers of puberty were not examined for timing and tempo differences (Corbett, Calvosa, et al., 2025).

Although there is some support for the notion that youth with ASD undergo gonadarche earlier, especially girls, there are discrepancies in menarche onset for girls and adrenarche timing that may be attributable to methodological differences across studies. For instance, the Corbett studies included >100 participants per group, while the Groenman study included <50, which could influence the latter study's power to detect meaningful differences. However, it should be noted that Groenman et al. used a Bayesian approach to overcome these potential power issues. Another key difference between these studies is how pubertal development was assessed. The Corbett studies used the gold-standard method of having a physician assess pubertal stage, whereas the Groenman study used the self-report Pubertal Development Scale (Carskadon & Acebo, 1993). This is important because at least one study has reported that parent and self-reported pubertal stage in ASD samples has shown some questionable reliability compared to staging from a physical exam (Corbett et al., 2019). Notably, it has been argued that perceived pubertal status gathered from self-report in NT youth may measure distinct psychosocial self-perceptions about their physical development, with meaningful links to mental health that are distinguishable from pubertal hormone levels alone (Barendse et al., 2022). Moreover, despite documented alterations in psychological self-awareness, youth with ASD have not been found to have difficulty with physical self-awareness (Uddin, 2011). Thus, more work is needed to establish the reliability of parent and self-reports in ASD samples, especially considering that the aforementioned reliability study only collected self-reports from the Tanner Staging line drawings (Morris & Udry, 1980) and parent-report from the PDS, but not self-report PDS. Although both are common methods for perceived pubertal development, future work should examine whether the PDS yields more or less superior reliability estimates for self-report in youth with ASD or if a combination of PDS and Tanner Staging line drawings is a more reliable combined measure.

3.3 Precocious Puberty in Autism

A few studies have examined precocious puberty (PP) in autism, which is defined as the onset of gonadarche or secondary-sex characteristics (e.g., breast development, menses in girls, and genital development) by age 8 in girls and 9 in boys (Sharma & Daley, 2026). This is distinguishable from earlier pubertal timing within the normal range, as PP is a diagnosable condition and may indicate more significant dysfunction in endocrine systems. However, it should be noted that most studies to date that have examined general pubertal patterns have not systematically evaluated their samples for a PP diagnosis, which does not rule out the possibility that some autistic children in those samples had undiagnosed PP. That said, the notion that PP is generally overrepresented in ASD is a crucial insight into potential alterations in patterns of endocrine development and how they differ in ASD compared to the NT population. Existing studies have used a range of methods, from case-based (Finkle et al., 2020) to epidemiological methods, to establish whether PP is disproportionate in autism (see Table 2). As an example, Tsai et al. (2024) utilized data from 11,104 children with ASD and age-, sex-matched NT children (85.8% male; N=22,208) based upon PP diagnoses made by medical professionals (e.g., pediatricians, endocrinologists). Results revealed that, on average, autistic youth received a PP diagnosis about one year earlier (ASD = ~8.8 years, TD = 9.9 years) and at a higher incidence rate than non-autistic youth (ASD = .7% and NT = .1%) (Tsai et al., 2024). Crucially, autistic girls were at an even greater risk of developing PP during the follow-up period than autistic boys (girls = 3.5%, boys= 0.3%). Although the rate of risk differs, two separate epidemiological samples have shown significantly increased risk of PP in autism compared to children without autism, ranging from a 1.94-fold increase to a 4-fold increase in risk for PP (Geier & Geier, 2021; Hueg et al., 2026). Notably, a recent meta-analysis has also highlighted a 3.6 fold increase in risk for PP in autistic individuals across studies, with the greatest risk of PP in autistic girls (Tian et al., 2026).

Taken together, this small collection of studies suggests that autistic youth, and autistic girls in particular, are at an elevated risk of undergoing PP than their non-autistic counterparts. This provides additional support for the idea that there may be systematic alterations or even subtypes of ASD that are characterized by altered neuroendocrine axis functioning that results in earlier onset of secondary-sex characteristic development. What is less clear in this literature is whether these patterns are due to central or peripheral PP. Central PP is thought to be due to earlier triggering of gonadarche or HPG axis activity (e.g., earlier increases in GnRH). At the same time, peripheral PP is due to an influx of sex steroid hormones instigated by processes independent of premature hypothalamus/pituitary activity (e.g., elevated sex steroid hormone production in gonads or adrenal glands). Moreover, the extent to which there are differential consequences for neural and behavioral development in individuals with ASD versus NTs to either central or peripheral PP is unexplored. Thus, future work should consider making more targeted attempts to disentangle sources of PP in autism and their associations with children's mental and physical health, given the known links between pubertal development and emergent mental health problems in the general population (Dehestani et al., 2023).

4.0 Neuroendocrine Associations with Mental Health in ASD

For autistic adolescents, particularly those who have lower support needs, risk for co-morbid mental health disorders is greater than otherwise NT youth, with elevations in anxiety and depression symptoms and diagnoses that far surpass comparison groups (Schwartzman et al., 2022). By age 30, about half of autistic individuals receive anxiety (50%) or depression (54%) diagnoses, nearly double the rate observed in non-autistic peers (Kirsch et al., 2020). Given this prominent risk and the known risk for mental health symptom onset and progression during puberty more broadly (Kretzer et al., 2024; Pfeifer & Allen, 2021), we briefly review the emerging literature documenting whether variability in pubertal factors influence mental health functioning in ASD (Table 2). In what follows, we combine our review of mental health links to physical markers of puberty and pubertal hormones, as the literature is limited across methods of pubertal assessment.

4.3 Pubertal Development, Hormones, and Mental Health

In terms of physical markers of puberty, there is a small collection of studies examining potential links between pubertal status and mental health functioning in youth with ASD. Cross-sectionally, one study has shown that earlier gonadal and adrenal timing are related to more depressive and externalizing symptoms in youth with ASD (Groenman et al., 2024). Conversely, in a 4-year longitudinal study across ages 10-16 years old, otherwise NT youth showed increasing depressive symptoms with age and advancing gonadal development, while youth with ASD showed decreasing symptoms with age and puberty (Corbett et al., 2024). In fact, ASD had higher depressive symptoms earlier in pubertal development. In the same cohort, Corbett and colleagues report that adrenarche and gonadarche timing were not associated with internalizing symptoms (i.e., anxiety, affective problems) in boys or girls with ASD (Corbett et al., 2022). Another longitudinal study of 11-17 year-olds suggests that boys with late pubertal onset, irrespective of ASD diagnostic status, had increasing emotional and peer problems across 3 time points, and girls showed no effects of pubertal timing on changes in symptoms (Dawe-Lane et al., 2025). In terms of pubertal hormones, multiple studies examining links between testosterone levels in boys and girls with ASD report no associations with social symptoms as a function of diagnosis (Corbett, McGonigle, et al., 2025; Muscatello et al., 2022).

Altogether, this scant, mixed literature underscores the need for more studies, especially with longitudinal designs, to continue to parse trajectories of mental health symptoms in youth with ASD, as they may continue to change in the transition from adolescence to emerging adulthood when social dynamics increase in complexity and access to mental health and support services decreases. Indeed, longitudinal population-based work has shown that during adolescence, those with ASD and autistic traits exhibit increasing depressive symptoms from 10 to 18 years old, and that bullying amplifies this risk (Rai et al., 2018). One potential explanation for the previously reviewed mixed findings is that puberty may not exert uniform effects on mental health in ASD, but instead interact with developmental timing, social context, sex, and pre-existing symptom profiles to shape heterogeneous trajectories across adolescence. Collectively, these findings suggest that adolescence likely represents a period of elevated vulnerability for co-morbid mental health conditions in ASD, though the findings reviewed here remain inconclusive with respect to the role of puberty pe se. Future work should also consider a broader constellation of symptoms beyond internalizing and externalizing behaviors. For instance, nearly 88% of autistic individuals in a sample of 7-70 year olds reported experiencing suicidal thoughts, with about a third attempting suicide in their lifetime (Schwartzman et al., 2025), underscoring the substantial psychiatric burden experienced by many autistic individuals. However, it remains unclear how suicidality may relate to pubertal development specifically. Moreover, it may be the case that puberty alone does not fully capture milestones pertinent to autistic youths' mental health and adaptive functioning. For instance, the transition into middle school may be especially disruptive for autistic youths' mental health functioning in ways that differ from NT youth, as highlighted in prior qualitative work (Makin et al., 2017; Nuske et al., 2019).

5.0 Future Research Agenda

Although emerging work suggests that pubertal development may relate to mental health trajectories in autistic youth, the biological and psychological mechanisms underlying these associations remain poorly characterized. In particular, it remains unclear whether variability in pubertal maturation and hormone dynamics in ASD reflects alterations in central neuroendocrine regulation, peripheral gonadal or adrenal processes, and/or broader interactions between neuroendocrine function and the social demands of adolescence. Greater mechanistic specificity will be critical for understanding how pubertal processes may contribute to heterogeneity in co-morbid mental health outcomes that are frequently reported in ASD. 5.1 Methodological Considerations

Despite existing work providing foundational insights, several methodological improvements should be at the fore of studies characterizing pubertal development in autism. Specifically, much of the earlier work in this area has consisted of small samples that, in some cases, were not appropriately matched on age, which can limit interpretability of puberty-related effects that are frequently confounded by age. Moreover, smaller samples reporting null effects (e.g., in the pubertal hormone literature) have made it difficult to discern whether the lack of effects is due to power and design issues, or actual phenomenon. It is also unclear how well the current literature captures the full spectrum, and whether extant samples are enriched for specific presentations of autism (e.g., more or less severe syndromic samples). Thus, greater investment in expressly designing well-powered studies with well-characterized samples examining pubertal development in autism are sorely needed to advance our understanding of these processes.

In addition, current research on pubertal development in ASD is largely cross-sectional, with only one ongoing longitudinal study specifically designed to rigorously track these developmental changes (Corbett, Calvosa, et al., 2025; Corbett et al., 2022 Corbett et al., , 2024;; Corbett, McGonigle, et al., 2025). While studies have explored specific pubertal and neuroendocrine processes in autism, such as precocious puberty (Tsai et al., 2024), menarche/menstruation (Skommer & Gunesh, 2025), and variability in hormones (Bakker-Huvenaars et al., 2020), there remains a significant gap capturing how these dynamic and nonlinear processes fluctuate over time. Future research should prioritize comprehensive longitudinal designs that track pubertal onset, progression, and the concurrent changes in mental health across adolescence in individuals with ASD. Such studies are essential for clarifying critical periods of vulnerability or resilience, establishing more causal links between pubertal processes and mental health trajectories, and ultimately informing timely and targeted interventions.

There also continue to be open questions regarding how to accurately assess pubertal development in individuals with ASD. Evidence suggests that parents of children with ASD tend to be less accurate and underestimate their child's pubertal stage compared to parents of NT youth, though there has been acceptable agreement reported with gold standard physical exam staging (Corbett et al., 2019). Notably, boys and girls with and without ASD had largely comparable levels of concordance between physical exams and their self-reported pubertal status, ranging from poor to fair. Although self-report measures tend to be less concordant with physical exams, they may offer important insight into how autistic adolescents perceive and interpret their bodily changes, and these perceptions may have greater relevance for mental health functioning than objective pubertal indices. Indeed, work in otherwise NT adolescents suggests that perceived pubertal timing and physically observable pubertal maturation tend to show more consistent associations with transdiagnostic mental health symptoms than hormonal measures alone (Barendse et al., 2022; Dehestani et al., 2024), potentially because adolescents' perceptions of their bodily changes may shape social experiences, self-image, peer relationships, and feelings of social difference during adolescence. This may be particularly relevant and uniquely challenging for autistic youth, as adolescence involves increasingly complex social milieu that occur alongside substantial physical and developmental changes. Ultimately, studies examining pubertal development in autistic youth should opt to take a comprehensive, multi-method approach whenever possible, incorporating parent and self-report measures alongside hormonal assessments.

5.2 Targeted Investigations Linking Pubertal Processes and Neurodevelopment in ASD

Ultimately, characterizing how pubertal processes contribute to mental health trajectories in autistic youth will require the integration of neurodevelopmental and pubertal measures. Pubertal hormones instigate enormous changes in the brain and social-affective behaviors during adolescence (Pfeifer & Allen, 2021; Sisk & Foster, 2004), and although there is an extensive literature examining neurodevelopment in autistic adolescents (Durkut et al., 2022; Rafiee et al., 2022; Uddin et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2025), studies have not explicitly linked these processes to concurrent pubertal maturation. Crucially, puberty captures biological variability in adolescent development that is not fully reflected by chronological age alone, potentially offering additional explanatory insight into heterogeneity in neurodevelopmental and mental health trajectories in ASD. In other words, integrating neuroimaging with hormonal and pubertal assessments could help address several outstanding questions in autism research that age-related development alone cannot capture. For instance, puberty may differentially shape the maturation of the sensorimotor-association axis in ASD versus NT youth, particularly given that higher-order association regions continue to undergo prolonged remodeling throughout adolescence, are enriched with sex hormone receptors, and support complex behaviors that are affected in autism (e.g., social-affective processing). Likewise, emerging work on menstrual cycle-related brain dynamics (Heller et al., 2025) raises important questions regarding how cyclical hormonal fluctuations affect brain function and mental health in autistic girls, who may be at elevated risk for more severe menstrual symptoms, irregular menses, and menstrual disorders (Skommer & Gunesh, 2025). Neuroimaging approaches may also provide insight into how pubertal indices influence excitatory/inhibitory balance in ASD, for example by leveraging magnetic resonance spectroscopy to examine neurochemical profiles in hormone-sensitive brain regions. Finally, emerging neuroimaging technologies such as optically-pumped magnetometer MEG (OPM-MEG), which enables wearable, motion-tolerant recording, and interpersonal hyperscanning (e.g., scanning dyads simultaneously), stands to provide novel opportunities to examine how autistic youth interact with peers and caregivers in real time and how these social neural dynamics change as a function of pubertal development. Collectively, integrating brain-based and neuroendocrine approaches will be critical for disentangling the interacting biological mechanisms that shape adolescent development and mental health risk in autism.

Further elucidating these biological mechanisms will also benefit from greater attention to interactions across neuroendocrine systems during puberty, particularly potential cross-axis coupling or decoupling between the HPA and HPG axes in autistic youth. If autism is characterized by differences in neuroendocrine dynamics, it will be important for different endocrine axes to be considered in tandem, rather than in isolation. Existing work suggests that there are potential alterations to HPA-axis functioning in ASD (Taylor & Corbett, 2014), primarily based on diurnal cortisol rhythms and acute responses to stressors, though findings are mixed. Although patterns are mixed, and likely context dependent, with respect to hypo-versus hyper-responsiveness of the HPA-axis in autism, altered coordination between stress-related and gonadal hormone systems may be one pathway through which pubertal development contributes to heterogeneity in mental health and developmental outcomes. Moreover, more attention to the component parts of neuroendocrine axes, including the hypothalamus and pituitary gland, will be necessary for capturing where within the feedback loops central to pubertal hormone regulation alterations may arise in autism. For instance, in otherwise NT youth, critical links between pubertal indices, pituitary gland volume, and mental health symptoms have been documented (Murray et al., 2016; Picci et al., 2025).

Complementing human studies, cross-species research utilizing animal models can provide invaluable insights into these complex biological interactions. Given the ethical and practical limitations of hormonal manipulation studies in humans, future research should leverage animal models to gain a deeper understanding of pubertal development in ASD. This should include comprehensive and prospective research design to probe pubertal development rather than retrospective secondary data analysis (e.g., observed timing of vaginal opening or first pregnancy as part of an unrelated study). Animal models offer controlled environments to investigate the direct impact of hormonal changes on neurodevelopmental processes and behavior relevant to ASD. Insights from animal studies can then inform observational research in humans, helping to identify potential biomarkers, refine hypotheses, and uncover novel therapeutic targets that are not feasible to explore through human studies alone. However, current popular model systems may not be sufficient, particularly for the study of adrenarche. Indeed, the spiny mouse, which does experience a natural rise in DHEA ahead of reproductive maturation (Quinn et al., 2016), has been used as an orphan model system for reproductive endocrinology for decades. This model may prove particularly powerful for studying the intersection between pubertal development and ASD but will require time and effort to generate the necessary genetic tools for doing so.

5.3 Careful consideration of co-morbid mental health and developmental transitions

Most major mental health disorders emerge during adolescence, with 50% onsetting by age 14 and 75% by age 25 (Solmi et al., 2022). This period of heightened psychiatric vulnerability, coupled with the substantial neural reorganization that occurs during puberty, underscores the importance of clarifying how pubertal processes may shape mental health risk in autistic youth. Although co-morbid mental health concerns during adolescence have long been documented in ASD (Anderson et al., 2011; Billstedt et al., 2005; Mayes et al., 2011), existing work linking puberty to mental health has largely focused on internalizing symptoms or exacerbation of core autism-related features (Corbett et al., 2024). Future research should broaden this scope to include other symptom domains and transdiagnostic processes that autistic youth are at elevated risk for. For example, suicidal ideation and self-injurious thoughts and behaviors increase markedly during adolescence in otherwise NT populations, and autistic individuals show substantially elevated rates of suicidality relative to NT peers across development and adulthood (Schwartzman et al., 2025). However, despite this heightened risk, virtually nothing is known about how pubertal timing, tempo, or hormone dynamics may contribute to suicidality risk in autistic youth specifically. More broadly, future work should examine how pubertal factors relate to the exacerbation or mitigation of diverse psychiatric and transdiagnostic symptoms across adolescence, and whether these developmental trajectories diverge from NT peers.

Another critical area for future research is understanding how pubertal and neurodevelopmental processes shape the transition into adult social roles for autistic individuals. This developmental transition may pose unique challenges for autistic youth, who are often at elevated risk for difficulties related to independent living, employment, higher education, and social integration in adulthood (Esbensen et al., 2010; Matson et al., 2009; Neely & Hunter, 2014; Solomon, 2020). Existing work suggests that autistic youth exhibit distinct trajectories of brain development relative to NT peers in domains supporting the successful transition into adult social roles (Picci & Scherf, 2015), raising the possibility that pubertal processes may interact with (or in some cases, provoke) atypical neurodevelopmental processes to influence such vulnerable domains and, by extension, mental health outcomes during this crucial developmental transition. Given that this developmental period coincides with the peak emergence of major psychiatric disorders, future longitudinal work should clarify how pubertal maturation and neurodevelopment jointly contribute to resilience versus vulnerability as autistic youth move into adulthood. Understanding these interacting developmental pathways will be critical for generating developmentally-informed supports and targeted interventions that are tailored to facilitate autistic individuals' long-term mental health and adaptive outcomes.

5.4 Leveraging Large Population Neuroscience Datasets to Explore Links between Puberty and Mental Health

The ongoing nationwide longitudinal Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study has collected neuroimaging, behavioral, mental health, and biospecimen data from over 10,000 youth for the past ten years. This publicly available dataset includes measures relevant to the study of pubertal influences on mental health in youth including PDS and salivary hormonal assays measuring testosterone, estradiol, and DHEA (Cheng et al., 2021; Herting et al., 2021). Alongside mental health measures (KSADS, CBCL), the ABCD dataset provides a valuable resource for charting developmental trajectories in a large representative sample. Although autism is not the focus of ABCD, Social Responsiveness Scale data are available, which can in principle be used to measure autistic traits. Future work may utilize this large longitudinal dataset to explore links between puberty, mental health, and autism symptoms.

6. Conclusions

Emerging evidence suggests that pubertal development may unfold differently in autism, with the most consistent findings pointing to differences in pubertal timing (i.e., primarily gonadarche) and increased risk for precocious puberty, particularly among autistic girls. However, findings regarding pubertal hormone concentrations and their associations with mental health remain mixed, highlighting substantial heterogeneity within autism as well as methodological limitations of the existing literature. Critically, puberty is a complex neuroendocrine process that cannot be adequately characterized by agerelated changes, circulating hormone concentrations, or physical maturation alone. We propose that progress in this area will require prospective, longitudinal studies like ABCD that are designed specifically to capture pubertal development in autism, with sufficiently powered and well-characterized samples and multimethod assessment of neuroendocrine, physical, neural, and psychological changes. Complementary cross-species work will also be vital for identifying mechanisms linking pubertal neuroendocrine changes to brain and behavioral development that cannot be readily or ethically isolated in humans, particularly when pubertal development is prospectively characterized rather than captured incidentally in existing animal models. Together, these approaches will be essential for determining whether and how pubertal processes contribute to divergent neurodevelopmental and mental health trajectories in autistic youth and for identifying developmental windows in which targeted supports may be most beneficial.
