Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p = .5 We excluded participants whose rule-judgment accuracy did not exceed chance, defined as a one-sided exact binomial test against p = .5 across all 360 trials (α = .05).

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Full text

Abstract

Cognitive resources are limited, yet people face an abundance of information. Often, which information matters is not stated and can change over time, so people must learn it through experience. This creates a tradeoff: uncertainty requires broad maintenance of multiple information sources, whereas certainty allows resources to be allocated more selectively. Does this reorganization of resources reflect enhancement of relevant information, degradation of irrelevant information, or both? Working memory provides a window into this process because differences in attentional priority should be reflected in what is remembered. To address these questions, we developed the Continuously Assessed Rule Inference Task, in which participants (N = 104) judged whether multidimensional stimuli followed a hidden rule that changed without warning, requiring them to continually update their beliefs about which information was relevant. On intermittent probe trials, participants reproduced the color, shape, and motion direction of the preceding stimulus. A reinforcement-learning model estimated trial-by-trial rule certainty from participants' choices. As certainty increased, rule judgments relied more on evidence from the rule-defining (active) dimension, and memory for inactive features declined. A mixture model showed that active features became more likely to be retained, and inactive features less likely, with little evidence that the precision of retained features changed. These findings suggest that learning what matters reorganizes working memory by changing which information is retained, rather than how precisely it is represented. This shift from broad to selective maintenance may reflect a tradeoff between flexibility under uncertainty and efficiency once relevant information has been identified.

[div-01]

Adaptive behavior requires us not only to attend to relevant information, but also to learn which information is relevant in the first place. For example, when buying food for a newly adopted cat, you first must weigh several factors, such as flavor and texture, because you do not yet know what he prefers. But once you learn that he consistently prefers salmon, you can scan for that ingredient and ignore the rest. Many everyday tasks share this structure, where what matters is not directly stated but must be inferred from feedback (Badre et al., 2010; Karagoz et al., 2025; Wilson & Niv, 2011). In these situations, a key challenge is to allocate limited cognitive resources across potentially relevant sources of information, and the optimal allocation should depend on how certain we are about what matters. Yet it remains unclear how inferred relevance shapes how people process and retain information in working memory.

One natural way to go about dividing cognitive resources is to allocate them broadly while relevance is uncertain, preserving evidence for evaluating and contrasting alternatives, and selectively once certainty grows, reducing the cost of maintaining information (Marshall & Bays, 2013). This allocation must be reconfigured when relevance changes, broadening again before narrowing on the newly relevant information (Egner, 2023; Monsell, 2003). Studies of learning and attention support this idea. In associative learning, attention is distributed across multiple cues when outcomes are uncertain and concentrates on the most predictive cue once it becomes reliable (Walker et al., 2019). When previously reliable cues stop predicting outcomes, renewed uncertainty broadens attention across cues again, facilitating learning of new associations (Easdale et al., 2019). Similarly, in multidimensional learning, attention shifts toward dimensions with higher learned value (Leong et al., 2017; Niv et al., 2015). However, these studies only measure how attention is deployed, focusing on gaze patterns or which dimensions drive choice, but not subsequently remembered for inference. Attending to a stimulus feature does not guarantee it is maintained in memory, and attending away from it does not guarantee that it is forgotten.

Working memory offers a window onto the distribution of cognitive resources across information sources. Explicit signals about goal relevance shape how information is encoded in working memory (Klyszejko et al., 2014; Krawczyk et al., 2007), through the strengthening of relevant information and the suppression of irrelevant information (Gazzaley & Nobre, 2012; Myers et al., 2017; Zanto & Gazzaley, 2009). Such modulation can also occur after encoding: directing attention to information already held in working memory improves memory for it relative to competing representations (Griffin & Nobre, 2003; Gunseli et al., 2015; Souza & Oberauer, 2016).

It is not obvious whether the same holds when relevance is not explicitly cued but must be inferred. Attending to an object often brings its task-irrelevant features into working memory (Luck & Vogel, 1997), although these features are represented less precisely than relevant ones (LaRocque et al., 2017; Shin & Ma, 2016). Combined with evidence that cued priority modulates working memory precision (Klyszejko et al., 2014), this suggests that learned relevance may redistribute precision across features while leaving all in memory. Alternatively, features that are not expected to be needed can be lost almost immediately (Chen & Wyble, 2015). Under this account, relevance acts as a gate on what information is maintained. Critically, these possibilities can be distinguished by using continuous-report tasks, asking participants to reproduce a presented feature value (Wilken & Ma, 2004). The continuous errors that arise from this procedure can be modeled as a mixture of memory-based responses and random guesses, separating the probability that feature values are maintained from the precision of their representations (Bays et al., 2009; Souza et al., 2016; Zhang & Luck, 2008). If working memory shifts from broad to selective maintenance with increasing certainty, memory for relevant information should improve relative to memory for irrelevant features. If this shift reflects a redistribution of resources, the divergence should appear primarily in precision. If it reflects gating, it should appear primarily in the probability that features are retained.

To address these questions, we developed the Continuously Assessed Rule Inference Task (CARIT), adapted from a paradigm developed by Karagoz and colleagues (2025) which was itself modeled after the Wisconsin Card Sorting Task (Berg, 1948). On each trial, participants judged whether a stimulus, defined by three features varying along continuous circular spaces (color, shape, and motion direction), conformed to a hidden rule on one of these dimensions (e.g., whether its color was cool). Because the rule switched unpredictably, participants had to repeatedly infer which dimension mattered. A reinforcement-learning model (RL) fit to participants' choices provided trial-by-trial estimates of their certainty about which rule was active. Intermittent probes asked participants to reconstruct all three features of the preceding stimulus, allowing us to track how memory for features on the active dimension (defining the current rule) and the inactive dimensions) changed as rule certainty evolved. We then used a mixture model of report errors to test whether any divergence between memory for active and inactive features reflected changes in retention probability, precision, or both.

Methods

Participants

We recruited 137 participants from the student population of Washington University in St. Louis. Participants received academic course credit through participation. All participants completed the experiment in person and provided informed consent before starting. All procedures were approved by the Washington University in St. Louis Institutional Review Board.

We excluded participants whose rule-judgment accuracy did not exceed chance, defined as a one-sided exact binomial test against p = .5 across all 360 trials (α = .05). This corresponds to a minimum accuracy of 54.7%. Thirty-three participants fell below this threshold, leaving a final sample of 104 (mean age = 19.23 years, 76 women, 28 men, 0 non-binary).

Task

To assess goal inference and working memory concurrently, participants completed the Continuously Assessed Rule Inference Task (CARIT; Figure 1), programmed in jsPsych (de Leeuw, 2015) using the RDK plugin (Rajananda et al., 2017). On each trial, participants viewed a field of 20 color-filled shapes moving coherently across a circular aperture. Colors were drawn from a circle in CIELAB space (L* = 50, a* = 20, b* = 20, radius = 60; Wilken & Ma, 2004), shapes from the Validated Circular Shape space, in which perceptual similarity decreases with angular distance (Li et al., 2020), and motion directions from the full 360° range. Feature values were initially sampled independently and uniformly from their respective circles. On the first trial after a rule switch, if the previous and new rules implied the same response, we resampled only the feature relevant to the new rule until the two rules implied opposite responses. This ensured that participants who continued to apply the previous rule received incorrect feedback.

On each trial, participants judged whether the current stimulus conforms to a hidden rule (Figure 1B) by pressing the "Y" or "N" key within a response window of 5000 ms. They then received feedback on correctness for 2000 ms. Trials were separated by an intertrial interval of 500 ms. The active rule was not cued, so participants had to infer it from feedback. The rule switched without warning every 9 to 11 trials (uniformly sampled), producing 36 rule blocks across 360 trials. At each switch, the new rule was randomly selected from the four rules along the two dimensions not shared by the current rule.

To assess how features were maintained, working memory probes followed the rule judgment on 36 of the 360 trials, yielding an average of one probe every 10 trials with the constraint that no two consecutive trials were probed. Each sequence of probes began with a pause symbol, signaling that the rule-judgment task was suspended and that the probes would not affect the active rule. After a 1000 ms delay, participants reconstructed the color, shape, and motion direction of the just-seen stimulus using three continuous response wheels (Li et al., 2020; Wilken & Ma, 2004), presented in random order. Clicking or dragging on a wheel displayed a preview of the selected feature value. After responding to all three probes, a play symbol then signaled that the rule-judgment task resumed. Because probes were distributed throughout rule blocks, they sampled working memory at different stages of rule inference. Participants viewed stimuli defined by three continuous dimensions: color, shape, and motion. On each trial, they judged whether the stimulus conformed to a hidden active rule and received binary feedback on their correctness. Intermittent WM probes were placed randomly throughout the task to assess WM representation for all three dimensions of the previously seen stimulus. Each probe was preceded by a pause symbol and followed by a play symbol, signaling that the probe did not change the active rule. Participants responded on feature response wheels to reconstruct the color, shape, and motion of the previous stimulus.

Behavioral measures and statistical approach

To characterize behavior on this task, we aligned trials to the first error following each switch, because this error provided the first evidence that the rule had changed. For reaction time (RT) analyses, we excluded trials with no responses and RTs below 400 ms (totaling 3.0% of trials). To distinguish rule-related slowing from general post-error slowing, we compared RTs on trials following the first error after a switch with RTs on trials following other errors, controlling for trial position. Perceptual evidence on each trial was indexed by the circular distance between each feature value and its nearest category prototype (e.g., the warmest color), ranging from 0° (at the prototype) to 90° (at a category boundary), with larger distances indicating weaker evidence. Error on the working memory probes was defined as the angular difference between the reported and presented feature value, ranging from -180° to 180°.

All statistical analyses used Bayesian mixed-effects models fit in R using the brms package (Bürkner, 2017) with four chains of 6000 iterations each (3000 warmup). Choices were modeled with a Bernoulli likelihood and logit link, RTs with a shifted lognormal likelihood, and absolute working memory errors with a beta likelihood. Each model included the maximal random-effects structure justified by the design (Barr et al., 2013), simplified only when necessary for convergence. Continuous predictors were scaled within participants. Convergence was assessed using the R ̂ statistic for all parameters. We report posterior means, 95% credible intervals (CIs), and evidence ratios (ERs).

Results

Participants tracked the hidden rule

We first asked whether participants could infer the hidden rule from feedback and update their beliefs when it changed. Overall, participants responded correctly on 62.96% of trials (SD = 4.76, mean RT = 1367 ms) and on 71.43% of trials in the final three positions of rule blocks. A Bayesian logistic mixed-effects model (Table S1) showed that accuracy increased with each trial after the first error following a rule switch (b = 0.095, 95% CI [0.079, 0.112], ER > 11999, Figure 2, left axis). RTs showed a complementary pattern (Table S2). They rose sharply on the trial after the first error following a switch (b = 0.214, 95% CI [0.179, 0.251], ER > 11999), and this slowing exceeded that after other errors (b = 0.058, 95% CI [0.015, 0.101], ER = 271.73), indicating that participants reconsidered the active rule when feedback signaled a change rather than merely slowing after an error. RTs then declined as the new rule was learned (b = -0.0052, 95% CI [-0.0072, -0.0032], ER > 11999).

To characterize this inference process more precisely, we fit the rule-inference model to participants' choices and computed model-derived rule certainty as KL divergence. Certainty increased toward the end of rule blocks, as participants accumulated evidence for the new rule (Figure 3). Following rule switches, certainty dropped sharply, reflecting the need to infer the new rule. Because certainty was estimated from choices alone, its relationship with RT, which the model was not fit to, provides an independent test of the measure.

Model-derived certainty tracks the speed of rule judgements

To validate the certainty measure, we tested whether participants responded faster when they were more certain about the rule, and whether judgements became increasingly sensitive to goal-relevant evidence as certainty grew. Therefore, we fit a mixed-effects Bayesian model to predict RTs as a function of rule certainty, trial position, perceptual evidence (distance from prototype) on the active and each inactive dimension, and the interaction of certainty with each evidence measure (Table S3). To ensure that each feature dimension contributed equally to both inactive-evidence regressors, inactive dimensions were assigned using a cyclic ordering, with the first inactive regressor taking the dimension following the active one in this cycle, and the second took the remaining one. Because this assignment is arbitrary, we report inactive-dimension effects as the average of the two regressors' effects, computed across posterior draws.

Participants responded faster later in a rule block (b = -0.0109, 95% CI [-0.0134, -0.0084], ER > 11999), and beyond this effect of trial position, faster when rule certainty was higher (Figure 4A; b = -7.628, 95% CI [-9.462, -5.787], ER > 11999). Directly comparing these effects further confirmed that certainty affected retention differently for active and inactive features (b = 26.59, 95% CI [10.72, 42.65], ER = 3999). Thus, as certainty about which rule was active increased, the estimated probabilities of retaining goal-relevant and irrelevant information increasingly diverged, rising for the former and falling for the latter.

In contrast, there was little evidence that certainty affected how precisely retained information was represented for either active (b = -0.245, 95% CI [-3.73, 3.13], ER = 1.21) or inactive features (b = -0.141, 95% CI [-2.85, 2.51], ER = 1.18). Their contrast likewise provided little evidence that these effects differed between active and inactive dimensions (b = -0.104, 95% CI [-4.44, 4.21], ER = 1.07; Figure 6B). Thus, as participants became more certain about which rule was active, the selectivity of working memory was expressed primarily in which features were retained, rather than in how precisely they were represented.

Together, these analyses show that increasing certainty about which rule was active reshaped both the use and the retention of stimulus information, but in complementary ways. Rule judgments came to rely more on evidence from the active dimension, whereas working memory increasingly lost features from the inactive dimensions.

Discussion

In dynamic environments, people must often learn from experience which information is relevant, rather than relying on explicit instructions. This requires a balance between maintaining broad access to potentially useful information and selectively allocating limited resources to what appears to matter. Although prior work shows that attention becomes more selective during learning (Leong et al., 2017; Niv et al., 2015), it has remained unclear how this adjustment changes how people retain information in working memory. We addressed this question using the CARIT, which combines continuous working memory reports with computational modeling, allowing us to track how memory for relevant and irrelevant features changed alongside participants' beliefs about which rule was active. As model-derived certainty increased, rule judgments came to rely increasingly on evidence from the active dimension. At the same time, working memory became increasingly selective, with memory for inactive features declining relative to memory for active features, suggesting a reorganization of the underlying priority structure. Decomposing report errors showed that this selectivity primarily reflected whether features were retained rather than how precisely they were represented: as certainty grew, features on the relevant dimension became more likely to be retained, and features on irrelevant dimensions less likely. These findings suggest that learning what matters is accompanied by a reorganization of the information maintained in working memory: as people become more certain about what matters, working memory increasingly retains goal-relevant information and drops competing information.

Our findings speak to how learned relevance shapes the content of working memory through the reorganization of attentional selection. Attending to an object brings its taskirrelevant features into memory (Marshall & Bays, 2013), albeit with reduced precision (Shin & Ma, 2016). Cued priority modulates working memory precision in a graded fashion (Bays & Husain, 2008; Klyszejko et al., 2014). Thus, one might expect that learned relevance redistributes resources across features, while leaving them all in memory. Our results instead suggest that learned relevance primarily affects whether features are retained, rather than how precisely they represented. As certainty grew, features on the active dimension became more likely to be retained and features on inactive dimensions less likely, with little evidence for changes in the precision of retained features. This pattern is more consistent with learned relevance acting as a gate on what is retained, similar to the loss of attended but unneeded information observed in attribute amnesia (Chen & Wyble, 2015). It also connects to accounts in which attention makes learning tractable by concentrating processing on a subset of relevant dimensions (Niv et al., 2015; Wilson & Niv, 2011), suggesting that this simplification extends beyond perceptual selection to the information maintained for subsequent use. Here, this means that poorer memory for irrelevant features is useful for task performance, with attentional narrowing constraining which evidence remains accessible for evaluating alternative rules.

We found that during rule judgments, irrelevant information affected judgment speed, yet the same features became increasingly likely to be lost from working memory. Thus, irrelevant information was processed without being retained. Conversely, increased certainty enhanced the use of relevant information but only produced a modest gain in its retention. This suggests that inferred relevance acts in different ways in different processing stages: during judgement, it increases the weight given to relevant evidence, while for storage it also determines whether irrelevant information is maintained at all. A similar dissociation is found in attribute amnesia, where features that were attended and used for a task can nevertheless be lost immediately when they are not anticipated to be needed (Chen & Wyble, 2015). It also has methodological implications. Measures of attention during learning, such as gaze, are often used to infer which information people represent (Leong et al., 2017; Walker et al., 2019). Our results suggest that such measures may overestimate what is subsequently available in memory.

Our results also differ in an informative way from studies in which priority is explicitly cued. When priority is cued before the stimulus is presented, it modulates the precision of working memory representations (Bays & Husain, 2008; Klyszejko et al., 2014). Similarly, when a retained feature in working memory is retro-cued, its representation becomes more precise (Park et al., 2017). In contrast, we found that learned relevance mainly affected whether features were retained, with little evidence for changes in precision. One possibility is that this reflects participants' confidence about the utility of information. In cuing studies, uncued information remains potentially relevant to the upcoming test (if only because researchers need invalid trials to measure the effect of cuing). In the CARIT, irrelevant information can truly be dropped once certainty grows rather than degraded.

The transition from broad to selective maintenance suggests an exploration-exploitation interpretation of working memory prioritization. Evidence from associative learning suggests that uncertainty promotes attentional exploration (Beesley et al., 2015; Walker et al., 2019), which can facilitate learning when previously uninformative cues become predictive (Easdale et al., 2019). Our results extend this account to the information retained during inference, suggesting that exploration may involve preserving multiple sources of evidence in working memory as well as sampling them perceptually. Under uncertainty, information outside the currently favored dimension could help evaluate alternative rules. As certainty increases, retaining that information contributes less to the current judgment. This suggests a reciprocal relationship between learning and working memory: beliefs about relevance shape what is retained, while what is retained shapes the evidence available for subsequent inference. A narrowing of working memory organization therefore comes at a cost when the environment changes, because the evidence needed to detect and learn a new rule is less likely to be available. Several limitations should be noted. First, working memory reports provide an indirect measure of attentional priorities. Differences in memory could arise during encoding, maintenance, or retrieval. The present design cannot distinguish these stages. Future work could combine working memory reports with eye tracking to examine how overt information sampling relates to what is subsequently retained, while varying probe timing to clarify when competing information becomes less available (Vogel et al., 2006). Second, rule certainty was inferred from participants' choices rather than directly measured or manipulated, and its effects on judgment and working memory were correlational. Future work could incorporate confidence reports or manipulate the information available about the active rule, for example by changing feedback informativeness or providing partial rule cues. This would help in assessing how model-derived certainty corresponds to participants' internal estimates across different levels of uncertainty, and how both relate to working memory prioritization.

In conclusion, the present findings suggest that learning what matters is accompanied by a reorganization of the information maintained in working memory. As participants became more certain about which rule was active, memory increasingly favored goalrelevant over competing information, primarily through differences in estimated retention probability. By combining computational estimates of rule beliefs with continuous working memory reports, the CARIT makes it possible to track how attentional priority changes as relevance is learned. More broadly, this approach offers a window into how people align limited cognitive resources with evolving beliefs to support adaptive goal-directed behavior in dynamic environments.

Figure 1. Continuously Assessed Rule Inference Task (CARIT). (A) Three circular dimensions were each divided into two categories, defining six possible rules. (B)Participants viewed stimuli defined by three continuous dimensions: color, shape, and motion. On each trial, they judged whether the stimulus conformed to a hidden active rule and received binary feedback on their correctness. Intermittent WM probes were placed randomly throughout the task to assess WM representation for all three dimensions of the previously seen stimulus. Each probe was preceded by a pause symbol and followed by a play symbol, signaling that the probe did not change the active rule. Participants responded on feature response wheels to reconstruct the color, shape, and motion of the previous stimulus.
