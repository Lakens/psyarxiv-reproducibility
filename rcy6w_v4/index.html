Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Existing adolescent mental health screening instruments are predominantly symptomfocused, lack integration of biological, psychological, and social dimensions, and have limited validation evidence in Eastern European populations. This study describes the development and validation of the Mental Health Screening Tool for Children and Adolescents (MHST-CA), a brief self-report instrument grounded in the biopsychosocial model. Item generation drew on 22 focus groups (N > 200) and interviews with 31 specialists. An initial 132-item pool was reduced through exploratory factor analysis in a pilot sample (N = 237, ages 10-20) and confirmatory factor analysis in a validation sample (N = 496, ages 10-20, 58.1% female). For the final 27 items, the hypothesized correlated three-factor model was not supported (CFI = .88), whereas a bifactor model with a general factor and three biopsychosocial facets fit well (CFI = .92, TLI = .91, RMSEA = .06, SRMR = .05, ωH = .88, ECV = .76) and is proposed post hoc as the measurement model. Internal consistency was high (α = .84-.94) and measurement invariance held across gender and age group. The total score correlated with the DASS-Y (r = .82), CPSS (r = .73) and WEMWBS (r = -.69) and identified elevated posttraumatic stress symptoms (AUC = .90). Controlling for age, gender and the DASS-Y, the Biological and Social subscales added ΔR² = .085 in predicting well-being. The MHST-CA offers a brief, culturally grounded screening tool for Romanian adolescents, best interpreted as a total score. Limitations include reliance on self-report and a geographically restricted sample.

Development and Validation of the Mental Health Screening Tool for Children and Adolescents (MHST-CA)

Mental health (MH) is a vital aspect of overall well-being and a fundamental human right (World Health Organization [WHO], 1948). Mental disorders rank among the leading causes of disability worldwide and their burden is expected to increase in the coming years (WHO, 2022).

This growing public health concern has led to the development of numerous screening instruments for adolescents. Yet, despite this proliferation, no clear consensus has emerged regarding which tools are most appropriate for this population (Jacobs et al., 2023). Three persistent limitations help explain this lack of agreement. First, most instruments remain predominantly symptom-focused and organized around psychiatric categories, with limited structural integration of biological and social determinants. Second, validation evidence in Eastern European adolescent populations remains scarce or inconsistent, raising concerns about cultural equivalence. Third, few brief instruments simultaneously achieve theoretical integration, developmental sensitivity, and practical feasibility for school-based screening. Together, these gaps suggest that simple adaptation of existing measures may not fully address current contextual and structural needs.

The developmental period from late childhood to adolescence represents a critical window for the emergence of mental health difficulties. Epidemiological evidence shows that approximately half of all lifetime mental disorders begin by the age of 14, and three-quarters by age 24 (Kessler et al., 2005; Jones, 2013). This finding highlights the need for early screening that begins before mid-adolescence. The World Health Organization defines adolescence as spanning ages 10-19 years (WHO, 2022), emphasizing a continuum of biological, cognitive, and social changes that begin in late childhood and extend through the teenage years (Sawyer et al., 2012). Consequently, including both children and adolescents in the present study aligns with developmental and public-health recommendations to address prevention and early detection within this full age range. In Romania and other Eastern European countries, UNICEF reports a persistent lack of standardized, age-appropriate tools for assessing emotional and behavioral difficulties among youth, particularly in school contexts (UNICEF Romania, 2023). Developing a culturally adapted screening tool that covers the 10-19 age range thus addresses both developmental continuity and a critical service gap in the region.

Beyond these epidemiological concerns, mental health is inherently complex and multidimensional, involving dynamic interactions between biological, psychological, and social factors (Engel, 1977). The proliferation of psychological assessment tools is inevitable and necessary, reflecting the continuous refinement of theoretical concepts and understanding of MH to better capture its complexity, while distinct instruments may capture different nuances and dimensions of the same overarching construct (Iliescu et al., 2024).

A systematic review of adolescent mental health assessment tools identifies at least 60 distinct instruments, yet there is no clear consensus on which should be recommended (Jacobs et al., 2023). Among these, only nine are endorsed by more than one source, reflecting a fragmented landscape of assessment tools. Additionally, reviews consistently highlight critical issues related to psychometric properties, validation methodologies, and reporting standards (Bentley et al., 2019; Jacobs et al., 2023). Notably, most recommended instruments remain symptom-focused-targeting anxiety, depression, suicide, and self-harm-rather than providing a comprehensive, holistic perspective on adolescent mental health (Jacobs et al., 2023).

Evidence on Cultural Transferability of Existing Measures

Although several adolescent mental-health measures are widely used across Europe, published evidence indicates cross-country variability in their psychometric performance, including in Eastern European contexts. For example, large multi-country studies of the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) have struggled to confirm a common, invariant measurement model for adolescent self-report across nations, prompting the development of a revised version (SDQ-R) for cross-cultural use (Duinhof et al., 2019). In addition, cross-national analyses have reported substantial differences in internal consistency across seven European countries-Romania among them-suggesting that measurement equivalence cannot be assumed (Husky et al., 2020). Romanian evidence based on the self-report SDQ further highlights the need for local adaptation: Sharratt et al. (2014) examined dimensionality and construct validity and identified alternative factor structures to the original five-factor model and poor fit for the five-factor itself.

In contrast, for two of the most frequently used self-report instruments, the Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-Being Scale (WEMWBS) and the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), no peer-reviewed validation studies were identified for adolescent samples in Eastern Europe. A recent systematic review of the WEMWBS found considerable variation in its psychometric properties across cultural and linguistic contexts and highlighted a lack of evidence from Eastern European populations (Perera et al., 2025). Similarly, a systematic review of the PHQ-9 concluded that, although the measure demonstrates strong reliability and validity overall, cross-cultural adaptations frequently require modifications to maintain construct equivalence, and validation data remain scarce for Eastern European settings (Carroll et al., 2020). Together, these findings emphasize that even well-established international measures cannot be assumed to generalize across cultural contexts, reinforcing the need to develop and validate locally appropriate screening instruments.

Beyond psychometric considerations, Eastern European countries present distinctive systemic and sociocultural characteristics that further justify the development of a culturally grounded mental-health screening tool for youth. Post Soviet legacies of centralized and hospital-centric mental-health systems, chronic underfunding, and the slow implementation of community-based care have produced significant service disparities (Krupchanka & Winkler, 2016). School-based psychological services remain fragmented, and mental-health stigma continues to shape help-seeking behaviors among adolescents and their families, as evidenced in Romanian young-adult samples (Manescu et al., 2023). Moreover, economic instability, ruralurban inequities, and regional migration and refugee dynamics have amplified psychosocial stressors among children and adolescents (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development [OECD], 2018; UNICEF Romania, 2023). UNICEF reports further document the absence of consistent early-screening mechanisms in national and local systems. Together, these systemic and cultural features underscore the importance of creating assessment tools that capture locally relevant expressions of distress and resilience-dimensions that may be overlooked or misinterpreted by Western-developed instruments.

The Biopsychosocial Model

The present study adopts a theoretically integrative framework grounded in the biopsychosocial model (Engel, 1977) rather than a single diagnostic theory. This approach is not atheoretical. Rather, it deliberately draws on an overarching, domain-spanning framework that integrates biological, psychological, and social determinants of mental health without privileging any single theoretical tradition. Unlike models that are either overly specific or excessively contextualized, the biopsychosocial model fills an important gap in the literature by offering a comprehensive, integrative perspective on mental health. It captures the dynamic interactions among three core dimensions: biological (genetic, neuroanatomical, neurophysiological aspects, and lifestyle factors), psychological (cognitive, emotional, and behavioral processes), and social (relationships with family, peers, community, societal norms, and cultural identity). This model stands out for its holistic, person-centered approach, allowing for personalized treatment and emphasizing both risk and resilience factors, making prevention a key focus (Wade & Halligan, 2017). Recognized as the gold standard for mental health assessment (Alvarez et al., 2012), it is widely used in research and practice (Van de Velde et al., 2016) and informs international guidelines for assessment, prevention, and treatment (World Health Organization, 2021).

Despite its advantages, many existing tools fail to fully implement the biopsychosocial model or are impractical for widespread use. Some instruments, such as INTERMED, require interviews and resource-intensive procedures, limiting their applicability. Others, like PHQ, focus primarily on symptom-based assessment rather than broader contextual factors.

Additionally, widely used tools (e.g., SDQ, WEMWBS) were not originally designed to cover several contemporary or context-specific challenges, which may limit local fit. The proposed instrument seeks to bridge these gaps by fully integrating the biopsychosocial model while addressing modern adolescent experiences, offering a more nuanced and up-to-date mental health assessment.

A key debate in adolescent well-being and mental health assessment revolves around the balance between objective and subjective indicators. While child-centered assessments prioritize subjective experience, lifestyle factors such as sleep, nutrition, and chronic illness significantly impact well-being (Hosker et al., 2019). These factors also serve as indirect measures of living standards (Gautam et al., 2023), bridging subjective and objective dimensions. This underscores the need to integrate biological components into any adolescent mental health assessment tool.

No mental health model can disregard the psychological dimension, which remains the focus of most assessment tools. A central debate here contrasts deficit-based approaches with strength-based models that emphasize resilience (Davis et al., 2018; Mierau et al., 2020).

Effective tools should not only assess distress but also capture coping mechanisms and emotional regulation strategies. Additionally, the bidirectional impact of digital media on well-being is critical-while young people use it for relaxation, support, and coping, excessive use can heighten distress (Purba et al., 2023; Shankleman et al., 2021). Given its pervasive role, evaluating internet use should be integral to mental health assessments.

The social dimension is equally crucial, particularly during formative years. Research highlights the importance of strong connections with parents, peers, and the community, as well as a sense of belonging within cultural and social frameworks (Blum et al., 2022). Conversely, loneliness and isolation increase the risk of adolescent depression (Dunn & Sicouri, 2022), while positive social relationships enhance well-being, life satisfaction, and long-term academic and professional success (Caspi et al., 2005). Therefore, prioritizing the social aspect in adolescent mental health assessment tools is essential.

A persistent issue in the literature is the tendency to prioritize theoretical frameworks over practical applicability in scale development. Many tools are based on abstract models while overlooking input from key stakeholders-experts, practitioners, caregivers, and, crucially, adolescents themselves (Davis et al., 2018; Jacobs et al., 2023; Krause et al., 2021). Recently, a rights-based approach has gained traction, advocating for children's active participation in decisions regarding their mental health assessments (Cho & Yu, 2020; Davis et al., 2018).

However, many self-report tools for adolescents were originally designed for adults and later adapted, contradicting this child-centered perspective. Thus, best practices suggest directly incorporating adolescents' feedback, alongside input from parents, teachers, and psychologists, when developing and implementing assessment tools.

A key challenge in adolescent mental health assessment is balancing the depth of measurement with the length of the instrument (Chorpita et al., 2010; Deighton et al., 2014).

Lengthy self-assessments can reduce time for intervention in clinical settings and burden adolescents when administered between sessions, reinforcing the need for shorter tools. Beyond theoretical concerns, practical usability remains an issue. The literature calls for instruments that cover a wide age range and diverse symptoms, as brief scales often target specific diagnoses or problem areas (Deighton et al., 2014; Jacobs et al., 2023).

The abundance of mental health assessment tools has led to reluctance in developing new ones. Many studies argue for refining existing instruments rather than creating new ones, given the lack of consensus on the most effective tools. However, no current screening tool simultaneously (a) integrates the biopsychosocial model, (b) remains brief, and (c) addresses the evolving challenges adolescents face. Mental health disorders vary significantly over time and across sociopolitical contexts, making existing tools insufficient (Iliescu et al., 2024).

Developing new scales is crucial for accurately measuring prevalence, informing public policy, and ensuring effective resource allocation for adolescent mental health interventions.

To bridge these limitations, we developed a MH screening tool grounded in the goldstandard biopsychosocial model, ensuring a holistic approach to MH assessment. Its development was guided by community focus groups that included adolescents, their parents, and teachers, ensuring alignment with their current challenges. Furthermore, in line with practitioners' needs, the proposed tool targets a wide age range and has a relatively small number of items, ensuring ease of use and efficiency in practice. Rather than adapting an existing symptom-based instrument, the MHST-CA was conceptualized from the outset to structurally integrate biological, psychological, and social domains within a unified framework. Many widely used measures focus primarily on symptom severity or global well-being, preserving their original construct architecture even after translation. By contrast, the present instrument embeds lifestyle factors and social-contextual determinants directly within the measurement model, allowing stakeholder-informed and culturally grounded content to shape the construct itself rather than be retrofitted into a pre-existing structure.

Thus, the present study aimed to develop and validate the Mental Health Screening Tool for Children and Adolescents (MHST-CA), a brief measure whose content is grounded in the biopsychosocial model. Following the biopsychosocial model on which the instrument was built, we hypothesized a correlated three-factor structure (Psychological, Biological, Social) and tested it against single-factor and exploratory alternatives. Because it did not fit adequately, we subsequently examined a bifactor model, which retains the three domains as specific facets while allowing a general mental health dimension. This model was not hypothesized in advance and is reported as a post hoc proposal, cross-validated on random split-halves. We further evaluated internal consistency, measurement invariance across gender and age, concurrent and discriminant validity, and screening accuracy in a Romanian youth sample aged 10-20 years. Because the distinctive feature of the MHST-CA is its biological and social content, we also tested its incremental validity directly: we expected the Biological and Social subscales to explain variance in well-being beyond age, gender and a symptom-focused measure, the DASS-Y.

Methods

The study employed both qualitative and quantitative methodological approaches, utilizing three data collection techniques: focus groups, semi-structured interviews, and a questionnaire-based survey. In the qualitative phase, information was gathered through focus groups and semi-structured interviews. Subsequently, during the quantitative phase, a questionnaire was administered. This questionnaire was developed from a comprehensive review of the scientific literature and the findings from the formative research phase. Simultaneously, additional mental health assessment scales were applied to validate the screening instrument.

Data Collection Techniques

The first data collection method, focus groups, was conducted by the Romanian Angel Appeal Foundation (RAA) in collaboration with the Institute for the Study and Treatment of Trauma (ISTT) and under the supervision of the authors of this study. This phase involved over 200 participants distributed across 22 groups, including Romanian and Ukrainian adolescents, adolescents from vulnerable groups, Romanian and Ukrainian parents, Romanian teachers and school counselors, and Ukrainian teachers.

The second technique, semi-structured interviews, was aimed at identifying relevant variables with the help of 31 specialists from various fields related to the mental health of children and adolescents (e.g., school managers, school doctors and nurses, family doctors, psychiatrists, psychotherapists, NGO representatives, and representatives of public institutions with roles in decision-making or regulation in education and mental health). All qualitative data were collected between October 1, 2023, and January 31, 2024.

Romanian and Ukrainian adolescents were included in the qualitative phase to ensure broad content relevance during item generation. Quantitative validation analyses were conducted exclusively on Romanian adolescents, as the MHST-CA was developed and validated in the Romanian language and cultural context.

Development of the Screening Instrument

The development process began with the conceptualization of the screening tool. The MHST-CA was conceptualized as a mental health screening instrument for adolescents and late adolescents/young adults aged 10-20 years. Although the World Health Organization defines adolescence as ages 10-19, this upper age limit was extended to 20 years to reflect developmental continuity in mental health symptomatology and the educational context in Romania, where individuals aged 19-20 may still be enrolled in secondary education. This approach is consistent with the intended use of the MHST-CA as a brief screening tool applicable in school and community settings.

This was collaboratively designed by a panel of three experts who evaluated the information extracted from focus group discussions, based on the biopsychosocial model.

Initially, a working group of three mental health specialists with PhDs in psychology was formed to select the essential items from the focus group discussions. These specialists analyzed the content of the discussions, identifying key words and phrases from the biological, psychological, and social perspectives. They then formulated a preliminary set of statements based on these key terms.

The qualitative data obtained from focus groups and semi-structured interviews were subjected to thematic content analysis. Three psychologists independently reviewed the transcripts and performed open coding to identify recurring themes and concepts within the biological, psychological, and social domains. Codes were compared and discussed until consensus was reached. Themes that were conceptually overlapping were merged, while unique themes were retained to ensure comprehensive coverage of the biopsychosocial framework.

During item drafting, conceptually redundant statements were identified and either eliminated or rephrased to reduce overlap while maintaining construct breadth. Particular attention was given to avoiding semantic duplication and ensuring age-appropriate clarity of wording.

The same expert panel developed a set of items based on the biopsychosocial concept, understood in the context of children's and adolescents' mental health. This process led to the creation of a set of statements ensuring that the three components of the biopsychosocial model were adequately reflected. The result of this comprehensive process was an initial instrument consisting of 89 items derived from focus group analysis and a set of 43 items created by the expert panel. Combining these two sets led to a robust instrument composed of 132 items (see ESM Table S1).

The initial pool of 132 items underwent preliminary qualitative review for clarity and conceptual alignment, followed by exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses in the pilot sample. Item reduction was guided by multiple criteria, including factor loadings, cross-loadings, conceptual relevance to the biopsychosocial dimensions, clarity for the target age group, and overall model fit indices. Items with factor loadings below .40 were removed, resulting in a 32item version. A second confirmatory factor analysis led to the removal of five additional items, yielding the final 27-item version of the MHST-CA (see ESM Table S2). Pilot Application of MHST-CA

The next step involved the application of the mental health screening tool for children and adolescents (MHST-CA), composed of 132 items. The initial sample consisted of 281 Romanian participants. Participants older than 20 were excluded to ensure alignment with the intended target age range of the MHST-CA. The final sample consisted of 237 participants aged 10-20 years (M age = 17.44, SD age = 2.81). Of these, 68.80% were girls (n = 163), 31.20% were boys (n = 74). Participants were recruited from secondary schools and community organizations across Romania. Data collection occurred online from March to April 2024, using a questionnaire created in QuestionPro. The study authors administered the questionnaire in schools, clinics, and organizations across Romania. It should be noted that no Ukrainian participants were included in either the pilot or validation phase, as no responses were obtained from this group.

The obtained sample enabled both exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses to test and investigate the instrument's structure. Given the practical need for a brief, feasible screening tool suitable for children and adolescents, the goal was to develop a shortened version of the MHST-CA. Item selection was guided by factor loadings (threshold ≥ .40), theoretical relevance to the biopsychosocial framework, and item clarity for the target age group. This process yielded 32 items structured across three factors: biological, psychological, and social, and was applied to the validation sample, alongside several mental health scales.

Selection of Participants for the Validation Study

For the validation phase of the MHST-CA instrument in this study, 496 Romanian participants aged between 10 and 20 years (M age = 15.10, SD age = 2.17) participated, with 15 participants not reporting their age. Of these, 37.90% were boys (n = 188), 58.10% were girls (n = 288), and 4.0% preferred not to answer (n = 20). The inclusion criteria were children and young people aged between 10 and 20 years. Data were collected by administering the questionnaire in its online version to students from schools in Bucharest.

In line with the joint standards for psychological testing (American Educational Research Association, American Psychological Association, & National Council on Measurement in Education, 2014), the present study seeks evidence from confirmatory factor analysis (construct validity) and from the relationships between the MHST-CA and other established wellbeing measures (concurrent and discriminant validity).

Inclusion criteria for the validation study were: (a) age between 10 and 20 years and (b) sufficient Romanian language proficiency to complete the self-report measures. Participants were recruited primarily through Romanian schools. No exclusion criteria were applied regarding diagnosed psychiatric disorders, physical health status, or prior mental health treatment, as the MHST-CA is intended to function as a screening instrument in both clinical and non-clinical populations.

Mental Health Scales Used in the Validation Study

The Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale for Youth and Adolescents (DASS-Y; Szabo & Lovibond, 2022) is a short adaptation of the DASS-42 scale for adults. It comprises 21 statements, equally distributed across three dimensions: depression (seven items), anxiety (seven items), and stress (seven items). Participants are asked to rate each statement using a four-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (does not apply to me at all) to 3 (applies to me a lot, almost always). Examples of items for the depression dimension include "I despised my existence", "I perceived my life as depressing" and "I found no pleasure in anything". The anxiety dimension includes items such as "My hands were trembling," "My heart was pounding, without engaging in physical activity," and "I felt panicked", and the stress dimension items such as "I felt overwhelmed by multiple concerns," "I had difficulty relaxing," and "I got irritated easily".

The Child PTSD Symptom Scale for DSM-5, self-report version (CPSS-SR-5; Foa et al., 2018), is an adaptation of the original instrument (Foa et al., 2001) designed to assess the specific manifestations of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in children and adolescents. The instrument contains 20 statements that measure PTSD symptoms, using a five-point Likert scale to assess frequency and severity, with values ranging from 0 (not at all) to 4 (six or more times a week/severe). Additionally, it includes seven items aimed at evaluating the individual's functionality, with yes/no responses. The total severity score is calculated by summing up the 20 symptom-related items. The symptom items are preceded by a trauma screen listing 15 types of potentially traumatic events (e.g., serious accident, physical or sexual assault, violent death of a family member or close person, war), to which a "none of these events" option was added.

Participants who endorse an event answer the symptom items with reference to the event that bothers them most. In the present sample, 60.7% of participants (n = 301) endorsed at least one event. The scale demonstrates excellent internal consistency in evaluating total symptom severity (Cronbach's alpha = .92) and satisfactory test-retest reliability (r = .80). Moreover, CPSS-SR-5 has convergent validity with the Child PTSD Symptom Scale for DSM-5 Interview (CPSS-I-5, r = .90) and discriminant validity with the Multidimensional Anxiety Scale for Children (MASC; March et al., 1997) and the Children's Depression Inventory (CDI; Kovacs, 2015). A score of 31 points (on the 0-80 scoring) is indicated as a threshold for probable PTSD in trauma-exposed children and adolescents (Foa et al., 2018). Example items include: "Unexpectedly experiencing disturbing thoughts or images related to the event," "Having unpleasant dreams or nightmares," and "Experiencing the sensation of reliving the event (images, sounds, or sensations that make you think the event is happening again)."

The Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-Being Scale (WEMWBS; Tennant et al., 2007) is a short instrument designed to assess mental well-being, comprising 14 statements such as: "I felt optimistic about the future," "I felt relaxed," "I had enough energy." Participants are asked to rate each statement using a frequency-based Likert scale ranging from 1 ("Never") to 5 ("Always"). Total scores range from 14 to 70, and a score of 40 or below has been proposed as indicative of probable depression in adult samples (Bianco, 2012; Warwick Medical School, 2023). The scale has been translated into Romanian by the team at the Laboratory for the Evaluation of Interindividual Differences at the University of Bucharest.

Data Analysis

All analyses were conducted in R (Version 4.6.1; R Core Team, 2026) using the lavaan (Version 0.7-3; Rosseel, 2012), semTools (Version 0.5-10; Jorgensen et al., 2022), psych (Version 2.6.9; Revelle, 2026) and pROC (Version 1.19.1; Robin et al., 2011) packages.

Exploratory factor analysis in the pilot phase used oblique (oblimin) rotation to allow intercorrelation among the biopsychosocial dimensions. In the validation sample, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) with maximum likelihood estimation was used to test the hypothesized three-factor model and to compare it with three alternative measurement models: a single-factor model, a three-factor exploratory structural equation model (ESEM; Asparouhov & Muthén, 2009) with oblimin rotation, and a bifactor model with a general factor and three orthogonal specific factors. The bifactor model was not part of the original analysis plan: it was added after the correlated three-factor model showed inadequate fit and high inter-factor correlations, and it was preferred to post hoc respecification of the three-factor model because it retains the three biopsychosocial domains while modeling the shared general dimension that the high correlations indicated. Its fit was therefore cross-validated on split-halves not used for item selection (see below).

Model fit was evaluated with the comparative fit index (CFI), the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) with its 90% confidence interval, and the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). CFI and TLI values of .90 or above together with RMSEA and SRMR values of .08 or below were taken to indicate acceptable fit, and CFI and TLI of .95 or above with RMSEA and SRMR of .06 or below good fit (Hu & Bentler, 1999; Kline, 2016; MacCallum et al., 1996). Nested models were compared with chisquare difference tests and with the Bayesian information criterion (BIC). For the bifactor model we computed hierarchical omega (ωH), the explained common variance (ECV), the percentage of uncontaminated correlations (PUC) and item-level ECV (Rodriguez et al., 2016) to evaluate whether the total score and the subscale scores are interpretable.

Because items were selected on the validation sample, the stability of item selection and the fit of the final model were assessed by random split-half cross-validation: in each of 200 random partitions, items were selected on one half (standardized loading < .40 in the 32-item model) and the final model was estimated on the other half. Measurement invariance of the three-factor model and of the bifactor model across gender and age group (10-14 vs. 15-20 years) was tested with multi-group CFA (configural, metric and scalar models), using ΔCFI ≤ .010 and ΔRMSEA ≤ .015 as criteria for invariance (Chen, 2007). Internal consistency was estimated with Cronbach's α and McDonald's ω, with 95% percentile bootstrap confidence intervals (1,000 resamples). Discriminant validity among the subscales was examined with the average variance extracted (AVE) and the Fornell-Larcker criterion (Fornell & Larcker, 1981) and with the heterotrait-monotrait ratio of correlations (HTMT; Henseler et al., 2015). Concurrent validity was assessed with Pearson correlations between the MHST-CA and the DASS-Y, CPSS-SR-5 and WEMWBS (95% confidence intervals from the Fisher r-to-z transformation), and incremental validity with hierarchical regressions predicting well-being (WEMWBS) and posttraumatic stress symptoms (CPSS-SR-5). Age and gender (0 = boys,1 = girls) were entered at the first step, the DASS-Y total score at the second, and the MHST-CA Biological and Social subscales at the third, so that the change in R² at the third step estimates what the somatic and relational content of the MHST-CA adds beyond a symptom-focused measure. In a stricter version, the MHST-CA Psychological subscale was entered before the Biological and Social subscales. As sensitivity analyses, the DASS-Y subscales were entered instead of the total score and the models were re-estimated without covariates in the full sample. Finally, the screening accuracy of the MHST-CA total score was examined with receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis against two criteria: clinically elevated posttraumatic stress symptoms, defined as a CPSS-SR-5 score ≥ 31 on the 0-80 scoring, the threshold proposed for probable PTSD in trauma-exposed youth (Foa et al., 2018), and low well-being, defined as a WEMWBS score ≤ 40, the adult-derived threshold for probable depression benchmarked against the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (Bianco, 2012; Warwick Medical School, 2023), and, as an age-appropriate alternative, as a score at least one standard deviation below the sample mean (≤ 33). Because the PTSD threshold presupposes trauma exposure, the CPSS-SR-5 analysis was repeated in the subsample that endorsed at least one event on the trauma screen. We report the area under the curve (AUC) with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (1,000 resamples), the cut-off maximizing the Youden index in the full sample, and the sensitivity, specificity and predictive values of this single cut-off against each criterion and within gender groups. Gender-specific Youden cut-offs are reported for comparison only. For these analyses the DASS-Y and CPSS-SR-5 items, which had been administered with 1-4 and 1-5 response options, were rescored to the original 0-3 and 0-4 metrics.

There were no missing responses on the MHST-CA items. Fifteen participants did not report their age and were excluded only from the age-group invariance analyses, so no imputation was required (Schafer & Graham, 2002). Regarding sample size adequacy for the CFA, with 27 observed indicators and three latent factors the model involved 87 free parameters, yielding a cases-to-parameters ratio of approximately 5.7:1, which is below the 10 cases per free parameter rule of thumb (Kline, 2016) but above the 5:1 minimum commonly considered acceptable for CFA (Bentler & Chou, 1987). Item distributions were approximately normal (all | skewness| ≤ 1.11 and |kurtosis| ≤ 1.22), within the bounds under which maximum likelihood estimation performs adequately (Finney & DiStefano, 2006).

Results

Construct Validity

The hypothesized three-factor model was first estimated with the initial 32 items (N = 496). Fit was below conventional thresholds, χ²(461) = 1629.11, CFI = .84, TLI = .82, RMSEA = .071, 90% CI [.068, .075], SRMR = .061. Five items had standardized loadings below .40, namely items 22 (λ = .18, "I have different methods and strategies to cope with stress or negative thoughts"), 23 (λ = .38, "I usually spend more than 3 hours on my phone every day"), 25 (λ = .20, "I feel that I belong to a community"), 26 (λ = .09, "I spend a lot of time online with my friends"), and 32 (λ = .35, "Most of the time, I eat fast food or street food"), and were removed following the .40 criterion (Hair et al., 2019), yielding the 27-item version. The full 32-item solution is reported in ESM Table S3. The item selection proved stable under split-half cross-validation: across 200 random partitions, items 22, 25 and 26 fell below .40 in the selection half in 100% of replications, items 32 and 23 in 88% and 78%, and item 24 in 34%. No other item did so in more than 4% of replications.

Confirmatory factor analysis was used again to test the 27-item version, comparing four measurement models (Table 1). The correlated three-factor model fit the data only marginally, χ²(321) = 1124.32, CFI = .88, TLI = .87, RMSEA = .071, 90% CI [.067, .076], SRMR = .052, and the latent correlations among the factors were high (Psychological-Biological r = .87, Psychological-Social r = .81, Biological-Social r = .78). A single-factor model fit worse, χ²(324) = 1558.86, CFI = .81, Δχ²(3) = 434.54, p < .001. The three-factor ESEM improved fit (CFI = .92, TLI = .89, RMSEA = .063, SRMR = .039) but did not recover the hypothesized structure: the Psychological and Biological items loaded together on a single factor (λ = .36-.79, except items 19 and 21 with λ < .30), the Social items split into a peer-relations factor comprising items 12, 13, 14, 16 and 27 (λ = .36-.82) and a parent-relations factor comprising items 15 and 17 (both λ = .86), and items 19, 21 and 24 had no salient loading on any factor (all λ < .30, see ESM Table S4). The bifactor model fit best, χ²(297) = 825.87, CFI = .92, TLI = .91, RMSEA = .060, 90% CI [.055, .065], SRMR = .050, and had the lowest BIC. Its improvement over the correlated threefactor model was significant, Δχ²(24) = 298.45, p < .001. The hypothesized three-factor structure was therefore not supported, and the bifactor model was retained as the measurement model of the MHST-CA.

Measurement Invariance

Measurement invariance of the three-factor model was examined across gender (188 boys and 288 girls, after excluding 20 participants who preferred not to indicate their gender) and age group (10-14 years, n = 187, and 15-20 years, n = 294) with configural, metric and scalar multigroup models (Table 3). Metric and scalar invariance were supported across gender (ΔCFI = .000 and -.008, ΔRMSEA = -.001 and .001) and across age group (ΔCFI = -.002 and -.005, ΔRMSEA = -.001 and .000). The chi-square difference tests were significant for the scalar models, as is common at this sample size, but all changes remained within the recommended bounds (Chen, 2007). Latent mean comparisons under the scalar models showed substantially higher scores among girls than boys on all three factors (standardized differences of 0.89, 0.83 and 0.55 for the Psychological, Biological and Social factors, respectively) and small differences between age groups (-0.02, -0.05 and -0.27). The same analyses for the bifactor model (ESM Table S5) supported metric and scalar invariance across gender (ΔCFI = -.003 and -.004, ΔRMSEA = -.002 and .000) and age group (ΔCFI = -.006 and -.003, ΔRMSEA = -.001 and .000), with girls scoring higher than boys on the general factor (standardized difference = 0.50) and the older group scoring slightly lower than the younger group (-0.29).

Concurrent and Incremental Validity

In this study, the correlations between MHST-CA and its subscales (Psychological, Biological, Social) are moderate to high, ranging from .67 to .94 (see Table 5). Additionally, the correlations between MHST-CA and DASS are high, ranging from .71 to .82, suggesting that MHST-CA has good concurrent validity in relation to DASS. The correlations between the MHST-CA subscales and the DASS subscales are varied, with the highest being between MHST-CA Psychological and DASS Depression (r = .69), and MHST-CA Biological and DASS Anxiety (r = .72). MHST-CA has a moderate to high correlation with CPSS (r = .73), which also suggests good concurrent validity in this case. The correlations between MHST-CA and WEMWBS are negative, ranging from -.62 to -.69, which is expected given that WEMWBS measures mental well-being, in contrast to the other scales that measure aspects of stress, anxiety, and depression.

Discriminant Validity

To assess discriminant validity, the latent inter-factor correlations among the three MHST-CA subscales (Biological, Psychological, Social) were examined together with the average variance extracted (AVE) and the heterotrait-monotrait ratio (HTMT), as reported in Table 4. Discriminant validity among the three subscales was not supported: the square root of the AVE (.66, .64 and .69 for the Psychological, Biological and Social factors) was lower than every latent inter-factor correlation (.78-.87), so the Fornell-Larcker criterion was not met for any pair of factors, and HTMT values were .89 (Psychological-Biological), .84 (Psychological-Social) and .79 (Biological-Social), with the first exceeding the conservative .85 threshold (Henseler et al., 2015). Notably, the correlations between the MHST-CA Psychological subscale and the DASS Depression subscale (r = .69) and the MHST-CA Biological subscale and the DASS Anxiety subscale (r = .72) reflect strong but not redundant overlap, a pattern consistent with convergent validity. Together with the bifactor results, these findings indicate that the three subscales are empirically related facets of a general dimension of mental health problems rather than distinct constructs. The negative correlations of all MHST-CA scores with WEMWBS (r = -.62 to -.69) reflect the expected inverse association between mental health problems and wellbeing rather than discriminant evidence.

Screening Accuracy

Because the MHST-CA is intended as a screening instrument, the accuracy of the total score (possible range 27-135, M = 73.8, SD = 22.2) was examined against the available criterion thresholds (Table 7). Against clinically elevated posttraumatic stress symptoms (CPSS-SR-5 ≥ 31, 34.7% of the sample), the AUC was .90, 95% CI [.87, .93], and the cut-off maximizing the Youden index (total ≥ 74) yielded sensitivity = .91, specificity = .74, positive predictive value = .65 and negative predictive value = .94. In the subsample that endorsed at least one traumatic event (n = 301, of whom 46.5% were above the threshold), the AUC was .87 [.83, .91], and the same cut-off retained sensitivity = .91 with specificity = .65. Accuracy was comparable against low well-being, whether defined by the adult-derived WEMWBS threshold (≤ 40, AUC = .86 [.83, .90]) or by scores at least one standard deviation below the sample mean (≤ 33, AUC = .83 [.77, .88]). At the ≥ 74 cut-off, sensitivity was .86 and .88 and negative predictive value .91 and .96, respectively, with lower specificity (.69 and .59). Within gender groups, the AUC was .87 [.79, .93] for boys and .89 [.85, .93] for girls, and the common cut-off performed similarly to the gender-specific Youden cut-offs: in boys, ≥ 74 gave sensitivity = .74 and specificity = .84, virtually identical to the group-specific cut-off of ≥ 78 (.74 and .85). In girls, ≥ 74 gave sensitivity = .94 and specificity = .65, whereas the group-specific cut-off of ≥ 82 traded sensitivity for specificity (.81 and .84). The proportion flagged by the common cut-off (25.5% of boys and 61.1% of girls) mirrored the prevalence of the criterion in each group (16.5% and 44.1%).

Discussion

The development of the MHST-CA was motivated by the urgent need for a comprehensive and culturally appropriate instrument for assessing mental health in children and adolescents in Romania. The instrument was built on the biopsychosocial model, and we hypothesized that its items would be organized into three correlated dimensions: biological, psychological, and social. This hypothesis was not supported. The correlated three-factor model fit the data marginally and did not improve on held-out data. The latent correlations among the three factors were high (.78-.87), and the subscales did not meet conventional criteria for discriminant validity. A bifactor model, examined post hoc, in which all items load on a general mental health factor and the three domains are retained as specific facets, fit well, replicated across random split-halves, was invariant across gender and age, and is therefore proposed as the measurement model of the MHST-CA, pending confirmation in an independent sample. The general factor accounted for most of the reliable variance (ωH = .88, ECV = .76), so the total score is the primary interpretable score, while the three facets describe the biopsychosocial content of the instrument rather than separable constructs. The high values of Cronbach's α and McDonald's ω coefficients indicate excellent internal consistency of the total score, and measurement invariance across gender and age group indicates that the items function equivalently in the groups for which the instrument is intended.

The exploratory structural equation model clarified where the hypothesized structure breaks down. Psychological and somatic items loaded on a single distress factor, consistent with the strong covariation of emotional and somatic complaints in adolescence, whereas the social items separated into peer-related and parent-related difficulties. Four items originally assigned to the Psychological facet (concentration, anhedonia, premature maturation, and reliance on the internet) and the item on economically motivated bullying behaved as indicators of the general factor only. These findings do not undermine the biopsychosocial model as a framework for generating content, which ensured that somatic and relational experiences were represented in a brief screener, but they caution against reporting and interpreting the three facets as independent scores. Concurrent validity was confirmed through significant correlations with established instruments such as the DASS-Y, CPSS, and WEMWBS. The relatively strong correlations with the DASS-Y (.71 to .82) may suggest overlap. Nonetheless, this pattern is theoretically expected given shared psychological distress components, and the incremental validity analyses locate the added value of the MHST-CA precisely in the content that symptom-focused measures do not sample. With age, gender and the DASS-Y controlled, the Biological and Social subscales explained an additional 8.5% of the variance in well-being, and they continued to do so after the Psychological subscale was also controlled, whereas the Psychological subscale itself added little beyond the DASS-Y and was not a unique predictor once the other two were included. The somatic complaints, health behaviors, and family and peer relationships represented in the MHST-CA therefore carry information about adolescent well-being that a symptom measure does not capture. The increment was smaller for posttraumatic stress symptoms, a criterion that is itself symptom-based. This result does not conflict with the bifactor findings: the Biological and Social subscale scores are largely saturated by the general factor, and their incremental contribution reflects the content through which the general dimension is expressed rather than the existence of separable constructs, so that, in practice, the total score remains the primary score and the added value of the MHST-CA over a symptom measure lies in what that score covers. Thus, the instrument should be viewed as complementary rather than redundant, capturing broader determinants of adolescent mental health within a single brief measure.

The screening accuracy of the total score supports its intended use. Against clinically elevated posttraumatic stress symptoms on the CPSS-SR-5, the area under the curve was .90, and a cut-off of 74 identified 91% of adolescents above the criterion with a negative predictive value of .94, a profile suited to first-stage school screening, in which missed cases are more costly than false positives. The result held in the trauma-exposed subsample and against low well-being, whether the latter was defined by the adult-derived WEMWBS threshold or relative to the sample distribution. Although girls obtained substantially higher latent means than boys on all facets, we do not recommend gender-specific cut-offs at this stage: scalar invariance indicates that the higher scores of girls reflect higher levels of the construct rather than measurement bias, the common cut-off performed as well as the gender-specific Youden cut-off in boys and more sensitively in girls, and the larger proportion of girls flagged mirrored the higher prevalence of the criterion among girls. The gender-specific cut-offs are therefore reported as exploratory, and all cut-offs are provisional until confirmed against clinical diagnoses.

Theoretical and Practical Implications

The MHST-CA represents an important step forward in improving mental health assessment and interventions for children and adolescents in Romania. The use of this instrument can facilitate the early identification of mental health issues, enabling faster and more effective interventions. Because its biological and social content explained well-being beyond a symptom measure, the MHST-CA may also flag adolescents whose difficulties are expressed through somatic complaints, health behaviors, or strained relationships rather than through emotional symptoms, and who could be missed by symptom-focused screening. Data collected through the administration of the MHST-CA can contribute to the formulation of more informed public policies and the more efficient allocation of resources.

Furthermore, the results of this study provide a significant contribution to the literature by validating a tool specific to the Romanian context. The results obtained with the MHST-CA highlight the importance of an instrument tailored to the Romanian cultural and social context. This is crucial as most available instruments are developed and validated in other cultural contexts, which may affect their validity and utility in Romania. Therefore, the MHST-CA not only fills an important gap in the assessment of mental health in children and adolescents in Romania but also sets a precedent for the development of culturally adapted tools in other countries. At the theoretical level, the results indicate that, in a brief self-report screener, the biological, psychological, and social domains of the biopsychosocial model manifest as strongly interrelated facets of a single dimension of mental health problems. The model retains its value as a framework for ensuring that somatic and relational experiences are represented in the content of the instrument.

Beyond its immediate utility in Romania, the MHST-CA may offer a replicable model for locally grounded instrument development in other Eastern European and post-Soviet countries facing similar systemic challenges, including Bulgaria, Moldova, Ukraine, and the Western Balkans, where school-based mental health screening infrastructure remains underdeveloped and Western-normed instruments may show limited cultural fit. The biopsychosocial content framework of the MHST-CA, combined with a general-factor scoring structure, provides a theoretically coherent and practically flexible template that could guide parallel tool development in collectivist or transitional-economy contexts. Cross-cultural adaptation studies using established procedures (e.g., TRAPD: Translation, Review, Adjudication, Pre-testing, Documentation; Harkness et al., 2010) would be a natural next step to evaluate the portability of the MHST-CA's content and factor structure. Such efforts would also contribute to international benchmarking of adolescent mental health prevalence, which currently lacks reliable comparative data from the Eastern European region.

Study Limitations

However, this study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. The sample used for the validation of the MHST-CA was recruited from schools in a single city (Bucharest) and, although large enough to allow for robust statistical analyses, may not be representative of the entire population of children and adolescents in Romania. Additionally, the instrument has not yet been tested in other cultures, which limits the generalizability of the results. Another important limitation worth mentioning is that the validation of the MHST-CA relied on selfreports, which may be influenced by subjective factors, such as the desire to respond in a socially acceptable manner or difficulty in recognizing and reporting one's mental health problems. To address these limitations, future studies could include multi-informant evaluation methods, involving parents, teachers, and mental health professionals to obtain a more complete and objective perspective on the mental health of children and adolescents. Moreover, the items were selected and the final model was tested on the same validation sample, and the bifactor model was introduced after the hypothesized three-factor model had failed to fit. Although split-half cross-validation indicated that the item selection and the bifactor structure were stable, a model proposed post hoc requires confirmation in an independent sample. The criterion thresholds used to evaluate screening accuracy were derived from self-report questionnaires rather than from clinical diagnoses. The CPSS-SR-5 threshold presupposes trauma exposure, which three fifths of the sample reported, and the WEMWBS threshold was established in adult samples (Bianco, 2012) with a Romanian version that has not yet been validated in adolescents, so that the samplebased criterion should be regarded as a sensitivity analysis rather than a validated cut-off. In addition, the analyses relied on maximum likelihood estimation of ordinal items. Estimators designed for ordinal data could be used in future work.

Although the age range slightly exceeded the WHO definition of adolescence, this decision was intentional and aimed at increasing the ecological validity of the instrument for real-world screening contexts.

Finally, the correlated three-factor model fell below conventional fit thresholds, and we did not attempt to rescue it through post hoc modifications. Model fit indices are influenced by factor intercorrelations, scale length, and theoretical constraints (Marsh et al., 2004), and the pattern observed here, with high inter-factor correlations and a well-fitting bifactor model, is common for brief multidimensional mental health measures. Users should therefore rely on the total score and treat facet scores as descriptive.

Future Research Directions

Future research should continue to validate the MHST-CA on diverse samples and in various contexts to ensure the generalizability of the results. It is essential to explore the instrument's adaptability in other cultures and investigate the relationship between the variables measured by the MHST-CA and other aspects of mental health and well-being. Additionally, it would be beneficial to examine the impact of interventions based on the early identification of mental health problems through the MHST-CA. Beyond cultural adaptation, future research should replicate the bifactor structure in an independent sample, examine test-retest reliability, establish norms by gender and age, evaluate whether gender-specific cut-offs are warranted, and investigate the criterion validity of the MHST-CA by comparing the results obtained with this instrument to formal clinical diagnoses. It would also be beneficial to explore the utility of the MHST-CA in different settings, such as schools, clinics, and public health services, to assess its practicality and effectiveness in various application contexts.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the MHST-CA demonstrates acceptable psychometric properties as a brief screener and represents a valuable tool for assessing mental health in children and adolescents in Romania. The analyses conducted have shown that the instrument has excellent internal consistency, measurement invariance across gender and age, concurrent validity, incremental validity over a symptom-focused measure, and good screening accuracy, making it suitable for use in various clinical and educational contexts. The development of the MHST-CA responds to a critical need for an assessment tool specific to the Romanian context, addressing unique aspects of the mental health of children and adolescents in this region. Although the hypothesized three-factor structure was not supported, the proposed bifactor model retains the biological, psychological, and social domains as facets of a general mental health dimension, so that the content generated together with adolescents, parents, and teachers allows for a holistic assessment aligned with the internationally recognized biopsychosocial model, while scores are best summarized in a single total score.
