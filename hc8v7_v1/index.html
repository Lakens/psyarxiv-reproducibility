Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 31 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Conceptually, novelty processing and episodic memory are closely linked. Yet it remains unclear whether they rely on common or partly independent mechanisms. We investigated whether their relationship depends on the type of novelty and representational content in healthy older adults. Seventy-eight adults aged 61-84 years completed eye-tracking tasks assessing Absolute novelty (unknown stimuli) and Associative novelty (novel configurations of familiar elements) for objects and scenes, as well as a pupillometry task assessing responses to novel objects. Novelty-processing indices were correlated with performance across various episodic memory tasks using preregistered Bayesian correlations. Older adults showed reliable gaze preferences for both Absolute and Associative novelty in object and scene tasks. Contrary to our predictions, however, Absolute novelty preference was generally unrelated to performance on familiarity-based recognition and perceptual discrimination measures hypothesized to rely on the perirhinal cortex. Associative novelty preference was likewise largely unrelated to episodic memory performance, although a small number of unexpected associations with verbal recall emerged for object novelty. Novelty-related pupil responses also showed little evidence of associations with memory performance. Exploratory comparisons with data from younger adults aged 18-30 years (from Servais & Bastin, 2026) indicated largely comparable gaze-based novelty preferences across age groups, although novelty-related peak pupil dilation was reduced in older adults. Overall, these findings suggest that individual differences in novelty processing, when assessed in paradigms with no memory confound, are largely independent of episodic memory performance in healthy older adults and that gaze-based novelty processing may remain preserved with age for both novelty types.

Introduction

Novelty detection, a fundamental property of human behavior, is conceptually associated with the cognitive function of episodic memory. Indeed, memorizing new information relies on distinguishing the new from the familiar (van Kesteren et al., 2012).

While the link between the two processes seems obvious, its exact nature remains unclearare they two sides of the same function or rather distinct yet interacting mechanisms? Surprisingly, consensus is lacking on whether novelty processing is a separate function or pertains to episodic memory. Several cognitive models make different predictions. Unitary models treat novelty as part of the memory system on a continuum with familiarity (Brown et al., 2002; Kumaran et al., 2007), whilst modular models (Kafkas et al., 2014) propose that information processing starts within two independent modules: one dealing with novelty and the other with familiarity. The two signals then interact to produce a signal of relative familiarity. Critically, we suggest considering the distinction between different types of novelty to investigate their relationships with memory. Absolute novelty (stimulus never met) is commonly distinguished from Associative novelty (new configuration of otherwise familiar stimuli) or contextual novelty (familiar item encountered in unexpected context) (Kafkas & Montaldi, 2018; Schomaker et al., 2015), and we suggest that these different novelty types could entertain different relationships with memory (Bastin et al., 2019; Servais et al., 2024; Servais & Bastin, 2024). Indeed, while it is well acknowledged that novelty processing and episodic memory both rely on the medial temporal lobe (MTL) brain regions (Schomaker et al., 2021; Tulving et al., 1994), Kafkas & Montaldi (2018) recently suggested separate brain circuits that process novelty differently depending on its nature. On one hand, Absolute novelty could be processed as an absence of familiarity by the perirhinal cortex (PRC)-linking it to the cholinergic system (Okada et al., 2022; Warburton et al., 2003). Defining novelty in relation to prior knowledge aligns with recent accounts of the PRC, which emphasize its representational role beyond memory (Bastin & Delhaye, 2023). In this view, the PRC is thought to support high-level object representations and the processing of complex objects, feature conjunctions, and familiarity independently of explicit recognition memory (Cowell et al., 2019; Ladyka-Wojcik et al., 2026; Martin & Barense, 2023; Ranganath & Ritchey, 2012). On the other hand, Associative and contextual novelty could activate the hippocampus, triggering functional loops that connect it to dopaminergic nuclei (Clos et al., 2019) including the ventral tegmental area (VTA), and to noradrenergic nucleus including the locus coeruleus (LC; Groves et al., 2025; McNamara & Dupret, 2017; Park, 2024; Schneider et al., 2024). Furthermore, novelty-and memory-related electrophysiological signals differ within the hippocampus, probably generated by different neuronal populations, allowing it to couple with two mutually exclusive networks and brain states (novelty processing vs remembering, Barbeau et al., 2017). This suggests that contextual novelty may be preserved despite memory impairment, which seems to be the case in patients diagnosed with Mild Cognitive Impairment (Servais et al., 2024).

Several experimental paradigms have been traditionally used to assess novelty processing, including oddball tasks, repetition-suppression designs, visual paired comparison (VPC) paradigms, old/new recognition tasks, and Associative novelty paradigms. However, many of these paradigms rely on a familiarization phase or on prior exposure within the laboratory to establish a contrast between familiar and novel stimuli, thereby making memory a key component of the task. In repetition-suppression paradigms, for instance, novelty is inferred from stronger neural responses to non-repeated than repeated stimuli (Barto et al., 2013; Nyberg, 2005; Ranganath & Rainer, 2003; Reichardt et al., 2020). Similarly, in oddball paradigms, infrequent deviant stimuli are interspersed among frequently presented standard stimuli, with enhanced behavioral or neural responses to the deviants taken as evidence of novelty detection (Friedman et al., 2001; Polich, 2007). Old/new recognition tasks require participants to discriminate newly presented stimuli from items previously encountered during an encoding phase, thereby making novelty judgments dependent on prior episodic exposure (Ranganath & Rainer, 2003). Associative novelty paradigms also usually require participants to first learn specific item-item, item-context, or item-location associations, after which novelty is defined as the violation, rearrangement, or recombination of these recently learned associations (Kumaran & Maguire, 2007; Kafkas & Montaldi, 2018).

Similarly, in VPC tasks, novelty preference is assessed after one stimulus has been familiarized, with longer looking times toward the novel stimulus typically interpreted as evidence of novelty detection (Allegretti et al., 2024; Boujelbane et al., 2024; Crutcher et al., 2009; Fagan, 1970; Manns et al., 2000; Meister & Buffalo, 2015). Although these approaches are highly informative, they make novelty processing difficult to disentangle from memory processes, because the "novel" response is defined relative to what has just been encoded, retained, or recognized. For example, this critical confound has been well acknowledged for VPC tasks, which have long been used in preverbal infants and non-verbal populations as measures of implicit recognition memory (Basile et al., 2024; Eizenman et al., 2019; Fagan, 1970; Leeman-Markowski et al., 2024; Manns et al., 2000; Richmond et al., 2007).

Consequently, such tasks may confound novelty processing with implicit memory, making them less suitable for examining the relationships between novelty processing and episodic memory. The rarity of studies investigating novelty against prior knowledge is especially surprising given the rising interest in differentiating episodic familiarity (i.e., the sense of recognizing something based on a specific encounter) from lifetime familiarity (i.e., the gradual accumulation of familiarity with object concepts over the course of life) in the memory literature (Anderson et al., 2021; Bowles et al., 2016; Duke et al., 2017; Servais et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2023). Despite this growing theoretical interest, experimental studies of novelty have rarely operationalized novelty specifically in relation to prior knowledge.

Although a small number of studies have examined Absolute novelty processing using unfamiliar non-objects (Harı & Demiralp, 2025), rare or uncommon objects (Blackford et al., 2010), or abstract fractals (Stoppel et al., 2009), and others have investigated what we refer to here as Associative novelty using familiar objects with unusual features (Michelon et al., 2003; Wang et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2013), no study, to our knowledge, has systematically examined both Absolute and Associative novelty as a function of pre-existing prior knowledge. This distinction is important because novelty in everyday life is not always defined by recent exposure. Rather, an object, entity, scene, or association can be novel because it is absent from an individual's long-term knowledge, or because familiar elements are combined in an unexpected way.

In addition to differences between types of novelty, novelty can also occur at different representational levels, such as objects or scenes (for example, Quent et al., 2021 distinguished the item from the context). In parallel, recent representational accounts consider that the ventral visual stream and MTL form a continuous representational hierarchy, organized along a posterior-to-anterior gradient of increasing representational complexity (Graham et al., 2010; Saksida & Bussey, 2010). According to this view, distinct brain regions of the MTL are involved in the processing of representations of different nature, rather than exclusively in terms of processes or operations (Martin & Barense, 2023). Notably, substantial evidence supports the critical role of the PRC in object processing (Cowell et al., 2019; Martin & Barense, 2023; Ranganath & Ritchey, 2012), whereas the hippocampus is assumed to play a central role in scene processing (Dalton et al., 2018; Gardette et al., 2024). This is particularly relevant for novelty processing because it contrasts with accounts suggesting that the PRC primarily supports the detection of Absolute novelty, whereas the hippocampus is more involved in Associative novelty processing (Kafkas & Montaldi, 2018).

Rather than mapping brain regions onto the processing of novelty types in a fixed manner, the representational account suggests that their contribution may further depend on the type of novel information. The present study therefore investigates novelty processing across both object and scene representations. This approach allows us to test whether the relationship between novelty processing and episodic memory depends on novelty types and representational content.

To this end, in the current study, we used the NOVITAS stimulus database we developed in Servais and Bastin (2026). This database contains images of both objects and scenes representing two types of novelty: Absolute novelty and Associative novelty. As novelty processing has been robustly associated with eye movements (see Meister & Buffalo, 2015, for a review) and pupil dilation (Beukema et al., 2019; Chen & Westermann, 2018; Kafkas et al., 2021), participants completed three eye-tracking tasks designed to assess novelty processing: two adapted VPC tasks (previously validated by Servais & Bastin, 2026), one using objects and one using scenes, and a pupil-recording task. In the adapted VPC tasks, a familiar object or scene (i.e., a well-known common object or place) was presented alongside either an associatively novel stimulus (i.e., a novel object or scene created by recombining features of familiar objects or places) or an absolutely novel stimulus (i.e., an object or scene that has never been encountered before). Novelty processing was assessed through fixation duration, fixation count, saccade count, and the proportion of trials in which the first fixation was directed toward the novel image. Although these eye-movement measures have been widely used in classical VPC tasks (Meister & Buffalo, 2015), they have also been validated in these novel VPC adaptations that omit a familiarization phase (Servais & Bastin, 2026). Moreover, as Kafkas et al. (2021) suggested that different forms of novelty may have distinct effects on pupil diameter, our pupil-recording task consisted of passive viewing of object images from the Familiar, Absolute novelty, and Associative novelty conditions presented one at a time to measure the related pupil response. According to Kafkas et al. (2021), we would expect pupil constriction for Absolute novelty and pupil dilation for Associative novelty.

Our main goal was to investigate how different types of novelty relate to episodic memory. Based on the literature described above, we hypothesized a unitary relation for Absolute novelty and relative independence for Associative novelty. To investigate these relationships, we included memory tasks that are thought to rely predominantly on perirhinal cortex (PRC)-dependent processes, such as familiarity-based recognition, as well as tasks that rely more strongly on hippocampal processes, such as free recall and associative memory.

Specifically, if Absolute novelty relies on mechanisms that overlap with familiarity-based recognition, we expected individual differences in Absolute novelty processing to correlate with performance on tasks involving PRC-dependent processes, including the entity recognition task (Besson et al., 2020), the object and scene odd-one-out tasks (Delhaye et al., 2025), the Doors memory test (Baddeley et al., 1994), and the DMS48 recognition test (Barbeau et al., 2004). In contrast, we expected weaker or no associations with tasks relying more strongly on hippocampal processes, such as the Free and Cued Selective Reminding Test (Grober et al., 1987) and a face-name associative memory task (inspired by Macoir et al., 2024). For Associative novelty, if its detection relies on mechanisms that are relatively independent of those supporting episodic memory performance, we expected weak or no correlations with performance across all the selected memory tasks. Regarding differences between objects and scenes, object-based Absolute novelty may be more closely linked to familiarity-based recognition and PRC representations. In contrast, Associative novelty, and scene novelty more broadly, may rely more strongly on hippocampal representations and relational processing, without necessarily mapping directly onto episodic memory performance. To answer our research question, main analyses examined correlations between episodic memory performance and the preference for different types of novelty. The study was conducted in healthy adults over 60 years of age, as aging is associated with increased interindividual variability in memory performance, thereby providing a suitable context in which to examine individual differences in novelty processing and episodic memory.

Participants

This study was approved by the local Ethics Committee (agreement number: 2324-123). The sample size was estimated a priori by means of the G*Power 3.1 software (Faul et al., 2007). To detect a medium effect size correlation (r=.30) with a statistical power of .80 at α-threshold .05, the required sample size was at least 64 participants. Participants were recruited using the existing GDPR-compliant volunteer database at GIGA-CRC Human Imaging and through advertisements on social media. Exclusion criteria involve neurological or psychiatric history, and eye diseases (macula, glaucoma, cataract, etc.). We only recruited participants who were able to clearly see images on a screen without wearing glasses to avoid interference with the eye-tracker and ensure data quality.

We recruited 78 healthy Belgian participants. We excluded data from five participants for whom an intern assisting with data collection forgot to collect demographic information, one participant for whom eye-tracking calibration was unsuccessful due to droopy eyelids, and one participant who stopped before completing the study. The sample considered at this stage therefore comprised 71 participants (52 females; 19 males) aged 61-84 years (M = 71.09 ± 6.37), with 8-24 years of education (M = 15.63 ± 3.22). Two participants were lefthanded, one was ambidextrous, and the remaining participants were right-handed.

Procedure

After being informed of the study procedures and providing written informed consent, participants completed two experimental sessions lasting approximately 75 and 90 minutes, respectively. The average time interval between the two sessions was 7.28 days (min = 1 day and max = 43 days). At the end of the experiment, all participants received financial compensation of 25 euros.

During the first session, participants first provided demographic information, including age, sex, and education level (defined as the number of successfully completed years of education since the first year of primary school). They then completed three experimental eye-tracking tasks assessing novelty processing: an adapted VPC task with objects, an adapted VPC task with scenes, and a pupil recording task. The order of these three eye-tracking tasks was randomized across participants. The session continued with the following neuropsychological tests: the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA; Nasreddine & Patel, 2016), the DMS48 memory test (Set 1; Barbeau et al., 2004), and the items from the Temperament and Character Inventory-Revised (TCI-R, Hansenne et al., 2005) assessing the novelty-seeking personality trait.

During the second session, participants completed a series of cognitive tasks. They first performed an entity recognition task, adapted and shortened from Besson et al. (2020), followed by the encoding and immediate free-and cued-recall phases of the French version of the Free and Cued Selective Reminding Test (FCSRT; Grober et al., 1987). Participants then completed odd-one-out tasks involving objects and scenes (based on Delhaye et al., 2025) and the Doors memory test (Baddeley et al., 1994). After a 20-minute delay, they completed the delayed free-and cued-recall phases of the FCSRT. The session concluded with a face-name associative memory task (adapted from the procedure described by Macoir et al., 2024), the Mill Hill Vocabulary Test (Deltour, 1998), and the Lexis 64 naming test (de Partz et al., 2001).

Details about each task are provided below.

Materials

Novelty detection tasks

The novelty detection tasks involved the two VPC-like tasks we previously validated for objects and for scenes (see Servais & Bastin, 2026), as well as a task measuring pupil diameter in response to novel images that we developed.

All these tasks were presented on a gray screen (hex colour # 808080) with a resolution of 1600 × 900 px. OpenSesame 3.2.8 software (Mathôt et al., 2012) was used to control stimulus presentation. Eye movements and pupil size were recorded at 500 Hz using the SMI Red 500 remote eye-tracker. Synchronisation between stimulus presentation and eye-tracking recording was handled using the PyGaze plug-in implemented within the OpenSesame software (Dalmaijer et al., 2014). The eye-tracker was positioned below the screen. The eye-lens distance was 50-70 cm. The tasks were administered in a room without windows to keep the room lighting conditions constant.

Adapted Visual Paired Comparison (VPC) tasks

We used the two VPC-like tasks previously developed by Servais and Bastin (2026): one using object stimuli and one using scene stimuli. In both tasks, participants passively viewed pairs of images presented side by side while their eye movements were recorded. They were instructed to view the images naturally, as if they were watching images on a television screen, and were explicitly informed that no memory test would follow. This passive-viewing procedure was used to assess spontaneous visual preference for novel stimuli.

In the object VPC task, each trial presented two object images simultaneously. The task included three conditions: a baseline condition, in which two images of the same familiar object were presented; an Associative novelty condition, in which an associatively novel object was shown next to a Familiar object; and an Absolute novelty condition, in which an absolutely novel object was shown next to a Familiar object. Each image pair was preceded by a fixation cross of 500 ms and was displayed for 2.5 s. One modification from Servais and Bastin (2026) was that object images in the present study were selected based on normative ratings obtained from older adults. Specifically, objects with a mean novelty score above 75 were selected for the Associative and Absolute novelty conditions, whereas objects with a mean novelty score below 50 were selected as Familiar control stimuli. The scene VPC task followed the same general procedure, but included four conditions: baseline, Associative novelty, Absolute novelty, and Incongruency. In the Incongruency condition, participants viewed two versions of the same Familiar scene, one containing a congruent object and the other an incongruent object. Scene pairs were displayed for 3.5 s. The normative results for the congruent and incongruent object-in-scene images obtained from older adults are available in Supplementary Materials.

See Figure 1A and 1B for illustration of the experimental procedures or Servais and Bastin (2026) for further details.

Pupil dilation task

Stimuli consisted of 90 images of objects that were extracted from the NOVITAS database (Servais & Bastin, 2026). To maximize differences between experimental conditions in terms of novelty, we selected the 30 stimuli from the Associative novelty condition and the 30 stimuli from the Absolute novelty condition that obtained the highest average normative rating of novelty, and the 30 stimuli from the Familiar condition that obtained the lowest average normative rating of novelty (see Servais & Bastin, 2026 for the associated normative study in 184 older adults). To avoid repeating stimuli across tasks, the stimuli that were selected for the pupil task were not included in the VPC tasks.

Because pupillary responses are sensitive to both light intensity (Cheng et al., 2006) and chromatic adaptation (Tsujimura et al., 2006), stimulus brightness, contrast, and the colour of visual images were controlled. The experiment took place in a room without windows to avoid changes in ambient luminosity. Following the method outlined by Kafkas and Montaldi (2015), we modified our images so that stimuli consisted of single objects presented in grayscale on a gray background (RGB = 130). A consistent RGB value (red = 130, green = 130, blue = 130) was applied across all stimuli. These modifications were made using the Pillow library from Python 3.0. Additionally, the luminance of each image was verified by calculating the mean of pixel intensity using histograms from the GIMP software (version 2.10.38). Stimuli with luminance values exceeding two standard deviations from the mean were further processed, and their luminance was reduced to 80%.

During the experiment, participants were presented with 30 stimuli per condition (Absolute novelty, Associative novelty, and Familiar) that were presented in a random order.

Each stimulus was presented at the center of the screen for 500 ms. Central presentation (Kafkas & Montaldi, 2015) and brief exposure duration (Ruiz Chiapello et al., 2026) were used to minimize saccades during stimulus viewing, thereby helping to preserve the quality of the pupil data. After each stimulus, a fixation cross was displayed at the center of the screen for 5500 ms. This relatively long interval was chosen to allow the pupil signal to return to baseline before the next stimulus was presented (Mathôt & Vilotijević, 2023; Ruiz Chiapello et al., 2026). See Figure 1C for an illustration of the task procedure.

Before the task began, a 13-point calibration was performed to ensure the quality of the eye-tracking data. Participants were instructed to keep their gaze fixed at the center of the screen and to remain still throughout the experiment. To reduce eye fatigue, participants were given a short break after every nine images, during which they could blink or close their eyes.

Standard Neuropsychological Tests and Questionnaires

We administered the French version of the Free and Cued Selective Reminding Test (FCSRT, Grober et al., 2009), a widespread verbal episodic memory test that is known to rely on the hippocampus (Quenon et al., 2016; Sarazin et al., 2010). For each of the three learning trials, we computed free recall, corresponding to the number of words recalled without cues, and total recall, corresponding to the sum of free and cued recall. Free recall and total recall scores were then summed across the three learning trials. We also calculated the Index of Sensitivity to Cueing (ISC), using the formula: ISC = (Total Recall -Free Recall) / (Total items -Free Recall). These three indices have been shown to be sensitive to hippocampal function (Pelletier et al., 2017).

We also selected two tests of visual recognition that are presumably relying on the PRC. First, we administered the DMS48 memory test (Barbeau et al., 2004). This task was specifically designed to detect dysfunction in the MTL, particularly the PRC (Barbeau et al., 2004). Neuroimaging studies showed that anterior subhippocampal regions, including the PRC, were indeed involved in the DMS48 (Didic et al., 2010; Guedj et al., 2006). During the test, participants start with incidental encoding of the images during which they are shown a set of 48 images and asked whether there are more or fewer than three colours in each picture. Then, participants completed a forced-choice recognition task in which they were presented with pairs of images and asked to identify the previously seen image. In our study, due to time constraints, we administered only Set 1 of the stimuli and assessed recognition after a 5-minute delay during which participants completed the items of the TCI-R questionnaire described below. We computed the total recognition score as the number of correctly recognized items out of 48. Second, the Doors test (Baddeley et al., 1994) was included as an additional measure of visual forced-choice recognition memory. Although the task is not specific to the PRC, patients with hippocampal damage can perform it successfully (Baddeley et al., 2001). Moreover, forced-choice recognition performance is thought to rely partly on familiarity-based processes, which have been linked to PRC functioning (Bastin & Van der Linden, 2003; Brown & Aggleton, 2001). For each participant, we computed two scores: the number of doors correctly recognized in Parts A and B of the Doors test. We analyzed the two subparts separately because Part A is generally considered to rely primarily on familiarity processes, whereas findings for Part B are mixed, suggesting that it may involve either familiarity or recollection depending on the strategy used by the participant to distinguish the target door from the three highly similar lures (see for example, Bastin et al., 2004). Given that dual-process theories suggest that recollection is associated with hippocampal activity, whereas familiarity is more closely linked to perirhinal regions (Yonelinas & Jacoby, 2012), this distinction seems appropriate for our purpose.

To further characterize the participants in our sample, we administered the Montréal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA; Nasreddine & Patel, 2016) to ensure that they did not show significant cognitive decline, using a cut-off score of 23/30 (as advised in Carson et al., 2018). Participants also completed the Mill Hill Vocabulary Test (Deltour, 1998), which was used as an index of socio-cultural level, and the Lexis 64 Naming Test (de Partz et al., 2001) to screen for substantial object-naming and lexico-semantic difficulties, as impaired processing or recognition of object stimuli could potentially affect novelty processing in our eye-tracking tasks. Finally, participants completed the items from the Temperament and Character Inventory-Revised (TCI-R; Hansenne et al., 2005) that assess the novelty-seeking trait, as this personality dimension could influence the propensity of participants to exhibit a novelty preference in the eye-tracking tasks (Krebs et al., 2009).

Odd-one-out tasks

We used the odd-one-out tasks for objects and scenes developed by Delhaye et al. (2025). In the object discrimination task, participants were presented with 48 triplets of images, each comprising two images depicting the same object from different viewpoints and a third image showing a different exemplar of the same object. Participants were asked to identify the odd image. The triplets were presented in a random order. The scene task followed the same procedure. For each task, we first computed overall accuracy as the number of correctly identified triplets. Following Delhaye et al. (2025), we also computed an index of sensitivity to visual distance, as this measure was shown to relate specifically to the structural integrity of the transentorhinal cortex, a region encompassing the medial portion of the PRC. For each participant, this index was calculated as the point-biserial correlation between trial-by-trial accuracy and each trial's visual-distance index. The resulting r values were then Fisher-transformed into Z scores. Higher Z scores indicate that performance was more strongly affected by visual distance, with better accuracy for more visually distinct trials.

Entity recognition task

We used a shorter version of the recognition task for entities developed by Besson et al. (2020). In the study phase, participants were shown 30 images of objects presented one by one for 3 sec each, and they were asked to indicate whether the depicted object could fit in a shoebox or not. Participants were instructed to memorize the items for a subsequent memory test. After a two-minute retention interval filled with arithmetic exercises, participants completed the recognition phase, which was run using the Speed and Accuracy Boosting (SAB) procedure with a response deadline set at 750 ms from stimulus onset to recognize 30 old items from 30 new/distractor items. The SAB is based on a Go/no-Go task. Participants were asked to give a Go-response by pressing a key before the response deadline for the 'old' items they saw during the study phase, or a no-Go response for the "new/distractors items" that were not presented during the study phase. Feedback was provided after each trial to inform the participant about the correctness of their responses. Before starting the task, participants performed a training block to get familiar with the procedure and speed.

Responses were classified according to both the stimulus type and the participant's response within the 750-ms deadline. A hit corresponded to a Go response to an old item, whereas a false alarm corresponded to a Go response to a new/distractor item. When no-Go response was given before the deadline, the trial was considered a no-Go response: this was classified as an omission when the stimulus was an old item, and as a correct rejection when the stimulus was a new/distractor item. For each participant, recognition sensitivity was then quantified using d′, calculated by subtracting the z-transformed false alarm rate from the ztransformed hit rate: d′ = z(hit rate) -z(false alarm rate). Higher d′ values indicate better discrimination between old and new items, independently of response bias.

The entity recognition task was selected because it has been shown to be associated with the integrity of the perirhinal and anterolateral entorhinal cortices (Besson et al., 2020).

Face-Name associative memory task

Participants performed a computerized face-name association memory task (inspired by the procedure from Macoir et al., 2024). In the encoding phase, they were presented with 32 face-name pairs and were instructed to memorize the association between each face and its corresponding first name. The stimulus set included an equal number of male and female faces, as well as an equal number of young and older faces, selected from the database published by Minear and Park (2004). To match the age category of the faces, we selected 16 names commonly given in France to individuals born between 1995 and 2005 for the young adult faces, and 16 names commonly given to individuals born between 1945 and 1955 for the older adult faces. Name selection was based on data from the Institut national de la statistique et des études économiques (INSEEfoot_0 ). Each encoding trial began with a 300-ms fixation cross, followed by the presentation of a face and its associated name for 5 sec. The complete set of face-name pairs was presented twice, with trial order randomized across participants, separately in each encoding block.

Following encoding, participants completed a 30-s distractor task in which they counted backward from 100. They then performed a two-alternative forced-choice recognition test. On each trial, a previously studied face was presented with two previously encountered names: the correct name and another name that was presented with a different face during the encoding phase. Participants were asked to select the name that had previously been associated with the face by pressing the left or right arrow key. They were instructed to respond as quickly and accurately as possible. The recognition test comprised 32 randomized trials. The associations between faces and names were counterbalanced between participants such that the same face was not always associated with the same name. For each participant, recognition accuracy was measured as the percentage of correct responses.

We used a face-name association task because this type of associative memory paradigm is known to engage the hippocampus (Macoir et al., 2024; Mayes et al., 2004; Polcher et al., 2017; Sperling et al., 2001).

Analyses

Preprocessing of eyetracking data from VPC-like tasks

The preprocessing pipeline was identical for object and scene tasks (and identical to the one previously used in Servais & Bastin, 2026). Gaze recordings were parsed into fixations and saccades using the event-detection algorithm provided by the SMI eye-tracking software. Analyses were restricted to two predefined Areas of Interest (AOIs) corresponding to the left and right stimulus locations. Each AOI covered a 300 × 300 px region, extended by a 25 px margin on all sides to accommodate possible gaze-estimation error.

Data quality was assessed at both the trial and participant levels. A trial was retained when at least 50% of gaze samples were valid and when at least one of two additional criteria was satisfied: at least 30% of the valid samples were located within one of the AOIs, or cumulative gaze duration within the AOIs reached at least 500 ms. Participants were included in the analyses if at least 75% of their gaze samples were valid overall and if they contributed a minimum of 15 valid trials, out of 30, in each experimental condition.

Eye-tracking measures were first extracted separately for the left and right AOIs and were then converted into indices of novelty preference. In line with previous work examining gaze-based markers of novelty preference (Crutcher et al., 2009; Ernst et al., 2020; Leeman-Markowski et al., 2024), fixation-related measures included total fixation duration (ms), fixation count, and the location of the first fixation. Saccade-related measures included the number of intra-AOI saccades, defined as saccades occurring within the same AOI. For trials containing a novel and a familiar stimulus (Associative and Absolute conditions), noveltypreference indices were calculated as Novel/(Novel + Familiar) (Leeman-Markowski et al., 2024). This transformation was applied to total fixation duration, fixation count, and intra-AOI saccade count. First-fixation bias was additionally quantified as the proportion of valid trials on which the first fixation was directed toward the novel stimulus. To control for participant-specific spatial preferences (Ković et al., 2009), novelty-preference measures were adjusted using performance on baseline trials. For each participant, a rightward attentional bias was estimated as the proportion of attention allocated to the right AOI relative to the total attention allocated to both AOIs, i.e., Right / (Left + Right). This value was then expressed relative to the location of the novel stimulus on each trial. When the novel stimulus was presented on the right, the rightward-bias value was retained; when it was presented on the left, its complement (1 -rightward bias) was used. Baseline-corrected novelty preference was then calculated as Observed preference -Baseline bias. Accordingly, a corrected score of zero reflected no novelty preference above and beyond the participant's spatial bias. The same correction procedure was applied to all dependent measures, and all statistical analyses were performed on these baseline-adjusted scores.

Preprocessing of eyetracking data for Pupil task

Pupil data were processed in Python at the native sampling rate of 500 Hz and in their arbitrary unit. Preprocessing was informed by the recommendations of Mathôt and Vilotijević (2023). For each participant, the eye containing the greatest number of valid pupil samples was selected for analysis. Samples with a pupil-confidence value below 1, according to the SMI software, were treated as missing.

Missing-data segments associated with invalid pupil samples were extended by 200 ms on either side to account for blink-related distortions. Additional abrupt changes in pupil size were identified from the absolute rate of pupil-size change calculated using the actual sample timestamps. Samples for which pupil-change speed exceeded the median speed by more than five median absolute deviations were marked as missing. Short internal missingdata segments of up to 500 ms were reconstructed using cubic-spline interpolation. Cubic splines were estimated using two valid samples preceding and two valid samples following the missing interval whenever these samples were available. When sufficient surrounding samples were unavailable for cubic-spline estimation, linear interpolation was used as a fallback. Missing segments longer than 500 ms and missing segments occurring at the edges of a recording were not interpolated.

Recordings were segmented into epochs extending from stimulus onset to 5 s after stimulus onset. However, the time window of interest was defined as 1-4 s for stimulus onset in order to remove the early time window generally associated with light constriction during visual stimuli presentation and focus on the pupil response reflecting cognitive processing (Ferrari et al., 2016). The selected pupil signal was standardized within participant across the recording, resulting in pupil values expressed in participant-level z-score units. Baseline pupil size was calculated from the final 500 ms of the immediately preceding SMI-labelled recording trial. This strategy was used because the recording was interrupted between every trial, which did not allow us to use the signal just before stimulus onset. The median pupil value within the cleaned baseline interval was then subtracted from the cleaned post-stimulus signal, yielding baseline-corrected pupil responses in participant-standardized units.

Trials were rejected when more than 20% of the raw pupil samples in the 0-5 s poststimulus interval were missing. In addition, the baseline period was required to contain at least 80% valid cleaned samples. Because the recording was interrupted between every trial due to a programming error, we have also discarded from the analyses all the trials that preceded breaks (11 trials per participant) as the interval between the end of the preceding baseline recording and stimulus onset exceeded 500 ms. Participants for whom not at least one third of experimental trials passed the preprocessing criteria were excluded at this stage.

Pupil signal was smoothed using a centered median filter of approximately 100 ms.

Because the native sampling rate was 500 Hz, an odd 51-sample window was used, corresponding to approximately 102 ms, in order to define the filter symmetrically around each sample. The filtered signal was subsequently downsampled from 500 Hz to 100 Hz. A regular 100-Hz analysis time grid was then assigned to the resulting signal, corresponding to one pupil observation every 10 ms. The original acquisition-relative timestamps were retained separately for quality-control purposes.

Pupil-response measures

Preregistered pupil measures were calculated from the baseline-corrected signal between 1 and 4 s after stimulus onset, thereby minimizing the contribution of the initial light-evoked pupil response. Peak constriction and dilation were defined as the minimum and maximum pupil responses, respectively, within this interval. Following an approach adapted from Kafkas and Montaldi (2015), peak amplitude was estimated as the mean of the three samples immediately preceding and three samples immediately following the identified extremum. Mean pupil response and signed area under the curve (AUC) were calculated over the same 1-4 s interval.

Statistical analyses related to the pupil responses

Differences of pupil responses between novelty conditions in older adults.

Differences in pupil response across conditions were examined using linear mixed-effects models. Separate models were fitted for peak dilation, peak constriction, mean pupil response, and AUC. Condition (Absolute novelty, Associative novelty, and Familiar) was entered as a categorical fixed effect, with the Familiar condition specified as the reference category. The resulting fixed-effect contrasts therefore tested whether each pupil-response measure differed between the Absolute novelty and Familiar conditions and between the Associative novelty and Familiar conditions.

To account for stimulus-level visual characteristics that could influence pupil responses, image saliency, visual complexity, and aesthetic value were included as fixedeffect covariates. Image complexity was quantified using edge count, a computational measure of visual complexity that has been shown to correlate with human complexity judgments (Machado et al., 2015). Image saliency was quantified as mean saliency using SaliencyToolbox 2.3 (Walther & Koch, 2006), and aesthetic value was represented by mean aesthetic ratings obtained from an independent normative study (Servais & Bastin, 2026). All stimulus-level covariates were standardized prior to analysis.

To account for the crossed participant-by-stimulus structure of the data, the initial random-effects specification included random intercepts for participant and stimulus, together with participant-specific random slopes for both condition contrasts (Associative novelty versus Familiar and Absolute novelty versus Familiar). Models were estimated by maximum likelihood using the MixedLM implementation in statsmodels with Powell optimization.

When the estimated variance of a random slope collapsed to, or effectively approached, zero, indicating a boundary solution and lack of support for that variance component, the corresponding random slope was removed and the model was refitted. Random-effects simplification was performed sequentially while leaving the fixed-effects specification unchanged.

Preregistered correlations between pupil responses and memory performance in older adults. For the preregistered pupil-memory analyses, trial-level pupil measures were first averaged within participant and condition. Three indices were considered for each type of novelty: peak response, mean response, and signed area under the curve (AUC), all calculated over the 1-4 s post-stimulus interval. To isolate novelty-specific pupil responses, each measure was expressed relative to the Familiar condition. Absolute-novelty indices were calculated as Familiar minus Absolute, such that larger positive values represented stronger Absolute-specific constriction. Associative-novelty indices were calculated as Associative minus Familiar, such that larger positive values represented stronger Associative-specific dilation.

To assess our hypotheses regarding the relationship between memory performance and novelty-related pupil responses, we conducted preregistered Bayesian correlation analyses between pupil novelty responses and memory performance on the same test battery as for the VPC-like tasks.

We preregistered positive associations between pupil responses to Absolute novelty (mean response, AUC, and peak constriction) and performance on the DMS48 test, Doors test, entity recognition task, and odd-one-out object task. Thus, individuals showing stronger pupil responses to Absolute novelty were expected to show better performance on these memory measures. In contrast, no association was expected between pupil response to Absolute novelty and performance on the FCSRT/RLRI16 or Face-Name associative memory task. For pupil responses to Associative novelty (mean, AUC, and peak dilation), we predicted null associations to all abovementioned memory tasks.

Bayes factors were used to quantify evidence for the preregistered hypotheses. For predicted positive associations, BF10 > 3 together with a positive correlation coefficient was considered evidence in favor of the preregistered hypothesis, whereas BF01 > 3 was considered evidence in favor of the null hypothesis. Bayes factors between 0.33 and 3 were interpreted as providing insufficient evidence to distinguish between the alternative and null hypotheses.

In addition to the preregistered peak-response analyses, we conducted exploratory analyses of the complementary peak response for each novelty type. Specifically, we examined peak dilation for Absolute novelty and peak constriction for Associative novelty.

Because these analyses were exploratory, evidence was quantified using two-sided BF10 values rather than the preregistered hypothesis-specific evidence classification.

Exploratory comparisons of novelty-related pupil responses between younger and older adults.

To explore whether novelty preference changes with aging, we compared novelty-related pupil responses in our group of older adults with those of a group of younger adults (aged 18-30 years; N = 80) from Servais and Bastin (2026). These younger adults had completed the same pupillometry task, but their data had not been previously analyzed. We therefore applied the same analysis pipeline as described above for the older adults. The results of these analyses are presented in the Supplementary Materials (see Supp. 4).

To compare pupil responses between younger and older adults, we first averaged pupil indices within participant and experimental conditions. For each pupil outcome (mean, AUC, peak dilation, and peak constriction), Familiar-corrected novelty indices were then calculated by subtracting the participant's mean response in the Familiar condition from the corresponding Associative and Absolute condition means. The resulting Familiar-corrected Associative and Absolute indices were submitted to Bayesian 2 (Age Group: younger, older) × 2 (Novelty Condition: Associative, Absolute) mixed repeated-measures ANOVAs, conducted separately for mean pupil response, AUC, peak dilation, and peak constriction.

Age Group was modeled as a between-participant factor, Novelty Condition as a withinparticipant factor, and participant as the repeated/random factor. Bayes factors were computed with the default "medium" fixed-effect prior (r = .50).

Discussion

The present study primarily examined whether eye-movement behavior and pupil responses to different forms of novelty, considered as behavioral and physiological indices of novelty processing, were associated with individual differences in memory performance across a range of tasks in older adults. Importantly, we distinguished between Absolute and Associative novelty, as well as between object-and scene-based processing. We hypothesized that Absolute novelty processing, which is assumed to depend on PRC integrity (Kafkas & Montaldi, 2018), particularly for objects, could be associated with performance on familiarity-based recognition tasks or perceptual discrimination tasks, known to involve the PRC (Bastin & Delhaye, 2023; Martin & Barense, 2023). In contrast, we expected Associative novelty processing to be relatively independent of episodic memory performance, including free recall and cross-domain associative memory. Although both Associative novelty processing and episodic memory have been linked to the hippocampus integrity (Kafkas & Montaldi, 2018), they may rely on partly distinct hippocampal mechanisms (Barbeau et al., 2017). Thus, shared reliance on the hippocampus does not necessarily imply that individual differences in Associative novelty responses should covary with episodic memory performance. Accordingly, our primary analyses used Bayesian correlations to examine the relationships between performance across the memory tasks and eye-tracking and pupillary responses to both Absolute and Associative novelty.

Novelty-preference on VPC-like tasks and memory performance

Before considering these individual-difference associations, it is important to establish that the VPC-like tasks elicited reliable novelty responses at the group level. Older adults showed robust novelty preferences on the VPC-like tasks. For both objects and scenes, participants preferentially attended to Absolute and Associative novel stimuli across eyetracking measures (fixation time, fixation count, intra-AOI saccades, and first-fixation direction). In contrast, the Incongruency manipulation (i.e., objects that were congruent versus incongruent with the surrounding scene) did not elicit reliable novelty preference. As the procedure was the same as that used with younger adults aged 18-30 years from a prior study (Servais & Bastin, 2026), we conducted exploratory analyses to compare novelty preference patterns across age groups. These additional Bayesian analyses suggested that novelty-preference patterns were largely comparable between younger and older adults.

Importantly, however, the presence of robust novelty preferences at the group level did not translate into the predicted relationships with individual differences in memory performance. The preregistered prediction that stronger responses to Absolute novelty would be associated with better performance on familiarity-based recognition or PRC-dependent perceptual discrimination tasks received no support. Instead, most correlations between Absolute novelty-preference measures from the VPC-like task for object and memory performance provided evidence in favor of the absence of an association, or remained inconclusive. A similar pattern emerged for scenes, for which correlations involving Absolute novelty preference predominantly supported the null hypothesis.

Regarding Associative novelty preference, the overall pattern largely supported the preregistered null hypotheses. For scenes, most associations provided evidence for the absence of a relationship, and none provided evidence for an association with memory performance. For objects, however, although most associations predicted to be null were supported as null by the Bayesian analyses, a few departures from the null involved FCSRT performance. Greater novelty preference in terms of saccade count was associated with better Free Recall, whereas greater fixation-count and first-fixation preferences were associated with poorer Total Recall or ISC performance. Because these effects differed both in the eyetracking measure involved, and in their direction, they do not suggest a straightforward relationship whereby stronger Associative novelty processing is associated with better episodic memory. Importantly, their absence in the VPC-like task with scenes further suggests that these associations may be specific to the way Associative novelty was instantiated or explored in the object task, rather than reflecting a general relationship between Associative novelty processing and memory ability.

Given that FCSRT performance has been associated with hippocampal functioning (Sarazin et al., 2010 , Zammit et al., 2017), one possibility is that some forms of prolonged attention toward associatively novel objects reflect greater processing demands or unresolved prediction error rather than more efficient novelty processing (Kumaran & Maguire, 2007; Wills et al., 2007). In this view, the negative associations with Total Recall and ISC could indicate that Associative novelty processing is more effortful in individuals with lower episodic-memory performance. This interpretation may be particularly relevant for the object task, in which Associative novelty was instantiated using chimeric objects that may impose greater perceptual or integrative processing demands (Magnié et al., 2003). This is also compatible with our results showing higher novelty preference and longer viewing time for

Associative novelty compared to Absolute novelty (Servais & Bastin, 2026). However, this account remains speculative, particularly because greater saccade-based novelty preference was positively associated with Free Recall. Moreover, the FCSRT provides partly distinct measures of episodic-memory performance. Free Recall primarily reflects spontaneous retrieval, whereas Total Recall and the ISC quantify the extent to which retrieval benefits from semantic cues (Grober et al., 2010; Quenon et al., 2016; Bussè et al., 2018). The opposing associations may therefore reflect relationships between distinct components of novelty-directed exploration and memory, rather than a single, uniform association between Associative novelty processing and episodic-memory ability. More broadly, novelty processing itself is supported by a distributed network encompassing sensory cortices, MTL structures, as well as frontal salience and control regions (Wong et al., 2026). Because performance on a complex episodic-memory task such as the FCSRT likewise depends on multiple cognitive operations, the isolated associations observed here may arise from partially shared processes rather than from a direct correspondence between hippocampal Associativenovelty processing and episodic-memory capacity. However, given the small number of effects and their heterogeneous directions, these possible interpretations remain tentative and require replication. Future studies combining eye tracking with electrophysiological measures such as EEG could help clarify the underlying mechanisms by disentangling attentional, prediction-error, and memory-related processes across the temporal cascade of novelty processing for both Absolute and Associative novelty.

Novelty-related pupil responses and memory performance

The pupil findings provided a complementary, but different, perspective. At the group level, neither Absolute nor Associative novelty differed reliably from the Familiar condition for peak constriction, mean pupil response, AUC, or peak dilation. This pattern remained unchanged after accounting for stimulus-level characteristics such as saliency, visual complexity, and aesthetic value, and was similarly observed when younger and older adults were examined separately (see Supp. 5). Thus, unlike the clear novelty effects observed in overt viewing behavior on VPC-like tasks, novelty was not accompanied by a robust average change in the pupil measures. This distinction suggests that spontaneous orienting toward novelty and pupil responses to novelty need not provide interchangeable measures of novelty processing.

When compared with younger adults, there was no compelling evidence for age-group differences in peak constriction, mean pupil response, or AUC. In contrast, peak dilation showed evidence for an age-group difference, with younger adults exhibiting more positive Familiar-corrected dilation than older adults for both Absolute and Associative novelty. The absence of an Age Group × Novelty Condition interaction indicates that this difference was not specific to either type of novelty, but instead reflected a more general age-related difference in pupillary responsiveness. This reduced peak dilation in older adults may partly reflect normative age-related changes in pupillary physiology, as ageing is associated with a reduced pupillary dynamic range and attenuated dilation responses (Bitsios et al., 1996; Riley et al., 2024). Beyond these peripheral physiological changes, task-evoked pupil dilation has also been linked to activity of the locus coeruleus-noradrenergic system (Murphy et al., 2014), which itself undergoes age-related changes (Shibata et al., 2006). However, because pupil dilation is not a specific measure of LC activity, this interpretation remains tentative and would require direct assessment of LC integrity to be confirmed.

The analysis of correlations between novelty-related pupil responses to novel (Absolute or Associative) objects and performance on memory tasks showed a pattern broadly consistent with the VPC one. Most of the preregistered positive associations between Absolute novelty indices and the DMS48, Doors, entity recognition, and odd-one-out object measures favored the null, and the remaining two were inconclusive. For Absolute novelty, most preregistered null associations were also supported as null. One notable exception was the positive relationship between greater Absolute novelty-related peak constriction and FCSRT Total Recall, which provided strong evidence against the predicted null hypothesis.

Although this association with FCSRT Total Recall was not predicted, it may point to processes shared between the pupillary response to Absolute novelty and episodic-memory functioning. Absolute novelty has previously been associated with reduced pupil dilation or greater constriction, which has been proposed to reflect parasympathetic and cholinergic mechanisms involved in prioritizing and encoding novel information (Kafkas & Montaldi, 2018). Given that FCSRT Total Recall has also been related to hippocampal integrity (Sarazin et al., 2010), individuals showing a stronger constrictive response to Absolute novelty may also possess more efficient neural mechanisms supporting episodic-memory processing. This hypothetical interpretation differs from our preregistered hypothesis linking Absolute novelty specifically to perirhinal functioning, and may instead suggest that the peak-constriction response captures a broader novelty-related encoding mechanism.

Nevertheless, this finding was relatively isolated. The corresponding association with FCSRT ISC remained inconclusive, and the effect did not generalize across the other pupil indices.

Similarly, for Associative novelty, the large majority of confirmatory correlations supported the preregistered null hypothesis, except the correlations of peak dilation with FCSRT Free Recall and ISC, which remained inconclusive. Consequently, the association between Absolute novelty-related peak constriction and FCSRT Total Recall should be considered preliminary and requires further investigation.

Novelty processing and episodic memory: partially dissociable processes?

The convergence between the novelty-related behavior on the VPC-like tasks and pupil responses correlation analyses is noteworthy, but should be interpreted in light of the different strength of the underlying novelty effects. For the VPC-like tasks, older adults showed robust group-level novelty preferences, yet these individual differences were largely unrelated to memory performance. This provides relatively strong evidence that spontaneous novelty preferences in viewing behavior are not entirely dependent on episodic memory abilities. The pupil findings pointed in the same general direction, but are more difficult to interpret because neither Absolute nor Associative novelty produced reliable group-level differences from the Familiar condition, even in younger adults. Consequently, Familiarcorrected individual-difference indices may have contained relatively more measurement noise, potentially limiting their ability to reveal associations with memory performance.

In VPC-like tasks, the dissociation between robust group-level novelty responses and weak individual-level associations with memory performance is theoretically informative.

Previous demonstrations of relationships between novelty processing and memory may partly depend on paradigms in which novelty detection itself requires recognition of, or comparison with, previously encountered information. By using novelty paradigms in which memory confound was minimized, the present study may provide a more selective assessment of spontaneous novelty processing and orienting. From this perspective, novelty processing may be more functionally separable from episodic-related memory abilities than initially hypothesized.

Outstanding questions regarding the neural mechanisms underlying Absolute and Associative novelty

An important next step will be to characterize more precisely the neural substrates underlying Absolute and Associative novelty, as operationalized in the present paradigms. A small number of fMRI studies have investigated forms of novelty broadly corresponding to our definitions of either Absolute novelty, using unfamiliar non-objects (Harı & Demiralp, 2025), rare or uncommon objects (Blackford et al., 2010), or abstract fractals (Stoppel et al., 2009), or Associative novelty using familiar objects with unusual features (Michelon et al., 2003; Wang et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2013). Like the present study, these investigations assessed novelty without relying on an explicit familiarization phase, allowing novelty to be defined in relation to pre-existing knowledge rather than recent episodic exposure, thereby reducing potential confounds with episodic recognition processes. Interestingly, these neuroimaging studies have implicated not only MTL structures but also the fusiform and occipital gyri (Harı & Demiralp, 2025), inferior frontal gyrus, anterior cingulate cortex, and precuneus (Blackford et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2013). These findings raise the possibility that novelty detection recruits a distributed neural network extending beyond regions traditionally associated with episodic memory, potentially accounting for the relative independence between novelty preference and episodic memory abilities observed in the present study.

Future studies combining whole-brain analyses with high-resolution fMRI to directly compare Absolute and Associative novelty across objects and scenes would therefore be particularly valuable for determining the extent to which these forms of novelty recruit shared or distinct neural networks, and how these networks relate to those supporting episodic memory.

Preservation of novelty processing in healthy aging and clinical perspectives

From an aging perspective, the preserved VPC-like novelty-preference patterns are potentially encouraging, as they suggest that the ability to detect and orient toward novel information can remain relatively intact despite variability in episodic memory performance.

Whether the same conclusion extends to pupillary novelty responses remains less certain and will require paradigms that elicit more reliable novelty-related pupil effects. A key direction for future research would be to investigate whether similar patterns are observed in clinical populations characterized by brain damage to the MTL and episodic memory impairment.

Such populations could provide a particularly informative test of the extent to which different forms of novelty processing depend on the integrity of the episodic memory system.

This question is particularly relevant given the growing interest in traditional VPC paradigms as potential markers of cognitive decline and Alzheimer's disease (Crutcher et al., 2009; Boujelbane et al., 2024). Importantly, however, traditional VPC tasks intrinsically incorporate a recognition-memory component: participants must retain information from a familiarization phase in order to distinguish previously viewed from novel stimuli at test. Indeed, Crutcher et al. (2009) found that individuals with mild cognitive impairment showed novelty preferences comparable to controls at a very short retention interval, whereas their novelty preference was reduced when the delay was extended to two minutes. Thus, reduced novelty preference may, at least partly, reflect difficulty maintaining or retrieving the familiar representation rather than a primary impairment in novelty detection itself. This distinction may also help reconcile the present findings with previous reports of age-related reductions in novelty preference obtained with paradigms containing stronger associative-memory demands (e.g., Allegretti et al., 2025).

In this regard, our paradigm provides a novel approach by reducing recognition and retention demands and focusing more directly on spontaneous responses to different forms of novelty. Applying these tasks to individuals with MCI, Alzheimer's disease or MTL amnesia would help determine whether novelty processing itself becomes impaired with medial temporal pathology, independently of the memory demands inherent in traditional VPC procedures (Basile et al., 2024; Eizenman et al., 2019; Leeman-Markowski et al., 2024). Such work could also test whether Absolute and Associative novelty show different trajectories of vulnerability.

Finally, the relative resistance of VPC-like novelty preferences to healthy aging may be advantageous from a clinical perspective. If future studies demonstrate that these responses remain stable during normative aging but become selectively disrupted by neurodegenerative pathology, they could potentially help distinguish disease-related changes from the effects of age itself with good specificity.

Limitations

We would like to draw attention to a couple of limitations that should be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, the memory tasks used here cannot be regarded as process-pure measures of perirhinal-or hippocampal-dependent functioning. Although our hypotheses were based on evidence linking particular tasks or memory processes preferentially to one MTL structure, the PRC and hippocampus operate within highly interconnected systems, and tasks intended to engage one region frequently recruit the other as well (Besson et al., 2020; Shrager et al., 2008; Staresina et al., 2012). Moreover, the variability in memory performance within our healthy older-adult sample is relatively restricted for some of the tests. Participants were generally cognitively high functioning, with a mean MoCA score of 27.68, and several memory measures approached their maximum possible scores. For example, mean performance was 45.27 out of 48 on the DMS48 and 46.17 out of 48 for FCSRT Total Recall. Such restricted between-participant variability may attenuate correlations and reduce the ability to detect meaningful individual differences in the relationship between novelty responses and memory performance. Future studies including individuals with a broader range of cognitive and MTL functioning, including clinical populations as mentioned above, may provide a stronger test of these relationships.

VPC scenes

Analyses. For the scenes VPC task, trial-level data were analyzed using mixed-effects models analogous to those used for the objects task, but Condition had three levels-Absolute novelty, Associative novelty, and Incongruency. The Condition was coded without an intercept, so each coefficient estimated condition-specific novelty preference and tested whether it differed from zero. Pairwise contrasts compared all conditions, with p-values adjusted using the Holm correction (Holm, 1979). Covariates were not included because their values were near zero in the Incongruency condition.

Initial orienting was analyzed using generalized linear mixed-effects models with a binomial distribution and logit link, as in the objects task. Condition again had three levels and was coded without an intercept. Condition-specific coefficients tested deviation from chance, and pairwise contrasts assessed whether initial fixation orienting differed across novelty types.

Procedure

In the first part of that experiment (Supp. 1.
