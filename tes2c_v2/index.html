Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Response time can identify useful judgments without a history of previous accuracy, but should the fastest respondent always be followed? The present study examines whether response speed identifies individual ratings that predict other people's average preferences and whether the useful direction of this cue is consistent across evaluative settings. In exploratory reanalyses of published music and visual-aesthetic datasets, six-person candidate groups were formed, responses were ranked from fastest to slowest for each item, and the rating at each rank was evaluated against the mean rating of people outside the candidate group. In the music data, mean absolute error decreased across response-time ranks: the fastest response had an error of 18.54, random individual selection 16.29, and the slowest response 14.62 on a 0-100 scale. The combined first-rating visual data showed a different profile: both endpoints improved on random selection, with a larger advantage at the fastest rank. The slowest advantage was not reproduced in both original visual experiments, and results varied across domains and rating occasions. In music, slow responses were less extreme, and their advantage nearly disappeared after descriptive adjustment for rating extremity. Selecting the slowest response also performed worse than always predicting the scale midpoint or averaging all six candidate ratings. Response time can therefore be a useful cue for selecting a single rating, but its predictive direction depends on the judgment setting and target. Differences in the temporal organisation of evaluated experiences may help explain this variation, although the present observational analyses do not identify that mechanism.

1 Introduction

Imagine that six people have listened to the same piece of music. One gives it a rating of 20 out of 100, another gives it 85, and the remaining four also give different ratings. A new listener, X, wants to use one person's evaluation to anticipate their own enjoyment. Whom should X follow? Figure 1a illustrates this problem using six evaluations of a common stimulus. The same question arises when choosing a painting, an exhibition, or a film. Research on advice taking has often examined judgments with an objectively correct answer (Bonaccio & Dalal, 2006; Yaniv, 2004). Matters of taste present a different problem: there is no correct answer that applies to everyone (Analytis et al., 2018). Someone may be certain about their own taste while providing a rating that tells us little about another person's likely experience.

Figure 1b shows the same six ratings together with additional information about how the judgments were made. One person answered quickly, whereas another took much longer. Some found the judgment difficult; others were familiar with the kind of music being evaluated. The ratings themselves have not changed, but response time, familiarity, and difficulty now provide possible cues for choosing among them. Would any of these cues help identify a useful source? The figure raises the intuitive question of whom X should follow; the analyses below operationalise one part of that problem by asking whose rating best predicts the average rating of people outside the candidate group, rather than X's particular preference. Response time (RT) is one candidate cue. RT can improve predictions of a person's subsequent choices and provide information about the strength of their preferences (Clithero, 2018; Konovalov & Krajbich, 2019). Observers also use RT to infer what another person prefers (Gates et al., 2021). In monetary allocation tasks, they can infer another person's social preferences from decision times alone and predict that person's later choices without seeing the earlier choices (Bavard et al., 2024). These findings establish that RT contains information about the respondent. The present study asks a different question: does it also indicate how useful that respondent's judgment is for predicting other people?

1.1 Choosing whom to follow Other people's experiences can help us predict our own enjoyment, even when we know little about the people reporting them. Gilbert et al. (2009) compared two ways of anticipating feelings about a future experience: learning how another person had felt or receiving detailed information about the experience itself. Learning about another person's feelings could produce more accurate predictions. In some settings, a stranger's experience can also be as informative as a friend's (Eggleston et al., 2015). Differences in taste therefore do not render other people's evaluations useless. The next questions are whose evaluations to use and how to use them.

One approach is to identify people with similar tastes. Analytis et al. (2018) examined social learning strategies that used past evaluations to estimate taste similarity. Relying heavily on similar people worked well when enough evaluations were available. With less experience, however, similarity was difficult to estimate, and choosing the mainstream option could be more effective. Dissimilar people can also provide useful information. Fujisaki and Yang (2025) examined a dissenting-preference strategy that identifies a person with dissimilar past ratings and predicts the opposite of that person's choice. Following a similar person performed better on average, but the dissenting-preference strategy could perform better for some people with atypical tastes. An opinion can thus be informative even when it should not be followed directly.

Professional critics offer another possible source of information. Using wine ratings, Analytis et al. (2024) compared critics and amateurs as guides to amateurs' preferences. Recommendations from similar critics predicted amateurs' preferences better than recommendations from similar amateurs; even the most similar critic could provide better predictions than several similar amateurs combined. These findings demonstrate the informational value of critics, but the strategy still requires identifying a critic with similar tastes. As with similar or dissimilar peers, doing so requires a history of evaluations from both the source and the recipient.

Such information is not always available. A visitor may choose an exhibition using reviews written by strangers, or a listener may explore an unfamiliar musical genre before knowing which reviewers share their tastes. In these situations, people must choose whom to follow before obtaining feedback about that person's usefulness. Subsequent experience may help, but it generally provides feedback only about the options they actually try. A useful guide may therefore have to be identified without a history of shared evaluations. Information accompanying the current response could help address this problem.

1.2 Following the fast

People already treat response speed as social information. They use another person's decision time to infer doubt, and these inferences affect judgments about potential interaction partners (Van de Calseyde et al., 2014). Faster decisions can also lead observers to expect more extreme preferences (Evans & van de Calseyde, 2017). The fact that people use a cue, however, does not establish that it improves prediction. Its usefulness must also be assessed against the responses it helps them select. Van de Calseyde et al. (2025) proposed a strikingly simple rule: follow the fast. The rule selects the answer given by the fastest respondent. It requires neither a record of who has been correct in the past nor a detailed assessment of each person's expertise; it requires only knowing who answered first. Across quiz responses and physicians' diagnoses, the rule produced more accurate answers than selecting a person at random or selecting the slowest person. A simple feature of responding can therefore help identify a useful answer without prior performance information.

Whether the same rule applies to taste is less clear. A factual answer may be given quickly because the relevant knowledge is readily available. A rapid preference judgment may instead express a strong personal like or dislike. Certainty about one's own taste does not necessarily imply representativeness of other people's tastes. Nevertheless, fast responses could also reflect reactions that many people share. Musical liking can form after very brief exposure, and these early evaluations can correspond to evaluations made after longer exposure (Belfi et al., 2018). Pham et al. (2001) compared reports of feelings with reason-based evaluations of photographs and television advertisements. Reports of feelings were faster and showed greater agreement in evaluation patterns across people. Neither finding directly establishes that the fastest respondent is the best guide. Together, however, they motivate a fast-advantage account: when rapid responses reflect broadly shared reactions, the fastest person's rating should predict others better than the slowest person's rating.

1.3 Following the slowest

A competing account predicts an advantage for slower responses. An immediate evaluation may strongly reflect a person's own likes and dislikes, whereas a slower evaluation may involve considering several aspects of the stimulus or difficulty deciding how much it is liked. Such responses may be less extreme. In food evaluations, encouraging a more complex perspective reduced evaluative extremity (Linville, 1982). In website evaluations, responses were faster for websites judged very attractive or very unattractive, whereas intermediate evaluations took longer (Tractinsky et al., 2006). If slower ratings are less affected by strong personal likes and dislikes, they may lie closer to the average evaluation of people with different tastes. A less decisive response could then be a more useful prediction of others. This account predicts lower error for the slowest respondent than for the fastest.

These alternatives do not amount to a simple contrast between good deliberation and poor intuition. Thinking about reasons for a preference can move food evaluations further from expert evaluations (Wilson & Schooler, 1991), and RT alone does not establish whether a judgment was intuitive or deliberative (Krajbich et al., 2015). Nor is a moderate rating invariably a better prediction: its usefulness depends on the distribution of the ratings being predicted. The question is whether response speed helps identify evaluations that predict other people's preferences. Figure 2 illustrates the competing possibilities at the fastest and slowest ends of the response-speed dimension. The downward arrows and question marks indicate that either endpoint could yield lower prediction error. The solid inverted-U curve is a schematic device, not an empirical result, a fitted model, or a prediction that intermediate ranks must form that precise curve. A rule that selects a useful response in one setting may select a different kind of response in another. Rapid judgments might express shared reactions in some environments, whereas slower judgments might be more moderate in others. This suggests that the value of RT should be assessed in relation to the rating distribution and prediction target, rather than inferred from response speed alone. Following the fastest and following the slowest need not be universal competing theories. They may be alternative rules whose effectiveness depends on how the judgment was formed and what it is intended to predict.

One possible source of differences between settings is the temporal organisation of the evaluated experience. In the datasets examined here, music evaluations follow one-minute excerpts, whereas visual evaluations follow three-second image presentations (Fujisaki et al., 2022; Vessel et al., 2018). A static image makes information spatially available within a common display, whereas a musical excerpt unfolds over time. The distinction is not simply that one presentation is longer. At the end of a temporally unfolding experience, a respondent may still need to relate reactions to different portions of that experience to produce a single evaluation. After seeing an image, a respondent may already have an evaluative impression and chiefly need to express it on the response scale. These are hypotheses about possible processing demands, not established descriptions of either task.

The relevant question is what evaluative processing remains when the response clock begins. Equal exposure duration within a task does not establish that every person has completed the same processing when the response period starts. Nor does a short response time after the stimulus disappear imply that the whole evaluation was formed instantaneously. Music can be liked after brief exposure (Belfi et al., 2018), and images can continue to be interpreted after they are no longer visible. The present analyses cannot isolate temporal structure from other differences between datasets, but the comparison motivates examining whether response-time rules have domaindependent performance rather than assuming a universal advantage for either speed or slowness.

1.4 The present study

The present study examines these possibilities through exploratory reanalyses of two existing datasets: music preferences from Fujisaki et al. (2022) and aesthetic evaluations of visual stimuli, including paintings, from Vessel et al. (2018). For each stimulus, six evaluators form a candidate group. Their responses are ranked from fastest to slowest, and the rating at each rank is used to predict the mean rating of people outside that group. Prediction error is compared with the error of selecting one evaluator at random from the same candidates, using the same target. Examining all six ranks makes it possible to determine whether the fastest, slowest, or intermediate responses provide more useful predictions. The rank is defined within a particular six-person group and item: the same participant can be fast for one song and slow for another. The study therefore evaluates responselevel selection rules, not the selection of people who are consistently fast or slow.

This task concerns how well one person's rating represents other people's average preferences. It does not directly test a personalized recommendation for X. Instead, it addresses an initial question: can a useful rating be identified without first learning whose tastes resemble X's? This formulation separates the strength of a respondent's own preference from the usefulness of that respondent's evaluation for predicting other people.

Supplementary analyses consider two additional cues available in the music dataset: familiarity with the musical genre and the perceived difficulty of stating one's liking. Processing fluency has been linked to aesthetic pleasure (Reber et al., 2004), but familiarity, difficulty, and RT may provide different information about an evaluation. The same task of selecting one person from six is used to compare these cues. Further analyses examine how the performance of RT-based selection relates to rating extremity. Together, these analyses return to the problem in Figure 1: can information accompanying a rating help us decide whom to follow?

2 Methods

2.1 Overview and analysis design

Existing ratings of music and visual stimuli were reanalyzed. These analyses are referred to as Study 1 and Study 2, respectively, to distinguish them from the experiment numbers in the original publications. Both were exploratory. Response-time patterns had already been examined before the present analyses were specified; the competing accounts therefore guide interpretation rather than constitute preregistered hypotheses.

The task was to select one person's rating from a group of six candidates and evaluate it against the mean rating of people outside that group. This target represented average preference, not an objective standard of aesthetic quality or the preference of a particular recipient. No new observations were collected of people choosing or following advisers.

2.2 Study 1: Music evaluations from Fujisaki et al. (2022)

The music data came from Study 2 of Fujisaki et al. (2022). The sample comprised 56 Japanese undergraduate and graduate students, each of whom evaluated 24 musical excerpts. Participants heard one minute of each excerpt in randomized presentation order. They reported their own liking, estimated average people's liking, and rated both the difficulty of stating their preference and their familiarity with the musical genre. The original study obtained informed consent and approval from the University of Tokyo Research Ethics Committee.

The present analysis used participants' own liking ratings and their associated response times, recorded as myanswer and time1 in the source data. Ratings ranged from 0 to 100. The analysis dataset contained 1,344 participant-by-song observations, with each row representing one person's response to one song. All participants had complete data for the analyzed items. Checks covered duplicate observations, missing values, invalid ratings, and nonpositive response times. No additional observations were excluded, imputed, trimmed, or winsorized.

The analysis retained the 100 saved candidate groups used in the preceding analysis. Each group contained six distinct participants and was evaluated across all songs. Within each group and song, candidates were ranked by their recorded response times, with rank 1 denoting the fastest response and rank 6 the slowest. Rank was therefore specific to a group and song; it did not classify a participant as consistently fast or slow across songs.

For each song, the prediction target was the mean rating of the remaining 50 participants. No member of the candidate group contributed to this target, and the rating at every rank was evaluated against the same target. When response times were tied, the losses of the tied candidates were averaged at each occupied rank. This procedure was equivalent to selecting uniformly among the tied candidates, rather than averaging their ratings before calculating error.

2.3 Study 2: Visual evaluations from Vessel et al. (2018)

The visual data came from both experiments reported by Vessel et al. (2018). Participants viewed each image for three seconds. After the image disappeared, a seven-point scale appeared on which they rated how aesthetically moving the image was. The archived response time began at image offset. The present analysis used this rating task, not the separate task in which participants controlled viewing duration. Participants in the original research provided informed consent under the participating institutions' human-subject procedures.

The first experiment assigned separate participant groups to five stimulus domains. The second used the same participants for landscapes and exterior architecture in separate sessions. The participant exclusions listed in the original data archive were applied. Table 1 shows the retained samples; the two rows for the second experiment refer to the same 19 participants.

First ratings were used for the main analysis, and second ratings were analyzed separately. Some domains also contained a third rating block, which was outside the present scope. Within each experiment and domain, checks covered completeness of the participant-by-image data, duplicate rows, rating validity, and positive response times. No further exclusions or imputations were made. The original participant exclusions were based on test-retest reliability. Consequently, although the main outcomes use first ratings, sample eligibility incorporates information from repeated ratings. The analysis does not reproduce an entirely prospective procedure using only information available at the first rating.

Within each experiment and domain, 500 six-person groups were sampled without replacement within each group. These groups were reused across images and rating occasions. For every image, candidates were ranked by response time, and their ratings predicted the mean rating of participants outside the group in the same domain and rating block. Candidate groups never combined participants from different domains or experiments.

For the main display, item-level performance was averaged within each domain. Domains received equal weight within each original experiment, and the two experiments then received equal weight. Each first-experiment domain therefore contributed 10% of the combined estimate, whereas each second-experiment domain contributed 25%. This weighting was chosen after the separate results had been inspected. The combined estimate is therefore an exploratory summary, not evidence that a result replicated in both experiments. Experiment-specific, domain-specific, and second-rating results were retained in the supplementary analyses.

2.4 Statistical analyses

Mean absolute error (MAE) was the primary outcome. For each group and item, the absolute difference between the selected rating and the outsider mean was calculated, then averaged across candidate groups and items. Mean squared error (MSE) was a secondary outcome. All six responsetime ranks were examined without selecting the most favorable intermediate rank as a new primary rule.

Random selection used the same candidates and prediction target as each rank-based rule. Its expected loss was calculated directly as the mean loss across the six candidates, rather than estimated through an additional random draw. The main contrast was the loss at each rank minus this randomselection baseline; negative differences indicated better performance than random selection. The mean of the six candidate ratings was evaluated separately as an aggregation benchmark. This benchmark averages ratings before computing loss, whereas the random baseline averages the losses of the individual ratings.

Vessel ratings were analyzed on their original scale. For the joint display and the results reported below, they were transformed to a 0-100 range using 100 × (rating -1)/6. MAE and MAE differences were multiplied by 100/6. This transformation places errors on a common response range without implying that the underlying tasks are psychologically equivalent.

Uncertainty in paired differences from random selection was described using 2,000 itembootstrap replicates. For music, songs were resampled with replacement. For the combined Vessel estimate, items were resampled within each experiment-domain stratum, and the resulting domain and experiment means were combined using the weights described above. Participants and candidate groups were held fixed. The resulting intervals are therefore conditional on the observed participants and candidate groups; they do not quantify uncertainty across new participants, domains, or experiments. Some Vessel images were reused across experiments, and the stratified bootstrap did not jointly resample matching images across those experiments.

Repeated candidate groups were not treated as independent samples of participants. Monte Carlo variability was assessed separately. For music, 2,000 additional candidate groups were evaluated in 20 batches. For Vessel, the 500 groups were divided into 10 batches. Batch variability described candidate-sampling error, not uncertainty about the participant population. Random seeds and group membership were saved with the R analyses.

Supplementary analyses examined rating extremity, defined as distance from the scale midpoint. The slowest rule was compared with selecting the candidate rating nearest the midpoint. Descriptive models of selected-response absolute error included group-by-song fixed effects and either a linear extremity term or a natural spline with four degrees of freedom. The music analysis also evaluated always predicting 50. This fixed prediction used no candidate rating and was therefore distinct from selecting the most moderate candidate.

Finally, music candidates were ranked by reported difficulty and genre familiarity using the same groups, outsider targets, and loss functions as in the RT analysis. Ties were handled in the same way, and all six positions were retained. Supplementary analyses also examined second ratings in Vessel and an alternative target that averaged outsiders' first and second ratings. Analyses using the latter target were supplementary because they require later ratings. All reported estimates were generated by saved R code. Group size was held at six throughout the present analyses.

3 Results

3.1 Study 1: Slower music ratings had lower prediction error

Figure 3 shows the difference in mean absolute error (MAE) between each response-time rank and uniform random selection from the same candidate group. In the music data (Figure 3, left panel), the difference decreased across response-time ranks: selecting faster responses yielded higher error, whereas selecting slower responses yielded lower error. The random-selection benchmark had an absolute MAE of 16.29. The fastest response had an MAE of 18.54, whereas the slowest had an MAE of 14.62. The fastest-minus-random difference was 2.25, 95% CI [1.03, 3.40], and the slowestminus-random difference was -1.67, 95% CI [-2.61, -0.71].

The pattern was not restricted to the endpoints. From fastest to slowest, the six MAEs were 18.54, 17.27, 16.34, 16.03, 14.92, and 14.62. The observed profile therefore decreased across ranks; it did not resemble the schematic curve in Figure 2, which was intended only to indicate the possibility of advantages at either end. Point estimates at ranks 1 and 2 exceeded the randomselection baseline, whereas those at ranks 5 and 6 were lower. The intervals for ranks 3 and 4 included zero.

Additional candidate draws reproduced the endpoint ordering. Across 2,000 new groups, the fastest-minus-random difference was 1.88 and the slowest-minus-random difference was -1.71, with Monte Carlo standard errors of 0.058 and 0.044, respectively. The secondary MSE outcome also favored the slowest response over random selection: 345.94 versus 422.64.

3.2 Study 2: Both endpoints improved on random selection in the combined visual data

The combined first-rating visual data showed a different profile (Figure 3, right panel). On the rescaled metric, the random-selection benchmark had an absolute MAE of 21.97. The fastest response had an MAE of 19.44, a difference from random selection of -2.53, 95% CI [-2.93, -2.11]. The slowest response had an MAE of 21.36, a difference of -0.60, 95% CI [-1.06, -0.19].

Error was highest at rank 4. Ranks 3-5 had higher errors than random selection, with itembootstrap intervals above zero in the combined visual analysis. Both endpoints had lower errors, although the reduction was larger at the fast endpoint. Unlike the music data, the visual data did not show a steady improvement with slower responses.

The original visual experiments also differed. In Vessel's Experiment 1, the fastest-and slowestminus-random differences were -1.47 and -1.47, respectively. In Experiment 2, the fastest-minusrandom difference was -3.58, 95% CI [-4.28, -2.91], whereas the slowest-minus-random difference was 0.26, 95% CI [-0.50, 1.01]. The combined slowest benefit was therefore not reproduced in each experiment separately.

Second ratings further qualified this pattern. In Experiment 1, the slowest rule improved on random selection for exterior architecture, interior architecture, and landscapes, but its point estimate was worse for artwork and faces. In Experiment 2, it performed worse than random selection in both domains on second ratings. These supplementary results show that the direction of the relationship depended on the experiment, domain, and rating occasion.

3.3 Supplementary analyses: Extremity, fixed predictions, and other cues

In the music data, the slowest responses were closer to the scale midpoint. Their mean distance from 50 was 18.38, compared with 21.37 under random selection. Selecting the candidate rating nearest 50 yielded an MAE of 10.84, lower than the slowest rule's MAE of 14.62.

The descriptive slowest-minus-random coefficient was -1.667 without adjustment for rating extremity. With a linear extremity term, it was -0.018; with a spline term, it was -0.001. The unadjusted advantage therefore did not remain after these adjustments. These coefficients describe associations and do not establish a mediation mechanism.

Always predicting 50 produced an MAE of 12.68, also lower than the slowest rule's error. Averaging the six candidate ratings yielded a still lower MAE of 6.83. Selecting the slowest respondent therefore improved on random individual selection but did not outperform these benchmarks in the music data.

Difficulty and familiarity showed distinct rank profiles. Selecting the highest-difficulty response produced an MAE of 13.16, a difference from random selection of -3.13, 95% CI [-4.42, -1.71]. The lowest-difficulty response had an MAE of 23.21. For familiarity, both endpoints performed worse than random selection: MAE was 19.97 at the lowest rank and 20.04 at the highest. Intermediate familiarity ranks had lower errors than random selection. These findings do not support treating difficulty as uninformative or familiarity as simply beneficial when high. Full profiles and paired comparisons are provided in the supplementary analyses.

4 Discussion

Should the fastest or the slowest respondent be followed when predicting other people's preferences? The present results do not support the same answer across evaluative settings. In the music data, selecting the fastest response produced greater prediction error than choosing a person at random, and the slowest response had the lowest error among the six ranks. The visual data showed a different pattern: in the combined first-rating analysis, both endpoints improved on random selection, with a larger improvement at the fast endpoint. These findings do not justify replacing follow-the-fast with a universally superior follow-the-slowest rule. Instead, they identify variation in the direction and shape of the relationship between RT rank and the representativeness of an individual rating.

This distinction matters for the use of social information. Follow-the-fast offers a simple way to select an answer without learning each person's past accuracy or estimating their expertise (Van de Calseyde et al., 2025). The present analyses examine where this simplicity remains useful when judgments concern taste. The visual results show that subjective judgments do not necessarily reverse the rule: faster responses can still be better guides. The music results show that the same rule can nevertheless select less representative evaluations. The relevant question is therefore what response speed reflects in each judgment environment, rather than whether speed is generally beneficial or detrimental. The variation should not be reduced to an established distinction between music and visual art. The visual data differed between experiments and rating occasions, and the datasets differed in multiple procedural respects. They motivate domain-dependent explanations without identifying a single domain characteristic as causal.

4.1 What response time selects in a judgment environment

Research on chess offers a useful conceptual example. Russek et al. (2025) examined thinking times in online chess and found that players spent more time on positions where additional computation was more beneficial. This relationship was stronger among more skilled players, and time allocation also reflected the costs of spending time. A long response can thus reflect selective investment in a decision that benefits from further thought rather than poor ability. The study concerned players' own allocation of time, not observers selecting whose move to follow, so its relevance here is conceptual rather than direct evidence for an adviser-selection rule. Elapsed time acquires meaning through the demands of the task. Applying this perspective to taste requires examining what additional time changes in a person's evaluation.

The two accounts developed in the Introduction remain possible explanations. Rapid responses may express reactions shared by many people, whereas slower responses may express less extreme evaluations. The present analyses do not identify either psychological process. At the level of rating distributions, the music results are compatible with the second account: the slowest responses were more moderate, and their advantage nearly disappeared after adjustment for extremity. This does not establish that spending more time caused moderation. Extremity and prediction error are calculated from the same rating, so adjusting for one can change its association with the other without identifying a causal process. RT alone also cannot determine whether a judgment was intuitive or deliberative (Krajbich et al., 2015).

The response procedures highlight why RT cannot be interpreted as a uniform measure of knowledge, deliberation, or carelessness. The music ratings followed fixed one-minute excerpts. The visual ratings followed fixed three-second presentations, and the archived visual RT began at image offset. Neither RT measures how long a participant was exposed to the stimulus. Slower music responding does not mean listening for more time, and faster visual responding does not mean judging the image at the instant it appeared. Moreover, equal exposure within a task does not guarantee that the same amount or stage of evaluative processing remained at the moment the rating scale was used.

For the music task, longer RT might accompany greater difficulty committing a graded preference to a number or combining reactions across the passage. Such responses could be more moderate and hence closer to an outsider mean in the observed rating distribution. The music results support the descriptive connection between slowness and moderation, but they do not show that the elapsed time caused this moderation. Memory, response mapping, motor actions, and attention can also affect the latency to record a rating.

For the visual task, a short post-stimulus RT might reflect an evaluation already available when the scale appeared. That possibility could be compatible with a fast advantage, but the present evidence does not show that fast responses tracked shared visual perception or had less noise. Other explanations, including familiarity with depicted scenes, memory for the image, differential use of the response scale, and attentional variation, remain open. Accordingly, the present contribution is to show that RT rank selects responses with different predictive value across settings, while leaving the process that generates those differences to subsequent work.

4.2 Choosing a person versus using a fixed prediction

The midpoint benchmark places an important limit on the practical claim. In the music data, choosing the slowest response improved on choosing a person at random, but always predicting 50 performed better. These comparisons address different questions. The first asks which available person's rating should be used; the second asks whether any person's rating is needed at all. A cue can improve the selection of an individual without outperforming a strong fixed prediction.

This distinction also clarifies the meaning of accuracy in the present task. The prediction target was the average rating of people outside the candidate group. Averaging reduces variation arising from individual tastes, so moderate predictions can perform well, particularly when item means are near the midpoint. A rating may poorly represent a particular person's experience yet remain close to the outsider mean. The present findings therefore concern the representation of average preference, not whether the selected respondent understands the taste of a particular recipient.

A useful next test would examine rating distributions located away from the midpoint. The separate painting study in Fujisaki et al. (2022) provides a relevant example: ratings were concentrated toward the low end of the scale. That dataset was not analyzed here, but its distribution motivates a specific question. Can response-time selection identify useful ratings when a prediction of 50 is poorly aligned with the ratings being predicted? Both the fastest and slowest rules should be evaluated in such settings. Doing so would test whether these rules identify responses that track the location of the preference distribution, rather than simply favoring responses near the scale midpoint.

Outperforming 50 in such a setting would not, by itself, demonstrate an advantage over fixed prediction. A more informative comparison would also include a constant estimated from independent training data. Under MAE, this constant would be the training median of the item-level prediction targets; under MSE, it would be their training mean. Test outcomes must not be used to choose the constant. Such a comparison would distinguish information carried by response time from an apparent advantage created by a poorly matched midpoint benchmark.

4.3 Difficulty, familiarity, and taste discrimination

The supplementary cue analyses raise a related question. In the music data, selecting a response from someone who reported greater difficulty was more useful than selecting a response from someone who reported little difficulty. The highest-difficulty rule also had lower error than the slowest rule. This pattern makes it important to ask what subjective difficulty indicates about a preference judgment. Difficulty need not carry the same meaning as it does in a task with an objectively correct answer. -Trede et al. (2018) provide a framework for distinguishing possible interpretations. They define taste discrimination through the ratio of variation in personal satisfaction values to judgment noise. Poor discrimination can arise either because a person differentiates little among stimuli or because their reports are noisy. These components have different implications. Reducing judgment noise improves prediction under their model, whereas the usefulness of greater differentiation depends on how closely the source's tastes match the recipient's. Fine distinctions help when tastes align, but when they do not, those distinctions can introduce variation that is unhelpful for predicting the recipient. The framework therefore does not imply that every increase in taste discrimination improves every prediction.

This distinction suggests one possible account of the difficulty result. Suppose that higher reported difficulty accompanies weaker differentiation among personal likes and dislikes. The resulting evaluations might deviate less from a broadly shared level of liking and could therefore be useful for predicting an outsider mean. Conversely, someone who finds a judgment easy may express a strong personal preference that others do not share. This proposed connection to the discrimination framework concerns differentiation; it does not require treating additional judgment noise as beneficial.

The connection nevertheless remains a hypothesis. The difficulty measure concerns the experience of evaluating a particular item and does not separately estimate differentiation and judgment noise. Moreover, differentiating less across items is not the same as being closer to the midpoint on one item. Neither component can therefore be inferred from difficulty rank alone. The present analysis also did not test whether difficulty retained predictive value after accounting for extremity or RT. Repeated preference measurements and independent estimates of taste similarity would help distinguish these possibilities. A further distinction must be preserved when applying the framework: its original target is an individual's satisfaction, whereas the target here is an outsider mean.

Familiarity showed a different profile. Neither the most familiar nor the least familiar candidate performed well relative to random selection, whereas intermediate ranks had lower errors. This pattern is not an absence of information in familiarity. Instead, it challenges simple rules that always choose one end of the familiarity distribution. A familiarity rank may capture a different aspect of the evaluation from a difficulty rank, and its usefulness need not be monotonic.

The measures also differ in what participants were asked to report. Genre familiarity concerns prior experience with a kind of music, whereas difficulty concerns the experience of stating a particular preference. Neither directly measures the reliability of that preference. Their distinct profiles may involve preference strength, response extremity, or taste typicality, possibilities that should be considered alongside the distinction between differentiation and noise. Because both cues were examined in the same music sample, they do not independently confirm a general selection strategy. Their present contribution is to identify more specific questions about what makes a rating informative for other people.

4.4 Relation to collective intelligence

Selecting a useful person and combining several people's judgments are related but distinct tasks. In the music analysis, the mean of the six candidate ratings outperformed every response-time rank. RT-based selection therefore did not replace the benefit of aggregation. Its contribution was narrower: improving selection when a rule used a single person's rating. This distinction connects the present task to research on social learning strategies for taste (Analytis et al., 2018), in which the information available about a source determines which strategies can be applied.

It also returns to the problem illustrated in Figure 1. The recipient wants to know whose evaluation will help. The present analysis addresses an initial part of that problem by asking whether a rating representative of other people's average evaluation can be identified without a history of shared ratings. Whether the same cue helps a particular recipient remains open and requires a recipient-specific prediction target. When past evaluations are available, RT-based selection should also be compared with strategies that use similar or dissimilar tastes. A response that represents the average well need not be the best guide for someone whose preferences depart from that average.

4.5 Future studies: The temporal organisation of preference formation

The contrast between the music and visual results motivates a question about the temporal organisation of what is evaluated. The hypothesis is not that a painting is intrinsically easy whereas music is difficult. It concerns how an object becomes available across time and what remains to be done when a respondent is asked to convert the experience into a single rating. A visual scene can present its elements within a common spatial arrangement, although those elements are inspected and interpreted sequentially. Music, by contrast, presents different moments in sequence. A final response may require relating reactions to a part of the experience that is no longer currently audible to reactions to later parts.

This distinction should not be equated with the numerical duration of stimulus presentation. The visual images were displayed for three seconds, whereas the music excerpts lasted one minute. These durations, participant samples, instructions, and rating scales were not experimentally matched across datasets. Furthermore, the response times here were recorded after the relevant stimulus presentations. We therefore cannot infer that either observed profile is caused by an art form's temporal structure. The comparison instead motivates a more specific question for future experiments: how does the relationship between RT and preference representativeness change when the evaluative information is distributed through time rather than made available within a common display?

Films and novels make this question especially interesting. An unfolding narrative can not only present different information at different moments but also change how much a person likes the work along the way. A viewer might enjoy the first half of a film and dislike the second, or an ending might alter the interpretation of earlier scenes. Two viewers could give the same final overall rating despite having very different evaluative histories. One might rate the first half 80 and the second half 30, with an overall rating of 55; another might rate both halves 55 and give the same overall rating. These values are illustrative, not observations from the present datasets, and they do not presuppose that an overall rating is the arithmetic mean of local ratings.

For such experiences, the duration of the final response could reflect the task of relating or reconciling local evaluations. A respondent who liked the beginning but disliked the ending might need more time to decide what overall value to report than a respondent whose liking remained stable. This would be a question about forming a single summary judgment from a temporally varied experience. Whether that longer response would be more or less useful for predicting other people's average evaluations is a separate question. An association between local preference inconsistency and final RT would not by itself establish a corresponding association between RT and social predictive value.

Future experiments could collect ratings for different parts of a narrative and an overall rating, together with the RT of the final judgment. They could then test whether disagreement between local evaluations is related to final RT and whether the rating selected by a particular RT rank better represents the average overall rating of others. Measuring preferences repeatedly within an experience may itself affect the final judgment; it should therefore be treated as part of the experimental design, rather than an entirely neutral observation. Manipulating temporal organisation within an artistic domain, where feasible, would help separate temporal structure from differences between domains.

Repeated evaluations offer another opportunity. The Vessel data include successive rating occasions, so one could ask whether the RT of a first rating predicts the stability of a later rating or its representativeness relative to other people's ratings at the later occasion. For example, a source could be selected by its first-rating RT and evaluated using that source's second rating against an outsider mean from the second occasion. This would extend the analysis beyond a same-response relationship between RT, extremity, and prediction error. It is different from separately applying RT rank rules to first and second ratings, which the present manuscript already reports.

Even such an extension would require a cautious interpretation. A stable rating may reflect memory, repeated use of the same portion of a response scale, or boundary constraints on extreme responses, rather than access to a shared perceptual process. Nor does rating the same object a second time measure adaptation to an unfamiliar environment or a response to an external shock. The role of repeated judgments is to test whether the informational value of an initial response-time cue carries over to a later observation. A comprehensive reanalysis of these processes is not required to establish the descriptive results reported here.

4.6 From informative cues to adaptive source selection

One priority is to examine how rank profiles change with the number of candidates. The present analyses held group size at six. Changing group size changes the pool from which the fastest and slowest respondents are selected and how extreme their response times can be. The useful direction could remain stable, strengthen, or reverse as the choice set changes. Comparing complete rank profiles across group sizes would help distinguish these possibilities and establish whether the observed advantages depend on the particular six-person choice set used here.

A second question concerns how people actually use response time when choosing whom to follow. The analyses applied explicit selection rules to existing ratings; they did not observe recipients selecting advisers. A behavioral study could present the ratings illustrated in Figure 1 together with information about response times and examine whether recipients prefer the fastest, slowest, or intermediate respondents. It could also measure how recipients revise their own predictions after seeing the selected rating. These outcomes would distinguish choosing a source from using that source's opinion.

Existing research provides reasons to expect RT to influence such behavior. Van de Calseyde et al. (2014) showed that observers use decision time to infer doubt and that these inferences affect their choices of interaction partners. Evans and van de Calseyde (2017) found that observers expected fast decisions to be more extreme than slow decisions in social dilemmas. Gates et al. (2021) likewise showed that people use response times to infer others' preferences. These studies establish that observers extract social information from decision time, but they do not establish whom recipients will follow when their goal is to predict average liking in the present task.

This distinction opens a specific test of ecological rationality. Suppose that recipients preferentially follow slower respondents in an environment where slower responses are more representative. Within the task of selecting one adviser, that preference could constitute adaptive use of the cue. Ecological rationality evaluates a heuristic through its fit with the structure of the environment (Gigerenzer & Gaissmaier, 2011). From this perspective, following a slow respondent is sensible when slow responses are more useful for the prediction being made. The behavior need not express a general belief that deliberation is superior; it could instead reflect sensitivity to the relationship between response time and useful social information.

The strongest evidence would come from comparing environments with different cue directions. Recipients might favor slower sources in an environment resembling the music data and faster sources where the fast advantage is larger. Their selections should then improve prediction relative to random source selection. A fixed preference for slow respondents across all environments would not demonstrate the same adaptive fit. Nor would following the slowest respondent establish superiority over averaging or a fixed prediction of 50. The appropriate comparison depends on which options are available. Such a program would connect the statistical usefulness of a cue to the conditions under which people use it effectively.

Several features of the present evidence are important when designing these tests. The analyses were exploratory reanalyses of existing datasets, and the decision to combine the Vessel experiments followed inspection of their separate results. Item-bootstrap intervals held participants fixed, and some visual stimuli were reused across experiments. The current estimates therefore do not establish generalization to new populations or entirely new stimulus sets. Independent studies should retain the selection rules while varying the location of the rating distribution and the response procedure. Comparing source selection before and after recipients receive feedback would also reveal whether useful cue use is present initially or develops through experience.

Faster is therefore not always better when choosing whose taste to follow. Response time can be informative, but the direction of its usefulness depends on the setting. The findings separate three questions: what does a response time reveal about the person who answered; does that information help predict other people's preferences; and do recipients use it in a way that fits their environment? Addressing these questions together would clarify when following the fast, or following the slowest, becomes a useful social learning strategy.
