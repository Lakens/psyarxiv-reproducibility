Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Psychotherapy generally begins with a person who has a problem and needs help resolving it.

Deconstructive Inquiry (DI) examines how the struggle to resolve a difficulty can form the apparent self who must overcome it. This conceptual paper draws on a relational ontology, which understands things as organized patterns of relations. The self who must overcome fear, for example, takes shape through judging, resisting, and trying to escape fear. This activity is experienced as someone separate from the feeling and responsible for ending it. I call this formation centering. Comparison with a remembered past or imagined future can sustain the struggle by making present life unacceptable until the person changes. DI proposes that the effort and its apparent controller can become recognizable as the same activity. The division need not continue, even while fear remains and the person responds to what needs attention. A composite clinical vignette illustrates this possibility and shows how the therapist's need to prove helpful can reinforce the client's struggle. I also propose that insight changes the knowledge shaping later encounters, allowing change to persist without requiring the person to recall or repeat the insight. Comparisons with other therapies examine whether a separate observing self remains when therapy succeeds. The paper considers how cultural expectations shape these struggles and how therapists and supervisors can participate in sustaining or questioning them.

Maya was a 27-year-old graduate student who came to therapy because she felt "stuck in every part of life." She struggled to complete assignments, was unsure about a relationship, and resented her parents. She compared herself constantly with peers who seemed to be finishing degrees, marrying, and starting careers. "I know what I need to do," she said in the first session.

"I just don't do it." Maya is a composite drawn from situations that recur in my practice. Details have been altered and combined so that the account describes no identifiable client.

Maya understood a great deal about herself. She could explain how her parents' expectations fed her perfectionism and how fear of failure led to procrastination. However, knowing why she struggled could become another reason to judge herself. If she understood this much, why was she still like this? A plan might help her complete an assignment, but following it could also become a test of whether she was capable of changing. Each unfinished assignment then seemed to confirm that something was wrong with her. Maya needed help writing the paper, yet our work kept returning to what her difficulty writing it seemed to say about her.

As we returned to this impasse, I began to feel ineffective and a little irritated. I prepared more structured interventions, hoping to help Maya find a way forward. That was a reasonable response to her difficulty, but I was also measuring my competence by whether she improved.

Her lack of progress made her seem like a resistant client and me a therapist who had not found the right intervention. We both needed something from her improvement. She needed evidence that she could change, and I needed evidence that I could help her change.

Counseling generally begins with a person who has a problem. The counselor's task is to help the person resolve it. Maya's difficulty suggested a further question. Finishing an assignment had become a way to prove that she was capable, while failing to finish it confirmed that she needed fixing. Each new strategy gave her another opportunity to succeed or fail at changing herself. The question is whether helping Maya finish her assignments was also reinforcing the idea that she needed to fix herself. I created Deconstructive Inquiry (DI) to examine how a self and its problem form together. I first presented the framework in the consciousness literature (Greenleaf, 2025).

Thought divides experience into fear and a self who appears to stand apart from it. When thought judges fear as weakness and demands strength, this self seems to exist already, as someone weak who has fear to overcome. But the judgment and the demand are what form this self: they make fear the weakness to correct and the self the weak one who must correct it. Thought invents the self. I call this the self-problem relation. The self described here is the one who must overcome fear, defend a belief, or bring uncertainty under control, not the whole person. The person's bodily life, memory, relationships, responsibilities, and ability to act extend beyond this struggle and can continue when the self no longer forms.

The same pattern formed when Maya compared her life with the life she believed she should already have. The comparison made her present life a problem to correct and formed the self who was behind and had to catch up. I call this claiming of experience centering. A feeling, belief, or situation is experienced as something a self has and must change or protect. The claiming forms the claimer. For Maya, that self took shape through measuring her life against an expectation and trying to catch up. The life she believed she should have was already shaping how she understood her life now and what she felt she had to do.

The question for counseling is whether this division can end while the client continues to feel and respond to what is happening. Maya might still feel afraid and work on the assignment without treating her fear as proof that she needs fixing. I also examine how the therapist's responses and the client's social circumstances shape this division.

Through this division, the person may feel that life cannot go on until the problem is solved. I use suffering to name the struggle between life as it is and life as the person believes it should or must be. Someone may believe they should already be happier or further along, or that they must recover the happiness they once had. They may also believe they can be happy only when a present difficulty ends. Their life now is judged against a remembered past or imagined future. Trying to hold on to present pleasure can create the same struggle, as the person fears losing it. Fear and grief can already be intense and require care. Someone hurt by rejection may also believe they cannot go on with life until the hurt is gone. The hurt then becomes the obstacle to living, and the self becomes the one who must overcome it. The effort to escape the hurt keeps forming the self whose life must wait for the hurt to end.

The Self-Problem Relation

The same division can form around a belief. A conclusion such as "I am intelligent" may become a position the person must maintain or defend. The believing forms the believer: the self whose position must be protected. A challenge to the belief then feels like a challenge to that self. The self seems to have existed before the struggle, but it takes shape through the effort to defend the belief. This is the owner-owned division: the apparent separation between the self and the belief it must defend.

Centering can also take the form of a demand for certainty. Someone insists, "I need to know that nothing will go wrong before I can relax." They feel uncertain now and believe they can be at ease only in a future where everything is secure. This demand forms the uncertain self, the one who must bring uncertainty under control. That self seems to stand apart from the search, but it takes shape through the search itself. The effort to become certain is uncertainty continuing in this form.

We use "I" and "my" for ordinary purposes. "My keys" identifies which keys belong to someone. "My anxiety" may simply tell the therapist how the client feels. Using "my" for both does not mean feelings are owned like keys. To tell whether centering is occurring, the therapist looks at what happens as the client tries to change the feeling.

A client wants relief because anxiety keeps them awake and they need sleep. They do not believe that ending the anxiety would prove their competence or let their life begin. Even so, as they try to end it, they may experience themselves as someone who stands apart from the anxiety and must control it. That is centering. The separate self is the anxious effort itself, not someone behind it. Therapist and client can examine how that self forms while also working on sleep. The inquiry itself should not become another demand: "Now I must stop struggling." Maya's life extends beyond the struggle to put herself right. Her bodily activity, memory, and relationships support her abilities and responsibilities. These remain whether or not an unfinished paper becomes proof that she is a failure. A person can still act when a self-problem no longer forms. If they recognize danger, they can leave. If a condition needs treatment, they can seek care. Life continues without the conflict thought creates between what is and what it demands should be. Gallagher (2000) uses agency to name the sense that one is causing an action. He uses ownership to name the sense that one is undergoing an experience. Neither sense, by itself, establishes a separate self who stands apart from the activity and directs it. In DI, ownership names the apparent separation between an experience and the self who takes shape through the effort to change or protect it.

Relational Grounding and Centering

A Relational Ontology

Ontology is an account of what reality consists of. A substance-based account begins with separate things and explains how they interact. A relational ontology understands the things themselves as organized patterns of relations. A whirlpool makes this easier to picture. It is water moving in a particular pattern. Different water passes through, but the whirlpool continues while that pattern holds together. The whirlpool is real as the organized movement of the water. The water itself is an organization of interacting molecules. Each water molecule is a pattern of relations among two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. The atoms, in turn, are organized patterns involving nuclei and electrons. Relational ontology extends this account to those constituents as well: at each level, things are understood through the relations that constitute them.

DI applies this account to the self who must solve a problem. When thought judges fear as weakness and demands strength, it forms the self who must overcome fear. This self is the pattern of judging fear and demanding its end. It continues as long as that pattern continues. Fear can remain when the self who must overcome it no longer forms.

DI uses grains to examine this change. A grain specifies which relations a description preserves and which details it leaves aside. We can describe the circulation of a whirlpool or examine the movements of the water that sustain it. Each description must track the actual organization of the moving water.

Mereology is the study of parts and wholes. This use of grain draws on refinement mereology. A finer pattern refines a coarser one when the coarser organization can be recovered by leaving aside some of the finer detail. The relations that define that organization must be preserved. An account of individual water movements, for example, must still account for the circulation identified as the whirlpool. DI draws on this approach to examine which relations The recognitional-categorical grain describes what the employee experiences. It is recognitional because their knowledge is operating as the experience. It is categorical because the experience is of a particular kind: the employee hears criticism and feels fear. They can feel afraid without naming the feeling or being able to explain why. Within DI, consciousness is this experience as it occurs, including the words, their meaning, and the fear.

Thought can also divide this experience into fear and a self who appears to stand apart from it. Fear becomes "mine to control." The employee may feel that life cannot begin until fear ends, or that they must keep fear away to remain acceptable. This demand makes fear the problem to overcome and forms the self who must overcome it. DI calls this claiming of experience the centering grain. The claiming forms the claimer. The self seems to direct the effort to end fear, but it is that effort experienced as someone separate from the feeling. The centering grain describes this formation of an apparent owner and the feeling it must control.

Knowledge Operating as Experience

DI proposes that consciousness is its content (Greenleaf, 2025). Seeing a color, hearing a voice, remembering an event, and feeling afraid are consciousness occurring. The content includes how each experience feels. Consciousness is not something separate that contains these experiences. DI explains them as recognition: knowledge operating as what is seen, heard, remembered, or felt. As U. G. Krishnamurti (1988) put it, "Without knowledge no experience of any kind is possible" (p. 51).

Much of this knowledge is learned, and it can change. If the employee described earlier finds that the supervisor now listens and offers help, a later question may feel less threatening.

The supportive encounter can change the knowledge through which later questions are understood and felt.

Knowledge, in this account, can also be inherited. Fendt (2006) found that rats with no prior exposure to predator odors responded defensively to predator urine but not to herbivore urine. This finding documents an unlearned defensive response. DI proposes that inherited knowledge can also operate as the experience of danger. Experience therefore need not depend on prior learning or language. I use thought in this broad sense of knowledge operating, whether inherited or learned and whether or not words are involved.

Language is one form of learned knowledge, and it can affect what people notice and distinguish (Lupyan et al., 2020). Russian speakers, for example, distinguished certain shades of blue more quickly when the shades fell into different categories in their language (Winawer et al., 2007), although later findings show limits to this effect (Martinovic et al., 2020). These findings support a role for language in perception. DI goes further: the knowledge operating in recognition is the experience itself.

As Maya pictured her classmates completing their work, her body tensed. What she had learned about achievement and falling behind was operating as her fear. Barrett's (2017) theory of constructed emotion offers a related account: categorizing bodily sensations through past experience is part of how an emotion forms. DI also examines how an apparent owner forms within this experience. When Maya treats fear as proof that she cannot manage her life, fear becomes a defect to correct, and the self becomes the one who must correct it.

Fear can remain when the self who must overcome it no longer forms. The fear still occurs through a particular body, history, and situation.

Maya experiences the assignment through what school and achievement have come to mean in her life. Another student may experience the same assignment differently. I use standpoint for the experience as it occurs through these particular relations. Standpoint is the experience itself, not a separate observer receiving it. Fear can have this felt perspective without forming a separate self who owns it and must bring it under control. Noncompletion occurs when claiming becomes recognizable without forming a separate owner. In the case of fear, what seemed to be a self controlling the feeling becomes recognizable as the effort to escape it. The division between fear and the self who must overcome it is not renewed. No separate observer decides to end it. Fear can remain, and memory and knowledge can still guide practical action.

How Centering Develops

In DI, the apparent owner of experience is a social construction. Through relationships, children learn to treat feelings as things a separate self has and must manage. This claiming forms the claimer. Caregivers address the child as someone who feels and wants, respond to distress, and reflect feelings through voice and expression. Reddy (2008) describes infants' awareness of others' attention in such exchanges. Gergely and Watson (1996) propose that caregivers' mirroring helps infants recognize their emotional states. DI suggests that these exchanges can also make it familiar to experience a feeling as belonging to a self who stands apart from it.

Language can reinforce this separation. A child hears "You are frightened" and learns to say "I am frightened," acquiring grammatical patterns through everyday exchanges (Tomasello, 2003). Parents and children also build accounts of past experiences together, and the child learns to speak of past events as things that happened to "me" (Nelson & Fivush, 2004). Such language can simply describe what happened or how someone feels, but it can also present a separate self who has that experience and must manage it.

DI proposes that children can learn to address and judge themselves as others have addressed and judged them. A child repeatedly told to stop being afraid may later think, "I should not feel this way. I must get rid of this fear." The demand now seems to come from a self who stands apart from the feeling. Yet that self is the learned activity of judging fear and demanding its end. What began as a demand between people continues as an apparent struggle between a self and its experience.

Psychological Time

Writing an assignment or learning a skill takes clock time. I use psychological time for the division thought makes between "what I am" and "what I must become, recover, or preserve." Krishnamurti (1969) argued that the effort to overcome this conflict only sustains it. If I must become fearless, the future I want is defined by the fear I want to escape now. For Maya, "I should already be further along" made her life seem late. The comparison formed the self who was behind and had to catch up. The change she wanted lay in the future, but trying to reach it kept that self and its problem active now.

A person who believes they must recover their old happiness compares life now with what they remember. The past supplies the image of a future they feel they must reach. Present pleasure can also become something that must continue. Thought imagines its loss, and the effort to preserve it brings fear into what is pleasurable. In both cases, the demand forms the self who must recover happiness or prevent its loss.

The past can also seem to rule out change: "I have always been this way, and I always will be." The person treats their past as proof that they cannot change. This judgment defines the self as someone whose future is already settled.

In each of these examples, the past and future enter only as images that thought forms now, and the judgment that defines the self is made in the present. Psychological time is therefore not a stretch of time the self must cross but the conflict itself, and the self forms each time thought measures the actual against an image. This is why Krishnamurti (1969) held that this conflict cannot end through a gradual effort to become free of it: working toward freedom over time is one more movement from "what is" toward "what should be," and it keeps that self in place. Clock time remains necessary: Maya's assignment still takes hours to write, and she can plan her work and continue while afraid. What need not continue is the demand that the outcome establish who she is or decide when her life can begin.

How the Self-Problem Relation Persists

Efforts to end a self-problem can keep it going. A setback seems to confirm "I need fixing," while improvement becomes proof that "I am finally putting myself right." Either way, the outcome remains a judgment about the self. For Maya, each unfinished assignment became further evidence that something was wrong with her. I could also use her progress to judge my competence. Completing the work and helping her complete it could then become ways of proving ourselves.

Wanting shows this clearly. A therapist asks, "What do you want?" and the client says, "I want to be free of fear." In centering, the wish for a future without fear forms a self who is still trapped in fear and must escape it. Fear itself becomes the threat to escape. The anxious effort to escape is experienced as a separate self responsible for ending the fear. Yet this effort is fear continuing as the attempt to become free of itself. At the centering grain, the relation can be stated directly: To want to be free of fear is the fear.

Understanding can also become part of the struggle. Maya understood why she procrastinated and believed she should therefore be able to stop. Her understanding became another reason to judge herself. A client may learn why they feel anxious and how to reduce it, and that knowledge may be accurate and useful. If staying in control is necessary to remain the person they want to be, each return of fear threatens that identity. They may feel less anxious while continuing to experience a separate self who has fear and must manage it. What they have learned can still help when this self no longer forms.

U. G. Krishnamurti (1982 Krishnamurti ( , 1988) ) argues that efforts to free oneself from the self can continue the activity they reject. DI is not exempt. When "I must become permanently uncentered" becomes the goal, inquiry turns into a demand to reach an ideal state and stay there.

The self forms again as the one who must achieve that state and prevent its loss.

Practical help remains important. Skills or medication may let someone face a difficulty that would otherwise overwhelm them. DI asks whether using this help also forms a self who appears to stand apart from the feeling and must control it. The same study plan can help someone finish a paper while also becoming a way to prove they are not a failure. The paper can still receive full attention when completing it no longer has to establish who the person is or become a condition they must meet before they can finally be happy. The encounter with Maya shows how this possibility can arise during the work itself.

I return to a session with Maya in which an unfinished paper had become evidence that she was the problem. She described losing most of her morning worrying about it.

She had opened the document, imagined her work would be inadequate, looked at social media, and gone back to bed. "I should be further along by now," she said. "I keep doing this to myself. I'm the reason my life isn't moving." Maya's struggle was organized in psychological time. She measured her life against the life she believed she should already have. The comparison made her present life unacceptable, while catching up promised a future in which she could finally be at ease. I asked, "What do you mean by further along?" "I should be finished with school. I should have a career. Everyone else seems to be getting somewhere." "And what would be different for you if you were there?" "I could relax. I could feel good about my life instead of always trying to get it together." Maya began explaining these expectations through her father and what achievement had meant in their family. I asked, "When you remember what he expected, what do you expect of yourself now?" "That I should be doing more. That I am wasting what I could have been." As she pictured her classmates completing their work, she described tightness under her ribs and heat in her face. What she had learned about achievement was already at work as the meaning of the unfinished paper and the fear. Her remembered expectations, imagined future, and sense of being behind belonged to this same experience. I asked, "What happens to the life you have while you try to reach that life?" "It feels like it is on hold. I have to sort all this out before I can enjoy anything." "What do you have to sort out?" "Me. I have to stop being like this." She began listing the paper, her degree, her relationship, and her age. I drew attention to how each difficulty had become another reason she needed fixing. "One unfinished paper has become your whole life," I said. "And now you're the person who must fix it. What do you do when you try to make yourself right?" "I look at what I should be doing. I tell myself to stop wasting time. I try to push myself." "And what happens as you do that?" "I become more frightened. There is so much I have to change." "You push yourself to change because you are afraid of staying as you are," I said. "Then you tell yourself that your whole life needs fixing, and you become more frightened. You're trying to get away from fear, but maybe trying to escape is what keeps you stuck."

We examined the effort she experienced as the self trying to bring everything under control. She compared her life with the life she should have, judged herself for falling short, and tried to escape that condition. Ownership can also form without a judgment of personal failure. "My anxiety is ruining my life" may describe serious limitations that need treatment. However, complete freedom from anxiety may also have become a condition for beginning the life the client wants. They may postpone choices they could make while anxious because they believe they must first become someone without fear. The self who cannot yet live takes shape through this demand. DI examines this requirement alongside the anxiety's actual limitations. Inquiry concerns both the help the person needs and what they expect relief to make possible.

Recognition and the Apparent Observer

Maya's report raises a question about the seeing itself. If she recognized how hurt was becoming a problem about who she was, had another self stepped back to observe it? Giving the task to a better observer would leave the same difficulty in place. Maya would need to observe herself correctly, and failing could become another reason to judge herself. DI therefore needs to explain how this pattern can be recognized without a separate self taking charge of ending it.

Maya may be thinking about how to answer her partner while still hearing what is said and feeling hurt. In DI, all of this is consciousness occurring. She does not also have to think, "I am hearing this," for the words to be heard and understood. Dretske (1993) and Tye (1999) likewise argue that experience can occur without awareness of having it. DI explains this through recognition: knowledge is operating as the experience, whether or not a further thought about experiencing it occurs. If Maya then notices her effort to put herself right, that effort has itself become something recognized. Recognizing the effort does not require a separate observer standing outside it. Nor does it necessarily form a self responsible for ending it.

The hurt had seemed to reveal a fact about someone already there: Maya ruined relationships and therefore had a life to repair. What became recognizable was that the demand for repair was also defining her as that person. The self who needed repair was this pattern of treating hurt as a verdict about her and demanding that she change. Recognition could include this pattern just as it included the hurt and the conflict with her partner. Maya could also respond, DI uses visibility for an event in which claiming becomes recognizable without completing into ownership. Maya could recognize how hurt was becoming a verdict about her without renewing the demand to repair her entire life. The owner had seemed to exist before this demand, but it was being formed through it. When the demand was no longer renewed, neither was that owner. Visibility and noncompletion describe the same event: the claiming becomes recognizable and does not form an owner-owned division. No separate observer first sees the division and then acts to remove it.

Put sharply: noncompletion is not something that happens to someone. It is the nothappening of the someone.

The someone in this statement is the particular owner formed within the encounter. Maya remained present and responsive, able to remember the conflict and discuss her relationship. The demand to repair her entire life was no longer defining who she was. The person continued, along with the capacities and relationships through which she could act.

Her hurt also remained situated within her life. It still concerned her partner, and her history still informed what the conflict meant. Knowledge continued as this recognizable experience, including its felt perspective. She could feel hurt and consider the conflict without renewing the claim that the hurt made her someone who ruined relationships.

Using I to describe this event does not establish that an owner had taken charge of the seeing. "I saw what was happening" ordinarily identifies the person telling us about it. We need to understand what they noticed and what they felt responsible for doing. The retelling is also a present encounter. A client may worry, "I need to know that I really understood," making the recollection another test they must pass. That demand is occurring now; it need not describe the earlier experience. Their report allows therapist and client to examine the difference.

I propose that insight into this claiming changes the knowledge through which a self and its problem form. Maya may again feel afraid about unfinished work, and the demand to change herself may begin again. However, that demand now occurs through knowledge altered by the earlier insight. The demand may no longer form that owner as readily. It may arise without forming an owner at all. This change does not depend on remembering the earlier encounter or trying to reproduce it.

A lasting change in this struggle does not mean that centering has ended throughout Maya's life. Other difficulties may still involve a self who must change or preserve an experience. The earlier insight may make these demands easier to recognize as they arise.

Therapist and client can examine whether she can address unfinished work without first having to correct who she is. If the same struggle continues unchanged, remembering or explaining the insight would not establish a lasting change.

Adjacent Approaches and DI's Specific Contribution

Maya's encounter can be understood through more than one therapeutic account. Several of the approaches considered here already treat the self as formed rather than given. I ask one question of each: when it works as intended, what happens to the self who appears to stand apart from experience as the one who has it and must manage it? A person's relationship to a thought or feeling can change while this apparent owner continues to form. DI examines how the observer forms within the activity it seems to observe (J. Krishnamurti, 1969). In noncompletion, that formation becomes recognizable without completing into an owner and what it owns.

Person-Centered Therapy

Rogers described a relationship in which the therapist is genuine and responds with unconditional positive regard and empathic understanding (Rogers, 1957 (Rogers, , 1961)). In this relationship, conditions of worth can loosen. The client can acknowledge feelings that once threatened their self-concept, and how they see themselves can come to agree more closely with what they feel. A client may admit feeling afraid without having to earn the therapist's acceptance by becoming fearless. Person-centered therapy already addresses the conditions through which clients judge their worth, including those the therapist's expectations introduce.

Contemporary person-centered writing also challenges the view that the approach treats people as isolated from social relations (Cornelius-White, 2025).

The change concerns how the client understands themselves in relation to what they feel.

They may now see themselves as someone who is allowed to feel afraid, rather than someone who must be fearless to be acceptable. Fear can be acknowledged within this changed selfconcept. DI asks how the image of oneself is being formed. The thought "I am someone who accepts fear" can give the apparent self a position to maintain. In noncompletion, Maya can feel afraid without having to become either the one who overcomes fear or the one who accepts it. A person-centered relationship can provide conditions in which this event becomes possible.

Gestalt and the Relational Field

Gestalt therapy understands self as the process of contact between organism and environment (Perls et al., 1951). Field-oriented work explains how self takes shape within the situation, including the relations through which therapist and client participate (Robine, 2011; Cavaleri & Iacono Isidoro, 2026). A Gestalt account would understand Maya's fear within these relations, including my part in them. Self is already being explained as activity within a field, rather than as someone who exists before contact begins. Perls et al. (1951) describe identification and alienation as functions of contact: what is accepted as one's own and what is set aside. Gestalt treats this identification as part of how self functions. DI asks whether the activity forms a self who appears separate from the feeling and responsible for changing it. This division cannot be inferred from the term identification alone. If fear becomes the condition of someone who must eliminate it before life can continue, the demand forms that apparent owner. When claiming does not complete, Maya can still feel fear, remain involved, and act. Noncompletion concerns this owner-owned division; it does not mean an unfinished gestalt or withdrawal from contact.

Decentering and Self-as-Context

Decentering concerns changes in how people relate to thoughts and feelings. Bernstein et al. (2015) identify three processes: meta-awareness, or noticing experience as it occurs; disidentification, or experiencing a thought or feeling without equating it with oneself; and reduced reactivity to thought content. A thought can be noticed as a mental event without being taken as a fact or a definition of who one is. Maya's thought that she was failing could still arise while having less influence on how she approached the assignment. Decentering is also studied as a momentary state in daily life (Naragon-Gainey et al., 2023). Suddenness does not distinguish DI. The question is whether the observing position continues.

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) addresses related concerns through defusion and self-as-context (Hayes et al., 2012). Defusion lets a thought remain present without dictating what the person does. Self-as-context is the perspective from which thoughts and feelings are noticed, explained through learned relations such as the distinctions between I and you or here and there. When ACT works, the person acts on their values with difficult thoughts and feelings present, observed from a context that remains stable while its contents change. ACT does not treat this context as an inner entity, and neither ACT nor DI treats the center as a substance. Even so, self-as-context is a sense of self distinct from and larger than its contents; in ACT's chessboard metaphor, the board holds the pieces and is not any of them (Hayes et al., 2012).

In Bernstein et al.'s (2015) model, likewise, the one who disidentifies from a thought remains as the one who notices it. DI asks how this observing self forms. ACT's self-as-content means the descriptions and evaluations a person holds about themselves. DI uses experienced content more broadly. It includes the felt sense of being the one who observes, as well as the thought or feeling observed. A quiet or accepting witness is therefore part of what requires explanation. When Maya recognized hurt becoming a verdict about her, no observer first stepped back and then withheld the verdict. The claiming became recognizable and did not complete. DI does not retain a witness as the self to which the rest of experience appears.

Constructivist and Narrative Approaches

Constructivist psychotherapy treats the self as formed rather than given. Kelly (1955) described how people use personal constructs, or ways of interpreting events, to make sense of their lives. The self is one such construct. Mahoney (2003) describes the self as an ongoing organizing process. Neimeyer (2009) explains how therapy reconstructs meaning when a person's way of making sense of life has broken down. Gergen (2009) locates the self within relationship: it is formed through what people do together. On this view, the bounded individual is a cultural tradition rather than a fact. Narrative therapy helps people distinguish themselves from their problems and develop a preferred account of their lives (White & Epston, 1990).

Anxiety can affect someone's life without defining who they are. Recent humanistic counseling scholarship also questions the assumption of a stable, authentic self (Lemberger-Truelove et al., 2024). DI shares the view that the self is formed in relation.

DI's account extends to inherited and bodily ways of recognizing, including experience before language. A child can see colors before learning to name them, and a gazelle may experience danger through inherited capacities. Neither experience requires a story about it.

Centering can occur without someone telling or revising a story. Externalization also distinguishes the person from the problem, allowing the person to oppose its influence. DI asks how telling the story or opposing the problem can form an apparent self who stands apart from the activity as the one directing it. When claiming does not complete, the person can remember what happened and describe their life without forming a self whose position the account must secure. The ability to tell a story continues. The owner whose position must be protected does not form.

Implications for Psychotherapy and Culture

Psychotherapy Training and Supervision: Centering in the Clinical Field

The therapist's participation in Maya's impasse has implications for supervision. A trainee may describe a client who has not followed through on agreed changes: "I've reflected the pattern, made plans with her, and challenged the avoidance. She still won't move. I think I'm failing her." This composite draws on situations that recur in my supervision. In the trainee's account, the client becomes the resistant one who must change, while the trainee's competence depends on making that change happen. My wish to provide an answer can also become a need to prove that I am the expert. The client's progress is being asked to establish who each of us is. Direct instruction or corrective feedback may still be needed. The supervisor remains responsible for assessing risk, evaluating competence, and protecting client welfare. Inquiry asks whether the need to prove ourselves is limiting our understanding of the client's goals, cultural context, or relationship with the therapist.

Culture, Identity, and Power

Culture helps shape what becomes recognizable and what a person feels required to do.

Family expectations may make disagreement feel like disloyalty, while expectations about age and achievement may make unfinished work feel like falling behind. These meanings belong to how the situation is experienced. Accounts in psychological anthropology and cultural psychology describe how self-understanding varies with social relationships and cultural meanings (Geertz, 1973; Markus & Kitayama, 1991; Rosaldo, 1984). Within DI, these expectations can also define the one who must meet them. The demand to be a loyal family member, for example, may make disagreement a defect to correct and the person someone who must suppress it to remain acceptable.

Power also shapes the positions people are made to occupy. A person may face discrimination or danger, and vigilance may accurately reflect those conditions. Institutions can keep imposing a position even when the person questions it. DI therefore examines both how a position becomes something the person must defend or escape and how others continue to impose it. Changing the client's interpretation cannot by itself remove these conditions.

Protection or collective action may be needed. Cultural affiliations and shared histories can support such action. Inquiry gives the therapist no basis for deciding that a client should relinquish a valued identity or justified anger. The client's account of harm deserves attention whether or not the demand to defend who they are is renewed.

Conclusion

DI proposes that this pattern can become recognizable without renewing the ownerowned division. Recognition continues: Maya can still feel afraid and understand what needs attention. What is no longer renewed is the demand that her work must repair who she is. When this division ends, the person continues, with their body, memory, relationships, and capacity to act. The sufferer, the one whose life depends on solving the problem, does not form. The freedom is not for someone. It is the absence of the one who needed freedom.
