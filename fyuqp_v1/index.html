Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Full text

Abstract

Developmental research has historically underrepresented families experiencing psychosocial adversity, partly due to barriers posed by in-person laboratory paradigms. Online, webcam-based methods may support more inclusive research, but their design must be informed by the communities they seek to include.

Four focus groups were conducted with caregivers of young children (n = 10) and professionals from child protection, health, and education settings (n = 12), exploring barriers and facilitators to participation, and attitudes toward online data collection. Transcripts were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis, with participant resonance checks.

Families expressed concerns about privacy and the use of children's images, while professionals described hypervigilance towards authority among multichallenged families. Developmental feedback and resources were valued over financial incentives, and online participation was viewed favourably, conditional on material and researcher support. Inclusive developmental science requires moving closer to families' spaces and priorities, for instance by running studies in places families already use.

1. Introduction

Developmental science has made substantial advances in understanding early cognitive, social, and emotional development, yet this knowledge remains disproportionately drawn from families in narrow sociodemographic and cultural contexts. Reliance on WEIRD convenience samples limits the generalisability of findings and the validity of developmental theories (Nielsen et al., 2017; Singh et al., 2023). This raises questions about whose experiences are represented, which contexts are treated as normative, and whether research methods are accessible and meaningful to diverse families (Broesch et al., 2023; Singh, 2024).

Families from lower-income backgrounds, younger and single parents, and minority groups are less likely to be recruited (Corr et al., 2024; Hurwitz et al., 2017). These patterns appear to reflect structural conditions (such as work schedules, and distance to research facilities). Conducting a study within early intervention centres in deprived areas, for example, yielded a considerably more diverse infant sample than a laboratory study that had specifically targeted diversity (Ballieux et al., 2016). Such conditions are particularly consequential in the first three years of life, when participation depends on caregivers balancing research with employment, household demands, and other family needs, and when their capacity to do so is shaped by access to childcare, social support, flexible work, and parental leave policies that differ across countries and social groups.

Calls to diversify infant research have therefore moved beyond recruitment towards addressing barriers embedded in research practices and infrastructure, such as how research is communicated, the accessibility of materials and procedures, and trust between researchers and communities (Garcini et al., 2022; Katus & Lloyd-Fox, 2025; Singh et al., 2023). Addressing these barriers requires rethinking the whole research process, from how questions are conceived to how engagement with communities is built and sustained, in line with their priorities and constraints (Broesch et al., 2023; Ghai et al., 2025).

Online methods which allow families to participate from home, may lower geographical and logistical barriers and include families for whom laboratory studies are less accessible (Zaadnoordijk et al., 2021). This is particularly relevant in Portugal, where well-equipped developmental laboratories are limited and concentrated in a few large urban centres. However, digital methods do not automatically make research more inclusive. As Ghai et al. (2024) noted, reaching more participants does not by itself yield a more representative sample. Participation may still depend on suitable devices, internet access, digital literacy, time, and a home environment suited to assessment; video recording of young children raises privacy concerns; and technical requirements or assumptions about family routines may create new forms of exclusion.

Recent reviews of strategies to improve participation converge on facilitators such as regular contact with study staff, flexible scheduling and home or community-based options, clear communication, returning results, and appropriate compensation (Corr et al., 2024; Litchfield et al., 2026). The HBCD study, a large US infant neuroscience cohort, implemented participatory principles by involving child welfare professionals in community advisory boards and employing study navigators with lived experience to engage families experiencing adversity (Harden et al., 2024). In Portugal, a cohort of very preterm children achieved high retention through flexible data collection, personalised contact, supported by parents' altruistic motivation (Marques et al., 2021). However, this evidence remains largely descriptive and researcher-derived, with strategies reported as what worked for a given team and little comparative evidence. Most studies also come from North American health research and include few lowincome or multi-challenged families (Corr et al., 2024; Litchfield et al., 2026).

Considerably less is known about how families themselves, and the professionals who support them, understand the conditions that shape participation. Parent-informed studies are beginning to highlight accessible and transparent procedures, trust built through relationships and institutions, cultural responsiveness, and sustained presence in community settings (Marques et al., 2021; Siddiqui et al., 2026). Professionals supporting families cannot substitute for caregivers' perspectives, but their experience with families in diverse circumstances may help researchers anticipate concerns and adapt procedures. To our knowledge, these perspectives remain largely absent from the literature, particularly for caregivers of infants and toddlers, in Southern European contexts, and among families in contact with child protection services.

The present study reports the first study of a larger project investigating attentional and socio-cognitive development in infants and young children across diverse geographic and psychosocial contexts in Portugal, using webcam-based online methods. This study examined community perspectives on barriers and facilitators to participation in developmental science research, in order to shape the recruitment strategy, materials and procedures of the subsequent laboratory and home-based studies.

2. Materials & Methods

>Materials & Methods

Participants

We conducted focus groups with two stakeholder groups. The first comprised 10 parents of young children, and the second comprised 12 professionals working with families and young children, including professionals of social and child protection, health (e.g. nursing and paediatrics), and early childhood education. Professionals were selected to cover a range of practice areas, including work with families exposed to adversity.

Based on an intentional sampling strategy, the participants were recruited using institutional, professional or personal networks, a recruitment flyer, email and instant messaging. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before the start of each group discussion through an online form (Microsoft Forms), including consent to audiovisual recording. Participants completed a brief sociodemographic questionnaire through the same form. Sample characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee [blinded for review]. Recordings were stored on institutional servers and deleted after transcription verification.

Procedure

Four Focus groups were conducted online via Google Meet in July 2026, two with parents, and two with professionals. Each session lasted approximately 75 minutes. A senior author moderated three groups and the first author one; the other acted as observer.

A semi-structured guide was developed by the research team, with parallel wording for parents and professionals. Sessions opened with an icebreaker in which participants shared their reasons for attending, which served as a bridge to the discussion topics. The guide then covered: (a) perceptions of or experiences with scientific research; (b) trust and privacy in the collection and use of personal data, including images and video; (c) practical and logistical barriers to participation; (d) forms of compensation considered fair and appropriate; and (e) participation in remote online studies, in which caregivers administer tasks (briefly illustrated with a video of a child completing a webcamrecorded looking task). The guide is available in Supplementary Material S1.

Sessions were recorded and transcribed using Google Meet functions. The first author checked each transcript against the recording and pseudonymised speakers (e.g., P1-P[n] for participants, M1-M2 for moderators) and any identifying details (e.g., names of children, services or places). Excerpts reported were translated from European Portuguese by the authors. In the excerpts, [...] indicates omitted text, and square brackets indicate clarifications or anonymised information added by the authors. Excerpts are identified by participant group, focus group number and participant code (e.g., parent, P3, FG2).

Data analysis

Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006 , 2019; byrne, 2022), supported by NVivo Version 14. We adopted a hybrid approach: coding was primarily inductive and at the semantic level, while the topics of the guide (barriers, including privacy and trust, facilitators, compensation, and online participation) provided a loose deductive frame. Transcripts were imported with speaker and group attributes (parents vs professionals), allowing patterns to be explored within and across groups.

The first author familiarised herself with the data through repeated reading of the transcripts. Initial coding was deliberately granular, yielding approximately 80 codes, clustered into candidate sub-themes and themes. A second researcher with expertise in focus group methodology and thematic analysis reviewed the coding and the candidate structure. The first author and the second researcher then met to jointly review the full coding framework, discussed each code, sub-theme, and theme, and developed a shared and consensual interpretation of the data. During this process, codes that were redundant were removed or merged into related sub-themes, and closely related codes were combined to form new sub-themes where this better captured the pattern of meaning.

Coding coverage was used only to check whether a pattern was shared across participants and sessions rather than raised by a single participant or within a single focus group. This process resulted in five themes and 20 sub-themes.

To check whether our interpretation resonated with participants, two first-person composite narratives, one for parents and one for professionals, were shared. The professional narrative was voiced by a professional working with multi-challenged families, while drawing in the perspectives of colleagues from other services. Narratives contained no verbatim quotes. Participants received them through an anonymous online form (Microsoft Forms) with a single question on whether the story reflected what had been shared in their group, answered on a four-point scale (not at all, a little, largely, completely). Both narratives are provided in Supplementary Material S2.

Researcher characteristics and reflexivity

The first author is a developmental neuroscientist with experience in infant laboratory and online data collection, and the principal investigator of the project these focus groups were designed to inform. Most participants were reached through her professional and personal networks, built through previous community-based research projects involving families and professionals working with them (e.g., in child protection and family support services). These existing relationships facilitated recruitment and openness. At the same time, they may have encouraged more favourable views of research. She is also the mother of a young child, which positioned her partly as an insider in relation to the parent participants and may have shaped which accounts felt most familiar during analysis. To mitigate these influences, three of the four sessions were moderated by a senior author with a background in psychotherapy research, therapeutic collaboration and qualitative thematic analysis. As she is not involved in the subsequent studies of the project, she provided an outside perspective on the interpretation. The coding framework was jointly refined between the two.

1.1. Distance between science and the community.

Parents noted that invitations to take part in research were uncommon, and most had never heard of laboratory-based developmental studies involving infants and young children. Some professionals noted that parents today are more receptive to evidence-based information, but that there is a gap between consuming evidence and seeing themselves as potential contributors to it. Limited understanding of how evidence is built was seen as reducing the value the general population places on taking part in research, and one professional suggested this might be particularly marked in Portugal, where scientific literacy and engagement in science are lower. As affirmed by a parent, "But now that I think about it, maybe for other families, I don't think it's even a matter of outreach (...) I think it might also be a lack of interest on the part of the parents themselves" (Parent, FG4, P3). Parents and professionals who already saw themselves as engaged with science tended to see this as an exception among the people they knew. Scientific literacy therefore emerged as both a barrier and a facilitator (see sub-theme 2.4.). Professionals working with multi-challenged families additionally reported that conveying the value and relevance of research to these families is particularly challenging, as their priorities often lie elsewhere (see Theme 4.2.). As said by a professional, "It's hard for us to explain to them how useful this is. I don't think they see it the same way." (professional, P5, FG1).

1.2. Concern for the child's well-being.

Participants worried that procedures might cause the child discomfort. Parents said they would be reluctant to take part if they anticipated any discomfort for their child, mostly referring to emotional comfort, such as interacting with unfamiliar people in an unfamiliar environment. As said by a parent, "If it were a study that involved the active participation of one of my children and could in some way cause them discomfort, that would be a deterrent for me" (parent, P5, FG2). In the same line, some professionals also anticipated this reluctance among the parents they work with, in relation to both physical safety (e.g., invasive neuroimaging) and emotional discomfort. For example, one of the professionals said: "For many parents, exposing a child to any kind of discomfort, even if it's mild, can sometimes be a challenge" (professional, P2, FG3).

1.3. Concerns about privacy and use of the child's image.

This was among the most discussed barriers. Participants feared that images could be misused or shared with third parties, and some were concerned about data security. Parents were clear that they would share data for scientific purposes but strongly opposed any commercial or media use. Participants also described feeling overwhelmed and uncertain about what they were consenting to, given the many data-sharing requests they face in daily life. For most parents and professionals, however, trust in the institution mitigated these concerns, based on research ethics and the perception that institutions have had to put substantial structures in place to comply with GDPR. For those more concerned, transparency about data management (e.g., for how long data would be kept, with a preference for keeping it for as short a time as possible), together with clear communication of its intended use, would be central to overcoming this barrier: "It's a cause for concern, of course. I think that coming from a credible institution (…) it provides some reassurance, and also understanding, if it's made very clear in the consent form exactly what the data will be used for, who will have access to it, and where it will be stored. I think just having that information explained clearly there already provides some reassurance, doesn't it?" (parent, P1, FG4). Professionals also shared experiences of parents' worries and resistance to image sharing in their own practice.

1.4. Fear of judgement about the child and parenting. Parents and professionals described a fear that the child's or the parent's behaviour would be evaluated, so that taking part could feel like being assessed as a parent. "I think that, in general, there's always a certain amount of uncertainty about whether we're being good parents or not. And the fact that we're in front of researchers probably adds a little more pressure in that regard. (…) Am I being a good parent or not? Can I do this?" (parent, P3, FG4) Professionals also mentioned a fear of learning something about their child or themselves that they did not wish to know, "(…) fear of being judged by others, of having issues that children might have scientifically identified, issues that parents aren't yet aware of." (professional, P4, FG3), and discussed its weight particularly for families in contact with child protection and social services (see subtheme 4.1.).

1.5. Logistical and family burden. Both groups described travel, time, disruption of family routines and the frequency of data collection as concrete obstacles. One professional described time concerns this way: "There's this issue of time; they're curious, but then, 'Oh, I'll have to read a lot,' or 'Oh, that takes too long.'" (professional, P4, FG4). Work schedules were also flagged as an important constraint. Parents who took part described themselves as privileged to have flexible working arrangements, which made them more available, but doubted that many parents around them had the same flexibility: "Maybe if it's during work hours, we might even be able to be more flexible, but that's because [partner's name] and I have that flexibility." (parent, P3, FG4) Theme 2. Facilitators

2.1. Building trust and ongoing relationships.

Participants described trust as something that develops over repeated contact with the same researchers and with the institution. Trust also depended on transparency and clear information about the study purposes and procedures. Having a reference person was considered key, with professionals stressing that "these families' participation exist on a relationship of trust with the person extending the invitation" (professional, P3, FG1); i.e., for multi-challenged families, a trusted intermediary (e.g., a professional already known to the family) was the only way to make participation possible (see sub-theme 4.1.).

2.2. Close, accessible and inviting participation settings.

Both parents and professionals suggested that linking research to spaces families already use and trust, such as day-care centres or health centres, would be a major facilitator. Some also proposed creating spaces designed for families, where parents and children could spend time together: "During the week there's sometimes a lot of free time that's lacking in things to do. (…) it could really be a draw if there were a sort of open-door space. Of course, people would have to understand that they'll be evaluated or guided by someone, but it could eventually function almost like a playground, almost like a space for children to play, where they could be observed.

Of course, with everyone's knowledge." (parent, P3, FG4).

2.3. Communication strategies and outreach channels.

Raised only by parents, this sub-theme covers how research should reach families. Most parents said that communicating well the purpose of a study, with a concrete picture of their contribution (which piece of the puzzle they would add), was crucial to their willingness to take part. Recruitment through trusted networks was considered key: the paediatrician or the childcare educator, a group of parents, and social media accounts and influencers. As one parent said "Science always ends up using much more traditional methods, doesn't it? [...] Now, I think we have to use the social media platforms that everyone uses, because I don't think there's any other way to reach people these days." (parent, P3, FG4). Influencers, however, were seen as a risky channel. The choice of person promoting a study would be decisive, and parents would accept or reject depending on whether that person's values on parenthood matched their own. Also, some parents added that any hint of a commercial connection would lead them to decline.

2.4. Personal motivations to participate.

Some motivations to take part were intrinsic.

Learning about their child's development and about their own parenting was a strong motivation. Participants also valued science and the societal benefit of knowledge creation, describing a moral sense of commitment and contribution, and identification with the cause. Scientific literacy thus appeared here as a motivator, mirroring its role as a barrier when absent (sub-theme 1.1.). Some parents, particularly those who had previously taken part in developmental science studies, also saw participation as a fun and novel experience for both child and parent: "I also know that it can be a lot of fun, by the way, to participate in experiments and studies with children." (parent, P4, FG4). This view surprised parents without such experience: "I hadn't thought of it that way, but it could be fun, right? Just another activity, going there, and they get to experience it too, don't they?" (parent, P2, FG4). This suggests that presenting research participation as a playful shared experience could make participation more attractive.

Theme 3. Compensation

Theme 3. Compensation

3.1.Facilitated access to healthcare and intervention.

Mentioned mainly by professionals, this referred to participation as a route to developmental screening, referral or support. In the words of one professional: "Anything related to interventions, partnerships with clinics that can help children with certain difficulties; that's what would make the most sense" (professional, P7, FG1). Discounts on treatments, or access to innovative interventions or multidisciplinary expert teams from academic settings, were seen as attractive forms of compensation.

3.2. Return of knowledge and results.

Participants valued information and training on the study topic, feedback on their own child, and a summary of the study's findings, The latter mattered because they wanted that their contribution was recognised as part of building something larger. As one professional put it: "Access to the results of that study would also be very important, because sometimes people participate and have absolutely no idea what happened. And I think people could know that they're taking part in something, but also that they'll get to know the result. I think that, in the future, it might even make people more willing to participate in new projects and everything else." (professional, P3, FG3)

3.3. Educational and community-belonging experiences.

Professionals and parents were very positive about cultural or educational activities as compensation, such as a family ticket to the zoo or a museum, that could also offer families a shared experience. Participants felt this aligned well with the educational mission of science. Participants also suggested creating a network of participating families, for example through a gathering at the end of the study. As said, "Also having a chance to share with other families, to feel included. I think so, that would be one of the things families usually really enjoy." (professional, P1, FG3). This was seen as a form of return that promotes a sense of identity with research and ongoing relationships, which in turn facilitates future participation.

3.4. Educational and meaningful gifts (e.g., themed book or toy).

A themed book or toy was valued as a thoughtful choice, for its educational and meaningful value. Both groups appreciated when the item was related to the research topic and accompanied by evidence-based information on that topic (related to sub-theme 3.2.). For example, a professional said: "In my view, an educational kit, a kit of activities designed to stimulate or raise awareness among families" (professional, P3, FG1).

3.5. Divergent views on monetary compensation.

This was the most contested topic. Some professionals saw financial compensation as fair, both as a way of making participation accessible to families with fewer resources and as recognition of time taken from work or household tasks. However, other professionals and some parents expressed moral discomfort. They worried that payment could compromise the authenticity and voluntariness of participation, as well as the institution's credibility and the educational and transformative mission of science: "Personally, if they told me about that, I wouldn't sign up. If they mentioned a voucher, I think it would lose its scientific credibility for me." (professional, P1, FG3). Some also noted that financial compensation could be perceived as a commercial transaction or conflict of interest, which would deter them from taking part: "I would probably immediately think that the study was being paid for by some brand behind the study, and so, for me, (…), that wouldn't be a positive thing" (parent, P4, FG2). Material goods (e.g., nappies, wipes, a snack at the end of the research visit) or a justification for work absence were suggested as a middle ground.

Theme 4. Family context as a constraint on participation 4.1. Hypervigilance toward authority and fear of consequences. Professionals working with families followed by child protection or social services described these families as living with constant hypervigilance and anxiety about being judged as parents and about the consequences for their family. As one professional explained: "They are naturally hypervigilant. So they will always think, no matter how much you explain that it's not the case. that it might have some kind of consequence" (professional, P5, FG1). Families might fear that what is observed in a study could be reported, used against them, or escalate their child protection process. Professionals worried that, if recruitment through protection services, families could feel unable to refuse or would not separate research from their support process, compromising voluntary participation. At the same time, professionals were firm that a reference person already close to the family was the only viable route to recruitment and engagement (see sub-theme 2.1.). This points to a tension between the role of trusted professionals as facilitators of participation and the risk that their involvement may compromise its voluntary nature and reinforce families' fears.

4.2. Perceived relevance of research given family priorities.

Professionals working with multi-challenged families reported that these families find it difficult to see the added value of research, as their priority is resolving day-to-day challenges (sometimes unmet basic needs). A professional described it this way: "One obstacle to participation is the question of what is truly a priority and relevant to these people, isn't it? Because in a context of vulnerability, participating in a scientific study, the benefit of doing so, is perhaps something that most people cannot grasp; they cannot see how their lives are actually positively impacted by being part of this type of research" (professional, P4, FG1). In contrast, professionals working outside the child protection system described a strong curiosity among today's parents to learn about their children and about parenting, and an active search for evidencebased knowledge: "Most are interested in consuming, so to speak, science; that is, they're interested in knowing the results of their practices in scientific terms, and they understand this very well." (professional, P2, FG3). As highlighted in Theme 1, parents similarly felt that openness to scientific knowledge and participation would depend on a family's scientific literacy and work demands.

Theme 5. Online modality

5.1.Initial receptiveness to the online modality.

Both groups reacted positively at first. Online sessions were seen as reducing burden (no travel, shorter sessions, asynchronous completion) and as more comfortable and safe for the child, with the caregiver leading the session in a familiar environment. One professional framed the participation as play like this: "I think parents would love it too, because I also think it's fun for them. It's a way for them to play with their child, I think. And I think the kid, the child, isn't aware that they're being studied; they're just playing." (professional, P1, FG3).

5.2. Challenges of caregiver-led administration.

Participants anticipated difficulties related to digital literacy and technical problems (e.g., internet connection, browser compatibility), access to suitable equipment (e.g., a laptop), and insecurity about running tasks without a researcher present (including the fear of facing technical issues). The latter is illustrated by a parent: "I think it would be the fear that the technology might fail, and then I'd be there alone at home, and suddenly it's not working, and the computer isn't working, and that's it." (parent, P4, FG4)

5.3. New concerns for the child's well-being.

The online format raised its own barriers. Participants worried that child's cooperation at home would be dependent on the child's temperament. A professional warned that "Just having the child sit in front of a screen to participate could already be a bit complicated" (professional, P7, FG1). Screen time was another concern: both parents and professionals described strict limits in current family routines. Finally, privacy concerns extended to the family home.

5.4. Technical support and guidance as facilitators.

Participants suggested that the presence of a support researcher or intermediary (remotely or in person), and clear instructions via demonstration videos would facilitate online participation. Participants also suggested making the format more flexible, for example through a platform where parents could return several times to complete trials, rather than going through the whole task in one session. This was seen as less demanding for both parents and children. For professionals, an in-person facilitator (e.g., a researcher visiting the home to run the task, or setting up the study within a service) remained crucial for participation of multi-challenged families, as explained by one professional: "The presence of someone in the home, someone from the research team, could help unlock situations where families either don't have the equipment or don't feel so comfortable carrying out the task." (professional, P5, FG1).

4. Discussion

The current study explored how caregivers and professionals in Portugal perceive families' participation in developmental research. Across five themes (barriers, facilitators, compensation, family context as a constraint on participation, and online modality), our findings suggest that participation depends on trust, perceived safety, and the fit between research and families' circumstances. Privacy concerns over the use of children's images were a salient barrier to parents; hypervigilance towards authority was raised only by professionals. Parents' discussions focused on motivation, communication, and trust, whereas professionals focused on structural constraints, hypervigilance, and compensation. This difference likely reflect proximity: professionals working with multi-challenged families see constraints that may be less visible among focus group parents, who likely represent more research-ready families.

Trust as the foundation of participation

Across themes, participants linked participation to intermediaries' or researcher's trust and presence in places families use. Participants described a distance between science and families and pointed to closer, accessible, and engaging spaces where families could encounter research as part of their everyday lives. Such spaces, designed for parents and children to spend time together, could double as settings for science dissemination and knowledge exchange, where taking part in research becomes a way for parents to learn about their child, and thus a form of compensation in itself. Siddiqui et al. (2026) also identified trust built through institutions, health professionals, and sustained presence in children's centres and community settings. Corr et al. (2024) and Litchfield et al. (2026) similarly found that familiar venues, culturally attuned community events, and continuity of contact with the same researcher supported recruitment. In Portugal, Marques et al. (2021) reported that face-to-face contact with the people behind a study mattered to families.

Privacy and the child's image

Whereas logistical constraints dominate much of the literature, privacy was a key barrier to our participants. This salience partly reflects our design, as privacy was a prompted topic and we highlighted the role of video-based methods in developmental neuroscience. Nonetheless, participants' concerns were specific: uncertainty about where data might travel, fear of data leaks, and a wish for data minimisation and timely erasure. Similar privacy concerns have been reported (Corr et al., 2024; Siddiqui et al., 2026). These expectations conflict with common research practice. Scientific and ethical considerations favour maximising each family's contribution, and in underfunded research systems there are strong incentives to collect more data than a single study requires and to retain them for future use. Infant video data are costly to obtain and valuable for secondary analyses but cannot be fully anonymised. Reconciling these positions will likely require stating openly who can access recordings, for how long, and for what purposes, so that families can make informed choices about reuse.

Multi-challenged families, hypervigilance, and relevance

Hypervigilance towards authority appeared only in professionals' accounts. This is likely due to self-selection, as the families described were unlikely to join parent focus groups. However, this converges with fear of child protection involvement reported among parents who use substances (Corr et al., 2024). Professionals stressed that while they can open access to families who are otherwise hard to reach, recruitment through services with authority over families risks compromising voluntariness. Researchers should state explicitly that participation is separate from case management and describe safeguarding procedures (including mandated reporting) transparently at consent. Community spaces not tied to authority-holding services might offer a lower-threat entry point, with professionals inviting families into these spaces; however, our data do not test this directly. Consistent with Harden et al. (2024), who argue that families facing multiple adversities need intensified rather than different strategies, professionals pointed to clearer explanation of research benefits, deeper relationship-building with families and their practitioners, flexible venues, and more thoughtful compensation.

Compensation

Views on monetary compensation diverged. Some participants saw payment as fair recognition and a means of accessibility; others feared it compromised voluntariness and the credibility of science. The most attractive returns were non-monetary: knowledge about the child and study results, educational and community experiences, and resources such as books and toys. Ballieux et al. (2016) found that gaze replay was particularly valued by lower-SES parents, suggesting a concrete return for eye-tracking studies such as ours. Although not explored directly, combining small returns (travel and childcare reimbursement, feedback on results, a souvenir such as gaze replay or a photograph of the infant in the research setting, and a modest material item or experience) may be fairer and more inclusive than any single form.

Making science visible

The distance we found between science and families, and the motivations described (learning about development and parenting, fun for the child, valuing science) show that participation is attractive when families see the benefit of it. Framing participation as an opportunity for parents to learn about and connect with their child may make research feel less like a duty and more like a shared experience. Precedents include "Learn About Your Baby" sessions in Children's Centres (Ballieux et al., 2016), laboratory playdates (Brand et al., 2019), and home-like research spaces with snacks and toys (Harden et al., 2024). Litchfield et al. (2026) likewise found that families preferred safe and friendly venues over clinical settings, with higher recruitment at community events. For communication, participants pointed the importance of making the concrete relevance of studies clear, and to social media as a channel (consistent with Siddiqui et al., 2026), with caution regarding influencers, whose values had to match their own.

Online participation

Participants were initially receptive to online participation, citing lower logistical burden, comfort at home, the caregiver leading the session, and its playful character. Participation depended on digital literacy and access to materials, and new concerns arose about the child's cooperation, screen time, and privacy at home. Facilitators included a support researcher or intermediary, shorter repeatable sessions, and demonstration videos. Intuitive participation flows may help caregivers feel confident running the tasks, while playful, game-like structures could make participation more rewarding, encouraging families to take part and to return. Because online tasks do not depend on a fixed setting, they could also be run in other venues, such as community spaces or services families already attend, with a researcher or trusted professional present. Families could then choose where to take part according to their needs and preferences. Such flexibility may be especially important for low-income and multichallenged families: Hurwitz et al. (2017) let families choose between home, lab or library sessions to accommodate work schedules, transport and sibling care.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. Combining caregivers' and professionals' perspectives allowed us to identify shared and distinct views, such as hypervigilance towards authority, which emerged only in professionals' accounts and would have been missed with parents alone. Including child protection professionals was particularly valuable, as they offered insight into the circumstances of families who rarely take part in research.

The study also addresses a Portuguese context that is underrepresented in the literature, which has been dominated by North American and Northern European samples. Finally, the interpretation was developed collaboratively by two researchers with different backgrounds and checked with participants using composite narratives, extending participants' engagement to the interpretation stage, which is still uncommon in the literature (Wagman et al., 2025).

Several limitations should also be considered. Parents who volunteered for focus groups were likely more research-engaged than the broader population, and their views may underrepresent the barriers faced by families who are less familiar with research.

Although professionals helped to bridge this gap, they spoke on behalf of families, and their accounts may reflect their own professional frameworks. Findings may not transfer directly beyond the Portuguese context, given differences in research culture, service structures, and data protection norms. Finally, our discussions focused on initial participation, and retention in longitudinal studies was not addressed. Future work should include families recruited through community rather than research channels and examine how perceptions change once families have taken part.

Recommendations

• Sharing research in trusted community venues and creating or repurposing family spaces (community centres, libraries, health centres) as hubs for dissemination, knowledge exchange, and participation, including as entry points to online studies; • Communicating transparently about data and image policies addressing minimisation and retention; • Engaging intermediaries as partners who invite families to participate, with research clearly separated from case management; • Implementing online protocols that allow caregiver-, intermediary-, or experimenter-led setups; • Designing compensation mixing knowledge return, practical goods, and cost coverage.

5. Conclusion

In our study, caregivers and professionals described participation as valued when research was credible and close to families (through institutional credibility, trusted intermediaries, and a presence in communities) and when it offered something meaningful in return (a clear purpose, the return of knowledge, and perceived benefit to the child). At the same time, concerns about privacy and the use of children's images, hypervigilance towards authority among families in adversity, and competing day-today priorities show that these conditions are not equally met for all families.

Inclusive developmental science therefore requires bringing science into the spaces where families live and play, so that taking part in research becomes part of how families learn about and enjoy their children. For online developmental science, this involves transparent data practices, human support for remote participation, and sustained community presence.

Such approaches, particularly with underserved families, demand time and resources that current funding models rarely accommodate. Funders and policymakers should recognise the investment needed to bring developmental science closer to families (Litchfield et al., 2026; Wagman et al., 2025).
