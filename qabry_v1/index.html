Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Children born preterm are at increased risk for attentional, social and emotional challenges, collectively referred to as the preterm behavioral phenotype. While white matter alterations have been linked to individual behavioral domains following preterm birth, it remains unclear whether distinct dimensions of behavioral variability are associated with specific patterns of white matter organization. Understanding neurobiological risk markers for preterm phenotypic traits may help identify children at greatest risk.

Objective: We investigated the extent to which these behavioral traits covary across the gestational age spectrum and whether latent behavioral dimensions are associated with white matter micro-and macro-structure in young children born preterm.

Methods: We collected behavioral and diffusion MRI data from 58 children born preterm ( 29females; mean age = 5.64 ± 0.99 years; GA range = 24-36 weeks) and 85 full-term children aged 4-8 years (44 females; mean age = 5.91 ± 0.95 years; mean GA = 39.26 ± 1.24 weeks). Principal component analyses were performed separately in preterm, full-term, and combined groups to characterize latent behavioral dimensions spanning autism-related traits, ADHD symptoms, and emotional functioning. Fixel-based analysis was then used to examine associations between behavioral dimensions and fiber density and fiber cross-section, adjusting for demographic and perinatal factors.

Results: In the preterm sample, autism-and ADHD-related behaviors emerged as relatively distinct dimensions, whereas behavioral variance in the full-term sample was captured by a broad neurodevelopmental dimension. In children born preterm, higher scores on the ADHD-related behavioral dimension were associated with reduced fiber cross-section in the splenium of the corpus callosum. No significant associations were found for fiber density in the preterm or fullterm samples.

Conclusions: These findings suggest that prematurity influences not only behavioral outcomes, but also how those outcomes relate to white matter organization, highlighting the importance of considering behavioral heterogeneity when investigating brain-behavior relationships in preterm populations.

Background

Preterm birth, defined as birth before 37 weeks of gestation, affects approximately 13 million infants worldwide each year (Ohuma et al., 2023). Children born preterm experience a heightened risk of persistent neurodevelopmental difficulties, with the severity of this risk increasing with earlier gestational age at birth (Aarnoudse-Moens et al., 2009; Ding et al., 2020; Moore et al., 2012). In addition to cognitive and motor challenges (Aarnoudse-Moens et al., 2009; Bhutta et al., 2002; Moore et al., 2012), children born preterm are two to three times more likely to develop autism (Crump et al., 2021; Johnson et al., 2010), approximately three times more likely to develop attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) (Franz et al., 2018), and two to three times more likely to develop anxiety disorders (Fitzallen et al., 2021) than full-term peers. Despite these group-level associations, developmental outcomes following preterm birth are remarkably heterogeneous (Burnett et al., 2011; Elgen et al., 2002; Mathewson et al., 2017). This substantial variability underscores the need to identify predictive factors that may help explain why some children born preterm develop behavioral difficulties, whereas others do not.

Autism-related traits, attentional difficulties, and emotional symptoms have been consistently found to be elevated in children born preterm, leading Johnson and Marlow (2011) to propose the concept of the preterm behavioral phenotype. This framework describes a characteristic pattern of attentional difficulties, autism-related social characteristics, and internalizing symptoms, often at subclinical levels (Fitzallen et al., 2020; Johnson & Marlow, 2011). Although co-occurrence across these domains has been established in preterm populations (Fitzallen et al., 2020; Johnson et al., 2010; Johnson & Marlow, 2011; Ritchie et al., 2015), datadriven profiling studies suggest that they are not uniformly co-elevated, with children born preterm exhibiting heterogeneous combinations and severities of behavioral difficulties (Burnett et al., 2019; Fitzallen et al., 2020; Lean et al., 2020). Although autism-related, attentional, and emotional traits have been shown to covary in general-population samples (Gordon-Lipkin et al., 2018; Shakeshaft et al., 2023; Stergiakouli et al., 2017; van der Meer et al., 2017), it remains unclear whether and how preterm birth influences the organization of these relationships.

Characterizing the extent to which these traits covary may provide a more nuanced understanding of behavioral heterogeneity following preterm birth and a foundation for investigating the underlying neurobiological correlates of shared symptom dimensions.

The third trimester is characterized by rapid axonal growth, oligodendrocyte maturation, and the establishment of structural connectivity, making white matter particularly vulnerable to disruption following preterm birth (Back, 2017). As white matter pathways support communication between distributed brain regions involved in attention (Hadaya et al., 2023; Vanes et al., 2021), social processing (Wang et al., 2018), and emotional regulation (Aarnoudse-Moens et al., 2009; Bhutta et al., 2002; Lee et al., 2021; Li et al., 2025), alterations in their development following preterm birth may contribute to the behavioral difficulties observed.

Several diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) studies have examined brain-behavior associations in children born preterm by investigating individual domains of behavioural functioning including autism-related traits, ADHD symptoms, anxiety, or social functioning (Hosoki et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025; Loe et al., 2013; Murray et al., 2016). Research using diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), a common approach for modeling DWI data, has shown that attentional difficulties and internalizing symptoms are associated with altered white matter microstructure across multiple major tracts, including the corpus callosum, cingulum, superior and inferior longitudinal fasciculi, inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus, anterior thalamic radiation, and fornix (Loe et al., 2013). In contrast, comparatively few studies have examined autism-related traits -although altered white matter microstructure in the left external capsule and superior fasciculus has been associated with higher overall autism screening scores (Skranes et al., 2007). Most diffusion MRI studies have examined these behavioral domains separately, rather than considering their co-occurrence within children born preterm. Consequently, despite some neuroimaging work beginning to use multivariate behavioral profiles to group individuals (Hadaya et al., 2023; Hadaya et al., 2025; Lean et al., 2020), relatively little is known about whether white matter organization relates to latent behavioral dimensions of the preterm behavioral phenotype.

Data-driven multivariate approaches can provide a means of characterizing this covariance by leveraging relationships across multiple behavioral domains to identify latent patterns of behavioral variability (Durham et al., 2023). Several studies have adopted data-driven approaches (e.g., latent profile analysis) to characterize behavioral heterogeneity following preterm birth, identifying behavioral profiles characterized by elevated co-occurrence of ADHD, autism-related and anxiety symptoms (Burnett et al., 2019; Fitzallen et al., 2020; Lean et al., 2020). Some studies have further related these data-driven behavioral profiles to brain measures, including neonatal brain volumes (Lean et al., 2020) and functional connectivity (Hadaya et al., 2025). However, these approaches characterize behavioral heterogeneity by assigning individuals to discrete profiles and examining neural differences between those groups. Although this framework is valuable for identifying subgroups with similar behavioral presentations, it does not directly characterize how continuous patterns of covariation across behavioral domains relate to individual differences in brain structure. In contrast, approaches such as principal component analysis (PCA) can characterize continuous patterns of covariation across behavioral measures, allowing individuals to vary along multiple behavioral dimensions. Whether such continuous behavioral dimensions are associated with white matter organization following preterm birth remains unknown.

Early childhood provides a unique opportunity for investigating these relationships, as white matter continues to undergo substantial maturation during this period while differences in white matter development associated with preterm birth can persist beyond infancy (Kelly et al., 2023). Concurrently, neurodevelopmental and behavioral difficulties become increasingly apparent during this age range. To investigate these emerging brain-behavior relationships, we combined PCA with fixel-based analysis (FBA), which provides fiber-specific measures of white matter organization (Raffelt et al., 2017), in a sample of preterm and full-term children aged 4-8 years. PCA was used to characterize continuous behavioral dimensions spanning autism-related traits, ADHD symptoms, and emotional functioning, while FBA was used to examine whether these dimensions were associated with white matter fiber density and fiber cross-section after accounting for gestational age. By integrating a dimensional behavioral framework with advanced diffusion MRI, we aimed to identify white matter markers associated with different dimensions of behavioral vulnerability following preterm birth and determine whether these brain-behavior relationships were specific to children born preterm or representative across the gestational age spectrum.

Methods

Participant recruitment

Data for the current study were collected as part of a longitudinal study investigating early childhood brain development in children born preterm. 83 children born preterm (42 females, < 37 weeks GA) and 137 children born full-term (79 females, ≥ 37 weeks gestational age) ages 4-8 were recruited into this study at the Alberta Children's Hospital, in Calgary, Canada. 25 preterm participants were recruited from the local Neonatal Follow-up Clinic, while other preterm and full-term children were recruited through community advertisements.

Participant visits included neuroimaging and behavioral and cognitive assessments. All participants were screened for history of head trauma or any contraindications to MRI scanning.

Participants were excluded if they had seizure disorders, cerebral palsy, bilateral neurosensory hearing loss, and severe bilateral blindness. Full-term participants additionally had no psychiatric or neurological diagnoses. This study was approved by the Conjoint Health and Research Ethics Board at the University of Calgary. Informed consent was obtained from the parents and informed assent from the participants.

After exclusions, the final sample included 58 preterm (29 females; mean age = 5.64 ± 0.99 years; mean GA = 30.85 ± 4.20 weeks) and 85 full-term children (44 females; mean age = 5.91 ± 0.95 years; mean GA = 39.26 ± 1.24 weeks). Exclusions were due to incomplete MRI acquisition (preterm N=2; full-term N=7), incomplete behavioral data acquisition (preterm N=10; full-term N=22), and poor-quality MRI data (preterm N=2; full-term N=7). In addition, the original sample included 14 sibling or twin pairs (11 preterm pairs and 3 full-term pairs) and one full-term set of triplets. Because the software used for FBA does not support modelling of random effects, only one child per family was retained in the final analyses, with the sibling exhibiting the least head motion during MRI acquisition selected for inclusion.

Demographic information and perinatal characteristics

For preterm participants, demographic and perinatal information was collected using parent-or guardian-completed questionnaires. Developmental information was collected from parents or guardians, supplemented by clinical chart review for children recruited through the Neonatal Follow-up Clinic. For full-term participants, demographic and perinatal information was collected through parent report.

Behavioral data

Behavioral assessments included the Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ) (Auyeung et al., 2008), the SNAP-IV 26 (Bussing et al., 2008), the Children's Alexithymia Measure (CAM) (Way et al., 2010), and the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS) (Crawford & Henry, 2004). The selected questionnaires assess behavioral domains commonly associated with preterm birth in pediatric populations, including autism-related traits, attentional difficulties, hyperactivity, and emotional dysregulation, with higher scores reflecting greater behavioral difficulties. The AQ provides separate subscales indexing distinct autism-related characteristics, allowing assessment of trait-level variation across social, communication, and attentional domains. We used scores from the attention switching (AQ-AttnSw), attention to detail (AQ-AttnDet), social skills (AQ-SS), imagination (AQ-image), and communication skills (AQ-Comms) subscales. The SNAP-IV is a widely used and validated measure of ADHD-related symptoms that can separately quantify inattentive (SNAP-I) and hyperactive (SNAP-H) behaviors. The CAM and PANAS capture emotional regulation and affective tendencies. Only the negative affect component (PANAS-NA) of the PANAS was used, as it more specifically reflects emotional dysregulation and anxiety-related traits.

Neuroimaging data acquisition

Participants completed two assessment sessions. The first included developmental data collection, behavioral and cognitive assessments, and a mock scan. The mock scan was designed to familiarize the child with the scanning environment, where they practiced remaining still while listening to simulated MRI scanner sounds. Parents were advised to guide their child in practicing staying still at home before the actual MRI scan.

The second visit involved the collection of diffusion and structural MR images.

Participants chose a movie, show, or internet video to watch during the scan. Strategies were used to minimize head motion, such as stabilizing foam pads. MRI data was acquired on a 3T GE MR750w (Waukesha, WI) scanner equipped with a 32-channel head coil. Diffusion images were collected using a 2D spin-echo EPI sequence (two runs, one at b = 1000 s/mm² and one at b = 2000 s/mm², 45 diffusion-weighted directions, 3 non-DWI (b = 0 s/mm²), 23 × 23 cm FOV, 2.5 mm isotropic voxels, 45 slices, TR = 10 s, TE = 86.2 ms). Structural T1-weighted images were also acquired using a T1-weighted 3D BRAVO sequence (24 × 24 cm FOV, 0.8 mm isotropic voxels, TI = 600 ms, flip angle = 10°).

DWI and structural image preprocessing

Diffusion-weighted imaging data preprocessing was conducted using in-house scripts that employ routines from MRtrix3 (Tournier et al., 2019) and FSL (Andersson et al., 2016; Jenkinson et al., 2012). FSL's Eddy was used to correct for eddy current-induced distortion and head motion, as well as detection and replacement of slices with signal dropout. DWI volumes with over 20% of slices with signal dropout were removed from the dataset. If scans had more than 10% of volumes removed, they were excluded from analyses (resulting in the exclusion of 2 preterm scans and 4 full-term scans). MRtrix3 was used to combine b=1000 s/mm² and b=2000 s/mm² shells, perform bias field correction using bias fields estimated from the mean b = 0 s/mm 2 images, and upsample all images to 1.25 mm³ to improve output contrast.

Intracranial volume (ICV) was estimated from structural T1-weighted images to account for age-related differences in head size that may influence morphological white matter metrics such as fiber cross-section. Global ICV was generated using the FreeSurfer (version 7.1.1) reconall pipeline for tissue segmentation and estimation of tissue compartment volumes (Fischl, 2012).

FBA processing

To obtain fixel-based metrics of fiber density and fiber cross-section, the MRtrix3 FBA pipeline was used. A fixel represents a specific fiber population within a voxel, allowing multiple crossing fiber populations to be distinguished within the same voxel (Raffelt et al., 2017). Fiber density reflects the intra-axonal volume of a fiber population, whereas fiber cross-section reflects the macroscopic cross-sectional size of the fiber bundle. Each participant's scan was used to generate three-tissue response functions representing white matter, gray matter, and cerebrospinal fluid. Response functions were averaged across participants to create a group response function, which was then used to perform multi-shell multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution (MSMT-CSD) to estimate white matter fiber orientation distributions (FOD).

Next, joint bias field correction and global intensity normalization of the multi-tissue compartments were conducted. A FOD template was generated using data from 20 preterm and 20 full-term participants (matched for age and sex) that had good quality, low-motion scans. This template was used to align all participants' FOD images, where they were then transformed into template space and segmented to estimate fixels. Template fixels were used to mask each participant's fixels. Following this, fiber density and fiber cross-section, which are expressed in arbitrary units, were computed. Fiber cross-section values were log-transformed prior to statistical analysis, following the standard MRtrix3 FBA processing framework. Connectivitybased fixel enhancement (CFE) was performed using MRtrix3's fixelcfestats to assess wholebrain fixel-wise associations. Statistical inference was conducted using non-parametric permutation testing with family-wise error correction.

In addition, region-of-interest (ROI) analyses were performed within masks of tracts previously implicated in research examining preterm white matter injury and neurodevelopmental vulnerability (Kaur et al., 2025; Kelly et al., 2020; Pannek et al., 2018; Pecheva et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2024), including the corpus callosum (CC), corticospinal tract (CST), inferior longitudinal fasciculi (ILF) and inferior fronto-occipital fasciculi (IFOF). Tracts of interest were delineated using TractSeg on the population template to generate tract orientation maps, where probabilistic tractography was conducted with 10 000 streamlines to produce tractograms. From these tractograms, fixel maps were generated to create masks for the tract-specific fixel-wise analyses.

Analyses of demographic data and behavioral dimensions

Differences in demographic characteristics between the preterm and full-term samples were assessed using independent-samples t-tests for continuous variables and Pearson's chisquare tests for categorical variables. Group differences in behavioral measures were evaluated using Welch's independent-samples t-tests. Differences in variance between the preterm and fullterm groups were assessed using Levene's tests. To account for multiple comparisons across the nine behavioral measures, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR).

To characterize latent dimensions of behavioral traits, PCA was first performed separately in the preterm and full-term groups using the R package FactoMineR (version 2.11; (Lê et al., 2008)), allowing the behavioral structure of each group to be characterized independently. PCA was then performed in the combined preterm/full-term cohort to characterize behavioral dimensions across the full group. The distinct principal component (PC) loading patterns identified across the three samples motivated separate examination of these behavioral dimensions in subsequent neuroimaging analyses. The first two PCs were retained for subsequent analyses based on inspection of the scree plots, which demonstrated a clear elbow between the second and third components across analyses. Together, the first two components collectively accounted for approximately 50-60% of the total variance, while subsequent components explained substantially smaller proportions of additional variance. The interpretability of the corresponding loading patterns was also considered when determining component retention. Spearman rank correlations were performed separately for the preterm and full-term groups to characterize relationships among the behavioral measures included in the PCA and to examine associations among demographic variables, perinatal variables, and principal component scores. Correlation matrices were generated and visualized in R using the Hmisc (version 5.2.3; (Harrell, 2026)) and GGally (version 2.2.1; (Schloerke et al., 2024)) packages.

Differences in corresponding Spearman correlation coefficients between the preterm and full-term groups were evaluated using Fisher r-to-z transformations. FDR correction was applied across pairwise between-group correlation comparisons.

Analyses of white matter / behavior associations

Retained PC scores were extracted for each individual participant and used as continuous predictors in subsequent neuroimaging analyses within the preterm or full-term group as appropriate. Associations between principal components and white matter metrics were tested using general linear models implemented in MRtrix3. Fiber density and fiber cross-section were modeled separately for whole-brain fixel-wise analyses and tract-specific analyses. Models included the relevant behavioral principal component as the predictor of interest and adjusted for age at scan, sex, gestational age at birth, birthweight adjusted for gestational age and sex, maternal education (a proxy for socioeconomic status), and days on mechanical ventilation (as a proxy measure for perinatal complication burden). Maternal education was coded ordinally as0 = did not complete high school,1 = high school education,2 = college/trade school/diploma,3 = undergraduate degree, and4 = professional/graduate degree. ICV was additionally included as a covariate in fiber cross-section models.

Results

Participants

There were no significant differences between children born preterm and children born full-term in sex distribution, age at scan, or maternal education (Table 1). As expected, children born preterm had significantly lower gestational age, lower birthweight, smaller intracranial volume, and greater perinatal burden than children born full-term.

Behavior scores

Children born preterm demonstrated significantly higher SNAP-I and SNAP-H scores compared with children born full-term (SNAP-I: t(106.05) = 3.76, FDR-adjusted q = 0.003; SNAP-H: t(104.72) = 3.42, q = 0.004; Table 2; Figure 1). Most AQ scales were not significantly different between groups; although children born preterm had higher AQ-CommS and AQ-image subscale scores at the uncorrected threshold (p < 0.05), neither remained significant following FDR correction (both q = 0.090). No significant group differences were observed for the remaining AQ subscales, CAM scores, or PANAS negative affect scores at the uncorrected threshold or following correction for multiple comparisons (all q > 0.05).

Between-group differences in behavioral score variance were additionally assessed using Levene's tests. Although greater variance was observed in the preterm group for several behavioral measures, no between-group differences in variance remained significant following FDR correction across the nine measures (all q ≥ 0.096; Table 2).

Correlations among behavioral scores

To characterize relationships among the behavioral measures included in the PCA, Spearman correlation matrices were generated separately for the preterm and full-term groups (Figure 2). Within the preterm group, autism-related traits showed strong positive correlations among several AQ subscales (ρ = 0.59-0.77). SNAP-I and SNAP-H scores were also strongly correlated (ρ = 0.73; Figure 2A). In contrast, correlations between SNAP scores and AQ subscales were generally weak (|ρ| ≤ 0.23). CAM scores likewise showed negligible correlations with AQ subscales (|ρ| ≤ 0.09) in the preterm group, indicating minimal overlap between these behavioral measures (Figure 2A). Within the full-term group, more consistent positive correlations were observed across ADHD, autism-related, and emotional measures (Figure 2B).

For example, AQ-CommS was moderately correlated with both SNAP-I (ρ = 0.52) and SNAP-H (ρ = 0.46), while CAM was moderately correlated with AQ-CommS (ρ = 0.59) and AQ-AttnSw (ρ = 0.46). Direct statistical comparisons of corresponding correlation coefficients between groups supported several of these differences in cross-domain covariance. The associations of AQ-CommS with both SNAP-I (PT: ρ = 0.06; FT: ρ = 0.52; z = -2.98, q = 0.033) and SNAP-H (PT: ρ = -0.01; FT: ρ = 0.46; z = -2.91, q = 0.033) were significantly stronger in the full-term group.

Similarly, CAM showed significantly stronger correlations with AQ-CommS (PT: ρ = 0.09; FT: ρ = 0.59; z = -3.22, q = 0.031) and AQ-AttnSw (PT: ρ = -0.01; FT: ρ = 0.46; z = -2.83, q = 0.033) in the full-term group. Thus, despite showing generally lower variability in scores -which could be associated with lower correlations between scores through range restriction (Carretta & Ree, 2022) -the full-term group instead demonstrated greater correlations across behavioral domains whereas cross-domain associations were comparatively weak in the preterm group. Green boxes outline correlations between ADHD symptom scores and autismrelated subscales, as well as correlations between CAM and autism-related subscales, illustrating comparatively stronger cross-domain associations in the full-term group than in the preterm group.

Principal component analysis of behavioral traits

In the preterm-only sample, PC1 and PC2 explained 37.5% and 22.7% of the total variance, respectively (Figure 3A). The loading structure demonstrated a clear separation between behavioral domains; PC1 was predominantly driven by autism-related traits, with strong positive loadings from the AQ subscales, whereas SNAP-I, SNAP-H, and CAM contributed primarily to PC2. This pattern indicates that behavioral variability in the preterm sample was organized into relatively distinct dimensions reflecting autism-related and ADHD characteristics.

In the full-term only sample, the first two PCs explained 43.0% (PC1) and 14.8% (PC2) of the variance (Figure 3B). PC1 was characterized by positive loadings on nearly all behavioral measures, consistent with a general behavioral dimension including autism-related traits, ADHD symptoms, and emotional regulation. PC2 included autism-and ADHD-related traits, with autism-related measures loading positively and ADHD/emotional regulation measures loading negatively. However, compared with the preterm sample, autism-and ADHD-related measures exhibited greater overlap in PC1 and far less distinct separation across the first two components.

In the combined preterm and full-term sample, the first two PCs accounted for 38.5% (PC1) and 19.3% (PC2) of the variance (Figure 3C). PC1 represented a broad behavioral dimension, with autism-related traits, ADHD symptoms, and emotional regulation measures all loading in the same direction, indicating that higher PC1 scores reflected greater overall expression of behavioral traits across domains. In contrast, PC2 captured a differentiation in behavioral profiles with ADHD traits loading positively and autism-related traits loading negatively. Thus, higher PC2 scores appear to reflect a behavioral profile characterized by relatively greater ADHD-related symptoms in relation to autism-related traits. Spearman correlations among demographic variables, perinatal variables, and principal component scores were also examined to assess relationships between PCAderived behavior scores and predictors included in general linear models (Supplementary Figure S1). Across all groups, principal component scores demonstrated generally weak correlations with demographic and perinatal variables. In the preterm cohort, neither the autism-weighted (PC1) nor ADHD-weighted (PC2) dimension was significantly associated with gestational age or other perinatal variables. These findings suggest that gestational age alone did not account for the observed behavioral variability within the preterm cohort.

Associations between behavior dimensions and fiber metrics -Preterm-only analysis

No significant associations were observed between either the autism-weighted dimension (PC1) or the ADHD-weighted dimension (PC2) and fiber density in whole-brain or ROI analyses. Likewise, no significant associations were observed between the autism-weighted dimension and fiber cross-section.

The ADHD-weighted dimension showed no significant associations in the whole-brain fiber cross-section analysis. However, ROI analysis revealed a significant negative association with fiber cross-section in the corpus callosum, with significant fixel clusters localized to the splenium (108 significant fixels; FWE-corrected p < 0.05; Figure 4A). Our findings suggest that higher expression of the ADHD-weighted dimension identified in children born preterm is associated with reduced fiber cross-section of the splenium of the corpus callosum.

No associations between behavior dimensions and fiber metrics: Full-term-only and combinedsample analyses

No significant associations were observed between either PC1 or PC2 and fiber density or cross section at the whole-brain or ROI level in either the full-term only group or in the combined sample.

Discussion

The present study characterized latent dimensions of behavioral traits in children born preterm and full-term and examined their associations with white matter fiber density and fiber cross-section. Our findings suggest that the organization of behavioral variability differed between groups, with the preterm group exhibiting clearer separation between autism-related and ADHD-related dimensions. Although fiber density is known to be altered following preterm birth, including in this sample (Kaur et al., 2025), neither of the behavioral dimensions identified within the preterm group were associated with fiber density. Higher scores on an ADHDweighted behavioral dimension were associated with reduced fiber cross-section within the splenium of the corpus callosum in the preterm group.

In the full-term group, the PCA revealed that autism-related traits, ADHD symptoms, and emotional measures all loaded positively onto the first principal component, indicating that children with greater difficulties in one behavioral domain tended to exhibit greater difficulties across the others. This pattern is broadly consistent with the general psychopathology ("p") factor described in dimensional models of psychopathology, in which diverse behavioral symptoms share a common source of variation and difficulties in one domain tend to accompany elevations across other domains (Caspi et al., 2014). A similar pattern emerged in our combined preterm and full-term analysis, where these behavioral domains also contributed in the same direction to a broad first component. This finding is similar to recent work by Lean et al. (2026), who used PCA across full-term and very preterm children and identified a common parentreported component capturing shared variance across ADHD, internalizing, and social communication difficulties (Lean et al., 2026). In the present study, this pattern was not observed when PCA was performed separately within the preterm group. Rather than contributing to a common behavioral dimension, autism-related traits and ADHD-related behaviors loaded onto distinct principal components, suggesting that although both may be higher in the preterm population on average, there is greater differentiation between those domains, as was also evident in pairwise correlations. These findings suggest that while children born preterm can exhibit co-occurring behavioral difficulties, the organization and covariance of these traits may differ from that observed in children born full-term. Thus, preterm birth may influence not only the overall burden of behavioral symptoms, but also the way these symptoms covary. Together, these findings provided a rationale for allowing behavioral dimensions to emerge independently within each group rather than assuming a common behavioral structure across the full sample. A consequence of this data-driven approach, however, is that the resulting components represented different behavioral constructs and limited our ability to compare brain-behavior across PT and FT groups.

Having identified distinct ADHD-weighted and autism-weighted dimensions within the preterm behavioral structure, we next examined whether individual variation along these dimensions was associated with white matter organization. Neither preterm-derived behavioral dimension was significantly associated with gestational age, suggesting that behavioral variability within the preterm group was not simply related to degree of prematurity. We found that higher scores on the ADHD-weighted principal component were associated with reduced fiber cross-section within the splenium of the corpus callosum. The corpus callosum is among the white matter structures most consistently affected following preterm birth given its particular vulnerability to perinatal white matter injury (Jo et al., 2012; Malavolti et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2024). The splenium facilitates interhemispheric communication between posterior cortical regions involved in visuospatial, sensory, and higher-order cognitive processing (Blaauw & Meiners, 2020; Knyazeva, 2013). Consequently, with this vulnerability, previous fixel-based studies have identified the splenium as particularly susceptible to the effects of prematurity, with reduced fiber cross-section associated with greater prematurity (Kelly et al., 2020; Pecheva et al., 2019), greater neonatal clinical risk (Kelly et al., 2020; Pecheva et al., 2019), and prolonged mechanical ventilation (Pecheva et al., 2019), as well as slower developmental increases in fiber density and fiber cross-section throughout childhood (Kelly et al., 2020). Our previous work in this cohort similarly identified reduced fiber cross-section in the posterior corpus callosum with decreasing gestational age during early childhood (Kaur et al., 2025). Abnormalities of the corpus callosum have also been associated with attentional difficulties and ADHD symptoms in children born preterm (Kim et al., 2023; Murray et al., 2016). Together, these findings suggest that variation in splenial fiber cross-section may represent a neurobiological correlate of ADHDrelated behavioral variation in children born preterm, in addition to reflecting the broader vulnerability of this region to the effects of prematurity.

Interestingly, the ADHD-weighted dimension was not significantly associated with fiber density in the preterm group. This should not be interpreted as evidence that splenium microstructure is unaffected in preterm birth, as previous neonatal and childhood fixel-based studies (including our own) have reported reductions in FD of the corpus callosum (Kaur et al., 2025; Kelly et al., 2020; Pannek et al., 2018; Pecheva et al., 2018). Rather, our findings suggest that individual differences in ADHD-related behavior may be more closely linked to variability in white matter bundle cross-section than to fiber density during early childhood. Fiber crosssection reflects the size and morphology of white matter fiber bundles and may provide information about the capacity of long-range interhemispheric communication that is complementary to that provided by fiber density (Dhollander et al., 2021; Raffelt et al., 2017).

Given the role of the corpus callosum in integrating distributed cortical networks (Wang et al., 2021), subtle alterations in bundle morphology may have functional implications for behavioral development. Future longitudinal studies incorporating repeated behavioral and neuroimaging assessments are required to determine the relative contributions of microstructural and macrostructural white matter alterations to behavioural development across childhood.

Despite accounting for the largest proportion of behavioral variance in the preterm sample, the autism-weighted principal component was not significantly associated with either fiber density or fiber cross-section. Together with the significant association observed for the ADHD-weighted dimension, these findings suggest that behavioral dimensions of the preterm phenotype may differ in the extent to which they are reflected in white matter organization. The absence of significant associations with autism-related traits may indicate that variation in autism-related traits is influenced by neurobiological processes that are not captured by the white matter measures examined here. Alternatively, autism-related behavioral variation may reflect the cumulative effect of subtle structural alterations distributed across multiple regions and tracts, rather than variation in a single white matter structure. Thus, the lack of detectable associations with individual white matter measures does not necessarily imply an absence of neurobiological contributions to autism-related behavioral variation but may instead reflect the distributed nature of the underlying neural substrates.

This study has several strengths. By using fixel-based analysis, we were able to characterize white matter micro-and macrostructure with greater sensitivity than conventional diffusion tensor imaging. We also examined behavioral traits as continuous dimensions rather than categorical diagnoses, allowing patterns of covariation across behavioral domains to be captured. Identifying these dimensions separately within preterm and full-term samples further allowed group-specific behavioral structure to emerge without assuming that the same latent dimensions applied across groups. Additionally, our preterm sample spanned a broad gestational age range, which enabled investigation across the spectrum of prematurity, as much of the existing literature has focused primarily on very-and extremely preterm populations (Burnett et al., 2019; Hadaya et al., 2023; Hadaya et al., 2025; Lean et al., 2026; Lean et al., 2020). We also acknowledge several limitations. Firstly, behavioral measures were derived from parent-report questionnaires, which may be influenced by reporting bias and may not fully capture a child's behavioral variability. Future studies could incorporate multiple informants, including parent, teacher, and child-report measures, alongside standardized behavioral or clinical assessments to provide a more comprehensive characterization of behavioral traits. Secondly, although deriving behavioral dimensions separately allowed group-specific behavioral structure to emerge, the resulting differences in component structure prevented direct comparison of brain-behavior associations between the preterm and full-term groups. Future multivariate work in larger independent preterm cohorts will be important to determine whether the observed separation of autism-and ADHD-related traits represents a reproducible feature of behavioral variation following preterm birth. In addition, the cross-sectional design of this study prevents conclusions regarding developmental trajectories or the predictive value of these brain-behavior relationships. Finally, although we examined the whole brain, our ROI analyses focused on tracts selected based on prior literature. As a result, associations in other white matter regions may not have been detected.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that patterns of behavioral covariation in children born preterm may differ from those observed in children born full-term, with autism-and ADHD-related traits emerging as more differentiated dimensions within the preterm sample.

Specifically, reduced fiber cross-section within the splenium of the corpus callosum was associated with greater ADHD-related behavioral traits in children born preterm, highlighting a potential relationship between white matter macrostructure and behavioral variation during early childhood. Together, these findings support the use of dimensional behavioral approaches and fiber-specific neuroimaging techniques to better characterize the neurodevelopmental mechanisms underlying behavioral heterogeneity following preterm birth. Further characterization of these relationships in larger, longitudinal samples may ultimately improve understanding of individual differences in neurodevelopmental outcomes following preterm birth.
