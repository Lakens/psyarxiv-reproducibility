Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Learning-disabled children and young people experience systemic barriers to their inclusion in research, and researcher expertise and experience can either act as one of those barriers or as a facilitator. Researcher accounts of conducting research with learning-disabled children and young people can provide useful insights into practices that facilitate inclusion, yet such accounts are scarce.

This study presents a secondary analysis of ethnographic data collected by four researchers during a study exploring the communication preferences of learning-disabled students in a special school setting in the North of England. It focuses on the researchers' reflections on their own experiences of data collection, as captured in their fieldnotes. Four themes were developed using thematic analysis: (1) Moving beyond a "one-size-fits-all" approach; (2) The importance of relationships; (3) Researching as an outsider/insider; (4) Why it matters who does the research.

Findings highlight the positive, yet sometimes challenging, experiences of working with learning-disabled students in a special school setting, and contribute to the knowledge base on supporting inclusive practices for qualitative research with learning-disabled participants. The article discusses actionable recommendations for how researchers can navigate the challenges they may encounter during data collection and considers the role of secondary analysis as a reflexive tool.

Introduction:

While many groups are underrepresented in research, people with learning disabilities 1 (known as intellectual disabilities in some contexts) face especially persistent barriers to inclusion (Doody, 2018; McClimens & Allmark, 2011; Porter & Lacey, 2004). Learning-disabled (LD) people can present with a complex range of physical, intellectual, and communication needs (e.g., they may be nonspeaking and/or have limited ability to access literacy (McClimens & Allmark, 2011). This means that LD people are often unable to access research participation because studies are not designed with their needs in mind, and this situation is even more challenging for LD children and young people (CYP) than it is for LD adults. Combined, this often results in LD CYP being excluded from research, or researchers using proxy reports from parents/carers, or key stakeholders rather than including LD voices (Smith et al., 2025; Yoon, 2023). This exclusion of LD CYP and overreliance on proxy reports are problematic for a number of legal and moral reasons. Article 12 of the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC, 1989) explicitly declares that children have the right to express their views freely in all matters affecting them, and Article 7 of the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (UNCRPD, 2006) emphasises the importance of enacting this right with disabled children. Alongside this, there is a strong societal and academic push towards inclusive, participatory, and rights-based research with LD CYP; however, the systematic exclusion of LD people from research continues to deny this group the benefits that research can provide, including more effective support and amplified voices in society (Bottomley et al., 2024; Doody, 2018; Fields et al., 2025). If research is not grounded in the lived experiences of LD people, it cannot meaningfully inform the services, policies, and practices that affect them. Therefore, it is necessary to develop an understanding of, and guidance on, how to conduct research effectively with LD CYP to facilitate their meaningful participation. The current study examined researchers' experiences whilst conducting qualitative research with LD CYP to contribute to the development of knowledge about practices that effectively facilitate participation in qualitative research for learning-disabled people.

There are several key reasons for the underrepresentation of learning-disabled people in research. Firstly, due to the range and complexity of additional needs, there is a perception that conducting research with LD individuals is more difficult (Benstead et al., 2024). In some cases, LD CYP may have additional behavioural challenges, which may be difficult for researchers to navigate (Mikulak et al., 2024). Others may have communication or comprehension differences, or use 1 For the purposes of this study, the term 'learning-disabled' refers to individuals with conditions that impact their ability to process information and live independently throughout their lives (Fields et al., 2025).

Augmented and Alternative Communication (AAC), which may be unfamiliar to researchers. There has historically been a lack of tools to facilitate communication with LD participants, meaning existing tools need to be carefully and collaboratively adapted to avoid confusing terms, complex grammar, and other barriers to understanding (Nicolaidis et al., 2020). More recently, research has identified a lack of awareness of which tools are available and how to use them effectively (Fields et al., 2025; Mikulak et al., 2024). This is especially true for LD CYP with higher support needs, and for individuals who do not communicate through speech, meaning these individuals may be left out of research (Doody, 2018). This presents issues for bias in research sampling and outputs that can reinforce existing inequalities (Shariq et al., 2023).

As a consequence of the challenges outlined above, research with LD CYP can be more timeintensive than with other populations in relation to, for example, the time required for researchers and participants to develop a relationship and get to know each other (including participants' specific needs and preferences), explain information thoroughly, and ensure that participants understand what is needed from them (Fields et al., 2025). Previous studies overwhelmingly highlight the need to cultivate relationships with learning-disabled participants, their caregivers, teachers, and other significant people to bring a successful research project to fruition, and this can be a lengthy and delicate process (D'Sa et al., 2024; Leishman et al., 2023).

In addition, navigating ethical approval and informed consent can be daunting when designing and carrying out research with LD CYP, often resulting in the need for research processes to be adapted and tailored to meet the individual needs of participants, meaning the level of methodological and ethical flexibility required can be difficult to achieve whilst meeting traditional research standards and expectations (Benstead et al., 2024; Runswick-Cole et al., 2024). The issue of how to establish informed consent is a point of continuous conversation in the world of research with learning-disabled participants (e.g., Bottomley et al., 2024; Porter & Lacey, 2004), as this requires a reframing of the standard research approach from one-off consent forms to encompassing concepts such as continuous consent and recognising different forms of communicating assent (McNeilly et al., 2020). Good practice guidelines highlight the need to take time to fully explain to LD participants what participation in the research project entails, using accessible language or alternative resources, and to remind participants that they can withdraw at any time while the research is ongoing (Bottomley et al., 2024). All of this can take more time and differentiation than working with and obtaining consent from non-LD individuals, and may be met with 'pushback' from academic institutions. Indeed, Leishman et al. (2023) highlighted the inability to proceed with their project using simple, easy-read consent forms due to the imposition of university standard documentation, which ballooned the length and complexity of their documents, making them more difficult to understand and access, and going against the LD co-researchers' wishes. Each of these factors can pose daunting challenges for researchers unfamiliar with methods for addressing them, whilst working within the constraints of institutional requirements, yet little is known about how researchers experience and navigate such challenges whilst in the field. This is further complicated when working with LD CYP within their educational setting. In the United Kingdom and many countries around the world, LD CYP with more complex needs are often educated in special educational settings. Special schools are designed to meet the specialist medical, social or welfare needs of the children and young people they support, and in England, only children with an Education and Health Care Plan (EHCP) are entitled to attend specialist provision.

This means that all children attending specialist settings have some kind of recognised additional learning need, including learning disabilities, and staff-to-student ratios are often much higher than in mainstream settings. These setting-specific differences have several implications for researchers.

Firstly, consent from a gatekeeper, usually a head of school or a member of senior leadership, must be obtained before access to the school can be granted, which creates potential access challenges before the project has begun (Fecke et al., 2022; Williams, 2020). Furthermore, as all children in special schools have additional and potentially more complex needs, researchers must navigate associated challenges, including medical needs, communication differences, and behavioural challenges, not only for the LD CYP they are working with but also for other CYP in the setting. These all come with safeguarding implications. Special schools settings are also less predictable working environments than mainstream settings. To accommodate for their students range of needs, pedagogical approaches are necessarily flexible and responsive, and account for not only learning differences, but the management of volatile or unpredictable behaviour which is more common in these settings. For researchers unfamiliar with special school settings and safeguarding, or, most importantly, working with LD CYP, navigating these differences may be particularly daunting. Accounts of researchers' experiences that highlight the challenges and practicalities of conducting research in these settings are imperative for designing and conducting more inclusive and ethical research; however, their accounts are often missing from the literature. Greenstein (2014) provides an important autoethnographic example of integrating researcher reflection into research findings, documenting a single researcher's experiences in a special needs unit within a mainstream school. The author expressed concerns around feeling intrusive in the classroom space and worrying about disturbing teachers' work and their uneasiness at translating the experience of working with learning-disabled participants into a standard format reliant upon verbal language.

Similarly, Mulhall et al. (2021) present an even rarer example of researchers' experiences conducting research with learning-disabled participants as the primary study focus. This study highlighted systemic issues such as navigating institutional ethics procedures, institutional gatekeeping, and the prevalent belief that people with learning disabilities are unable to provide informed consent.

Researchers described how, when collaborating with organisations, they had to navigate barriers such as workload pressures, high staff turnover, and the need to reduce the burden on participants and caregivers. These challenges were often rooted in negative attitudes towards the value and feasibility of research with this population, even from funders and professionals, emphasising how the academic research system's reliance on standardisation often fails to accommodate diverse needs and real-world circumstances, impacting inclusive research with LD participants. While these accounts establish that qualitative work in special education introduces distinct reflexive and institutional challenges, they primarily examine single-observer dynamics or macro-level administrative barriers.

Method

Methodology

This study used qualitative secondary data analysis to re-examine an existing set of ethnographic fieldnotes. The analysis was carried out by an independent researcher who was not involved in primary data collection. A reflexive interpretivist stance was adopted, acknowledging that the fieldnotes were qualitative representations shaped by the primary researchers' experiences in the field and subsequently interpreted through the first author's analytical lens (Willig, 2013). Alongside re-analysing the dataset, we propose this analytical framework as a novel, multi-perspective reflexive tool for qualitative research. Using an independent researcher provides a layer of objectivity, allowing for the exploration of any assumptions or normalised classroom dynamics that researchers in the field may unconsciously overlook, and facilitating the identification of areas of good practice.

Primary Dataset

The dataset comprised secondary fieldnotes originally recorded by four researchers during an ethnographic study exploring children's communication choices. Fieldnotes were compiled over seven weeks of visiting two classes in a special school in the North of England between November and December 2024. The full final textual data sample was 197,776 words. All final field notes were anonymised by the primary research team ahead of this secondary data analysis.

Participants

This study examined ethnographic fieldnotes from four participants. Participants were members of the same research team at the University of York, UK. All participants were female and ranged in levels of research experience and seniority. Researcher information, along with their number of visits to the school, is presented in Table 1.

Procedure and Analysis

The data set was thematically analysed following Braun and Clarke's (2006) framework. To ensure that analytical distance was maintained, and that the analysis worked as a reflexive tool, the first author, who was not involved in the primary data collection, led the process. Familiarisation was established by reading and re-reading the fieldnotes before conducting initial coding in NVivo 15 (Lumivero, 2025). The first author then engaged in peer debriefing by sharing their initial analysis with the co-authors who gathered the primary data. This process integrated their field insights to clarify any contextual nuances, challenge potential analytical blind spots, and refine code groupings.

Following this, the first author revisited the codes and clustered them into candidate themes, which represented overarching patterns across the dataset. Themes were then reviewed against the dataset to ensure their suitability, refined where needed, and assigned conceptual theme names. The entire author team critically reviewed and agreed upon the final thematic structure.

Ethics and Reflexivity

The study for which the data were originally collected received ethical approval from the University of York, Department of Education]. The original ethical approval included approval for the data to be used anonymously in secondary analysis, in line with Open Science Guidelines. This was made clear to participants when they agreed to take part in the primary data collection. As a result, no further ethical approval was sought for this secondary analysis.

The first author of the current study was not involved in the initial data collection and had no prior association with the study setting or primary participants. However, all authors had prior experience working with learning-disabled children and young people in educational contexts, which may have impacted the interpretations of the data. All authors were colleagues in the same research lab at the University of York, so it is possible that close association may also have introduced some bias into the analysis of experiences. However, this close association also meant it was possible to fact-check and minimise the potential for misunderstandings on the part of the first author during the secondary analysis. Regular meetings were held by the team to challenge individual assumptions, explore alternative interpretations of the text, and ensure the final themes were grounded in the data.

Results

Four themes were developed from the data: (1) moving beyond a "one-size fits all" approach; (2) the importance of relationship building; (3) researching as an outsider/insider; (4) the role of researcher characteristics and practices.

Personalised approaches to data collection

A key challenge evident throughout the dataset was the researchers' experiences of navigating the complex individual needs of the students, who presented with a wide array of cognitive, behavioural, and social characteristics. These differences affected how researchers interacted with CYP throughout the study and emphasised the need for a bespoke approach to research which respected the individuals' needs and preferences.

Although the researchers received some prior information on each student's communication profile, they were usually learning and familiarising themselves with these as they went along: '[Student] strikes me as processing very slowly but having very good non-verbal social skills.' (P1). However, occasionally they could not interpret what the student meant and had to adapt accordingly.

Researchers described how drawing on multimodal communication strategies, such as sign language or Augmented and Alternative Communication (AAC) devices, could help overcome communication challenges. These strategies were often individual to each student, and often involved more than one form of communication, highlighting the complexities of interacting with LD CYP and making it very clear that a 'one-size-fits-all' approach is inappropriate and likely to be ineffective with these CYP in this setting. This is evidenced by a researcher's experience of observing how a teaching assistant navigated a multimodal communication interaction with a student: notice [student] gives her AAC to [teaching assistant], and she begins signing/gesturing.

[Teaching Assistant] asks -"do you want to show me something?" and [student] starts pointing at her AAC and points for [teaching assistant] to change the page that her AAC is on. [Teaching assistant] moves between the pages -now [teaching assistant] and [student] are both stood up looking together. [Student] points to the picture of the toilet and looks at [teaching assistants] face -[Teaching Assistant] asks do you want the loo? And [student] nods. (P3)

The research team also had to navigate fluctuations in the behaviour of some young people. On rare occasions, the researchers documented examples of transgressive behaviours on the part of the students, and very rarely there were examples of aggressive incidents. The following incident highlights the importance of being aware of potential triggers, but also that even with awareness and preparation, behaviours can be sudden and unpredictable, and may be an unpleasant experience for researchers:

Student] leans out of her beanbag chair towards me and grabs my hair close to my scalp and is pulling it very hard. She does this with two hands, and [Teaching Assistant] helps me get free -it hurts a lot and she asks if I'm ok -I say I am. I was slightly surprised by the reaction of [Student], and not sure what it was linked to -perhaps I was sitting too close, perhaps because I wouldn't try the beef jerky. While I'm thinking this and regrouping, [Teaching Assistant] suggests it's likely because [Other Staff Member] went out of the room, which will probably have upset her. (P3) Working ethically with diverse individuals A range of experiences was evident in the assent and consent process, and researchers reflected that what worked for some young people was not appropriate for others, highlighting the need to adapt research to be bespoke to individuals from the very beginning of the research process.

Researchers were aware of and consistently sought to obtain students' assent to participate in the project. After presenting an initial explanatory video and receiving feedback at the start of data collection, they attempted to check in with each student about their understanding of what they were taking part in and whether they were happy to participate. There was a range of responses from students regarding the process of obtaining initial and ongoing consent/assent. Mostly, students expressed their assent to take part in the immediate activities the researchers were doing and were happy for them to take notes about them: 'I tell her that we're writing about how people communicate and ask if that's ok. She says "yes" and looks very bored by my explanation and walks off.' (P2).

Others were clear in stating that they did not want to take part: 'She is polite and smiles sweetly and just quietly says "no thank you."' (P1).

However, researcher experiences reflected that while the students may have been happy to take part in the immediate activities, they may not have understood the broader meaning and purpose of their involvement in the research project. Likewise, some students opted out, but later opted back in, or chose to take part in some tasks but not others. The researchers appeared to understand that they must revisit points with students to check not only the participant's understanding of the assent process, but that researchers own understanding of how students were interpreting the assent information provided to them. The quote below highlights how doing so identified challenges with the wording in one of the assent questions:

Working through these, I ask her each time she says yes or no to one of the questions and permissions why. I explain it's not because I'm changing her mind but because it's helpful for me to know why people like some things and don't like other things. For example, she said no to videos, but yes to photos. I asked her why this was important for her, and she says "Because I don't like to share" -this was for the question 'are you happy for us to share the videos with other people' -and she said yes for photos […] "Because I like posing.

" (P3)

There were also a smaller number of examples in which the researcher was rejected or rebuffed by the student when attempting to engage with them. This drawing of boundaries represented some of the most explicit gestures of autonomy and (lack of) assent by the students, and the researchers needed to show tact and respect in these situations, demonstrating that knowing when to back off is an important skill in the context of research with learning-disabled participants: [Student] was at the head of the table with his talker […] I could see the last words used were: "Still in sonic song."

I asked him if he liked Sonic, and he looked at me and moved his talker. I felt a bit guilty, like I was reading his thoughts, despite trying to start [a] conversation with him about something I thought he might be interested in. (P3)

Theme 2: The importance of relationship building

Researchers noted that relationship-building was vital to overcoming challenges and enhancing data collection. Their experiences showed that communication and behavioural barriers could be alleviated by taking time to get to know the student, becoming familiar with their needs and preferences, and establishing a strong relationship. Relationships with school staff were also described as important, as staff helped facilitate the research in the school setting, and their cooperation and support were often essential in adapting research practices to be appropriate for individual participants.

One key facilitator experienced across the four researchers was that familiarity was essential There were frequent examples of researchers building relationships by getting directly involved with activities such as playing physical games, doing crafts, music lessons, puzzles, and relaxing or playing together during breaks. Researchers frequently expressed genuine affection for the students and desire to get to know them better, using existing knowledge of students' likes and dislikes to further develop relationships:

During this time, [Student] grabs my arm to get my attention. She gives me the bells, and I assume she wants me to shake them for her. I hold them up to her ear (as I've been told before that she really likes loud noises) and begin to shake them. She smiles. (P2) Likewise, students appeared to develop strong bonds with the researchers over time, showing preferences for spending time with them and sometimes confiding their feelings or worries, experiences which were seen as essential to successful research: Yesterday lunchtime in school, [Student] saw me from the other playground with the small class. She gestured me over, and asked why I couldn't be with her class then. I told her I would the next day -Friday -and she was pleased. (P3) However, researchers occasionally experience relationship building as challenging. Researchers sometimes perceived instances where students became too fixated on the researchers' presence or absence, which could be as disruptive to the rest of the class: "[Name]! [Name] is reading book! [Name]! [Name] don't forget to write in book! [Name] is reading and make sentence!". I said I will write down some of the things and show [student] after tryday so everyone could see, as I was aware he was focusing more on me and my writing more than the tryday session, and I didn't want to derail the class or distract students. (P3)

Furthermore, relationships with staff were seen as crucial to successful data collection and were often cultivated by spending time in the classroom before class began or during lunch breaks. Building relationships gave rise to ad hoc and casual interactions, and these more spontaneous interactions were seen as key parts of the research process, despite the additional time needed to 'be around' outside of specific sessions with students: "These moments for quick opportunistic chats about friendship feel like a good and low-pressure way of data collection." (P3).

Researchers were clear and consistent in their view that successful data collection could not adopt a 'one-size fits all approach', and time spent engaging informally and building up positive and familiar relationships with students and staff was essential in facilitating this.

Theme 3: Navigating research as an outsider/insider

This theme captures contrast: the researcher in the school setting is an outside observer, yet, to varying extents, becomes involved in the day-to-day activities and social dynamics of the school and classroom. Researchers often expressed feeling out of place at the school, including worries about being intrusive or disruptive and a heightened awareness of their status as interlopers. These experiences were often prompted by moments when a lack of familiarity with the setting left them unsure what was going on or why. However, these were often countered by feelings of inclusion and camaraderie for staff throughout the research process.

The extent to which researchers became integrated was sometimes reflected in their descriptions of the classroom layout and their positioning within it. Researchers sometimes chose to position themselves on the outside of the group in an observer role, and sometimes as part of the group in an active participant role. There were benefits to the active participant role, as opportunities to play and interact directly with the students were valuable for relationship-building and data collection. Researchers often expressed that they were happy to help and pleased to be able to contribute positively to the classroom: 'As I go out of the door [Teacher] thanks me for helping. She hasn't said this before and I'm glad that she feels I have done something useful.' (P1). There were many instances, likewise, where the researchers expressed empathy and camaraderie for the staff, observing that there are many complexities to working in the setting that the staff need to navigate while being overworked and, in many cases, underpaid: 'It is very clear that the teams have to adapt to constant change and to be incredibly agile to make the environment safe and meet the needs of the students.' (P1).

However, it seemed that becoming more actively involved could make role boundaries more malleable, as researchers were sometimes asked or expected to help or look after the students, particularly when they were acting hands-on with the students and activities. These instances were sometimes experienced as stressful by the researchers:

Being told to keep people safe whilst observing feels quite stressful. (P2) I had to leave [Student 1] and run after [Student 2]. For a while, then, I ended up responsible for both students. (P4) On one occasion, a researcher and a member of staff came into direct conflict when the staff member asked them to perform duties the researcher felt were inappropriate and outside their remit. As part of this, the researcher was asked to share what had been recorded about some students in their observation notes; this put the researcher in the difficult position of deciding whether to disclose any of that information. Deciding not to disclose meant setting boundaries with the staff member, and it was difficult to do so while maintaining a good relationship to ensure the research project's success. The tensions in the dynamic between the insider/outsider position of the researcher and the full insider position of the staff member were evident in this exchange: I'm quite taken aback […] I have the feeling that [Staff Member] perhaps took that tone with me because of feeling threatened in some way by the research, and that she was trying to take me down a peg. (P3)

This may have been related to uncertainty about the extent to which staff understood the purpose of the research and the researcher's role. Researchers sometimes expressed concerns that their presence might seem threatening or intimidating to staff, or that staff may be changing their behaviour because they were aware of being observed: 'I think me asking questions is making her nervous and unconfident but she does seem happy to talk.' (P1).

Actively participating in classroom activities occasionally resulted in students blurring boundaries, sometimes mistaking the researcher for another classroom staff member. Researchers reflected that this appeared to be partly related to systemic factors, such as understaffing and the continuous coming-and-going of additional supply staff: 'I guess [the students] are used to a lot of supply workers, and even though my behaviour is odd (making notes etc.) I do have the appropriate lanyard and fob.' (P1). This further highlights the need for clear information and ongoing monitoring of assent, as discussed in Theme 1.

Researchers continuously navigated the tension between being outsiders and insiders, balancing the benefits of rapport and meaningful contribution against the stressors of blurred boundaries and conflicting responsibilities within the school environment. Researchers reflected that the ability to be flexible and responsive to the changing demands of students and the school environment was imperative, accepting that sometimes, students would not be up to chatting or doing activities that they might be more able to do at another time: I wondered if there was something not quite right with [Student] today as usually she is caring and considerate of other classmates and eager to chat. I overheard her earlier in the day saying that she was crying on the bus, so perhaps something had happened out of school.

(P4)

Indeed, structured data collection activities did not always go as planned, sometimes due to constraints in the setting or due to students interpreting the task in an unexpected way. The researchers needed to make decisions in the moment about how much control to exert over the activities and whether to redirect a student's focus, balancing the need to allow students to authentically express themselves with the need to collect data relevant to the research aims. This was sometimes overcome by drawing on staff members' support, in the moment: '[Teacher] did a fab job of helping us facilitate conversations and starting the task off with the group, though the task ended up being slightly different to what we had planned.' (P4).

On this, the researchers reflected on integrating the good practices and useful strategies modelled by staff to adapt their own data collection methods. For some, this was during data collection to better meet an individual student's needs: 'She suggested changing some of the language in their forms, e.g. instead of "share" to change that to "show", which was helpful to think about and sounds more accurate and more specific to the meaning.' (P3). For others, it was a wider reflection on adapting future methods to be more inclusive: I notice that posting works really well and wonder if we could include a resource in our library that involves asking participants to make a picture-based choice and post their choice in a post-box. It might suit some people. (P1)

It was clear that research in this setting requires researchers to be comfortable with some level of physical touch from students. Some of the researchers were comfortable with greater levels of touch (e.g., hugging, hands touching the face), or unexpected touch, while others found similar levels of touch uncomfortable. Levels of prior experience working with LD CYP appeared to play a role, with those who had spent more time in similar settings consistently expressing greater awareness of and sensitivity to boundaries and safeguarding, but being aware of the need for navigating physical touch, given that some students used this as a form of communication, and that personal space may be encroached upon:

As we get into the room [student] heads straight for me and [teacher] says 'watch her, she's a bit grabby' (she's known to pull hair sometimes). She comes over and says "fish". This includes the need for patience, time, and openness to changing their broader practices.

Discussion

This study was a secondary analysis of ethnographic data collected by four researchers within a special school setting, analysed by an independent fifth researcher who had not been involved in the primary data collection. The findings offer a nuanced exploration of the ethical, relational, and practical complexities that arise when conducting research alongside learning-disabled children and young people (LD CYP). This study also demonstrates how secondary data analysis of team fieldnotes can act as a reflexive tool in qualitative research.

Researchers in this study routinely had to navigate the boundary between gathering rich qualitative data and respecting participant autonomy, building relationships with students and staff, and ensuring that patience, flexibility, and empathy were woven throughout the research process.

Researchers had varied experiences which echo and extend those reported in existing literature on research with LD CYP. Although researchers encountered distinct challenges, strategies were often developed to overcome them, resulting in predominantly positive overall experiences. Throughout the research process, feelings of inclusion and camaraderie were identified, showing that integrating into a special school setting can foster a deeply collaborative research environment. Researchers were welcomed into the school setting by staff and students and made to feel part of the broader school community.

The findings support the adoption of pluralistic and adaptive qualitative methodologies when working with LD CYP and suggest that there is no singular 'right way' to do this kind of research.

While previous research with LD individuals has primarily focused on adapting research tools (Cluleyhttps://paperpile.com/c/3AXQtqFN/lTvR+vIqA 2017; Nicolaidis et al., 2020) , our findings show that methodological success is dependent on researcher flexibility and responsiveness. Researchers' experiences changed hour to hour and day by day. In these instances, adaptability was a key facilitator, supporting Fields et al. (2025)'s findings.

Importantly, our analysis extends prior literature by demonstrating that researcher flexibility and adaptability extend to modes of communication, not just the ability to adapt research methods.

Researcher competence in multimodal communication, such as interpreting physical gestures, AAC, and sign language, must be paired with a flexible, responsive approach to work with LD CYP and problem-solve when communication breaks down.

A key contribution of the current study is highlighting how relational dynamics extend beyond participant rapport, showing that researchers must have the ability and willingness to develop and maintain good relationships, not only with the LD CYP, but with the staff that support them. As discussed by Fields et al. (2025), familiarisation is a key and essential component of successful research. However, existing accounts often treat staff primarily as gatekeepers for initial access (Williams, 2020). In contrast, our findings show that relationships not only supported access to the data collection site but also actively shaped the researchers' overall experiences. Interpersonal relationships with staff fostered inclusion and camaraderie within the team; however, they also introduced complex role blurring, such as informal requests to supervise students or share observation notes. This tension highlights the interface between academic research standards and the established cultural norms of special school environments, demonstrating that fieldwork experiences are strongly impacted by school climate. These findings suggest that researchers' experiences will differ across special school settings, and that having a well-prepared, reflexive research team equipped to navigate relational discomfort will be key to ensuring a successful research project.

Furthermore, navigating consent and assent, although complex, proved to be a positive and constructive experience, but one that researchers could not treat as static. Rather than seeing consent as an initial barrierhttps://paperpile.com/c/3AXQtqFN/NHa9+EBhw as highlighted in previous literature (Leishman et al., 2023; Mulhall et al., 2021) , our findings confirm that consent functions as an ongoing, relational process (McNeilly et al., 2020).

Navigating this required researchers to remain privy to changes in participant behaviours or expressions of disengagement. Although this added complexity to the process, it ultimately appeared to empower LD CYP by respecting their agency and choices, reinforcing a research ethos grounded in trust and dignity rather than compliance. Our findings suggest that with thoughtful and skilled researchers, the consent process can and should be managed in a way that is accessible to LD CYP.

To facilitate this in future work, Institutional Review Boards and ethics committees must accommodate flexible, process-oriented consent frameworks that prioritise participant non-verbal assent over rigid written forms, and researchers must acknowledge that their experience of navigating consent may be different to what they are used to.

Beyond using multiple methodologies and communication modalities, the application of established qualitative principles was central to navigating these flexible methodologies. Continuous researcher reflexivity served as both an analytical lens and an ethical safeguard (Olmos-Vega et al., 2022). Reflexivity during the initial writing of fieldnotes allowed researchers to interrogate their own positionality, acknowledge emotional reactions to classroom events, and deliberate on power imbalances (Day, 2012). Throughout, this experience was regarded as a helpful part of the research.

Establishing structured space for team debriefing and individual reflexivity is therefore critical for managing the emotional labour of ethnographic work in special school settings, and ensuring high standards of research integrity are maintained. Taking this a step further, having an independent researcher review and interrogate the notes, in conversation with the researcher who made them, can add a useful additional layer of reflexivity and understanding.

Participant heterogeneity, communication, and behavioural differences were the most common challenges researchers encountered during this project (e.g., Benstead et al., 2024; Bottomley et al., 2024). The study confirmed that patience, flexibility, longer data-collection windows, and multimodal communication methods all facilitated the research (Fields et al., 2025).

Our findings suggest that using methods that 'lean in' to what may be thought of as 'difficulties' in working with this population, such as allocating longer amounts of time for data collection and collecting data in natural settings, can not only make research more robust to unpredictability but also enhance the data collected and improve the research experience for everyone. Using open-ended ethnographic techniques ensures data collection remains inclusive and ethically sound with this population. Furthermore, researchers entering these settings must have access to training on managing boundaries and sensory communication beforehand to safeguard both participants and themselves.

The findings here have implications for future qualitative research with LD CYP, and the wider LD community. It is clear that there is room to develop more specific guidance on working with learning-disabled participants, for example, on responding to behaviours such as touch, aggression, and intensive interactions (Mikulak et al., 2024). While staff who work regularly with LD people may have routines and procedures in place, researchers entering special school settings may lack the experience or knowledge to navigate situations as they arise. Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this paper is its examination of researchers' first-hand experiences conducting qualitative research in a special school setting. Few papers have focused on researcher experiences as the primary topic of a research paper, particularly when the research was carried out by a team, yet understanding these experiences are key to developing ethical and inclusive research. A further strength is that the first author was not involved in the primary data collection, which brings a fresh perspective and a lack of expectations about what might come up during the analysis. At the same time, members of the original research team were available to review the coding and proposed themes and to offer insights into the situations, which enriched the analysis and interpretation process.

However, secondary data analysis relies on the depth and detail of the original fieldnotes, meaning unrecorded contextual nuances cannot be directly probed. Furthermore, the analysis is based on the experiences of a small group of researchers from a single institution within a single special school setting. As special schools vary widely in the types of students they support, their school culture, and their leadership style, the experiences documented here may differ across mainstream inclusive settings and alternative educational provisions.

Future research should represent a wider range of settings or compare different settings to provide a broader overview of how researchers experience conducting research with learning-disabled participants in schools. Further investigation into how engaging with longitudinal qualitative research impacts researcher-participant power dynamics would also provide valuable insights for the field.

Conclusion

This study examined the experiences of researchers conducting ethnographic research with LD participants in a special school setting. Thematic analysis explored their experiences navigating the research process, the barriers and facilitators they encountered, and the strategies they developed as a result of these experiences. By using an independent researcher to conduct the secondary analysis, the paper also demonstrated how secondary fieldnote analysis can be used as a multiperspective reflexive tool for qualitative research.

The findings extend existing research by examining the complexity of working not only with LD CYP, but also within a special school setting across team members, highlighting the importance of the characteristics and practices of the researchers, including the application of reflexivity. It also demonstrated that consent can be successfully navigated if treated as a dynamic, relational process.

Finally, our study highlights the importance of multimodal researcher communication and structured team reflexivity. The paper calls for researchers to move beyond generic calls for patience and flexibility, and offers actionable recommendations to support ethical and inclusive research with learning disabled children and young people in a special school setting. Although conducting qualitative research in special school settings presents distinct relational and ethical challenges, our findings demonstrate that with appropriate reflexive frameworks, research with LD CYP is not only viable but also an enjoyable and enriching experience for those involved, and one that can amplify LD voices in a meaningful way, and in accordance with the rights of LD CYP.
