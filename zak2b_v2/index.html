To improve Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

We try to exclude figure and table notes like “* p < .05”, but may not succeed at excluding all false positives.

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

The English orthography exhibits probabilistic, rather than strictly rule-governed, mappings between orthography and phonology across multiple grain sizes (see Ziegler, & Goswami, 2005; Venezky, 1999). Its structure reflects a complex interplay of morphological regularities, syllabic constraints, and numerous sublexical regularities in spelling-sound correspondences -features that have led Seidenberg (2005 Seidenberg ( , 2017) ) and colleagues (e.g., Plaut et al., 1996; Rueckl & Seidenberg, 2011; Seidenberg et al., 2020; Seidenberg & McClelland, 1989) to characterize English as quasiregular. For this reason, English poses unique and significant challenges for children as they work to develop accurate and efficient word reading skills (see Seymour, 2013; Seymour et al., 2003). Consequently, the English orthography gives rise to substantial individual differences in word reading accuracy in developing readers, reflecting the joint effects of various word-level characteristics such as frequency, length, and orthography-tophonology and orthography-to-semantics consistency (for details, see Castles, Rastle, & Nation, 2018; Compton et al., 2014; Deacon & Levesque, 2024; Kearns, 2015 Kearns, , 2020;; Kearns et al., 2016; Leslie & Calhoon, 1995; Martens & de Jong, 2006; Marinus, Nation, & de Jong, 2015; Rueckl, 2016; Seidenberg, 1989; Siegelman et al., 2020; Steacy & Compton, 2019; Steacy et al., 2022; Treiman et al., 1990; Wade-Woolley & Heggie, 2015).

Early work by Seidenberg and colleagues (e.g., Backman et al., 1984; Seidenberg et al., 1984; Waters et al., 1984) and subsequent studies examining children's word naming and word identification established that variables such as word frequency, spelling-sound consistency, word length, and neighborhood structure influence reading performance, with effects often varying as a function of reading skill. These findings helped establish the view that word reading is shaped not only by characteristics of the reader but also by statistical regularities of words.

Subsequent studies extended this work by examining increasingly large sets of words and a broader range of lexical characteristics. For example, Spencer (2010) investigated the effects of frequency, transparency, and other lexical variables on the reading accuracy of British 6-yearolds across a set of approximately 150 words. This study demonstrated that substantial variation in children's word reading accuracy could be explained by characteristics of individual words.

More recent investigations have expanded the range of lexical variables examined, incorporating semantic characteristics (e.g., imageability, concreteness, and age of acquisition), morphological structure, orthographic and phonological neighborhood measures, and indices of spelling-sound consistency (e.g., Apfelbaum et al., 2013; Kearns et al., 2016; Steacy et al., 2019; Kellenberger et al., 2024; Yap et al., 2012). Collectively, this work has shown that word-level characteristics account for meaningful variation in children's word reading performance and that the influence of these characteristics often interacts with child-level factors such as decoding skill, reading experience, and vocabulary knowledge.

Despite these advances, most developmental studies have been limited by relatively small word samples, often ranging from a few dozen to a few hundred words. As a result, estimates of the influence of lexical characteristics may be constrained by the particular words selected for study. Large-scale item-level databases such as the developmental English Lexicon Project (d-ELP) provide an opportunity to examine lexical influences across thousands of words spanning a broad range of difficulty levels and lexical characteristics. Such databases can support more comprehensive investigations of how established lexical characteristics-including frequency, length, transparency, semantic variables, and neighborhood structure-contribute to word reading difficulty in developing readers and provide a foundation for evaluating the contribution of emerging theoretically motivated predictors.

Attempts to explore, understand, and predict the combined effects of multiple word-level characteristics on word reading accuracy in developing readers have been constrained by several limitations in the developmental reading literature. These include: (1) although the field has produced several large-scale databases of word recognition -commonly referred to as "lexicon projects" and related "crowdsourcing projects" (e.g., Adelman et al., 2014; Balota et al., 2007; Keuleers et al., 2012; Mandera et al., 2020) -these resources primarily reflect adult reading behavior; (2) empirical studies of word reading acquisition have predominantly focused on monosyllabic, monomorphemic words (e.g., Balota et al., 2007; Coltheart et al., 2001; Perry et al., 2010; Roberts et al., 2011; Seidenberg & McClelland, 1989; Siegelman et al., 2020; Yap & Balota, 2009); (3) most developmental studies rely on small samples of both children and words and employ factorial designs with limited control variables (e.g., Strain, Patterson, & Seidenberg, 2002); and (4) publicly available developmental databases generally do not provide worddifficulty estimates linked to the lexical, orthographic, and functional characteristics of words commonly encountered in children's print input, including both published (e.g., Korochkina & Rastle, 2025) and unpublished (e.g., Dawson et al., 2023) sources.

Predicting English Oral Word Reading Difficulty in Developing Readers

Children's word reading development follows a curvilinear growth pattern, with more rapid progress in early childhood and slower growth as they get older (for details see Francis et al., 1996). By eighth grade, a typically developing reader can recognize roughly 10,000 unique word forms, excluding inflectional variants (Ehri, 2005; Harris & Jacobson, 1982). The development of word reading skill in children has traditionally been characterized at a micro level by item-learning processes (e.g., Share, 1995) and at a macro level by stage-based processing (Ehri, 2002). Item learning refers to the process by which children gradually establish high-quality lexical representations through phonological recoding and repeated encounters with print. Through the process of fully analyzing each word, typically through phonological recoding, a unique orthographic entry is added to the lexicon, enabling efficient, automatic retrieval and access to phonological, syntactic, and semantic information during reading (Castles et al., 2018; Humphreys, 1985; Perfetti, 1992; Perfetti & Hart, 2001; Perfetti & Stafura, 2014; Snow, 2002; Stanovich, 1991). Stage-based theories, in contrast, describe developmental regularities in the strategies and knowledge, both implicit and explicit, that children bring to word identification. These models specify how a child's available linguistic knowledgephonological, orthographic, and semantic -conditions the probability of successfully reading a given word, thereby linking child-level skill with word-level properties. Early stage-based models characterized this evolution as a series of qualitative shifts from visual recognition to phonological decoding and ultimately to automatic word recognition (e.g., Chall, 1983; Ehri, 1995; Frith, 1985). Modern perspectives increasingly view these stages not as discrete, but as emergent from continuous item-level learning. Children's experience-driven tuning of orthography-phonology and orthography-semantics mappings lead to the apparent stage-like patterns observed at the behavioral level, while promoting expanded and strengthened connections between orthography, phonology, and semantics (Siegelman, Kearns, et al., 2020). This evolving lexical organization enables children to generalize learned patterns to increasingly varied and challenging forms, ultimately allowing them to process novel and ever more complex letter strings with greater flexibility and efficiency (see Share, 2025; Siegelman, Rueckl, et al., 2020).

Child-by-Word Models of Word Reading Development

The emergence of explanatory item response models (see De Boeck & Wilson, 2004) has allowed the merging of micro-and macro-perspectives into what is now referred to as "child-byword" models of word reading development (e.g., Harm & Seidenberg, 2004; Nation & Snowling, 1988; Steacy et al., 2022). Child-by-word models of word reading development assert that: word reading is not uniform across words or readers; the same child may read different words with varying success depending on word properties; the same word may be read differently by different children depending on a child's reading skills and experience; and thus, reading development involves interactions between the attributes of the word and characteristics of the child, including experiences with text (e.g., Kellenberger et al., 2024; Petscher et al., 2020; Steacy, 2020). These child-by-word differences are shaped, in large part, by children's statistical learning of orthography-phonology and orthography-semantic correspondences (see Siegelman, Rueckl, et al., 2020). Through repeated exposure to print, statistical learning mechanisms allow children to detect and internalize the probabilistic relationships between written and spoken word forms, becoming increasingly sensitive to the covariance structure linking orthographic patterns with their phonological and semantic representations in English (e.g., Apfelbaum, Hazeltine, & McMurray, 2013; Chetail, 2015; Nation & Castles, 2017; Treiman & Kessler, 2006). This growing sensitivity supports the development of a probabilistic mapping system that facilitates decoding and word recognition, enabling readers to navigate the quasiregular nature of the English orthography -capitalizing on high-frequency patterns while flexibly adapting to less common mappings (Sawi & Rueckl, 2019; Steacy et al., 2019; Treiman et al., 2003 Treiman et al., , 2006)).

To illustrate, early connections between orthography and phonology in developing readers are initially based on simple grapheme-phoneme correspondences that are relatively insensitive to orthographic context (see de Jong & Molenaar, 2025; Share, 1995). With reading development, initially incomplete and oversimplified representations evolve to become more sophisticated context-dependent connections (see Steacy et al., 2019; Treiman et al., 2003 Treiman et al., , 2006)). A good example of this is the ea diagraph which appears frequently in one-syllable words and is inconsistent in its pronunciation. As children develop word reading skill and practice reading in text, they encounter various pronunciations of ea, such as /i/ in meat, /ɛ/ in head, and /eɪ/ in great along with other less frequent pronunciations (e.g., /ə/ in ocean and /I/ in Guinea). Experiences with "ea-words" shape and influence the nature of orthography-tophonological connection for ea slowly evolving into a set of representations that mirror the probabilistic relationship between orthography and phonology for ea in the English corpus.

These sub-lexical connections between orthographic and phonology are influenced by the regularity/consistency of the diagraph pronunciation and the frequency of the words with which the diagraph appears. Difficulties Predicting Word Difficulty in Developing Readers

These relatively simple examples -varied vowel pronunciations of the digraph ea in single-syllable words (e.g., -eat pronunciations) and nonwords across two fairly frequent rime patterns (-eam vs. -ead) -illustrates one of the most persistent challenges posed by quasiregular orthographies such as English: accurately predicting word reading difficulty in developing readers while accounting for word characteristics such as frequency, age of acquisition, phonological and morphological complexity, orthographic neighborhood size, and concreteness.

Predictive models are essential for quantifying how word-level features interact with child-level knowledge and skill across development -for example, capturing the shift from reliance on simple spelling-sound correspondences to greater use of morphological and syllabic structure during word reading development (see Castles, Rastle, & Nation, 2018) -while also enabling the testing and refinement of theoretical accounts of lexical development and word recognition. Such models provide a principled framework for examining the child-by-word interactions that underpin variability in reading performance, particularly in populations with reading difficulties, and for understanding how instructional components influence these interactions (e.g., Steacy et al., 2016). Ultimately, understanding these complex interactions between child knowledge -in this case, word reading skill -and word features is critical for developing instructional systems that address the needs of individual students as they learn to read (e.g., Austin et al., 2022; Papalexandratou et al., 2024; U.S.-DOE, 2023).

Achieving this goal, however, is complicated by the quasi-regular nature of English, which poses significant challenges for generating accurate predictive models across a wide range of words. Existing models tend to be underspecified and often biased by our limited ability to fully capture the quasiregular relations that determine word reading difficulty across words and children (e.g., Lim et al., 2024; Siegelman, Kearns, et al., 2020; Steacy et al., 2022). As a result, the explanatory power of word difficulty predictive models is constrained, reducing their utility for advancing our theoretical understanding of word reading development and providing limited input to instructional design.

To illustrate the nature and potential severity of the problem of predicting difficulty across a broad range of word characteristics, we pooled child and word data across multiple studies with the intent of developing a word-level prediction model of accuracy that could be used for predicting items difficulty (Grade Equivalent) on the 63 words from the Woodcock-Johnson-III Letter-Word Identification (WJ3-LWID; Woodcock et al., 2001) task. Accuracy data from 349 words pooled across five datasets, along with 16 word-level predictors (representing psycholinguistic word properties as well as measures of behavioral performance from the adult ELP), were used as training data to develop a general word difficulty predictive model which was subsequently applied to predict item difficulty on the WJ3-LWID items (for details on the sample and analytic approach see the supplemental materials). Based on the pooled data an additive linear regression model predicting (known) accuracy scores was fit from all predictors with the adjusted-R² of this model being 71.7%. This model was applied to the list of 63 WJ3-LWID items (pseudo-R² was estimated at 60.4%) and the relation between the predicted accuracy scores and WJ's GE scores plotted (Figure 2). The plot suggests that the predictive model of word difficulty is quite accurate for relatively simple words (i.e., lower right of the figure) but loses precision as words become more complex (i.e., upper left of the figure), as indexed by the distance of the word from the regression line. These results underscore the need for a large-scale behavioral database capturing word reading difficulties across a wide range of word characteristics while sampling the full age range of reading skills in English-speaking children in the early stages of learning to read.

Method Design and Procedures

Data collection procedures varied across the project timeline and are described in three formats (Waves 1-3) for the primary data collection, with supplemental archival data included from a prior study. Changes to the data collection procedures across Waves 1-3 were made to increase the probability that children would complete their assigned list of words while also trying to maximize the number of responses per word across the sample. During all aspects of data collection, children worked one-on-one with a trained research assistant in their school, read printed words aloud, and had each response scored as correct or incorrect without performance feedbackfoot_0 . For Waves 1-3, children completed only the word reading tasks, with sessions lasting approximately 20 minutes. In contrast, archival participants completed word reading as part of a larger assessment battery. Across all data collections, children provided verbal assent at the start of the session, and parental consent was obtained beforehand. Data collection procedures were approved by the University Institutional Review Board.

Linking Items

Two sets of linking items, comprising 99 total words, were used throughout Waves 1-3; the 63 words from the discontinued WJ3-LWID and 36 words from the NIH Toolbox (LaForte et al., 2024). For the supplemental archival data only the 63 WJ3-LWID items were available as linking items. Ceiling rules were only used on the WJ3-LWID, as described by the assessment manual, whereas all other items were only scored if the child attempted that item. For the supplemental archival sample, only the 63 WJ3-LWID words were available as linking items.

The primary purpose of these linking items was to place children and words on a common latent reading scale despite the planned-missing design, in which each child read only a subset of the full item pool. Because the same linking words were administered across testing forms and cohorts, they served as common anchors that connected otherwise non-overlapping sets of words and participants. Within the Rasch calibration, performance on the linking items allowed information from individual children to be integrated across forms, enabling the estimation of item difficulties and child reading abilities on a common metric. In essence, the linking items provided the psychometric bridge needed to support scale identification and comparability across the multiple forms, cohorts, and waves of data collection. Ceiling rules were applied only to the WJ3-LWID items, following administration procedures described in the assessment manual. All other words were scored only if attempted by the child.

Scores reflected untimed accuracy on each word with words read correctly scored as a 1 and words read incorrectly scored as a 0. Self-corrections were counted as correct. Participants

Our sampling philosophy for participants was to assess the full range of reading skills across children from the selected grade ranges and allow every child to contribute to the database to the fullest of their ability. Thus, we did not have strict inclusion/exclusion criteria for participation; however, we did ask teachers to identify children within their classroom for which this study was not appropriate, and these children were excluded from assent and assessment. All students with parental consent and child assent were eligible to participate. In rare instances (<0.2% of potential participants), teachers informed the research team that a student was unlikely to be able to meaningfully participate in the assessment due to significant cognitive, behavioral, or communication challenges. These cases were considered individually when determining participation. Data was collected across schools, both public and private, in five different U.S.

States (Connecticut, Florida, Georgia, Maryland, and Pennsylvania). Stimuli

Our initial corpus consisted of approximately the 15,000 most frequently printed words in English for school age children from The Educator's Word Frequency Guide (Zeno et al., 2005).

We trimmed the list of words in the following ways: 1) removed all hyphenated words, words with spaces, and words with apostrophes; 2) removed all first names; 3) retained names of major cities, states, and countries; 4) retained names of historical figures (e.g., Einstein); 5) retained names of groups of people (e.g., Egyptians); 6) removed approximately 1700 simple pluralsaddition of s with no shifts in orthography or phonology (e.g., bats) -to increase the number of unique words in the database; 7) retained plurals that change orthography or phonology (e.g., cities, changes); 8) added back a set of 75 simple plurals (roughly matched to 75 irregular plurals) to allow for a plurals analysis, and 9) removed words that might be considered objectionable to children, teachers, or parents. This resulted in a list of 10,056 to which we added the linking items. We applied the difficulty prediction model/routine described above and rank ordered the words from easiest to most difficult. We selected the first 10,000 words from this list (i.e., eliminating 56 words), retaining all linking items, representing the set of words to sample reading accuracy in children grades 1-5.

Graded word lists were initially constructed for Wave 1 using the rank order list with the NIH linking items mixed throughout the list in position of expected difficulty and the WJ3-LWID removed (these items were administered to identify the grade level list appropriate for each child). Forty lists were built at each reading grade level (1-5) with the idea that lists would sample from a wide range of difficulties while oversampling at the grade level of the list. We anticipated that children at a 1 st grade reading level would be able to read fewer words during an assessment session and so 1 st grade lists were 400 words, whereas 2 nd -5 th grade lists contained 500 words. The Grade Equivalents (GE) of the 63 WJ3-LWID items were used as anchors within the 10,000 words to gauge the general difficulty of words for over sampling at a particular reading grade level.

Sampling for each list varied by reading grade level. Specifically, 1 st grade lists systematically sampled 200 words rank ordered from 1®1000 difficulty, 100 from the 1001®2000 difficulty, 50 from the 2001 ® 3000 difficulty, and 50 from 3001®5000 difficulty; 2 nd grade lists systematically sampled 100 words from 1®2000 difficulty, 300 from the 2001®5000 difficulty, 50 from the 5001®6000 difficulty, and 50 from 6001®8000 difficulty; 3 nd grade lists systematically sampled 100 words from 1®3000 difficulty, 300 from the 3001®6000 difficulty, 50 from the 6001®7000 difficulty, and 50 from 7001®9000 difficulty; 4 th grade lists systematically sampled 100 words from 1®4000 difficulty, 300 from the 4001®7000 difficulty, 50 from the 7001®8000 difficulty, and 50 from 8001®10000 difficulty; and 5 th grade lists systematically sampled 100 words from 1®4000 difficulty, 100 from the 4001®6000 difficulty, 200 from the 6001®8000 difficulty, and 100 from 8001®10000 difficulty. Lists within each grade level can be considered roughly equivalent in terms of overall word difficulty with lists becoming systematically more difficult across grade levels.

Apparatus

A majority of the students completed the word reading assessment on an iPad (whereas the first 67 administrations were presented in a typical paper list format). The iPads were 9th generation iPads with 10.2in retina displays with a resolution of 2160 x 1620 pixels at 264 pixels per inch. The iPad administration was powered using a Shiny app (Chang et al., 2020) created in R and deployed on the web via shinyapps.io. The administration included a two-screen solution such that the child had an iPad and the tester had an iPad each with a unique but linked display.

The tester would first enter the student id into both the student and tester iPad. This would either bring up the appropriate list for that child (when using preassigned fixed form lists) during Wave 1 or set the seed for word randomization to be the same on both the student and tester versions during Wave 2-3 (for later versions with randomization to ensure words were randomized yet in the same random order on both the student and tester iPads). The student iPad presented words in black 30-pixel font on a white background arranged in three columns of 25 words each. Words were presented in lowercase letters except when capitalization was required by standard spelling conventions (e.g., proper nouns). The "Word" variable in the database reflects the exact word form presented to participants. The tester iPad looked similar but had a checkbox for scoring next to each word. Testers would select the words which the child read incorrectly (allowing for unchecking for self-corrections, and data were only sent to the database once the tester hit submit). In Waves 2 and 3, when a child did not complete the full list, on the page that a child stopped, the tester would click a button that said "stopped on this page" and then select the last item that the child attempted; the rest of the words (which were not attempted) would be entered into the database as missing. (Wave 1 data underwent a manual data correction procedure to account for where children stopped before this feature was added to the app.) After completion of a page, both the student and the tester would hit the "Next" button at the bottom right of their screen, which would present the next list to be read. After the assessment was complete, the tester hit the "submit" button to send the item response data via the web to secure database storage.

Analytic Approach

Creating Difficulty Estimates

Before moving to IRT modeling, items were explored using classical test theory in order to evaluate which items were appropriate to include in the IRT model. Because of the sampling procedures of items to children, based on expected item difficulty and child ability, proportion correct was not included in this evaluation. Number of correct and incorrect responses along with item correlations with WJ3-LWID total score were examined. A total of 67 items were removed from the subsequent IRT analysis for having negative correlations with the latent word difficulty trait or not having at least 2 correct and incorrect responses, suggesting the items estimates were not trustworthy. These were removed because items with these characteristics may lead to misleading estimates while not necessarily say anything about the item itself given our sampling procedures (i.e., it may just be due to insufficient data for that item). This resulted in a total of 9,961 items for model fitting and difficulty estimation.

Next, a Rasch model (with planned missing design) was fit to the child-level data using the TAM package (Robitzsch et al., 2024) in R using marginal maximum likelihood estimationfoot_2.

TAM was selected because it permits estimation of item difficulty in the presence of the planned-missing response structure, in which individual children responded to only a subset of the full item pool. The final dataset used here consisted of 631,322 total responses from 1907 children across 9,961 words. This package was selected because it offered the capability to model difficulty in the presence of missing data (i.e., not all children saw all words). However, it should be noted that these procedures assume that data are missing completely at random.

Although it is true that data were missing because the child did not attempt that word, which words were attempted and not attempted did relate in some way to the child's ability level in at least 2 ways: 1) children were assigned to lists of words to attempt based partially on likely ability level and 2) during Wave 1 words were ordered by potential difficulty with children attempting easier words first, children often ran out of time/energy before completing the list and tended to attempt less words above their ability level. It's possible that there is some relation between whether an item was attempted and a child's ability level (i.e., difficult words were less likely to be attempted by poor readers). However, given the linking items attempted by all children span the full range of the item difficulty distribution, we believe this anchored the analysis in a way that allowed for item difficulty to be estimated while taking the ability of the students who attempted that item into account. Because the number and reading skill of students who attempted each item differed, it's not the case that estimates for items with more attempts are necessarily more precise because the precision of the estimate also depends on whether those students represented an appropriate level of ability for that item. For this reason, we avoid using number of attempts in the discussion of precision of individual estimates but still present this data in the relational database. The dataset was collected using a planned-missing design, thus traditional residual-based global fit indices were not computable. Specifically, the standardized root mean square residuals (SRMR, SRMSR) and Q3-based statistics returned infinite or undefined values, which is expected when item pairs share limited overlap or when some items exhibit near-zero variance among respondents. Despite this, alternative global fit indices indicated acceptable overall model performance. The mean absolute deviation of item covariances (100×MADCOV = 3.46) suggested that the Rasch model adequately reproduced the observed covariance structure among items. The Global Homogeneity Parameter (GHP = 0.33) was lower than the ideal value of 1.0, indicating that the model underestimates the variability present in the observed data. This underfit likely reflects the sparse response matrix and not substantive model misspecification. Taken together, the pattern of results suggests that the Rasch model provided a reasonable representation of the data within the constraints of the plannedmissing design, though localized dependencies or multidimensionality cannot be ruled out.

Most of the items did not have what is typically considered to be a sufficient sample size

for this type of model. The average number of responses per word for nonlinking items was 47.14 with a range of 10-592 responses/word. Given this variability and its effects on difficulty rating, we provide standard errors associated with the difficulty estimate to help users gauge the certainty associated with a given difficulty estimate. It is important to interpret the difficulty estimates in the context of the standard error to note the uncertainty surrounding that estimate.

We also provide 95% confidence intervals for the difficulty estimates using this standard error to help the user see the plausible value range for that estimate.

Creating Grade Equivalent Scores

Grade equivalent scores were created by first fitting a function for the WJ3-LWID linking items between the IRT derived difficulty scores and the grade equivalent score from the WJ3-LWID subtest corresponding to the associated raw scores. Plotting the relation between the IRT derived difficulty and WJ grade equivalent scores revealed a very strong, albeit non-linear, relation (see Figure 3). We therefore fitted a function with linear, quadratic, and cubic terms to predict grade equivalence from IRT difficulty, which indeed accounted for 97% of variance in WJ items. The coefficients from this regression were then used to estimate grade equivalent scores from difficulty scores on the full sample of items. This variable is referred to as "grade_eq" in the database.

Interpreting Difficulty Scores

Difficulty estimates were derived from the IRT item difficulty parameter in which easier items have lower values and harder items have larger values. Difficulty had a mean of 0.41 and a standard deviation of 2.03, ranging from -7.02 to 9.15 (see Figure 7). It is important to interpret the difficulty estimates in the context of the associated standard errors to appreciate the uncertainty surrounding individual word difficulty estimates (i.e., the precision of the estimates).

It is noteworthy that our average number of responses (47.14 attempts/item for nonlinking items) is in line with other "Lexicon Projects" across languages (for a summary of average observations per word across studies see Table 1, Stein et al., 2024), with the range being 20-55 average observations per word with one outlier being the Spanish Lexical Project (SPALEX; Aguasvivas et al., 2018) which had an average of 333 response per word. An important caveat is that the d-ELP database presented here is the only "Lexical Project" to fit an IRT-based difficulty model across a sample of participants differing widely in word reading ability.

Figure 7 Here As a tangible example of the interpretability of individual difficulty across items we compare two orthographically related and similarly difficult items "abandon" and "abandoned".

The item "abandon" has a difficulty estimate of 1.02 and "abandoned" has 1.07. While it is reasonable to interpret the difficulty of these words as being close to one another, it is incorrect to conclude that "abandon" is easier than "abandoned". Looking at the confidence interval, the difficulty of "abandon" is likely to occur anywhere between 0.18 and 1.85 and the difficulty of "abandoned" could be anywhere between 0.08 and 2.06. With these overlapping ranges, we are unable to say for sure that the true difficulty of "abandon" is easier than "abandoned"; however, it would be appropriate to conclude that both items are easier than "abdomen" which has a difficulty estimate of 4.48 with a plausible value range between 3.32 and 5.63. And so, while caution is warranted when directly comparing difficulty estimates that are close in value; it is also important to note that the Rasch model empirically orders item difficulties by drawing on the full information available in the data and thus order does matter.

Interpreting Grade Equivalent

Grade equivalent (GE) scores are the difficulty scores described above translated to be on the WJ3-LWID grade equivalent scale norms. These norms were published in 2001 and may not reflect the true performance of students in that grade today. Estimates of difficulty derived from students in grades 1-5 were converted based on WJ3-LWID norms data with words receiving GE scores outside of the grade range of the sample (i.e., 0.4 to 19.7 GE). Similar to difficulty scores, GE scores contain a range of uncertainty around the estimate; words with larger standard errors will have more uncertainty in the grade equivalent scores.

Example: Established Word-Level Predictors

There are numerous potential uses for the d-ELP database. Here, we present one example illustrating how d-ELP data can be used to evaluate the potential contributions of theoretically important word-level characteristics when included in a model that also contains established word-level characteristics of word difficulty. In defining a set of established word-level characteristics associated with word reading difficulty, we draw on the work of Strain et al. (2002), who demonstrated the independent contributions of various word characteristics (i.e., including frequency, word length, transparency, semantic variables, and orthographic neighborhood size) to word recognition performance in adult readers. Our goal here is twofold: (a) to examine whether these word-level characteristics similarly contribute to variation in word difficulty among developing readers and (b) to demonstrate how failure to account for known sources of lexical variance may result in underspecified models that overestimate the importance of less well-established, though theoretically interesting, predictors.

In this set of regression analyses we use the following variables as estimates for established word-level characteristics representing each of the following word traits: frequency (Tasa_sfi), word length (Length), transparency (Spell2pron), semantic measures (Conc_Brys and AoA_Kuper), and neighborhood size (Ortho_N). We include two sematic measures as a means of highlighting the influence of word semantic characteristics on word reading difficulties in developing readers (see Kellenberger et al., 2024), variables that are often overlooked as control variables in developmental word reading studies. TASA_SFI was selected for the illustrative analyses because it is derived from educational print materials and was developed to approximate the frequency with which words occur in texts encountered by school-aged readers (Zeno et al., 1995). The database also includes several alternative frequency measures (e.g., SUBTLEX-US, OpenSubtitles, HAL, and CYP-LEX) to support investigations using different conceptualizations of lexical exposure. Spell2pron is a consistency rating that asked adults to rate the ease of arriving at a correct pronunciation for a word by applying typical decoding rules. The regression analyses examined both the overall contribution of these word characteristics and the unique variance explained by each predictor after accounting for the remaining variables.

Complete data were available for 6,397 words after excluding cases with missing data.

Spell2Pron was log-transformed to address skewness and improve linearity with word difficulty.

To evaluate potential multicollinearity among predictors, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were examined. All VIF values were low (maximum VIF < 2.0), suggesting that multicollinearity was not a substantial concern in these analyses.

Descriptive statistics and correlations are presented in Table 2. All correlations were statistically significant (p < .001). Associations with word reading difficulty ranged from large (AoA_Kuper, Spell2Pron, and Length) to moderate (Ortho_N and TASA_SFI) to small (Conc_Brys). In the initial multiple regression model, all six lexical characteristics were entered simultaneously as predictors of word difficulty. All predictors except concreteness contributed significantly to the model (Table 3, Model 1). Results indicated that words that were longer, less frequent, less transparent, acquired later, and characterized by fewer orthographic neighbors were more difficult to read. Collectively, these predictors accounted for 64.5% of the variance in word reading difficulty across the 6,397 words. We note, that although orthographic neighbors may increase lexical competition in some recognition tasks, larger orthographic neighborhoods have frequently been associated with facilitative effects in word naming, particularly among developing readers, presumably because they provide greater support from familiar orthographic patterns (for details see, Andrews, 1997; Carreiras, Perea, & Grainger, 1997).

To gauge the unique contribution of each variable in predicting word reading difficulty six additional regression models were run (see Table 3, Models 2-7). This is in keeping with the approach advocated for by Balota et al. (2012) of assessing the extent to which a novel predictor accounts for unique variance in megastudies, after controlling for other correlated variables. In these models the unique R² associated with each of the variables (represented by DR²) was estimated by block entering the other five variables followed by adding the variable of interest.

Overall, DR² values ranged from .02% to 11.2% of the variance, with the magnitude of unique contributions rank ordered (from largest to smallest) as follows: age of acquisition, transparency, orthographic neighborhood size, frequency, length, and concreteness. While the results clearly suggest there is considerable shared variance among the 6 predictors (67.9% of the explained variance is shared) there is a sizeable amount of the variance that is unique to the various predictors (32.1% of the variance explained is unique). Notably, variables representing age of acquisition, transparency, and orthographic neighborhood size accounted for the largest portion of unique variance but are largely missing as word-level predictors in developmental experiential and individual differences studies of word reading. This is one example of how the d-ELP can allow researchers to improve their work by providing an evidence-based way to decide which variables to include (here as control variables, but possibly as independent variable as well) in their experimental designs.

Discussion

The English orthography is best described as quasiregular, consisting of a complex interplay between morphological regularities, syllabic constraints, and numerous sub-lexical regularities in spelling-sound correspondences. For this reason, English presents a set of unique and significant challenges for children as they work to develop efficient and automatic word reading skills (see Seymour, 2013; Seymour et al., 2003), with individual differences in word reading development representing a complex interaction between the property of words and the skills and knowledge that children bring to the process of learning to read (e.g., Steacy et al., 2022). Teasing out these complex interactions has been difficult due to the complex task of characterizing "word difficulty" from a developmental word reading perspective. The inability to precisely quantify word difficulty empirically across the wide range of words encountered during reading development has also been related to the lack of a large-scale developmental word reading database. To help alleviate these issues we developed the d-ELP database of word reading performance in a diverse sample of US children. In this first paper, we use these data to derive IRT-based estimates of oral word-reading difficulty on a common developmental scale across a broad range of words and to identify several key word-level characteristics that should be considered in future studies of word reading. As with previous "lexicon projects" we have made our representational database publicly accessible, along with downloadable raw trail data, through the use of an interactive Shiny app. We believe this initial iteration of the d-ELP, and any subsequent versions, will have important educational, psychological, and computational uses going forward that will improve our basic understanding of reading development and practices of improving reading outcomes for children.

Limitations

Placing the 9,961 most frequently printed English words on a continuous difficulty scale represents a major advancement in promoting the understanding and modeling of word reading difficulty across the early reading developmental span. However, this achievement is best viewed as a foundational step. Because it is impractical to ask children to attempt all 9,961 words, the resulting database necessarily has limitations for modeling child-by-word interactions across both a wide developmental range and a large corpus of words (for details, see Keuleers, Diependaele, & Brysbaert, 2010). In addition, the word characteristics contained in the d-ELP were "harvested" from other open-source databases (e.g., CELEX; Baayen, Piepenbrock, & Gulikers, 1993; ELP; Balota et al., 2007; SCOPE; Gao et al., 2023) without any type of verification other than spot checks that the merging processes at the level of the item were accurate. This introduces two immediate problems: 1) there are inaccuracies/inconsistencies in some of the specific word characteristics variables within these mega-database (e.g., number of morphemes and syllables, stress pattern designation, and age-of-acquisition measures) further complicated by discrepancies occurring across sources and 2) many of the existing word variables we include in the d-ELP are based on adult corpus (e.g., orthographic-semantic consistency, frequency, semantic diversity, etc.) or adult ratings (e.g., spelling-to-pronunciation transparency, familiarity, concreteness, etc.). As such, these word characteristics may not necessary be appropriate as measures representing word characteristics in children by systematically overestimating the strengths of word representations as predictors (see Kuperman & Van Dyke, 2013 for the case of age-of-acquisition). In terms of addressing the first issue, we recommend that when using the d-ELP some verification be done on variables known to be subjective in nature or containing inaccuracies. For the second issue we suggest whenever possible using word variables based on child corpus or child rating; keeping in mind there are often not parallel word characteristic variables based on children and adults. Going forward we acknowledge the need to compute and validate "child-directed measures" for many word characteristic measures used in developmental reading studies; we believe that the d-ELP data would be useful in validating such new quantifications. And finally, subgroup comparisons by ethnicity or language ability were outside the scope of the present study and were not conducted.

Nonetheless, the d-ELP provides a powerful tool for selecting words that match specific developmental periods and linguistic characteristics, thereby facilitating more precise and targeted data collection in which children can reasonably attempt all items. We emphasize that this initial iteration of the d-ELP represents a first step toward a more comprehensive understanding of how word characteristics influence reading difficulty in developing readers.

The precision of the current word difficulty estimates is constrained by the number of child-level observations per item, but we aim to establish a mechanism for researchers to contribute additional data to improve item calibration, similar to our integration of archival data in the present study. In this sense, the d-ELP serves as an initial, open-ended framework for estimating and refining word reading difficulty across development.
