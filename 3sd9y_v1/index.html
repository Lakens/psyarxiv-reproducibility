N = 104 SGM emerging adults (age: M = 20.21 years, SD = 1.92 years; 76% assigned female at birth; 35% gender minority; 53% Bisexual+; 55% White) were recruited to codesign (Phases 1-2) or complete for 14 days (Phase 3) a daily diary measure of identity-related and non-identity-related stressors and positive identity-related experiences.

In Phase 1, participants completed individual qualitative interviews to provide feedback on a first draft version of the measure that was created by the research team.

In Phase 3, a larger sample of participants completed the final measure for 14 days.

We recruited 104 participants (n = 8 in Phase 1, n = 6 in Phase 2, n = 90 in Phase 3) between April 2025 and April 2026.

Participants were recruited via the University of Virginia Psychology Department Participant Pool, digital flyers shared to LGBTQ+-focused email lists, and physical flyers posted in general and LGBTQ+-focused spaces around the University of Virginia and broader Charlottesville, VA community.

Participants were eligible for the study if they were between 18 to 29 years of age, self-identified with a sexual and/or gender minority identity, and owned a smartphone.

Participants were compensated via research credits or digital gift card (1 credit or $20 for Phase 1, 2 credits or $40 for Phase 2, and 4 credits or $80 for Phase 3).

Participants were predominantly college students (79%).

During Phase 1 of the study, n = 8 participants completed a one-hour study visit that included a demographics questionnaire followed by an audio-recorded individual qualitative interview.

Participants were first asked to describe the everyday stressors they experience (including both non-identity-related and identity-related stressors) and how they regulate their emotions in response to these stressors.

Participants were asked about identity-related stressors broadly, not just those related to sexual orientation and gender identity.

Then, participants were given a description of the rationale for the daily diary measure, reviewed the first draft measure that was created by the research team, and were asked to provide feedback on how to improve the measure (e.g., how language or response options should be changed to best capture their experiences).

Finally, participants completed a 30-minute audio-recorded individual qualitative interview and provided feedback on how to improve the measure.

One Phase 2 participant was lost to follow-up, so only n = 5 Phase 2 participants completed a qualitative interview.

During the visit, participants were given additional context by the researcher for several measure items (e.g., clarification that they can consider both acute and chronic stressors; see Supplement Section F for more detail).

During this review, the graduate students noted all potential changes to the daily diary measure and study methods that participants had suggested directly (e.g., participant specifically recommending a change) or indirectly (e.g., participant mentioning a certain item was confusing, or discussing aspects of their experience that were not yet captured by the questionnaire).

Specifically, participants completed an average of 84.4% of surveys and took an average of about seven minutes to complete each survey, suggesting the measure yields good compliance and is brief.

The finding that participants had an identity-related stressor on 43.0% of study days is consistent with a prior study of sexual minority adolescents (age 12 to 18 years), which found participants experienced an identity-related stressor on 40.2% of study days (Mereish et al., 2021; though notably their measure captured just distal stressors, whereas ours captured distal and proximal stressors).

For example, our sample had a higher percentage of college students than SGM emerging adults as a whole, participants were all residents of the same city in the southeastern U.S. (which is relevant given prior research finding different levels of minority stress by geographic region; Pachankis et al., 2021), and certain racial/ethnic groups (e.g., Black SGM emerging adults) were not represented in Phases 1 and 2.

Phase 1 participants were on average 19.75 years of age (SD = 1.28 years).

Phase 2 participants were on average 20.67 years of age (SD = 1.03 years).

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. Abstract

Emerging adulthood involves impactful developmental milestones for sexual and gender minority (SGM) individuals and is a critical period for understanding stress processes. Most existing SGM stress research is cross-sectional, with relatively little known about the everyday experiences that may confer mental health risk or resilience for SGM emerging adults. N = 104 SGM emerging adults (age: M = 20.21 years, SD = 1.92 years; 76% assigned female at birth; 35% gender minority; 53% Bisexual+; 55% White) were recruited to codesign (Phases 1-2) or complete for 14 days (Phase 3) a daily diary measure of identity-related and non-identity-related stressors and positive identity-related experiences. Phase 1 participants (n = 8) completed qualitative interviews and provided feedback on an initial draft measure. Phase 2 participants (n = 6) piloted a revised measure for seven days before providing feedback in qualitative interviews. Phase 3 participants (n = 90) completed the final measure for 14 days. Qualitative data from Phases 1-2 were analyzed using the immersion-crystallization framework, and descriptive statistics were computed for Phase 3 quantitative data. In Phases 1-2, participants emphasized the need to capture chronic stressors and stress tied to recent sociopolitical events in the U.S. In Phase 3, non-identity-related stressors were more frequent than identity-related stressors, proximal identity-related stressors were more frequent than distal identity-related stressors, and negative intersectional experiences and positive identity-related experiences were both common. Results highlight the importance of involving participants with lived experiences in all stages of survey design.

Daily Diary Study of Sexual and Gender Minority Emerging Adults' Stressors: Codesign and Preliminary Findings

Sexual and gender minority (SGM) individuals experience mental health problems at higher rates than their cisgender, heterosexual peers (Dhejne et al., 2016; Wittgens et al., 2022).

Minority stress theory is the leading framework through which researchers understand these disparities. The theory posits that, in addition to the general life stressors experienced by everyone, SGM individuals face additional unique stressors related to the stigmatization of SGM identities, the cumulative stress of which is thought to confer higher risk for mental health problems (Hendricks & Testa, 2012; Meyer, 2003). Minority stress theory distinguishes distal minority stressors (i.e., stigma-related processes originating externally, such as discrimination and microaggressions) from proximal minority stressors (i.e., stigma-related processes activated internally and arising from socialization, such as internalized stigma, expectations of rejection, and SGM identity concealment; Frost & Meyer, 2023). Recent theoretical and empirical work has extended minority stress theory by acknowledging stress associated with negative intersectional experiences (e.g., perceived conflict between an SGM identity and another identity) as a contributor to mental health problems among SGM individuals who hold multiple marginalized identities (Jackson et al., 2020; Rivas-Koehl et al., 2023). Additionally, recent work has emphasized positive identity-related experiences as influencing mental health by enhancing positive affect and potentially buffering the deleterious impacts of identity-related stress (Clark et al., 2025; Dyar, Curtis, et al., 2024; Flynn et al., 2026; Mohr & Sarno, 2016; Perrin et al., 2020). Given the important roles of general life stressors, stressors related to the stigmatization of marginalized identities, negative intersectional experiences, and positive identity-related experiences in contributing to mental health in SGM populations, the current study co-designed and pilot tested a novel measure of these experiences in daily life.

Emerging adulthood (ages 18-29; Arnett et al., 2014) is a developmental period characterized by unique stressors and opportunities for SGM individuals (Wagaman et al., 2016).

Gender and sexual identity continue to develop during emerging adulthood (Scroggs & Vennum, 2021), and many people first identify as LGBTQ+ and connect with LGBTQ+ community during this period (Schmitz & Tyler, 2018). Many SGM individuals move away from their hometown during emerging adulthood and must navigate when and with whom to disclose their SGM identities in new contexts (Garvey et al., 2019). Emerging adulthood is also often a time in which individuals first explore romantic and sexual relationships (Gala & Kapadia, 2013), which may mean navigating, or at least anticipating, the potential social consequences of being in a visibly queer relationship for the first time. Many gender minority individuals first become eligible for gender-affirming medical interventions during emerging adulthood (Dai et al., 2024).

Lastly, recent increases in legislation and sociopolitical discourse that threaten the rights of SGM emerging adults have had a major impact on LGBTQ+ individuals in this age group (Lee et al., 2024). Emerging adulthood is therefore a unique period of identity development, opportunity, and stress for SGM individuals, so it is important to understand the daily experiences of

LGBTQ+ individuals during this unique developmental stage.

Hundreds of prior studies have empirically tested components of minority stress theory and its extensions among SGM individuals in emerging adulthood and other developmental stages, effectively documenting the myriad mental health correlates of minority stress, such as higher negative affect, lower positive affect, and higher internalizing psychopathology (Hoy-Ellis, 2023; Nicholas & Bresin, 2024). However, the majority of existing empirical research on SGM populations-91% by a recent estimate-is cross-sectional (Walch et al., 2020). Given the limitations of cross-sectional research (e.g., retrospective bias, inability to separate within-person and between-person effects; Hamaker, 2024; Schwarz, 2007), researchers are increasingly using experience sampling methods (ESM), which are designed to capture psychological processes as they occur real-time in everyday-life situations (Csikszentmihalyi & Larson, 1987), to study SGM stress processes (Nicholas & Bresin, 2024). However, using ESMs, such as daily diary surveys, to study SGM mental health is still a nascent field, so gaps remain in our understanding of how mental health-related processes unfold in SGM emerging adults' daily lives.

Many of the prior SGM mental health studies leveraging ESM have focused on capturing which identity-related stressors individuals experience on a day-to-day basis, revealing initial insights into the types and frequencies of identity-related stressors SGM emerging adults may face. To highlight a few examples, one study found that sexual minority adolescent participants experienced at least one distal identity-related stressor on 40% of study days, with seeing or hearing negative messages about individuals with the same identity being the most frequently endorsed (Mereish et al., 2021). Similarly, a study of gender minority emerging adults found that participants experienced stress related to their gender minority identity approximately 10 out of 21 study days, though the authors did not report the relative frequencies of types of identityrelated stressors endorsed (Dermody et al., 2025). Another study of sexual and gender minority adolescents asked participants to write about their identity-related stressors each day and found that participants reported an identity-related stressor on 42.1% of days, with a variety of stressor themes described (e.g., social rejection, identity disclosure, negative representation, etc.) (Sullivan et al., 2024). Finally, looking at one specific type of identity-related stressor, a study of Black sexual minority adults found that participants endorsed negative intersectional experiences (specifically, distal stressors that simultaneously engaged a participants' racial and sexual orientation identities) on 11.4% of study days (Jackson et al., 2020). Prior research therefore suggests SGM emerging adults may experience an identity-related stressor on roughly one quarter to one half of days (depending on the sample and measurement approach), and that individuals experience a range of specific types of identity-related stressors, including negative intersectional experiences. However, no prior work to our knowledge has investigated the affective impact of identity-related stressors compared to all the other non-identity-related stressors (e.g., school, work, and social experiences) that define emerging adulthood and contribute to risk for psychopathology (Ringwald et al., 2025). Further, little is known about the contexts in which daily stressors occur, such as how often they are happening online versus offline, and how often they involve other people versus occurring alone. Understanding which daily stressors are most impactful, and contextual features of these stressors, is necessary to develop effective, adaptive interventions that can support SGM emerging adults when these various stressors occur (i.e., just-in-time adaptive interventions; Nahum-Shani et al., 2018).

Method Participants

We recruited 104 participants (n = 8 in Phase 1, n = 6 in Phase 2, n = 90 in Phase 3) between April 2025 and April 2026. Participants were recruited via the University of Virginia Psychology Department Participant Pool, digital flyers shared to LGBTQ+-focused email lists, and physical flyers posted in general and LGBTQ+-focused spaces around the University of Virginia and broader Charlottesville, VA community. Participants were eligible for the study if they were between 18 to 29 years of age, self-identified with a sexual and/or gender minority identity, and owned a smartphone. The informed consent process was conducted with a researcher prior to the start of study procedures. Participants were compensated via research credits or digital gift card (1 credit or $20 for Phase 1, 2 credits or $40 for Phase 2, and 4 credits or $80 for Phase 3). In Phase 3, credits/payment were prorated based on the percentage of surveys completed. Among those who participated for payment, the five participants with the highest daily diary completion percentage received a $10 bonus. The full sample was 76% assigned female at birth, 53% Bisexual+ (i.e., identified as Bisexual or Pansexual), 35% gender minority, 55% White, and the mean age was 20.21 years (SD = 1.92 years). Participants were predominantly college students (79%). See Table 1 for detailed demographic information broken down by study phase.

Procedure

All study procedures were approved by and conducted in compliance with the University of Virginia Institutional Review Board. Study visits were conducted in person or via Zoom video call, depending on participant preference and researcher availability.

Phase 1

During Phase 1 of the study, n = 8 participants completed a one-hour study visit that included a demographics questionnaire followed by an audio-recorded individual qualitative interview. Participants were first asked to describe the everyday stressors they experience (including both non-identity-related and identity-related stressors) and how they regulate their emotions in response to these stressors. Participants were asked about identity-related stressors broadly, not just those related to sexual orientation and gender identity. This allowed for discussion of stressors related to other identities (e.g., race, ethnicity, body size, disability status, religion, socioeconomic status, etc.) and intersectionality (e.g., stressors related to specific combinations of identities). Then, participants were given a description of the rationale for the daily diary measure, reviewed the first draft measure that was created by the research team, and were asked to provide feedback on how to improve the measure (e.g., how language or response options should be changed to best capture their experiences). See Supplement Section A for the interview guide used in Phase 1.

Phase 2

During Phase 2, a separate sample of n = 6 participants first completed a 30-minute initial study visit that involved completing a demographic questionnaire and downloading the RealLife Exp application (an experience sampling platform; LifeData, 2026) on their personal smartphone. Then, participants went about their lives for seven days while completing the daily diary measure each evening via the RealLife Exp application. Finally, participants completed a 30-minute audio-recorded individual qualitative interview and provided feedback on how to improve the measure. See Supplement Section B for the interview guide used in Phase 2. One Phase 2 participant was lost to follow-up, so only n = 5 Phase 2 participants completed a qualitative interview.

Phase 3

During Phase 3, a third sample of n = 90 participants first completed a one-hour study visit that involved completing a demographic questionnaire, completing several other baseline questionnaires (not analyzed in the present study), downloading the RealLife Exp application on their personal smartphone, and getting oriented to the smartwatch that recorded passive data (also not relevant to the present study) during the subsequent assessment period. During the visit, participants were given additional context by the researcher for several measure items (e.g., clarification that they can consider both acute and chronic stressors; see Supplement Section F for more detail). Then, participants went about their lives for 14 days while completing the daily diary measure each evening. In this phase, study staff followed up over email or phone every other day if surveys were missed to encourage survey completion and offer technical support.

Materials and Measures

Demographic Characteristics

The demographic self-report questionnaire assessed age, sex assigned at birth, gender identity, sexual orientation, race and ethnicity, first language, occupation, disability status, and estimated household income.

Initial Daily Diary Measure

Prior to the start of the study, the research team created an initial draft of the daily diary measure to be reviewed by participants in Phase 1. The measure was designed to capture several content areas relevant to psychopathology risk in SGM emerging adults: 1) Day-level positive and negative affect, 2) the two most intense stressors experienced that day, 3) the emotion regulation strategies participants used in response to these two most intense stressors,foot_0 3) identity-related stressors experienced that day, and 4) positive identity-related experiences.

We designed the measure to be modular, such that future researchers can choose to administer the sections relevant to their research questions. The initial draft measure used in Phase 1 is presented in Supplement Section C. Several existing measures informed the initial daily diary measure. Day-level positive and negative affect were captured with the Internationally Reliable Short-Form of the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (I-PANAS-SF; Thompson, 2007). No changes were made to these items during the study to retain the psychometric properties of the I-PANAS-SF. The two most intense daily stressors were captured via participant free response, and the first-draft follow-up questions about contextual features of these stressors (e.g., domain of stressor) were based partly on our prior research on anxiety-related stressors among university members (Leventhal et al., 2025). The first draft of the list of emotion regulation strategies that participants could endorse using in response to the two most intense daily stressors was based on our team's prior experience sampling research on emotion regulation strategy use in the daily lives of socially anxious college students (e.g., Daniel et al., 2020 Daniel et al., , 2023)). The first draft list of identity-related stressors from which participants could select was adapted from the Everyday Identity Stress Scale (Mereish et al., 2021). Finally, the first draft of positive identity-related experience items were created by the study team.

Refining the Measure After Phases 1 and 2

Our data collection and analysis approach during Phases 1 and 2 was based on the "immersion-crystallization" framework. Immersion-crystallization is a rapid, iterative, inductive analysis process whose core elements, as the name implies, include immersion (reviewing the data in depth, such as through research team members individually reading and listening to transcripts) and crystallization (reflecting on the analysis experience to identify and articulate patterns or themes noticed during the immersion process, such as through discussion among the research team; Borkan, 2022). A key difference from other qualitative research approaches, such as thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006), is that immersion-crystallization does not typically generate themes via systematic coding, but rather through a holistic review of the data and researchers' insight. It therefore tends to be a faster approach, which aligned with our goal to develop and rapidly iteratively refine a measure for use in a large data collection.

Four trained undergraduate research assistants conducted the qualitative interviews, which were then transcribed to text. Then, following each study phase, a team of four clinical psychology graduate students independently reviewed the transcripts, such that each transcript was reviewed by two individuals (i.e., the immersion step). During this review, the graduate students noted all potential changes to the daily diary measure and study methods that participants had suggested directly (e.g., participant specifically recommending a change) or indirectly (e.g., participant mentioning a certain item was confusing, or discussing aspects of their experience that were not yet captured by the questionnaire). Next, the research assistants who led the interviews and the graduate students met as a team to discuss interpretations of the data until consensus was reached regarding how to change the daily diary measure to incorporate participant feedback (i.e., the crystallization step). Additionally, following Phase 2 of the study, participants' daily diary measure data from Phase 2 were discussed among the research team to further guide measure design. Specifically, the team checked how long the survey took to complete, how often a top two daily stressor was identity-related, and the frequency with which all identity-related stressors that day were endorsed to check whether the overall measurement approach (i.e., capturing all stressors that day but focusing primarily on the two most stressful experiences) sufficiently captured a range of stressors.

The final version of the measure is presented in Supplement Section E. Researchers interested in using the measure are encouraged to use the final version. Tables 2 3contain descriptive statistics corresponding to each non-free-response item in the final measure. The final measure first asks about daily affect, then asks about contextual features of the first and second most stressful daily experiences (e.g., the domain of the stressor; whether it was identity related; if identity related, which from a list identity-related stressors best describes what happened; etc.).

It ends with questions about the day as a whole, including presenting participants with checklists to select all identity-related stressors and positive identity-related experiences that occurred that day.

Descriptive Statistics of Phase 3 Quantitative Data

To understand overall endorsement rates for categorical items (i.e., multiple choice and select-all-that-apply questions), we computed the total number of times and percentage of times each response option was selected across the entire sample. Note that for single-select items, percentages add up to 100%, and for select-all-that-apply items, percentages add up to greater than 100%. Additionally, to understand endorsement rates at the participant level, we computed the number of participants and percentage of participants who selected each response option at least once during the 14-day study. For numeric items (i.e., Likert scale, sliding scale, and numeric free-response questions), we computed the means and standard deviations across the entire sample. In the Results section, we highlight the median (rather than mean) when the distribution of a numeric variable was highly skewed.

Stress Related to Sociopolitical Events

Several participants reported that recent sociopolitical events were significant daily stressors. For example, one participant discussed how changes in federal and local policy had affected their access to healthcare: "…with all the stuff going on with, like, the administration, that's been a little stressful, because everything got a lot more expensive, and I can't get my testosterone through [the student health clinic] anymore." -P 103

Similarly, in the daily diary free-response question asking participants to describe the most stressful experience that day, several Phase 2 participants wrote about sociopolitical events. For example, on one day, one participant wrote: "I learned that the Supreme Court ruled today that states can ban gender affirming care for minors." -P 201

Chronic Stressors

Several participants described being impacted by chronic stressors that the initial versions of the daily diary measure did not capture well. For example, one participant noted, "This survey is only asking about... daily stress. Sometimes stress, often it's actually... over a long period of time. It's significantly stressful even though on any given day it's not the most stressful thing that happened." -P 201

Another participant discussed chronic stigma-related stress that they felt was not well captured in early versions of the measure: "That subtle dehumanization that you encounter every single day can build up in a way that gets really exhausting, even if there isn't necessarily one instance one day that, you know, really cements it." -P 205

Distal vs. Proximal Stressors

Consistent with minority stress theory (Meyer, 2003; Testa et al., 2017), early versions of the measure differentiated between distal/external stressors (e.g., discrimination) and proximal/internal stressors (e.g., internalized negative attitudes). However, several participants reported instances where, in practice, this distinction was difficult to make. For example, one participant noted: "The first couple of days I had... trouble deciding which of these events is external? Which of these was internal?" -P 202

Another participant discussed a specific example where they were made to feel uncomfortable by another person and were unsure how to categorize the experience in the measure: "External events... I don't know, because there's no solid line between internal and external events... I was made to feel uncomfortable...that's external stimuli, but in an internal feeling." -P 201

Emotion Regulation Strategies

Participants discussed engaging in emotion regulation strategies that were not captured well by early versions of the daily diary measure. For example, early versions of the measure included an option for expressing emotions to other people, but one participant explained that there are times they let out difficult emotions by themselves: "I feel like for some of these it's… expressing your emotions, like getting them out physically, like a physical reaction. So, sometimes that's like crying, screaming… I feel like that's a fairly common experience. But, I don't think if I was doing that, I would know where to categorize that under." -P 104

Another participant recommended we add an option explicitly capturing engagement in hobbies: "You could add something about hobbies… Because maybe someone doesn't do any of these [other listed] things, but there's like other hobbies." -P 107

Phase 3 Measure Completion Phase 3 participants combined completed 1,064 out of 1,260 possible surveys (84.4% overall completion rate) over the 14-day assessment period. An additional 32 surveys were initiated but discontinued prior to completion. The total number of surveys out of 14 (reflecting one assigned per day) completed by a given participant ranged from one to 14, with a mean of 11.82 (SD = 2.32). The survey took participants a median time of 6.85 minutes (SD after removing outliers greater than or equal to 60 min = 6.69 minutes) to complete.

Phase 3 Measure Descriptive Statistics

In this section, we highlight descriptive statistics for items related to daily affect, stressors, and positive identity-related experiences, from the final measure deployment in Phase 3. For the full reporting of descriptive statistics, refer to Tables 2 and 3.

Affect

The I-PANAS-SF items showed acceptable internal consistency within and between participants for both positive affect items (ω-within = 0.73, ω-between = 0.89) and negative affect items (ω-within = 0.69, ω-between = 0.92).

Domains of Top Two Stressors

Participants most frequently reported that their first-and second-most stressful daily experiences (termed top two stressors below, for brevity) were in the domains of academics, work, or career development (47.5% of stressors), social situations (25.2%), and family and home life (13.5%).

Identity Relatedness of Top Two Stressors

When asked whether a top two stressor was related to an identity they hold, or discrimination or stigma towards an identity they hold, participants most frequently responded "no" (84.6% of stressors) followed by "yes" (10.0%) and maybe (5.4%). "No" was selected at least once during the 14 days by all 90 participants, "yes" by 60 participants (66.7%), and "maybe" by 46 participants (51.1%). When a top two stressor was identity-related or maybeidentity-related, participants most often reported that the stressor was related to a sexual minority identity (59.6% of stressors), followed by gender minority identity (29.5%), identity as a woman or man (22.9%), cognitive/psychological disability (12.7%), race (5.7%), or ethnicity (5.7%).

Participants reported that an identity-related top two stressor was related to a single identity for 65.1% of stressors, and multiple identities for 34.9% of stressors. When asked for the categorical description(s) that led them to endorse an identity-related or maybe-identity-related top two stressor, participants most often selected, "My identity interfered with my life" (31.6%), followed by, "I worried that others would reject or not accept me due to my identity" (25.9%), "I actively concealed my identity from others" (16.9%), and "I felt negatively about my identity or people with my identity," (16.0%).

Other Contextual Features of Top Two Stressors

Participants reported that top two daily stressors involved other people (vs. involving no other people) about half the time (50.8%). Top two stressors most often occurred offline (86.2%) vs. online.

All Daily Identity-Related Stressors

When asked to report all daily identity-related stressors (not just those among the top two most stressful experiences that day), participants reported experiencing one or more identityrelated stressors on 43.0% of days, the most frequent being "My identity interfered with my life" (12.6% of days), followed by "I worried that others would reject or not accept me due to my identity" (12.3%), "I actively concealed my identity from others" (11.7%), and "I saw or heard negative messages or stereotypes about my identity" (10.6%).

Positive Identity-Related Experiences

Participants reported having one or more positive identity-related experiences on 49.8% of days, the most frequently endorsed experiences being, "I had positive interactions with another person who shares my identity" (27.9% of days), "I saw or heard something positive about individuals with my identity (18.7%) and, "I felt that someone understood my experience as a person with my identity" (14.8%). Additionally, participants frequently endorsed socially rewarding experiences not necessarily related to identity: "I felt close or connected to someone" was endorsed on 47.4% of days, and "I felt accepted or well-liked by someone" was endorsed on 27.9% of days. On average, participants estimated that identity-related stress accounted for a relatively low percentage of overall daily stress (median = 8% of daily stress).

Discussion

Measure Refinements and Lessons Learned from Phases 1-2 Qualitative Data

Qualitative feedback from Phases 1 and 2 informed several design choices for our measurement development, with broader implications for SGM mental health research. First, participants reported stressors tied to current sociopolitical events, suggesting that measures need to comprehensively capture these experiences. Notably, in the months prior to and during data collection for Phases 1 and 2 (which spanned April to June 2025), several significant federal and local sociopolitical events occurred and were directly cited by participants during qualitative interviews, including the start of President Trump's second term in January 2025, the Trump administration's executive order directing health agencies to restrict gender-affirming medical care to individuals younger than 19 (Protecting Children From Chemical and Surgical Mutilation, 2025), and the University of Virginia health system ceasing offering these services to new patients younger than 19 (Allard & Wu, 2025). To address this feedback in our measure, we added "politics/current events" as a response option for the initial question in which participants indicate the overall domain of their first/second most stressful experience that day. Additionally, we added the following response options to the select-all that apply questions characterizing daily identity-related stressors: 1) "I learned about laws/policies that would harm those with my identity," and 2) "I was directly impacted by laws/policies harmful to those with my identity."

The fact that the existing measures used to create the initial draft of the daily diary measure did not include such response options suggests that when studying phenomena tied to dynamic social and structural forces (e.g., changing cultural attitudes and policies), the way we measure these phenomena needs to change over time.

Second, participants emphasized that their most salient stressors each day were sometimes chronic, not necessarily starting and ending that day. To address this feedback, we broadened the wording of certain questions to allow participants to answer with respect to chronic stressors (e.g., changing "Please think of the MOST stressful experience you had today" to "Please think of the experience that caused you the MOST stress today") and explicitly informed participants in the Phase 3 baseline visit that they could respond with respect to acute or chronic stressors: "… please think of the experience that caused the most stress that day. So, it could be an experience that actually happened that day, but it could also be something that happened in the past, OR something that is more chronic and does not have a clear start and end time." In general, experience sampling research about stress largely focuses on acute stressors (Weber et al., 2022), but the present study suggests measurement approaches that can additionally capture chronic stressors are warranted. Future research should investigate how best to capture how chronic stressors are experienced day-to-day among SGM emerging adults by comparing various strategies head-to-head (e.g., continuous scales vs. inventories vs. free response, etc.).

Finally, participants found the distinction between distal and proximal identity-related stressors to be confusing. Initial versions of the daily diary measure specifically asked participants to describe that day's most and second most stressful distal stressors (phrased as the most stressful experience that day that "involved external events or other people"). This distinction was made in line with minority stress theory's separation of distal versus proximal stressors (Hendricks & Testa, 2012; Meyer, 2003). However, the qualitative interviews suggest this theoretical distinction may not fit with SGM emerging adults' phenomenological experience, and hence, that assessing one of these stressor types in isolation from the other may be difficult.

To address this feedback, for the current measure, we included both distal and proximal stressors in the same inventory, removing the need for participants to make this distinction, but allowing researchers to optionally separate these constructs after collecting the data.

Phase 3 Descriptive Statistics

The rates with which participants in Phase 3 completed the final version of the SGM-DEM support the feasibility of using the measure to capture SGM mental health-related experiences in the context of a multi-week data collection. Specifically, participants completed an average of 84.4% of surveys and took an average of about seven minutes to complete each survey, suggesting the measure yields good compliance and is brief.

Descriptive statistics from Phase 3 revealed several findings that build on existing SGM mental health research. First, identity-related stressors occurred often, but the most intense stressors were typically not identity-related. That is, participants reported one or more identityrelated stressors on 43.0% of days, but identity-related stressors accounted for 15.4% of top two stressors, and a median participant-estimated 8% of total daily stress. The finding that participants had an identity-related stressor on 43.0% of study days is consistent with a prior study of sexual minority adolescents (age 12 to 18 years), which found participants experienced an identity-related stressor on 40.2% of study days (Mereish et al., 2021; though notably their measure captured just distal stressors, whereas ours captured distal and proximal stressors). Our study is the first to our knowledge to assess daily identity-related stress in the context of SGM emerging adults' overall daily life stress. In line with prior work finding relationships, health, and work/school-related difficulties to be among the most salient stressors during emerging adulthood (Descary et al., 2026; Matud et al., 2023), our study found that similar stressor domains (academics/work/career development, social situation, family and home life, mental health) were most salient for SGM emerging adults (though notably both identity-related and non-identity-related stressors could be included in these domains). The present study suggests identity-related stressors may account for a relatively small (though still substantial) proportion of overall daily stress among SGM emerging adults.

Second, proximal identity-related stressors were more common and more frequently a top two daily stressor relative to distal identity-related stressors (based on traditional proximal/distal categories). For example, the proximal stressors, "I worried that others would reject or not accept me due to my identity" (capturing expectations of rejection), "I actively concealed my identity from others" (capturing identity concealment), "I felt negatively about my identity or people with my identity" (capturing internalized stigma), and "I felt like different identities I hold were in conflict" (capturing identity conflict) were all more common than the most frequently endorsed distal stressor, "I was misunderstood because of my identity" among top two stressors. 4 This result is aligned with a prior experience sampling study that found proximal identity-related stressors were more strongly associated with day-level negative mental health outcomes than distal stressors among Bisexual+ adults (Feinstein et al., 2022). Additionally, this result appears consistent with the social safety perspective, a theoretical alternative to minority stress theory proposed by Diamond and Alley, which posits that SGM communities' elevated psychopathology risk is driven primarily by chronic safety-motivated threat vigilance (e.g., monitoring others for signs of potential rejection) during situations in which the level of social safety (i.e., reliable social connection, inclusion, and protection) is ambiguous or low (2022).

These findings support the notion that threat vigilance is a primary component of identity-related stress, and future work should assess if and how these experiences at the daily level may serve as a core pathway to mental health problems, consistent with the social safety perspective.

Third, results revealed that identity-related stressors were most typically related to SGM identities, but that stressors related to other identities (including combinations of identities) were common. For instance, while participants most often reported that a top two stressor was related to a sexual minority identity followed by gender minority identity (59.6% and 29.5% of identityrelated/maybe-identity-related stressors, respectively), they also frequently reported that stressors were related to identity as a woman or man (22.9%), cognitive/psychological disability (12.7%), race (5.7%), and ethnicity (5.7%). Negative intersectional experiences were also common. For instance, participants reported that a stressor was related to two or more identities for 34.9% of identity-related or maybe-identity-related top two stressors, and the proximal stressor, "I felt like different identities I hold were in conflict," was endorsed for 12.3% of identity-related/maybeidentity-related top two stressors and 6.0% of days. These results are consistent with prior work finding that SGM populations experience frequent stressors tied to the stigmatization of other marginalized identities they hold (e.g., racial/ethnic identities), as well as negative intersectional experiences (Jackson et al., 2020; Mereish et al., 2021). This suggests that models of minority stress that account for such intersectional experiences (e.g., Rivas-Koehl et al., 2023) may more comprehensively capture the range of stressors faced by SGM emerging adults. Further, results support the inclusion of identity conflict as a proximal stressor in models of SGM mental health, despite its absence from early models (Meyer, 2003).

Fourth, the most stressful daily experiences were largely offline (86.2% of top two stressors) versus online and involved other people about half the time. That the top stressors were overwhelmingly offline is notable given emerging adults often form relationships and communicate with others through digital means (Hossain, 2026), and given recent research emphasizes the harms associated with social media use (Agyapong-Opoku et al., 2025). The present study suggests the most salient daily stressors are offline experiences, which is consistent with research indicating LGBTQ+ individuals in particular often find supportive community online (despite also being at risk for cyber-harassment; Ramirez et al., 2025) Fifth, the finding that stressors involved other people about half the time (and were explicitly in the domain of relationships about a quarter of the time) supports prior research indicating social relationships are among the most important but stressful life domains in emerging adulthood (Descary et al., 2026; Matud et al., 2023). It also suggests solitary stressors (e.g., perhaps academic-or work-related stressors) are just as salient. Notably, the majority of participants in the Phase 3 sample were undergraduate college students, for whom prior research and the present study suggest that academics are the most frequent stressor (Pitt et al., 2018). It could be that academics (which may presumably be coded by participants as offline and nonsocial) are driving these effects, and that findings would differ in samples of SGM emerging adults that have a smaller proportion of students.

Finally, results support the assertion that positive identity-related experiences are common, perhaps more so than identity-related stressors. Specifically, in the present study, positive identity-related experiences occurred on 49.8% of days (vs. 43.0% of days for identityrelated stressors). That positive identity-related experiences were so common among Phase 3 participants suggests they are core parts of SGM emerging adults' daily experiences and feasible to study in a daily diary format. The similar prevalences of positive identity-related experiences and identity-related stressors in this sample (of mostly college students) is consistent with prior research finding SGM college students have mixed perceptions regarding whether their college environment is supportive, neutral, or hostile (Pitcher et al., 2018).

Limitations

This study's findings should be considered in light of its limitations. First, the research team decided on key aspects of the daily diary measure (e.g., overall content areas) prior to codesign, perhaps limiting the scope of recommendations the sample offered. Second, while codesigning our daily diary measure with input from our target population is a strength, our study did not include a comprehensive psychometric evaluation of the measure (a weakness of experience sampling research in general: and minority stress experience sampling research in particular: Nicholas & Bresin, 2024). Third, our sample differed slightly from the target population (SGM emerging adults) in some ways that may limit generalizability of the measure and study findings. For example, our sample had a higher percentage of college students than SGM emerging adults as a whole, participants were all residents of the same city in the southeastern U.S. (which is relevant given prior research finding different levels of minority stress by geographic region; Pachankis et al., 2021), and certain racial/ethnic groups (e.g., Black SGM emerging adults) were not represented in Phases 1 and 2. Further, while a broad measure of SGM mental health-related experiences may be useful for certain studies, researchers interested in narrowing in on experiences of SGM subcommunities (e.g., bi+ stress; Feinstein et al., 2022) may require more targeted measures. Finally, given the present study provided tiered compensation (up to $80 or 4 research credits) based on survey completion, as well as occasional email/phone check-ins to support compliance-it is unclear how completion rates for the present measure may differ with different payment structures and compliance monitoring approaches.

Conclusion

The present study recruited SGM emerging adults to codesign a daily diary measure of SGM mental health-related experiences and deployed the final measure in a separate sample of SGM emerging adults for two weeks. Qualitative data revealed several areas for improvement in the measure, including adding questions to explicitly capture stress tied to sociopolitical events, and adjusting language to also capture chronic stressors. Quantitative data from participants who completed the final measure for two weeks revealed that non-identity-related stressors occurred more frequently than identity-related stressors, that proximal stressors were experienced more frequently than distal stressors, and that negative intersectional experiences and positive identityrelated experiences were both common. Collectively, findings highlight the importance of involving participants with lived experience in all stages of the study design process when researching constructs linked to dynamic social and structural factors.
