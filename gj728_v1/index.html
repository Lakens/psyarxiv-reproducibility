Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

This chapter describes a neuroscience-informed psychoeducation (NIPE) approach to cognitive rehabilitation (CR) following acquired brain injury (ABI), with primary emphasis on traumatic brain injury and stroke. NIPE is conceptualised as more than information provision: neuroscience-informed explanations are used to help patients and families understand cognitive difficulties, recovery, rehabilitation strategies, and their implications for everyday functioning. Neuropsychological feedback is presented as a common context for NIPE, with attention to tailoring, functional translation, multimodal support, and verification of understanding.

A dual-lens framework is proposed for NIPE delivery in CR. The therapeutic lens emphasises alliance, empathy and warmth, person-centred collaboration, shared explanatory models, and goal setting. The cognition-informed lens addresses how cognitive difficulties may affect learning through adjustment of information load and pacing, repetition, clear and concrete language, integration with existing knowledge, teach-back, and multimodal delivery. Together, these lenses inform considerations of timing and context and seven structural principles of NIPE: trustworthiness, interaction, salience, motivation, intensity, repetition, and multimodal/multimedia delivery. The chapter also considers NIPE content related to neuroplasticity, prognosis and recovery, rehabilitation approaches, and factors that may influence functioning and participation. Throughout, emphasis is placed on distinguishing established evidence from plausible but untested mechanisms and on presenting recovery in probabilistic rather than deterministic terms.

1. Introduction

Neuroscience-informed psychoeducation (NIPE) can be used within cognitive rehabilitation following acquired brain injury (ABI) to help patients and families develop a workable understanding of what has changed, why difficulties may be occurring, and what can be done to support functioning in everyday life. Following ABI, cognitive difficulties such as slowed processing, attentional impairment, and reduced new learning can affect how information is acquired and retained (Maas et al., 2022; El Husseini et al., 2023). The manner in which information is presented is therefore clinically important. In this context, NIPE may support self-awareness, realistic expectations about recovery, understanding of rehabilitation strategies, and engagement in treatment.

This chapter considers NIPE within cognitive rehabilitation, with particular emphasis on traumatic brain injury (TBI) and stroke, and describes neuropsychological feedback as a common context for its delivery. Building on the foundational NIPE framework introduced in Chapter 1 (Rezapour et al., 2026), this chapter applies NIPE specifically to cognitive rehabilitation following ABI. A dual-lens framework is proposed for this purpose. The therapeutic lens emphasises alliance, empathy and warmth, person-centred collaboration, shared explanatory models, and goal setting. The cognition-informed lens considers how cognitive difficulties affect the acquisition, retention, and application of new information. Together, these lenses inform considerations of timing and context and seven structural principles of NIPE: trustworthiness, interaction, salience, motivation, intensity, repetition, and multimodal/ multimedia delivery. These principles provide a practical framework for delivering information in ways that are credible, understandable, memorable, and relevant to everyday functioning.

2. Main clinical populations

This chapter focuses primarily on TBI and stroke because both are common forms of ABI and have substantial cognitive rehabilitation literatures. Other causes of ABI include hypoxic-ischaemic or anoxic injury, central nervous system infection, brain tumours and their treatment, and toxic or metabolic insults. Although cognitive rehabilitation has also been applied to other neurological conditions, including dementia (Kudlicka et al., 2023), and broader cognition-oriented interventions have been studied across other older adult populations (Gavelin et al., 2020), these populations are beyond the scope of this chapter.

2.1 Traumatic brain injury

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) refers to an acquired disruption of brain function caused by an external mechanical force. Its cognitive, emotional, behavioural, and physical consequences vary according to factors such as injury severity and characteristics, premorbid functioning, and the broader clinical and psychosocial context (Maas et al., 2022; Rauchman et al., 2023).

Cognitive sequelae may include difficulties with attention, processing speed, memory and new learning, executive functioning, and social cognition. Emotional and behavioural changes are also common and can include depression, anxiety, irritability, emotional lability, fatigue, sleep disturbance, post-traumatic stress symptoms, impulsivity, disinhibition, apathy, and reduced frustration tolerance. Moderate to severe TBI may also affect self-awareness and self-monitoring. Longer-term consequences can include changes in social participation, relationships, and behaviour (Maas et al., 2022).

2.2 Stroke (cerebrovascular accident)

Stroke results from cerebral infarction or haemorrhage and can produce focal neurological and cognitive impairments depending on the location and extent of injury (Sacco et al., 2013; Coupland et al., 2017; Hilkens et al., 2024). Major stroke subtypes include ischaemic stroke, intracerebral haemorrhage, and subarachnoid haemorrhage. Ischaemic strokes account for most strokes and result from interruption of blood flow, whereas haemorrhagic strokes result from bleeding within or around the brain.

Post-stroke cognitive impairment is common and can affect processing speed, attention, memory, executive functioning, language, and visuospatial abilities (El Husseini et al., 2023). The pattern of impairment varies with lesion location and distributed network involvement. Emotional sequelae, including depression and anxiety, are also common (Chun et al., 2022). Impaired awareness or anosognosia may occur in some patients and can affect safety, goal setting, and participation in rehabilitation (Orfei et al., 2007). Longer-term consequences can include reduced independence, social participation, and community functioning.

3.1 Purpose and rationale

NIPE content focuses on brain-based explanations of the mechanisms and symptoms of brain disorders, as well as the neural processes associated with treatment response, remission, and recovery (Rezapour et al., 2026). In this chapter, the emphasis is specifically on neuroscience-informed explanations relevant to recovery and rehabilitation following acquired brain injury. The mechanisms and symptoms of acquired brain injury are addressed elsewhere in this volume.

Psychoeducation provided as part of cognitive rehabilitation is often neuroscience-informed, but the inclusion of brain-related information alone does not necessarily make an intervention NIPE. Within cognitive rehabilitation, NIPE refers more specifically to the deliberate use of neuroscience-informed explanations to help patients understand how brain function relates to cognitive difficulties, rehabilitation strategies, and everyday functioning (Rezapour et al., 2026). In this sense, NIPE extends beyond information provision by helping patients develop a more coherent and personally meaningful understanding of both their difficulties and the rehabilitation process. Accordingly, neither neuropsychological feedback nor cognitive rehabilitation should be considered NIPE simply because neuroscience is discussed. What distinguishes NIPE is the purposeful use of neuroscience-informed explanations to support understanding and rehabilitation, together with attention to how that information is structured and delivered.

Cognitive rehabilitation provides a natural context for NIPE because it explicitly links brain function to everyday behavior. By making these relationships understandable and personally relevant, NIPE may support mechanistic self-awareness by helping patients recognize patterns of cognitive difficulty and anticipate their impact. It may also strengthen the rationale for rehabilitation strategies by linking specific techniques to underlying cognitive processes and clarifying when and why those strategies may be useful across contexts. In addition, NIPE may help align expectations about recovery and support motivation and persistence by framing recovery as a process that can involve neural adaptation, learning, compensation, and adjustment rather than as a fixed limitation. Together, these proposed pathways suggest that NIPE may function as an active component of rehabilitation rather than solely as a supplementary source of information.

3.2 Neuropsychological feedback as an entry point

Psychoeducation can occur throughout cognitive rehabilitation, but neuropsychological assessment feedback provides a natural entry point for NIPE. Early work emphasised the clinical importance of communicating neuropsychological findings in ways that patients and families could understand and use (Gass and Brown, 1992). Consumer research also identified variability in whether feedback was provided and whether patients experienced it as useful and understandable, highlighting the importance of clear and meaningful communication of assessment findings (Bennett-Levy et al., 1994). Subsequent collaborative and therapeutic assessment models increasingly conceptualised assessment feedback as a potentially therapeutic process rather than solely as the communication of test results (Fischer, 2000; Gorske, 2008).

Early empirical work on assessment feedback consisted largely of consumer and practitioner surveys addressing satisfaction, perceived usefulness, and clinical practice (Bennett-Levy et al., 1994; Smith et al., 2007). More recent studies have examined broader outcomes. In an observational study, patients who attended neuropsychological feedback reported greater improvement in quality of life, understanding of their condition, and ability to cope than patients who did not receive direct feedback (Rosado et al., 2018). Evidence from controlled research is more limited. A randomised controlled crossover trial in multiple sclerosis found no between-group improvement at one week in knowledge of cognitive functioning or coping, although within-subject analyses at one month showed improvements in perceived cognitive functioning, self-efficacy, stress, and depression (Longley et al., 2023). These findings suggest potential benefits of feedback while also highlighting the limited and heterogeneous evidence regarding specific therapeutic effects.

A major scoping review found considerable heterogeneity in neuropsychological feedback methods and outcomes but identified several convergent practice recommendations, including tailoring information to the individual, translating findings into everyday functioning, involving family or support persons when appropriate, using multimodal materials, and checking understanding (Gruters et al., 2022). A more recent clinical review similarly emphasises neuropsychological feedback as a therapeutic communication process in which the way information is conveyed can influence understanding and followthrough (Armstrong et al., 2024). Neuropsychological feedback is now common in neuropsychological practice, although its format, duration, and supporting materials vary across settings and patient populations (Smith et al., 2007; McRae et al., 2024). Efforts to improve consistency have included development of the PsyCET-F, a competency framework for feedback delivery intended for use in training, clinical practice, and research (Wong et al., 2024). In a recent expert consensus study, the highest-rated potential outcomes of neuropsychological assessment were improved patient or caregiver understanding of presenting problems, improved understanding of how to manage and cope with cognitive symptoms, and diagnostic clarification (Wong et al., 2026).

From a NIPE perspective, neuropsychological feedback provides a useful opportunity to connect assessment findings with cognitive processes and everyday functioning. These explanations may help patients develop a more coherent and actionable understanding of their cognitive strengths and difficulties, which can in turn inform goal setting, strategy selection, and participation in rehabilitation.

4.1.1 Acute phase (days to weeks)

During acute inpatient and early post-acute care, factors such as posttraumatic amnesia, confusion, fatigue, pain, sleep disruption, and medication effects can limit new learning. Psychoeducation may therefore be easier to process when information is provided in brief, manageable segments, revisited over time, and, where appropriate, also provided to family or other support persons (Hart et al., 2018). 4.1.2 Subacute phase (weeks to months)

In the subacute phase, NIPE can be integrated more directly with cognitive rehabilitation, coping and adjustment work, and goal-directed participation. Information can be linked to emerging cognitive difficulties, rehabilitation strategies, and everyday activities as patients and families develop a clearer understanding of ongoing needs. In stroke rehabilitation, caregiver-and dyad-focused interventions commonly combine psychoeducation with skill-building approaches such as problem solving, stress management, and goal setting (Bakas et al., 2022).

4.1.3 Chronic phase (months to years)

In the chronic phase, psychoeducation can remain relevant as new participation demands emerge, including return to work or study, driving, parenting, and changes in relationships or other roles. Patients and families may also need to revisit earlier information as they adapt to persistent difficulties or encounter new challenges. The TBI literature supports viewing education as an ongoing process that may need to address evolving concerns over time (Hart et al., 2018). Similarly, cognition-related psychoeducation needs after stroke are evident throughout the post-stroke continuum and may change over time as patients and families encounter different concerns and demands (Hobden et al., 2025).

4.2 Context

The context in which NIPE is delivered after acquired brain injury (ABI) may influence whether information is understood, remembered, and applied in everyday life. Relevant considerations include the format of delivery, such as in person, via telehealth, self-guided, standalone, or embedded within a broader intervention, as well as who participates in the psychoeducation process.

4.2.1 Format

NIPE may be delivered in person or via telehealth, either as a discrete intervention or as part of ongoing cognitive rehabilitation. Neuropsychological feedback, for example, is commonly delivered in a single 30-to 60-minute session (McRae et al., 2024), whereas NIPE can also be incorporated across rehabilitation sessions as questions, goals, and needs emerge. This allows neuroscience-informed explanations to be revisited and linked to rehabilitation strategies and everyday experiences over time. Self-guided digital formats are another option. The choice of format should reflect the individual's cognitive abilities, preferences, access to technology, and need for clinician or family involvement.

4.2.2 Participants

Family members or other support persons can be included in NIPE because they may have their own psychoeducation needs and can assist with understanding and implementation when cognitive difficulties affect learning, memory, or follow-through (Hart et al., 2018; Gruters et al., 2022). They may also help the person remember information, structure routines, and apply strategies in everyday settings. Greater family involvement during inpatient TBI rehabilitation has been associated with better community participation and some cognitive functional outcomes after discharge, while caregiver-and dyad-focused interventions following stroke have shown benefits for some caregiver and stroke-survivor outcomes (Bogner et al., 2019; Bakas et al., 2022).

Family involvement may be particularly useful when insight, memory, or executive functioning is limited, although the appropriate level of involvement should be tailored to the individual and clinical context. Other rehabilitation professionals can also be involved when this promotes consistency in explanations, goals, and recommendations across disciplines (Larsson-Lund et al., 2022).

4.3 The therapeutic lens

Although much of the evidence regarding therapeutic and relational factors comes from psychotherapy rather than NIPE specifically, therapeutic outcomes are influenced not only by specific interventions but also by contextual factors such as the therapeutic alliance, collaboration, shared goals, and expectations about change (Norcross and Lambert, 2018; Wampold, 2015). These factors are particularly relevant to NIPE because explanations about brain injury, cognitive difficulties, prognosis, and rehabilitation can be personally and emotionally significant. Clinicians therefore need to balance realistic discussion of difficulties and uncertainty with warmth, support, and appropriate hope (Postal and Armstrong, 2013; Waldron-Perrine et al., 2021). These therapeutic factors are reflected in several NIPE structural principles, including trustworthiness, interaction, salience, and motivation. Goal setting and self-awareness are also considered because they can help connect NIPE with rehabilitation goals and action.

4.3.1 Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness in NIPE depends on more than factual accuracy. It may be strengthened by clarity, transparency, consistency, and relevance to the patient's experience (Armstrong et al., 2024; Hart et al., 2018; Gruters et al., 2022). Clinicians can support trust by linking explanations to everyday difficulties, providing a coherent formulation that acknowledges cognitive, emotional, and contextual influences, and making recommendations that are feasible in daily life.

Trust also requires appropriate acknowledgement of uncertainty. Recovery trajectories following ABI are variable, and clinicians should distinguish what is known from what remains uncertain rather than convey unwarranted confidence (Hart et al., 2018). Consistency across the rehabilitation team may further support credibility when patients and families receive compatible explanations, goals, and recommendations.

4.3.2 Interaction

NIPE can be delivered collaboratively rather than as a one-way transfer of information. Interactive approaches allow patients to ask questions, clarify misunderstandings, relate explanations to their own experiences, and develop a shared understanding of their difficulties and rehabilitation needs (Gorske and Smith, 2008).

Gorske and Smith's Collaborative Therapeutic Neuropsychological Assessment (CTNA) model illustrates this approach. The clinician works from the patient's questions and goals, shares formulations in accessible language, invites the patient's response, and uses lived experience to refine a shared understanding of cognitive and functional difficulties. The elicitprovide-elicit technique similarly involves first eliciting what the person already understands, providing additional information with permission, and then eliciting the person's response to that information (Gorske and Smith, 2008).

A shared explanatory model can connect information with action. When the clinician and patient develop a mutually plausible understanding of the problem and rationale for intervention, recommendations may be more readily incorporated into the patient's own goals and plans (Wampold and Imel, 2015; Armstrong et al., 2024; Gruters et al., 2022). Additional support may be needed when impaired self-awareness limits recognition of important difficulties.

4.3.3 Salience

In NIPE, salience can be supported by making information meaningful, personally relevant, and connected to everyday functioning (Postal and Armstrong, 2013). This can include linking explanations to the patient's concerns and goals, using examples from daily life, and drawing on observations or experiences that illustrate how cognitive difficulties affect functioning (Armstrong et al., 2024; Gruters et al., 2022).

The clinician should also consider the patient's readiness and emotional response to the information being discussed. Topics such as identity change, prognosis, return to work or driving, and relationship difficulties may be especially salient because they are emotionally significant. Information therefore needs to be relevant enough to engage attention and reflection while being paced to avoid overwhelming the patient (Hart et al., 2018; Gruters et al., 2022). Visual supports, metaphors, and other concrete forms of explanation may further support understanding of personally meaningful information (Postal and Armstrong, 2013).

4.3.4 Motivation

Motivation and engagement are widely regarded as important in cognitive rehabilitation (Nowell et al., 2020). In ABI, impaired self-awareness, low confidence, and ambivalence about change may complicate engagement (Medley and Powell, 2010). NIPE may support motivation through autonomy-supportive communication, collaborative agenda setting, and neuroscience-informed explanations that provide a credible and personally relevant rationale for rehabilitation.

Motivational interviewing (MI) provides one useful framework because it emphasises collaboration, evocation, and respect for autonomy (Medley and Powell, 2010). MI-informed approaches have been developed to support engagement after TBI (Hsieh et al., 2012) and have been feasibly incorporated into goal setting in broader rehabilitation settings (Wintle et al., 2026). Similarly, NIPE may support motivation when explanations are linked to concerns and priorities that matter to the individual and to actions they can realistically undertake.

4.3.4.1 Goal setting

Goal setting can help translate psychoeducation into action by connecting information about cognitive difficulties and rehabilitation to personally meaningful outcomes. Commonly identified principles of goal setting in ABI rehabilitation include collaboration, client-centredness, realistic and measurable goals, and explicit links between goals and therapy activities (Prescott et al., 2015).

In NIPE, goal setting can clarify why particular strategies are relevant, where they should be used, and how progress will be evaluated. The INCOG 2.0 guidelines similarly incorporate goal identification and psychoeducation within metacognitive strategy instruction, reflecting the importance of linking strategies to the situations in which they will be needed and anticipating barriers to their implementation (Jeffay et al., 2023).

4.3.4.2 Self-awareness

Self-awareness refers to an individual's awareness of their own thoughts, actions, and cognitive functioning (Stuss, 1991). Impaired awareness of deficits is common following ABI and may reduce recognition of the need for rehabilitation or behaviour change (Dromer et al., 2021; Orfei et al., 2007).

Psychoeducation and feedback may help patients develop greater selfawareness by connecting cognitive difficulties with their effects on everyday functioning. Feedback-based interventions have produced modest improvements in self-awareness after brain injury (Schmidt et al., 2011), and a recent meta-analysis found a moderate overall effect for interventions targeting self-awareness, although effects varied considerably across studies (Villalobos et al., 2025). For NIPE, the aim is not simply greater awareness of impairment, but an understanding that can guide realistic goals, strategy use, and participation in rehabilitation.

4.4 The cognition-informed lens

Cognitive difficulties following ABI can affect how new information is acquired, understood, retained, and applied. NIPE should therefore be adapted to the individual's cognitive profile whenever possible. Neuropsychological assessment can inform this tailoring, but several general principles are relevant even when formal assessment data are unavailable. These include calibrating the amount and complexity of information, repeating important material, using clear and concrete language, linking new information to existing knowledge, checking understanding, and providing information through multiple modalities.

4.4.1 Intensity

Difficulties with attention, memory, processing speed, and fatigue can make it challenging for people with ABI to take in large amounts of information at one time. NIPE should therefore be delivered at a pace and level of complexity that reduces cognitive load while allowing sufficient opportunity for questions and discussion.

Practitioner surveys suggest that neuropsychological feedback sessions commonly last approximately 30-60 min (Varela et al., 2024; McRae et al., 2024), but session length alone does not determine appropriate intensity. The amount, pace, and complexity of information should be tailored to the person's cognitive abilities, fatigue, emotional state, and learning needs. Some individuals may benefit from shorter sessions or information divided across multiple contacts, whereas others may be able to engage with more detailed explanations.

4.4.2 Repetition

Repeated exposure to important information can support learning and memory when cognitive impairment makes acquisition of new information more difficult. In NIPE, this may occur within a session by revisiting key concepts, or across sessions by returning to important information over time.

People with moderate to severe TBI may require additional learning trials to acquire new information (DeLuca et al., 2000). The amount and frequency of repetition should therefore be tailored to the individual's cognitive profile and preferences, as excessive repetition may feel unnecessary or condescending. Checking the person's understanding and comfort with the pace and amount of information can help determine how much repetition is useful.

Follow-up or booster NIPE sessions may provide opportunities to revisit important information when material from an initial session is not well retained. Providing a written reminder following neuropsychological feedback improved recall of recommendations among caregivers, although not among patients, and did not improve adherence to recommendations (Meth et al., 2016). Multi-session rehabilitation interventions can also provide repeated opportunities for education, strategy practice, and feedback (Bakas et al., 2022; Fleming et al., 2024). Because psychoeducation needs may change over time, NIPE may be best viewed as an ongoing process in which key information is revisited as recovery, goals, and circumstances evolve (Hart et al., 2018).

4.4.3 Clear and concrete language

Communication should take account of both pre-existing language abilities and cognitive-communication difficulties following ABI. Neuroscience terminology can be unfamiliar even across professional disciplines and may be particularly difficult for patients when combined with impairments in attention, memory, processing speed, or language. NIPE should therefore use plain language whenever possible and introduce technical terminology only when it adds useful understanding.

The level of abstraction should also be tailored to the individual. Concrete explanations and examples are generally useful when abstraction or executive functioning is impaired. Metaphors can sometimes make complex ideas easier to understand and remember, but figurative language also places demands on pragmatic language and executive processes and may be misunderstood by some people with ABI (Thoma and Daum, 2006; Yang et al., 2010; Zimmermann et al., 2011; Fujimoto et al., 2019). Metaphors should therefore be used selectively and checked for understanding.

Whenever possible, explanations should be linked to concrete examples from the person's daily life. Demonstrating how a strategy is used can also complement verbal description (Postal and Armstrong, 2013).

4.4.4 Integration with existing knowledge

Drawing on broader learning theory, new information may be easier to understand when it can be connected to what the person already knows. Meaningful learning approaches therefore emphasise linking new concepts to existing knowledge, beliefs, and experiences rather than presenting information in isolation (Bryce and Blown, 2024).

In NIPE, the clinician can begin by identifying the patient's current understanding of the injury, cognitive difficulties, and rehabilitation, and then build from that starting point. Linking explanations to familiar experiences and everyday examples can make new information easier to integrate while reducing large conceptual leaps that may interfere with comprehension. This process also supports development of a shared explanatory model that can be revised as the patient's understanding develops (Gruters et al., 2022; Armstrong et al., 2024).

4.4.5 Teach-back

Teach-back is a structured method for checking whether important information has been understood. The clinician asks the patient or caregiver to explain key information or instructions in their own words, clarifies misunderstandings, and checks understanding again as needed (Talevski et al., 2020; Shersher et al., 2021). It is typically framed as a check on the clinician's explanation rather than a test of the patient.

In practice, teach-back involves presenting a small number of essential points in clear language, asking the person to explain what they understood or how they will apply the information, and then clarifying or rephrasing as necessary. Across diverse healthcare settings, teach-back has been associated with improvements in knowledge, skills, self-care behaviours, and some health outcomes (Talevski et al., 2020; Shersher et al., 2021; Yen and Leasure, 2019). Although this evidence is not specific to ABI or NIPE, teach-back provides a practical method for identifying misunderstandings before they interfere with implementation.

4.4.6 Multimodal and multimedia delivery

NIPE may be delivered using multiple modalities and media to support accessibility, retention, and opportunities for review. Multimodal approaches present information in more than one form, such as combining verbal explanations with written or visual material. In a randomised study of neuropsychological feedback, supplemental written information in addition to oral feedback resulted in better recall of recommendations at one-month follow-up than oral feedback alone (Fallows and Hilsabeck, 2013).

Multimedia approaches, including video, web modules, apps, and telehealth platforms, provide ways to present information that can be revisited, shared with family, and standardised across services. For example, a protocol for the MyTBI online psychoeducation platform describes approximately six brief modules (range 4-8) addressing psychosocial challenges across acute, post-acute, and chronic phases of TBI recovery (Troeung et al., 2024).

Video-based approaches have also been evaluated in stroke education. MyStroke was associated with greater satisfaction with stroke education and better identification of the cause of the individual's stroke, while Movies4Stroke used brief educational films delivered through a mobile app to stroke survivor-caregiver dyads (Favilla et al., 2024; Kamal et al., 2020). Video teach-back has also been associated with improvements in caregiver outcomes and some patient outcomes following stroke (Wang et al., 2024). Although these studies do not directly evaluate NIPE, they illustrate ways in which multimedia can be used to supplement interactive patient and caregiver education.

Patient narratives and documentaries about living with ABI may also supplement NIPE by illustrating cognitive, emotional, and functional changes in everyday life. Carefully selected examples may provide material for discussing adjustment, identity, and recovery, but should complement rather than replace evidence-based information about brain injury, rehabilitation, and the variability of recovery trajectories.

4.5 Applying the framework in practice

Taken together, these elements can be translated into a brief clinical framework for delivering NIPE during cognitive rehabilitation: Clinical implementation of NIPE in cognitive rehabilitation 1. Assess understanding and capacity. Assess the patient's current understanding, concerns, goals, and cognitive capacity. 2. Select salient neuroscience concepts. Identify a small number of concepts that are directly relevant to the patient's current difficulties or rehabilitation goals. 3. Connect concepts to everyday functioning. Link the explanation to a concrete everyday experience or functional difficulty.

4. Connect the explanation to a rehabilitation strategy. Explain why the strategy is being recommended and when it may be useful. 5. Adapt delivery. Present information at an appropriate pace and use written, visual, or other supports as needed. 6. Check understanding. Use teach-back or another collaborative method to identify and clarify misunderstandings. 7. Revisit and refine. Return to the explanation and strategy over time as understanding, recovery, and rehabilitation goals evolve.

5. NIPE for CR content

Neuroscience-informed explanations of the mechanisms of injury and associated neurocognitive and neurobehavioural consequences of ABI are addressed elsewhere in this volume. This section therefore focuses on NIPE related to neuroplasticity, prognosis and recovery, and rehabilitation interventions. The aim is not to provide a comprehensive review of the neuroscience of recovery, but to identify concepts that can help patients and families understand what recovery may involve, why rehabilitation strategies are recommended, and how improvement can occur through restoration, compensation, and adaptation.

5.1 Neuroplasticity

Neuroplasticity is an important concept for understanding recovery and rehabilitation following ABI. Broadly, it refers to the capacity of the nervous system to change in response to experience, learning, injury, and other influences (Kleim and Jones, 2008; Nudo, 2011). Plasticity can occur through changes at synaptic, cellular, and network levels and provides a biological basis for learning and adaptation.

Within NIPE, however, neuroplasticity should not be presented as synonymous with recovery or as evidence that any particular intervention will restore impaired neural systems. Neural change is difficult to measure directly in routine clinical settings, and improvements in everyday functioning can occur through multiple pathways, including biological recovery, relearning, compensatory strategy use, environmental modification, and adjustment. Neuroplasticity is therefore most useful as an explanatory framework for the brain's capacity for change rather than as a promise of neural restoration.

The type and extent of change following ABI depend on many factors, including the nature and severity of injury, time since injury, the cognitive function involved, prior abilities, health and psychosocial factors, and the rehabilitation experiences available to the individual. NIPE should therefore present neuroplasticity alongside the substantial variability in recovery across people and cognitive domains.

5.1.1 Experience-expectant plasticity

Experience-expectant plasticity is primarily a developmental concept describing neural processes that anticipate species-typical environmental input during sensitive periods of development (Greenough et al., 1987; Nelson, 2024). Following brain injury, periods of heightened biological responsiveness have sometimes been compared with these developmental processes. Evidence from stroke motor rehabilitation, for example, suggests that responsiveness to rehabilitation can vary according to the timing of intervention (Dromerick et al., 2021).

These findings provide a rationale for considering timing in rehabilitation, but they should be applied cautiously to cognitive rehabilitation. Evidence for discrete critical or sensitive periods specifically in cognitive recovery following ABI remains limited. NIPE should therefore avoid implying that a narrow opportunity for cognitive recovery will be permanently lost if rehabilitation does not occur within a particular timeframe.

5.1.2 Experience-dependent plasticity

Experience-dependent plasticity refers to neural change associated with an individual's experiences and learning across the lifespan. This concept is directly relevant to rehabilitation because cognitive rehabilitation involves structured opportunities for learning, practice, strategy use, and adaptation (Kleim and Jones, 2008; Bayley et al., 2023). Kleim and Jones (2008) described several principles of experiencedependent plasticity relevant to rehabilitation, including the familiar idea of "use it or lose it," as well as specificity, repetition, intensity, salience, timing, transference, and interference. These principles provide useful ways of explaining why targeted and meaningful practice matters in rehabilitation. However, they should not be interpreted as universal rules of cognitive recovery, because much of the supporting evidence derives from basic neuroscience and motor rehabilitation, and the extent to which individual principles generalise to cognitive rehabilitation varies.

For NIPE, the most useful message is therefore not simply that patients should "use" a cognitive function more intensively. Rather, learning is more likely to be useful when practice is targeted, meaningful, appropriately challenging, repeated sufficiently, and linked to the situations in which the skill or strategy will ultimately be needed. The amount and timing of practice should also be balanced against fatigue, cognitive overload, and other individual factors. Functional improvement may reflect both changes in underlying abilities and increasingly effective use of compensatory strategies and environmental supports.

5.2 Moderators of recovery and rehabilitation

The World Health Organization's International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) framework emphasises that functioning and disability reflect interactions among health conditions, personal factors, and environmental factors (World Health Organization, 2001). Following ABI, relevant factors may include cognitive and emotional functioning, psychosocial support, health behaviours, and the opportunities and demands present in the person's environment. These factors vary considerably across individuals and over time and may contribute to the heterogeneity of recovery trajectories.

NIPE can help patients and families understand how modifiable factors may affect cognitive functioning and participation in rehabilitation. Sleep disturbance can exacerbate cognitive difficulties and fatigue following ABI (Pilon et al., 2021), while physical activity may contribute to broader physical and cognitive health within stroke rehabilitation (Li et al., 2024). The purpose of NIPE in this context is not to imply that lifestyle factors determine recovery, but to help patients identify factors that may support functioning and rehabilitation within the context of their individual needs and circumstances.

Depression and anxiety are common following both TBI and stroke (Chun et al., 2022; Robinson et al., 2024; Dehbozorgi et al., 2024a Dehbozorgi et al., , 2024b)). These symptoms may interact with sleep, fatigue, motivation, and cognitive functioning and can complicate participation in rehabilitation. NIPE can help patients understand these interactions and why identifying and addressing psychological symptoms may support participation in cognitive rehabilitation.

More broadly, presenting recovery as the product of interacting neurological, psychological, behavioural, and environmental influences can support realistic expectations while identifying areas in which patients and families may be able to take constructive action.

5.3 Prognosis and recovery

A useful distinction in NIPE following ABI is between spontaneous biological recovery and improvement associated with rehabilitation, learning, compensation, and adaptation. Spontaneous biological recovery refers to improvement associated with endogenous post-injury processes occurring in the absence of a specific targeted rehabilitation intervention. Following stroke, these processes can include resolution of oedema and diaschisis, restoration of perfusion and metabolism, and endogenous neural reorganisation (Nudo, 2011; Bernhardt et al., 2017).

Recovery trajectories vary considerably according to the nature and severity of the injury, the outcome being measured, and individual and environmental factors. Symptoms following mild TBI often improve during the first weeks, although a substantial minority of adults experience persisting symptoms (McIntosh et al., 2025). Recovery following moderate to severe TBI can extend over considerably longer periods. Longitudinal TRACK-TBI studies show that functional status, cognition, psychiatric symptoms, and quality of life can continue to change years after injury, with both improvement and decline observed across individuals (Nelson et al., 2023; Brett et al., 2023).

Recovery following stroke is similarly heterogeneous. Functional change is often most rapid during the early months, but the rate and extent of recovery differ across individuals and domains, and meaningful improvement can continue during the chronic phase (Li et al., 2024; El Husseini et al., 2023). Cognitive, emotional, and participation outcomes may also follow different trajectories. These differences make it important to distinguish population-level patterns from predictions about an individual patient.

NIPE can help patients and families understand that meaningful functional improvement does not necessarily require complete restoration of an impaired cognitive process. A person may improve through some combination of biological recovery, relearning, compensatory strategies, environmental modification, psychological adjustment, and changes in participation. Conversely, relatively mild cognitive impairment can have substantial functional consequences when accompanied by factors such as depression, anxiety, fatigue, sleep disturbance, pain, or environmental demands. Prognosis therefore reflects interacting neurological, psychological, behavioural, and environmental influences rather than the severity of cognitive impairment alone.

When discussing prognosis, clinicians should generally use probabilistic rather than deterministic language. Patients and families can be informed about typical recovery patterns while also being told that the eventual degree and timing of recovery cannot be predicted precisely for an individual (Postal and Armstrong, 2013). This approach allows clinicians to provide realistic information without unnecessarily limiting hope or implying that continued rehabilitation and adaptation are futile after a particular point in time.

5.4 Rehabilitation interventions

Because TBI and stroke are common forms of acquired brain injury, many current cognitive rehabilitation guidelines focus on these conditions. Cognitive rehabilitation has been defined as a systematic, functionally oriented service based on assessment and understanding of the person's brain-behavioural deficits (Cicerone et al., 2000). Broadly, cognitive rehabilitation approaches may involve restoration of impaired cognitive processes, compensation for persistent impairments, environmental modification, and adaptation to disability. Current guidelines emphasise that interventions should be functionally relevant, meaningful to the individual, and designed to support generalisation to everyday activities (Bayley et al., 2023; Cicerone et al., 2019).

From a NIPE perspective, these distinctions are important because they provide patients with different explanatory models for how rehabilitation may improve functioning. Some interventions aim to strengthen or restore cognitive processes through repeated practice, whereas others reduce the functional impact of persistent impairment by teaching compensatory strategies or modifying the environment. Explaining these distinctions can help patients understand why particular interventions are recommended and develop realistic expectations about what improvement may involve.

5.4.1 Restorative approaches

Restorative approaches are designed to improve an impaired cognitive process through structured and repeated practice, often using graded levels of difficulty. Examples include interventions targeting attention, memory, processing speed, or specific cognitive skills. Evidence suggests that gains are generally strongest for trained or closely related tasks unless treatment explicitly promotes transfer to everyday activities (Cicerone et al., 2019; Ponsford et al., 2023). NIPE can support restorative interventions by explaining the cognitive processes being targeted, the rationale for repeated practice, and the limits of expected generalisation.

5.4.2 Compensatory approaches

Compensatory approaches aim to reduce the functional consequences of cognitive impairment rather than directly restore the impaired process. Internal strategies include techniques such as mnemonic methods, prospective memory strategies, and metacognitive strategies that help individuals regulate their own behavior. External strategies use tools or systems, such as notebooks, electronic reminders, or other assistive technologies, to reduce reliance on impaired cognitive processes (Cicerone et al., 2019; Velikonja et al., 2023). The choice of strategy depends on factors such as the nature and severity of impairment, capacity for new learning, insight, motivation, and the functional context in which the strategy will be used.

NIPE may facilitate compensatory strategy use by helping patients understand why a strategy is needed, what cognitive limitation it is intended to address, and how it can support functioning in everyday situations. This may be particularly important when compensatory approaches are initially experienced as unnecessary, burdensome, or inconsistent with the person's expectations for recovery.

5.4.3 Environmental approaches

Environmental approaches reduce cognitive demands by modifying the physical or social environment. Examples include reducing distractions, organising frequently used items, and placing visual cues where they are needed. Environmental modifications can reduce reliance on memory, attention, or self-initiated strategy use by embedding supports directly in everyday settings (Hart et al., 2019; Velikonja et al., 2023). NIPE can help patients and families understand how environmental changes reduce cognitive demands and how they can function as part of a broader rehabilitation approach.

5.4.4 Adjustment

Rehabilitation following ABI may also support adjustment to persistent cognitive, emotional, behavioural, and social consequences of injury. Adjustment can involve changes in identity, roles, relationships, expectations, and participation and is influenced by both personal and environmental factors (Livneh, 2001; Yates, 2003). Rehabilitation may therefore include helping individuals develop an accurate understanding of their strengths and limitations, identify workable roles and goals, and adapt to changes that cannot be fully reversed. NIPE may contribute to adjustment by helping patients integrate the possibility of continued recovery with the use of compensation and adaptation when difficulties persist.

5.4.6 Supplementing CR with other interventions

Patients may also ask whether interventions outside cognitive rehabilitation can support recovery. Depending on the clinical context, these may include pharmacological, neuromodulatory, physical, or psychological interventions. Evidence varies considerably across intervention types and clinical populations. Within NIPE, the primary role of discussing these approaches is to provide an accurate and appropriately qualified explanation of their potential role in recovery rather than to review them comprehensively.

6. Proposed mechanisms of NIPE

Although mechanisms underlying cognitive rehabilitation strategies have been proposed in the cognitive rehabilitation literature (Hart et al., 2019), the mechanisms through which NIPE may influence outcomes remain uncertain. Therapeutic factors and NIPE content and structural features may contribute to proximal outcomes such as understanding, selfawareness, and engagement, which may in turn support strategy use and behaviour change. These proposed pathways have not been directly established and require empirical evaluation.

7. Conclusion

This chapter has outlined how NIPE may function as an active component of cognitive rehabilitation rather than simply as supplementary information. Its potential clinical value lies in helping patients and families develop a coherent and usable understanding of brainbehaviour relationships, cognitive difficulties, recovery, and rehabilitation. When neuroscience-informed explanations are linked to everyday functioning and rehabilitation strategies, they may support understanding, self-awareness, realistic expectations, engagement, and application of strategies in contexts that matter, including home, community, work or study, and relationships.

The delivery of NIPE can be guided by two complementary lenses. The therapeutic lens emphasises warmth, alliance, collaboration, shared explanatory models, and personally meaningful goals. The cognitioninformed lens considers how difficulties in attention, memory, processing, language, and executive functioning can affect learning and informs adaptations such as calibrating the amount and complexity of information, repeating key material, using clear and concrete language, linking new information to existing knowledge, checking understanding, and providing multimodal support. Together, these approaches are intended to make neuroscience-informed information more understandable, relevant, and actionable.

Further research is needed to determine which components of NIPE contribute to outcomes, for whom, and under what conditions. Greater attention to intervention specification, fidelity, and outcome measurement will be important for evaluating whether NIPE improves proximal outcomes such as understanding, self-awareness, and engagement, as well as longer-term outcomes including strategy use, participation, quality of life, and functional cognition.
