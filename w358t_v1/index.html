Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Disordered eating (DE) can be particularly concerning during pregnancy, as it is associated with various obstetric complications. Findings on DE variations during pregnancy have yet to be systematically synthesized. This review seeks to summarize research on DE change across pregnancy.

Four databases were searched using PRISMA Scoping Review and Cochrane Rapid Reviews guidelines. Eligible studies were longitudinal and assessed DE, including eating disorder (ED) symptoms (e.g., dieting, purging) and related forms of DE (e.g., emotional eating), more than once during pregnancy.

The review included 19 articles. Though many forms of DE decreased across pregnancy, some forms of DE did not change, while some forms of DE had conflicting or unclear patterns. Body image constructs were the most examined forms of DE in pregnancy, followed by weight and dieting constructs. Research comparing DE patterns in people with versus without EDs was scarce.

Few studies have examined DE across pregnancy using ED samples, and several DE symptoms had conflicting or unclear patterns across pregnancy. Moreover, some DE outcomes (e.g., quality of life related to EDs, guilt related to overeating, susceptibility to hunger) and the comparison of DE patterns in samples with versus without EDs remain underexplored in pregnancy.

Changes in Disordered Eating Across Pregnancy: A Rapid Scoping Review

Disordered eating (DE) refers to a wide range of unhelpful and unhealthy cognitions and behaviours related to eating that may negatively affect one's emotional, social, and physical well-being (APA, 2013; National Eating Disorders Collaboration, 2015; Bannatyne et al., 2018). DE is not limited to individuals who meet Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) criteria for eating disorders (EDs) and may occur across the population at different rates and intensities (Reba-Harrelson et al., 2009; Wells et al., 2020). Indeed, a study found that 31% of adult women (age range 25-45) without a history of anorexia nervosa (AN) or binge eating reported purging (i.e., behaviours used to prevent weight gain or promote weight loss such as self-induced vomiting, laxative abuse, and diuretic abuse) to control their weight (Reba-Harrelson et al., 2009; Forney et al., 2016). Though DE is associated with negative consequences in the general population (Solmi et al., 2014), it can be particularly problematic during pregnancy.

Several studies have found that the presence of DE in the prenatal period is associated with an elevated risk of obstetric complications such as hyperemesis, anemia and/or infections, miscarriage, cesarean section, fetal distress, abnormal birth weight, preterm birth, or neonatal death (Koubaa et al., 2005; Franko et al., 2001; Micali et al., 2012; Charbonneau & Seabrook, 2019; Linna et al., 2014; Kimmel et al., 2016; Watson et al., 2017). In addition, a recent systematic review reported strong associations between DE during pregnancy and symptoms of anxiety and depression during pregnancy (Baskin & Galligan, 2019). Moreover, studies have shown that DE during specific trimesters of pregnancy may be differentially associated with negative obstetric outcomes (Bye et al., 2021; Chan et al., 2019). For instance, infants whose mothers had higher DE in the second trimester were more likely to have a baby with low birth weight, while infants whose mothers had higher DE during both the second and third trimesters were more likely to be large for gestational age (Chan et al., 2019). Understanding how certain forms of DE may change across pregnancy is crucial, as it may improve screening efforts and allow for earlier and more targeted interventions for DE in this time-limited period.

Past research on changes in DE during pregnancy shows mixed findings in both ED and non-ED samples. In women with current or past EDs, some studies report decreases in symptoms across pregnancy (Easter et al., 2015), while others found symptom worsening or relapse (Makino et al., 2020; Sommerfeldt et al., 2022). Similarly, studies in women without EDs have found several patterns including decreases (Chan et al., 2019), no significant changes (Wu et al., 2024), or new onset of symptoms such as binge eating (i.e., eating large amounts of food while experiencing loss of control over eating) during pregnancy (Bulik et al., 2007; Watson et al., 2014; Giel et al., 2022). Differing results may be attributed to various factors. Trajectories may not only vary across different types of DE but may also depend on individual characteristics (e.g., ED diagnosis status). Patterns of change in DE may also vary among individuals of the same group, as indicated by a recent study that found that ED trajectories across pregnancy and early motherhood vary greatly among individuals with a history of ED (Sommerfeldt et al., 2024).

Despite associations between DE during pregnancy and adverse health outcomes, EDs and DE are often overlooked by perinatal clinicians. Studies report that there is limited knowledge and awareness about EDs and DE during pregnancy among professionals specialized in perinatal care (Supina et al., 2016; Bye et al., 2018). Given that different patterns of DE during pregnancy have been observed, synthesizing such findings could be helpful to obtain a more complete overview of these changes, which could potentially help clinicians better recognize signs of DE during pregnancy. To our knowledge, no review has systematically examined the course of DE across pregnancy in individuals with and without EDs. Bye and colleagues (2021) addressed changes in ED symptoms across pregnancy in their narrative review, which aimed to provide an overview of the impact of maternal ED on mothers' and infants' health during pregnancy and the postnatal period. They found that while ED symptoms tend to decrease throughout pregnancy, they usually resurface during the postnatal period (Bye et al., 2021). However, methods regarding search strategy, study selection, and quality assessment were not specified (Bye et al., 2021).

We conducted a rapid scoping review to map the existing literature and provide an overview of knowledge on variation in DE across different stages of pregnancy. This rapid scoping review aimed to identify: (1) changes in different forms of DE that have been studied across pregnancy, and (2) differences in changes in DE across pregnancy in people with and without EDs. We hypothesized articles would mostly find lower levels of DE from early to mid-pregnancy but moderately higher levels toward late pregnancy, and that this general pattern in DE would be found in both samples with and without EDs.

Methods

Protocol and Registration

To ensure rigour and replicability of this rapid scoping review, we followed guidelines set by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI), the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR), as well as the Cochrane Rapid Reviews Methods (Peters et al., 2020; Tricco et al., 2018; Garritty et al., 2021). The review was registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) on August 1 st , 2024 (registration: https://osf.io/m6qtc).

Search Strategy

Four databases (i.e., PsycInfo, MEDLINE, Web of Science, and CINAHL) were searched. Google Scholar was also searched to ensure no relevant articles were missed. The search strategy aimed to locate peer-reviewed articles on DE across pregnancy and was established with the guidance of two academic research librarians (EK, DD). Search terms included two main concepts: 1) pregnancy, and 2) DE. Terms were adapted for each database. The first search occurred on July 23 rd , 2024, and an updated search occurred on May 13 th , 2026.

Eligibility Criteria

Eligibility criteria (see Table A1) were established after preliminary searches to ensure appropriate inclusion of studies aligning with the aims of the review. Studies were included if they (1) recruited pregnant participants, (2) measured DE at least twice during pregnancy, (3) adopted a longitudinal design (4) were published peer-reviewed articles, and (5) were written in English, in French or in another language with available translation. No restrictions were imposed regarding date range or settings.

Source of Evidence Selection

All identified records were collected and imported into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, 2024), where duplicates were automatically removed. The screening process followed the guidelines from PRISMA-ScR and Cochrane Rapid Reviews Methods (Tricco et al., 2018; Garritty et al., 2021). Two reviewers (EP and SE for the initial search, EP and NTM for the update) completed screening of titles and abstracts, which involved independent reviews and consensus discussions conducted in blocks of 10% of articles until inter-rater reliability reached an acceptable level (i.e., Cohen's κ = 0.50; Cohen, 1988).

Screening of full texts was done by a single reviewer (EP), with a second reviewer (SE for the initial search and NTM for the update) screening all excluded full texts. Full-text screening was completed using Covidence and a customized screening table in Microsoft Excel. Authors of articles that met inclusion criteria at full-text screening were contacted via email for missing data. Eight of the twelve authors contacted responded to our requests. Disagreements between reviewers at each screening stage were resolved through consensus discussion or with an additional reviewer (LC).

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

Data extraction procedures followed guidelines established by Cochrane Rapid Reviews (Garritty et al., 2021). Extracted data included: author(s), year of publication, journal name, study design, research objectives, sample size, characteristics of recruited participants, assessment tools used to evaluate DE, timepoints in pregnancy when DE was measured, methods used to report changes in DE, and key findings relevant to the review question. One reviewer (EP) independently extracted data from the articles, and a second reviewer (SE for the initial search and NTM for the update) independently checked for correctness, with meetings to resolve discrepancies. Additionally, all articles included at the extraction stage had their reference lists scanned to ensure that all potentially relevant citations were considered for inclusion (Peters et al., 2022).

Methodological quality of studies included at the extraction stage was assessed using the National Institute of Health (NIH)'s Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies (NIH, 2021). The NIH's tool, which originally contains 14 items, was modified to fit the criteria of this review. Notably item 9, which assesses exposure measures (independent variables), item 10, which examines if exposures were assessed more than once, and item 12, which assesses whether outcome assessors were blinded to the exposure status of participants, were removed from the score count given that there was no independent variable of interest in our criteria other than time. One reviewer (EP) assessed the quality of all included studies, and a second reviewer (SE for the initial search and NTM for the update) completed a full verification of all judgments, as suggested by the Cochrane Rapid Reviews guidelines (Garritty et al., 2021). Given that the NIH does not provide specific cut-off values to determine the quality of studies, ratings were completed based on recommendations from previous reviews that have used the NIH's tool (Sommer et al., 2018; Shu et al., 2021). Thus, quality of studies was rated as poor if less than 50% of criteria were met, fair if between 50-75% of criteria were met, and good if more than 75% of criteria were met.

Overview

A total of 7144 records were identified. The PRISMA-ScR diagram (Figure A1) summarizes the flow of studies meeting criteria for final inclusion. The sample size at baseline ranged from 20 to 1,470, while the average age of participants at baseline ranged from 25 (SD = 5.4; Carandang et al., 2024) Eleven studies only included individuals without EDs and did not screen for current nor past EDs, though one of these studies did sort their participants according to their risk level of developing an ED based on whether their scores on the Maternal Body Image and Eating Behaviours Scale (MBIEB-S) were below or above clinical threshold (Blouin et al., 2025). Most studies used questionnaires (k = 14), whereas others used interviews (k = 3), both methods (k = 1), or a single Likertstyle item (k = 1). The EDE, including its questionnaire version, the EDE-Q, was the most common tool (k = 7). Across studies, five measured general DE scores (e.g., EDE-Q Global Score), nine examined body image (e.g., shape concerns, body dissatisfaction, negative attitudes about changes in appearance, feeling fat, preoccupation with size), seven assessed weight-related constructs (e.g., weight concerns, negative attitudes about change in weight during pregnancy, weight control via supplement use), seven assessed dieting-related constructs (e.g., dietary restraint, dietary restriction, dieting), and a smaller number examined binge eating (k = 4), purging (k = 4), eating concerns (including risk of orthorexia nervosa; k = 4), and emotional eating (k = 2). Constructs such as quality of life related to EDs, overeating, hedonic hunger, loss of control over eating, susceptibility to hunger, disinhibition, and feelings of guilt related to overeating were each only assessed in one study.

Five studies used only descriptive methods (e.g., narrative summaries, graphs, and/or tables) to report changes in some or all forms of DE assessed across pregnancy (Easter et al., 2015; Emery et al., 2021; Lacey & Smith, 1987; Morgan et al., 1999; Nansel et al., 2020). Forms of DE that were examined by such studies include purging, loss of control over eating, and hedonic hunger. Findings from these studies will thus be reported separately in the results section.

Patterns of Change in DE Symptoms During Pregnancy -Statistical Methods

Statistically significant decreases in general DE scores (i.e., combined DE scores, such as global EDE scores) were found across pregnancy in three samples without EDs (Baskin et al., 2021; Chan et al. 2019; Miller et al., 2025). DE was found to increase in Fairburn et al. (1992)'s sample, of which 3-4% met criteria for an ED diagnosis during pregnancy.

Body image-related constructs mostly decreased during pregnancy. Studies using samples without EDs found decreases in : shape concerns (Baskin et al., 2021), body dissatisfaction in participants at both high and low risk of ED (Blouin et al., 2025); feeling fat and salience of weight and shape (Duncombe et al., 2008; Clark et al., 2009); body image dissatisfaction, but not shape concerns (Linde et al., 2024); preoccupation with body size (Miller et al., 2025); and negative attitudes towards changes in appearance, but an increase in body dissatisfaction (Yamamiya & Omori, 2023). Fairburn et al. (1992) reported a decrease in shape concerns between the 15 th and 32 nd weeks of gestation in their sample of which 3-4% met criteria for a clinical ED diagnosis.

Results for weight-related constructs were conflicting across studies. In samples without EDs, Baskin et al. (2021) and Linde et al. (2004) found no significant changes in weight concerns throughout pregnancy; Clark et al. (2009) and Duncombe et al. (2008) found that salience of weight and shape decreased across pregnancy; and Yamamiya & Omori (2023) reported an increase in negative attitudes towards changes in weight, as well as an increase in weight control via supplement use. Fairburn et al. (1992) also reported increases in weight concerns between the 15 th and 32 nd weeks of gestation.

Dieting-related constructs decreased across pregnancy in four samples without EDs (Baskin et al., 2021; Blouin et al., 2025; Plante et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2023). Blouin et al. (2025) found that restriction only decreased in the group of participants at high risk of having an ED, but remained unchanged in the group at low risk of having an ED. Only one sample without EDs reported an increase in dietary restraint and dieting across pregnancy (Yamamiya & Omori, 2023). Fairburn et al. (1992) also reported an increase in dietary restraint across pregnancy in their sample where a small minority had an ED.

Emotional eating decreased across pregnancy in two samples without EDs (Carandang et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2023). Feelings of guilt related to overeating also decreased across pregnancy in a sample without EDs (Yu et al., 2023). Overeating was examined in Blouin et al. (2025) and was found to decrease during pregnancy in the group of individuals at high risk of an ED, but remained unchanged in the group of individuals at low risk of an ED. In contrast, binge eating did not significantly change throughout pregnancy in a sample without EDs (Yu et al., 2023). Susceptibility to hunger and disinhibition did not significantly change throughout pregnancy in one sample without EDs (Plante et al., 2020). Quality of life related to EDs improved in the ED group in the second and third trimesters compared to pre-pregnancy, whereas quality of life scores in the non-ED group did not significantly differ change in pregnancy compared to pre-pregnancy (Coker et al., 2013). Patterns of change in eating concerns were conflicting. Among samples without EDs, Baskin et al. (2021) identified no significant changes in eating concerns, whereas Öztürk & Şenol (2024) found decreases in risk of orthorexia nervosa across pregnancy.

Patterns of Change in DE Symptoms During Pregnancy -Descriptive Methods

The following results are taken from the five studies that used descriptive methods to report changes in some or all forms of DE measured across pregnancy. They should be interpreted with caution.

In Easter et al. (2015)'s study, which sought to compare patterns of change between people with current EDs (during pregnancy), with past EDs (occurring before pregnancy), and in healthy controls (HC), a decrease in mean EDE-Q scores was observed in the current ED (C-ED) and past ED (P-ED) groups, and an increase in general DE observed in the healthy control (HC) group. However, it is unclear whether these changes are significant given that statistical analyses were only performed between the first/second trimester and the 8 th week postpartum. Statistical testing and patterns of change for dietary restraint, shape concerns, weight concerns, and eating concerns were also not performed or discussed, though mean scores for each of these symptoms by lifetime ED diagnosis and by trimester of pregnancy (first/second compared to the third) are reported in the supplementary materials provided by the authors (Easter et al., 2015). Frequencies of objective and subjective binge eating were higher in the C-ED group than in the P-ED and HC groups, with the highest frequency being in the first/second trimester for the C-ED group (Easter et al., 2015). Three forms of purging (i.e., self-induced vomiting, laxative misuse, and diuretic use) were examined in Easter et al. (2015). Self-induced vomiting was endorsed by six women in the C-ED group during the first/second and third trimesters and was endorsed more frequently by individuals with anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa during pregnancy. No participants reported laxative misuse or diuretic use during pregnancy. Fairburn et al. (1992) reported a table showing that the average eating concern score was 0.08 (SD = 0.6) and 0.04 (SD = 0.3) at the 15 th and 32 nd week of gestation, respectively, without specifying whether this change was statistically significant. Authors also reported that the number of people who endorsed self-induced vomiting during pregnancy decreased from 3 to 1 between the 15 th and 32 nd week of gestation, and that no participants endorsed laxative misuse across any pregnancy timepoint (Fairburn et al., 1992). Lacey & Smith (1987) examined binge eating and purging behaviours (i.e., selfinduced vomiting, laxative abuse) in a sample of people with BN and descriptively reported decreases in both from the first to the last trimester of pregnancy. Similarly, Morgan et al. (1999) examined binge eating and purging behaviours (i.e., self-induced vomiting, laxative abuse, slimming pill misuse, alcohol intake) in people with BN and descriptively reported decreases in both across pregnancy. Emery et al. (2021) examined loss of control over eating in a sample without EDs (where 15% were found to have a lifetime history of a feeding or ED) but did not report any results on change across pregnancy. The supplementary data showed the number of individuals endorsing loss of control eating at six different timepoints in pregnancy without any statistical tests on changes in pregnancy (Emery et al., 2021).

Thirteen percent of the sample reported loss of control eating prior to and during pregnancy and that 25% of the sample reported loss of control eating only during pregnancy (Emery et al., 2021). Nansel et al. (2020) examined hedonic hunger in a sample without EDs but did not report any patterns of change across pregnancy in the original article. The supplementary data showed mean scores of hedonic hunger at each trimester of pregnancy without any statistical tests on whether scores changed during pregnancy (Nansel et al., 2020). Paired comparisons in the original article only revealed whether the general mean score in pregnancy was significantly different from the mean score postpartum. In the case of hedonic hunger, which was measured via the score on the Power of food scale (PFS), scores did not significantly change from pregnancy to postpartum (Nansel et al., 2020).

Quality Assessment

All 19 studies included in this review had their methodological quality assessed using the NIH Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies (National Institute of Health, 2021). The score was calculated out of 11 for the included studies. Seven of 19 (37%) were rated as fair, while twelve (63%) were rated as good.

Discussion

This rapid scoping review aimed to summarize findings on DE during pregnancy and to identify gaps in research regarding: (1) changes in different forms of DE that have been studied across pregnancy and (2) differences between individuals with and without EDs. Many forms of DE generally decreased across pregnancy, including general DE, dieting constructs, body image constructs, emotional eating, quality of life related to EDs, guilt related to overeating, and overeating. Weight-related constructs showed conflicting patterns across studies, while disinhibition and susceptibility to hunger appeared unchanged (though these constructs were examined in only one study). Changes in eating concerns, binge eating, purging behaviours, loss of control eating, and hedonic hunger were mostly or only examined descriptively; thus, their pattern of change across pregnancy remains unclear. Body imagerelated constructs were the most frequently examined, followed by weight-and dieting-related constructs. Only two studies compared ED and non-ED groups (Coker et al., 2013; Easter et al., 2015).

They found that people with EDs reported improved quality of life and decreased general DE, whereas those without EDs experienced no change in quality of life but increases in DE, across pregnancy. Studies including only ED samples and studies including only non-ED samples mostly reported decreases in DE across pregnancy. However, methodological differences were apparent: ED-only studies were generally older, descriptive, and retrospective, while studies of non-ED samples used more varied approaches.

The general pattern of decrease in DE during pregnancy may reflect shifts in priorities toward infant health and changing social expectations during pregnancy (Clark et al., 2009). A systematic review found that pregnant women often prioritize infant well-being over body image and tend to view bodily changes as natural and indicative of healthy pregnancy progression (Watson et al., 2015). Reduced influence of "thin-ideal" internalization during pregnancy may partly explain declines in body image concerns and DE, as these constructs are closely related (Fitzsimmons-Craft et al., 2012; Thompson & Stice, 2001; Shroff & Thompson, 2006).

Decreases in DE during pregnancy may also relate to fluctuations in ovarian hormones, particularly estradiol and progesterone. Estradiol suppresses food intake, peaking during preovulation, while progesterone increases food intake in the presence of estradiol by attenuating its inhibitory effects (Eckel, 2011; Buffenstein et al., 1995; Baker et al., 2012; Pelkman et al., 2001). As both hormones rise throughout pregnancy, their interaction, especially in late pregnancy, may reduce dietary restraint. Supporting this, body dissatisfaction and drive for thinness have been shown to be inversely associated with estradiol and positively associated with progesterone in community samples (Racine et al., 2012).

However, studies in non-ED samples found no significant changes in susceptibility to hunger or disinhibition across pregnancy (Jacob et al., 2023; Bond et al., 2001; Plante et al., 2020). Thus, the role of ovarian hormones in shaping specific DE symptoms during pregnancy remains unclear. Future studies should examine hormone associations longitudinally across pregnancy, as existing work has focused on the menstrual cycle rather than pregnancy.

Weight-related constructs (i.e., weight concerns, negative attitudes about change in weight during pregnancy, weight control behaviours) demonstrated conflicting patterns of change during pregnancy. While variability in patterns may be attributed to differences in tools used to measure these constructs, sociocultural factors may also be at play. For instance, generational shifts in body weight perceptions across time could explain why Fairburn et al. (1992) found an increase in weight-related concerns across pregnancy, as opposed to the decrease or unchanged pattern found in more recent studies (Lu et al., 2015; Burke et al., 2010; Johnson et al., 2008). In addition, the increases in weightrelated concerns across pregnancy found in Yamamiya & Omori (2023) may reflect the greater pervasiveness of the thinness ideal in Japanese society compared to Western society (Wardle et al., 2006). Thus, generational and cultural differences in body weight perception may influence the pattern of change in specific DE symptoms.

Eating concerns, binge eating, purging behaviours, loss of control over eating, and hedonic hunger had unclear patterns of change given that they were mostly or only examined via descriptive methods. Thus, future studies should aim to replicate findings using statistical testing to verify the significance of these changes.

Strengths, limitations and future directions

A major strength of this review was adherence to established methodological guidelines, including JBI, PRISMA-ScR, and Cochrane Rapid Review standards, as well as preregistration on the Open Science Framework (Peters et al., 2020; Tricco et al., 2018; Garritty et al., 2021). However, comparisons across studies was challenging for several reasons: (1) heterogenous analytic approaches, ranging from descriptive reporting to varied inferential methods; (2) lack of standardized effect sizes, which made it difficult to assess the magnitude of change in DE across pregnancy; (3) initial and final measures of DE being taken at different ranges of weeks gestation; ( 4) lack of reporting of patterns of changes between trimesters specifically (as most studies only reported the overall pattern across pregnancy, even if they had more than two measures); and (5) most studies did not report whether levels of DE endorsement were above or below clinical cut-offs.

Based on the limitations and gaps identified in this review, future studies should use samples consisting of both people with and without EDs to not only replicate findings and assess a broader spectrum of DE outcomes in pregnancy, but also to clarify the patterns of change of the forms of DE that were found to be conflicting or unclear. In addition, researchers should also report whether DE scores go above or below clinical cut-off through timepoints. Studies should also prioritize effect size reporting, standardization of assessment timepoints during pregnancy, and meta-analytic approaches to improve reliability, generalizability, and clinical relevance.

Clinical implications

Findings support that DE may affect pregnant women regardless of ED status and that it is highly heterogenous presentation-wise. Treatments should thus be tailored to the specific type of DE being endorsed, as well as the person's ED status or ED history. This review also shows that not all forms of DE follow the same pattern through time, as some may have higher levels of endorsement early during pregnancy, while some may sustain a stable level throughout trimesters. Thus, DE support should be continuous throughout pregnancy, with supplementary support being provided during periods where DE endorsement is higher.

Conclusion

This rapid scoping review aimed to summarize broad patterns of change in DE symptoms across pregnancy and differences between people with and without EDs. Most studies reported decreases in DE throughout pregnancy. Body image constructs were the most examined outcomes. Though some differences in general DE and quality of life related to EDs emerged between ED and non-ED groups, additional research is needed to determine the robustness and clinical significance of these findings.
