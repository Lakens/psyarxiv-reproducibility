What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

Abstract

Background: The causal effect of attending Early Childhood Education and Care (ECEC) for infants and toddlers on child behavior problems is contested. Yet, most previous research is correlational. Findings from previous research are mixed, but with some evidence of harmful effects on children's behavior. Some correlational studies also suggest that ECEC may be most harmful for children from socioeconomically advantaged families. Methods: We estimate the effect of age of entry into ECEC on externalizing behavior problems at ages 3, 5, and 8 years in a large sample (n=66,394) from The Norwegian Mother, Father, and Child Cohort study (MoBa). We use two quasi-experimental methods, Two-Way Fixed-Effects models and Instrumental Variable models, that exploit variations in how early families enrolled their children in ECEC due to a national scale-up of publicly funded centers.

Results: In this high-powered, rigorous approach to testing the causal impact of age of entry into ECEC, we find no evidence that entry around age 1 relative to later affects behavior problems either during preschool years (at ages 3 or 5) or after school entry (at age 8 years).

We also find no evidence that these effects vary by parental education or family income.

We find no evidence suggesting that early entry into ECEC has harmful consequences for children's behavior regulation, during the preschool years or after school entry. We argue that our null results may be due to ECEC pedagogy in Norway that emphasizes young children's well-being and playful learning experiences.

[div-01]

There are long-standing concerns about the harmful effects of early, extensive, and continuous non-parental care on children's behavior regulation, especially center-based Early Childhood Education and Care-ECEC (Belsky, 2001; Huston, Bobbitt, & Bentley, 2015).

These concerns appear reasonable in light of several correlational studies, most prominently based on U.S. data from the NICHD Study of Early Child Care and Youth Development (SECCYD). In these data, there were no associations between the total amount of care and behavior problems when children were 3 years old, but the associations appeared at age 4½ years (NICHD Early Child Care Research Network, 2003) and continued through age 15 (Vandell, Belsky, Burchinal, Steinberg, & Vandergrift, 2010; see also review by Huston, et al., 2015). Taken together, researchers have argued that starting ECEC early and spending more time in care across the early childhood years may disrupt behavioral development.

However, results from correlational studies like these may be biased by unobserved confounding variables and are therefore not credible for causal inference.

A review of quasi-experimental studies-with credible causal identification-found inconsistent support for the hypothesis that ECEC quantity (defined variously as age of entry, hours per week, and attending vs. not) has adverse effects on behavior problems during the preschool years (Dearing & Zachrisson, 2017). More recently, harmonized within-person change analyses of seven datasets from five countries found no evidence that more hours per week in ECEC caused behavior problems during the preschool years (Rey-Guerra et al., 2023). Yet, several studies do report harmful effects. In France, short-term negative effects of ECEC use in the first year of life were evident at age 2 in a quasi-experimental study (Berger, Panico, & Solaz, 2021). Moreover, although correlational longitudinal studies in Quebec, studying amount of time spent in care, did not find harmful effects (Orri et al., 2019; Pingault et al., 2015), a quasi-experimental evaluation of a policy reform in Canada (Quebec) found both short-and long-term harmful effects of a rollout of subsidized slots (Baker, Gruber, & Milligan, 2008; Baker, Gruber, & Milligan, 2019). In addition, reports from the randomized Tennessee Preschool program have reinvigorated concerns about harmful effects of attending ECEC for preschool aged children. Specifically, while the short-term effects on behavior problems from this RCT initially were small and insignificant, they grew larger and became statistically significant during the elementary schooling years, suggesting a sleeper effect in which a causal effect that is at first undetectable later becomes evident (Durkin, Lipsey, Farran, & Wiesen, 2022; Lipsey, Farran, & Durkin, 2018).

In this study, we test whether there are adverse effects of ECEC for infants and toddlers on behavior problems through early school age in Norway. Specifically, we tested for causal effects of age of entry (from age one) into ECEC on externalizing behavior problems during the preschool years (ages 3 and 5) and at age 8, two to three years after school entry (which in Norway happens in August, the year the child turns six). We also tested whether these effects differed as a function of parental education and income. Previous correlational research has shown socioeconomic gradients in associations between both age of entry into, and intensity of, ECEC on behavior problems. Huston et al.'s (2015) review suggests that harmful effects across child ages are concentrated among children from higher socioeconomic status (SES) households, while Orri et al. (2019) found that attending ECEC prevents later behavior problems among children from lower SES families, yet with no associations for others.

Norway is a particularly relevant context for testing adverse effects of age of entry into ECEC for multiple reasons. First, a policy reform in Norway during the 2000s led to a sharp increase in the availability of ECEC for one-and two-year-old children. The reform led to a vast majority (nearly 80%) of infants and toddlers entering ECEC in their second year of life (and remaining there on a full-day basis until school entry age). Therefore, many children are exposed to early, extensive, and continuous care in Norway. Yet, the Norwegian ECEC context is characterized by a holistic focus on child well-being rather than a strong focus on school readiness, which is common in many other countries. Theory suggests that an earlier age of entry into early education should be the most harmful because of early separation from attachment figures (Belsky, 2001). Whether this applies regardless of context is uncertain.

Second, Norway provides a unique opportunity for a quasi-experimental design to test the longer-term effects of ECEC. Specifically, the policy reform caused the national coverage rate of infant and toddler ECEC to rise from 38% to 77% through the 2000s. (For details on the ECEC expansion, see Online Appendix 1.) The policy expansion coincided by serendipity with the rollout of a multi-cohort longitudinal study, the Norwegian Mother, Father, and Child Cohort Study, which collected data on children and their families on multiple occasions, including behavior problems. This unique combination of a policy reform and survey data allowed us to test the effects of age of entry into ECEC on behavior problems using two analytical approaches: Two-Way Fixed-Effects regression models and Instrumental Variable models.

Finally, there is evidence that the scale-up of ECEC in Norway had positive effects on achievement outcomes up to age 15 for children from families where parents had low levels of education, but null-effects for others (Almås, Drange, Meghir, & Zachrisson, 2026; Zachrisson, Dearing, Borgen, Sandsør, & Karoly, 2024). Knowing whether a reform with positive effects for a subgroup also has negative effects is important. Other studies from Norway not exploiting the reform have found that while an earlier age of entry into ECEC (starting at about one year of age) caused slightly elevated levels of child aggression at age 2, these effects had faded by age 4 (Dearing et al., 2015). Also, hours per week in ECEC (from age 1 ½ to 3 years) have been found to be unrelated to behavior problems once unobserved confounding was accounted for in the research design (Zachrisson et al., 2013), which was replicated up to age 4 in a different sample (Rey-Guerra et al., 2023).

Methods

The Norwegian Mother, Father, and Child Cohort Study (MoBa; for complete details, see Brandlistuen et al., 2025, and www.fhi.no/morogbarn) is a population-based pregnancy cohort study conducted by the Norwegian Institute of Public Health, including participants from all over Norway from 1999 to mid 2009 (we used version 12 of the quality-assured data files released for research in January 2019). The full sample at baseline included 114,500 children, 95,200 mothers, and 75,200 fathers, with a baseline participation rate of 41%. All women giving birth in Norway were the target population, yet only hospitals and birth units with more than 100 births annually were included, and the roll-out of the study was gradual across the first years (Brandlistuen et al., 2025). We linked MoBa to national registries that include the population registry (demographic information), the income registry (family income), and the education registry (parents' education level). Linkage, via person identification numbers, was available from the birth cohorts of 2002 onwards. Hence, our data included all children in MoBa born in 2002-2008 (2008 being the last full birth cohort), resulting in a sample of 102,251 children. For the present study, we use data from questionnaires completed by the mother at child ages 3, 5, and 8 years, with retention rates for the 2002-2008 cohorts of 54%, 49%, and 41%, respectively (the 5-year questionnaire was included from 2004-participation rate for this questionnaire is based on eligible cohorts).

The establishment of MoBa and initial data collection were based on a license from the Norwegian Data Protection Agency and approval from The Regional Committees for Medical and Health Research Ethics. The MoBa cohort is now based on regulations related to the Norwegian Health Registry Act. The current study was approved by The Regional Committees for Medical and Health Research Ethics. The data linkage with administrative records used in the present study was approved by The Regional Committee for Medical Research Ethics and the Data Protection Officer at the first author's host institution.

Measures

Externalizing problems. At ages 3 and 5, we used seven selected items from The Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL; Achenbach, 1991; Achenbach, 1992), covering aggressive and oppositional behavior. The items were selected for the MoBa survey by a team of specialists, using theoretical as well as empirical criteria for selection (see https://www.fhi.no/en/ch/studies/moba/for-forskere-artikler/questionnaires-from-moba/#8-year-olds for details on measures and items). At age 3, scores on this short form correlated .92 with scores on the full CBCL externalizing subscale (Zachrisson et al., 2013). The items were rated by the mothers on a Likert-type scale ranging from 1 "very true/often true" to 3 "never/rarely". Cronbach's alpha for ages 3 and 5 was .67 and .69, respectively in this sample.

We used standardized raw scores in the analyses.

At age 8, we used 8 items from the Parent/Teacher Rating Scale for Disruptive Behavior Disorders (RS-DBD; Silva et al., 2005) measuring conduct problems. The RS-DBD replaced the CBCL in the MoBa as the scale is designed to cover symptoms as described in the DSM. Frequency of behaviors was rated by mothers on a four-point scale ranging from 1 "never/rarely" to 4 "very", and the Cronbach's alpha for the items was .84.

Age of Entry into ECEC.

We used registry data to identify the timing of cash-for-care, a universal handout given to all parents of one-and two-year-old children who did not attend public ECEC for the time period. Children were assigned to entry by 12 months of age if parents did not receive the handout, or to entry by the month after the last handout was received at 36 months (14% of the sample). Cross-validation with self-report of age of entry in the MoBa and with population-level coverage rates supported the validity of this assignment. Details about the age of entry measure are in Online Appendix 2.

Municipality-level ECEC coverage.

For our instrumental variable analyses, we used municipality-level ECEC coverage rates as an instrument. Coverage rates were derived from public records of the total ECEC coverage for children aged 1 and 2 in municipalities by year (https://www.ssb.no/en/statbank/table/12562/). Because the municipalities fund public ECEC, these public records accurately represent the availability of ECEC. We link municipality by year coverage rate to children via the child's residential municipality at age 1.

Household income and family education. We combined the after-tax income for each parent, averaged across the year of the child's birth and the prior year, and adjusted incomes to 2018-levels of the consumer price index. The lowest 2% of annual incomes (in the population) were set to missing and imputed (see description of imputations below) because very low or negative incomes can indicate that losses from private companies have been deducted on the tax records rather than their reported income being low. Moreover, incomes were truncated at the 98 th percentile to remove the influence of outliers. We then centered it at the lowest observed income (approximately NOK 180,000, or USD 22,500, 2018 average exchange rate) and divided it by 100,000.

The highest family education was drawn from the education registry, recording the highest completed level of education for mothers and fathers. We defined four groups: 1) less than completed high school; 2) high school; 3) short tertiary degree (equivalent to BA); 4) long tertiary degree (MA, PhD, or equivalent).

Covariates. Our choice of family and child covariates was primarily informed by previous research on selection into ECEC in Norway (Sibley, Dearing, Toppelberg, Mykletun, & Zachrisson, 2015; Zachrisson, Janson, & Nærde, 2013), and the covariates are, with the exception of child temperament, the same as those used by Zachrisson et al. (2024).

From the registries, we include demographic information: Child gender; Maternal age (in years, truncated at 3 rd and 97 th percentile); Child age at testing; Immigrant status: one western immigrant indicator (0, 1) for having at least one parent born in Europe (except Norway), North America, or Oceania, and one non-western immigrant indicator (0, 1) for having at least one parent born in the rest of the world (except Norway); Number of children in the household (count of household members <=18 years as an average across the child's first 3 years of life, truncated at 4. i.e., the 99 th percentile); Number of adults in the household (count of household members >18 years in child's first year); and birth month dummy variables.

From the Medical Birth Registry, we included birth weight (dichotomized [0,1]: < 2500 and ≥2500 grams), Apgar score (five minutes after birth), and multiple birth (i.e., twins). From MoBa, we included mothers' reports on their anxious/depressive symptoms (Tambs & Moum, 1993) and partner/spouse relationship satisfaction (Rosand, Slinning, Eberhard-Gran, Roysamb, & Tambs, 2011) when children were 6 months old, and child temperament reported by the mother when the child was 6 months old with the Infant Characteristics Questionnaire-6 months form (IBQ-6; Bates, Freeland, & Lounsbury, 1979).

Statistical Analysis

We used two approaches to estimating the effects of age of entry into ECEC on measures of behavior problems at child ages 3, 5, and 8 (i.e., 2 nd /3 rd grade): Two-Way Fixed-Effects (FE) regression models and instrumental variable (IV) regressions. Extensive descriptions of statistical analyses are in Online Appendix 3.

The FE model compares children living in the same municipality and belonging to the same cohort who start ECEC at different ages, using the full variation in age of entry into ECEC to estimate the average treatment effect. This model provides efficient (i.e., precise) estimates. However, while the FE model controls for unobserved variability between municipalities, and our comprehensive set of covariates (e.g., household income, maternal depression, and birth weight) addresses concerns related to within-municipality sorting, we cannot fully rule out the possibility that children who start ECEC early may differ from those who start later.

To provide more robust causal estimates, the IV model uses variation in age of entry into ECEC caused by the policy expansion to estimate the local average treatment effect (LATE) for those induced to start earlier because of the policy. Using only the variability in age of entry induced by the available slots leads to less precise estimates relative to the FE models, highlighting a common trade-off between estimation efficiency and risk of bias.

Additionally, because LATE identifies effects for those affected by the policy, the estimates cannot be directly compared to the ECEC effects estimated by the FE model. The IV model relies on assumptions about instrument relevance, independence, and exclusion. We describe these IV assumptions thoroughly in Appendix 3, along with empirical validation efforts.

As a preliminary step, we probed the functional form (i.e., linear versus non-linear) of the association between age of entry into ECEC and externalizing problems and found little evidence of non-linearity (see online Appendix 5). Thus, in our primary models, we employ linear estimators. All standard errors were clustered by municipality.

Missing data.

There was substantial attrition in the MoBa dataset. As mentioned, retention rates for the 2002-2008 cohorts were 54%, 49%, and 41% at ages 3, 5, and 8 years, respectively. Item-level missingness was low (<2%). Note that we do not have missing data for age of entry into ECEC or any of the demographic variables, as these were drawn from registry data. We used multiple imputation with chained equations in Stata 18 to estimate 20 datasets based on the full available MoBa sample (cohorts 2002-2008). The imputation models included all variables listed in Table 1 (including fixed effects for cohort and birth month), as well as fixed effects for municipality. National test scores in math at age 10 were also included as an auxiliary variable, as it was available for 95% of the sample (and is correlated -.07, -.10, and -.04 with externalizing behavior at ages 3, 5, and 8, respectively).

Because of the high rates of missingness, we included only children with at least one nonmissing outcome measure in our estimation models (n=66,394, app. 65% for outcomes at ages 3 and 8 years). Since the 5-year questionnaire only included the cohorts 2004-2008, we restricted the estimation sample for the 5-year outcome to these cohorts (again including only those with at least one non-missing outcome, n=52,253). The results from our MI models were consistent with estimates using complete data only (listwise deletion, see Table A5 in Online Appendix 5).

Discussion

We found no evidence of harmful effects on behavior problems through age 8 following early and continuous ECEC in Norway. This result is contrary to the theoretical prediction of harmful effects of early, extensive, and continuous care (Belsky, 2001) as well as the conclusion based on a review of correlational studies that harmful effects were restricted to children from higher SES families (Huston et al., 2015). The finding is also contrary to correlational studies finding protective short- (Cote et al., 2007) and long-term (Orri et al., 2019) effects of early ECEC for children from lower SES families from Quebec.

When discussing null-findings, it is important to acknowledge that absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. The logic of null-hypothesis testing allows us to fail to reject a hypothesis, but not to prove the null hypothesis (Schuengel, 2022). Interpreting our results therefore requires us to state what magnitude of effect we would regard as substantively meaningful, independently of statistical significance. We adopt two benchmarks estimated in the same sample and on the same measures as our treatment effects: the raw gender difference in externalizing behavior, which was 12.6%, 24.0% and 12.1% of an SD at ages 3, 5 and 8; and the difference between children whose parents both had less than high school and children whose parents both held a long university degree, which was 40.0%, 49.3% and 18.0% of an SD. Both are differences this literature routinely treats as consequential. Following Schuengel (2022), we then applied equivalence tests, which ask whether the hypothesis of a meaningful effect can be rejected against a declared smallest effect size of interest in both the positive and negative direction (implemented as two onesided tests). Our IV estimates are equivalent to zero at a threshold of 0.20 SD at all three ages, the smallest thresholds at which equivalence holds being 0.16, 0.18 and 0.16 of an SD at ages 3, 5 and 8. Set against our benchmarks, the IV estimates thus exclude harmful effects as large as the difference associated with parental education at every age, and as large as the gender difference at age 5; at ages 3 and 8, where the gender difference is smaller, the equivalence bound approaches but does not fall below it. Our fixed-effects estimates, which are considerably more precise but rest on a selection-on-observables assumption within municipality and cohort, point to the same conclusion and are equivalent to zero at a threshold of 0.02 SD. While smaller effects cannot be ruled out, we therefore read these results as evidence against harmful effects of early, extensive, and continuous ECEC.

Our data are unique because we leverage a population-wide longitudinal health survey (MoBa) in combination with registry data, providing detailed information on behavior problems across time and family-level covariates, with the rollout of the survey coinciding with a large policy expansion. This allows us to use two strong research designs, FE and IV, to probe the harmful effects of early age of entry into ECEC. Each design offers considerable advantages compared to traditional designs, where accounting for selection into care relies solely on observed covariates. Yet, because they are both based on distinct sets of assumptions, our results are considerably strengthened by the consistent patterns observed across these complementary designs. The designs used in this paper have different strengths and weaknesses and provide estimates with different interpretations.

In summary, we believe our strong research designs with distinct non-overlapping assumptions provide support for the lack of evidence for harmful effects of ECEC for infants and toddlers in Norway, well into school age. These findings are consistent with, and extend, previous quasi-experimental studies investigating potentially harmful short-term effects of ECEC in Norway, including age of entry (Dearing et al., 2015) and hours in care (Zachrisson et al., 2013). However, our findings are inconsistent with some causal studies in other universal access contexts, like Quebec and France (Baker et al., 2019; Berger et al., 2021). This discrepancy may indicate between-country differences in the quality and content of the ECEC services and wider family policies. Lack of evidence for harmful effects of ECEC in Norway may be explained by the care and pedagogical approach of ECEC in Norway, which is less focused on school readiness and academic skill learning than in other contexts like, e.g., North America.

Specifically, in Norway, ECEC policy and practice standards place a major emphasis on a holistic approach to early childhood development in which free play, child-initiated activities, and child well-being are central elements, and formal instruction is virtually absent (OECD, 2015). This contrasts with contexts where teacher-led instructions and formal learning are more strongly emphasized and may be more facilitating for development of behavioral regulation. For example, in the US, while the Tennessee Preschool Program met most national recommendations for content of the program, those recommendations put emphasis on formal group instruction, akin to a school setting (Lipsey et al., 2018). Scholars have expressed concerns about the developmental appropriateness of these kinds of instructions for young children (Dearing & Farran, 2022). Specifically, they argue that an alignment between the pedagogy guiding the environment in ECEC and the children's developmental needs is crucial for providing an enriching environment.

Moreover, structural quality in Norway is highly regulated and homogeneous across settings. This includes staff-child ratios of 1:3 for one-and two-year olds and 1:6 for older children; a national curriculum (framework plan) guiding the pedagogical content; and regulations for indoor and outdoor spaces. Overall ECEC quality may also play a role in reducing the association between ECEC quantity and behavior problems, as has been shown in correlational studies (e.g., McCartney et al., 2010). While the evidence from causal designs is lacking, a study found the observed quality of ECEC for the youngest children in Quebec at the time of the scale-up to be worryingly low (Japel, Tremblay, & Cote, 2005).

Previous research on the Norwegian ECEC reform from the 2000s-using identification strategies similar to the ones we use-has shown positive effects on early language development and national test scores at age 10 and 15 for children from low-income families and children of parents with few years of education (Dearing, Zachrisson, Mykletun, & Toppelberg, 2018.; Zachrisson et al., 2024; Almås et al., 2026; Note that the first two of these studies used binary 18 month age of entry), and some effect on the labor supply of mothers (Eckhoff Andresen & Havnes, 2019). As the current evidence on the reform suggests benefits for cognitive and achievement outcomes, while suggesting no apparent effects on behavior, it may be reasonable to speculate that the child-centered and holistic approach of the Norwegian ECEC system is overall beneficial.

While our study has considerable strengths in numbers and design, there are notable limitations. First of all, although MoBa is population-wide, it had a relatively low participation rate (41%) and considerable attrition. Children of non-Norwegian background are especially underrepresented, as are children from families with less money and less parental education. While Zachrisson et al. (2024) showed that associations between socioeconomic indicators and achievement test scores were similar in families attending MoBa and in the general population, we have no guarantee that this is true for behavior problems, or that the treatment effect of ECEC is identical in the two groups. The generalizability of our results is contingent on the effects of ECEC being similar for the attenders as for the non-attenders.

Additionally, the measure of behavior problems changed in MoBa between the age 5 and age 8 assessment, precluding direct comparisons of the two outcomes beyond the broad construct.

Moreover, the internal validity of our models is only as strong as the support for our assumptions.
