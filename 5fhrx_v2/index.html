Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Boys and Things, Girls and People? Gender-Related Interest Patterns in Elementary School 1. Introduction

A central developmental question concerns when interests become systematically organized, how stable such patterns are, and whether they already differ by gender in childhood. From early childhood onward, interests guide which activities children seek out, persist in, and avoid; thereby shaping not only what children engage with but also how they learn from these experiences. These selective engagements accumulate over time, shaping learning experiences in elementary school and laying foundations for later educational and career pathways (Ainley, 2006; Elsworth et al., 1999; Krapp, 2007; Nye et al., 2017; Schiefele et al., 1993). In learning contexts, interest is associated with sustained attention (Ainley, 2006; Hidi & Renninger, 2006), a greater willingness to invest effort (Ainley et al., 2002; Hidi & Renninger, 2006), and deeper processing (e.g., Schiefele, 1990), all of which can support comprehension and meaningful skill acquisition.

Such processes may become particularly visible in elementary school, where children encounter a broader range of structured and self-chosen activities. Repeated engagement can consolidate into relatively stable preferences that become part of children's developing selfunderstanding and identity (Hidi & Renninger, 2006; Krapp, 2007; Renninger, 1992). If enduring patterns of engagement begin to take shape already in elementary school, examining interests this early becomes relevant for understanding how motivational orientations connect to knowledge acquisition and emerging competence differences (Lechner et al., 2019).

Importantly, interest is not a unitary construct. Rather, educational research distinguishes more situational states of interest from more enduring individual traits and emphasizes that interest is tied to specific objects, activities, and domains (Hidi & Renninger, 2006; Renninger & Hidi, 2011). Interest development in childhood is therefore not only about overall engagement, but also about how preferences become organized across activities and domains. One prominent source of such organization is gender, which has been shown to structure interests and preferences later in development and may already do so in childhood (Jacobs et al., 2002).

Accordingly, the present study examines whether gender-related interest patterns are already observable among third graders (mean age = 8.62 years), using a newly developed RIASEC-based self-report measure of broad activity preferences. The measure assesses preferences for concrete activities, such as building a tree house or caring for other children, rather than interest in school subjects such as mathematics or German. We also examine how these preferences relate to subject-specific academic interests and school grades.

1.1. Subject-Specific and Activity-Based Interests in Elementary School

Research on interest has developed along two mainly separate traditions: an educational-psychological tradition focused on interest in school domains and learning contexts, and a vocational-psychological tradition focused on broader activity preferences and interest profiles. Both perspectives are relevant in elementary school, where children's interests may be organized both within curricular domains and across activities that cut across school subjects.

In the more educational-psychological tradition, interest is typically conceptualized as a domain-or subject-specific construct tied to school-related content and activities, such as mathematics, language, or science (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020; Jansen et al., 2016; Köller et al., 2001; Renninger & Hidi, 2011; Tröbst et al., 2016). This line of research focuses on how interests develop through instructional experiences, how they support engagement and achievement, and how they can be fostered through educational interventions (e.g., Hübner et al., 2017). Consistent with this focus, interest is typically associated with achievement, especially when measured with appropriate specificity, although effect sizes are usually modest (Schiefele et al., 1993). Situated expectancy-value theory provides a useful framework for this tradition because it conceptualizes children's engagement and choices as shaped by their expectancies for success and by the subjective value they attach to specific tasks, activities, and domains, including intrinsic value or interest (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020 , 2024).

This tradition also aligns with research on academic self-concept, which conceptualizes children's self-beliefs as multidimensional and increasingly differentiated by school subject as domain-specific experiences and feedback accumulate (Arens et al., 2021; Marsh, 1990; Möller, 2024; Shavelson et al., 1976). From this perspective, subject-specific interests, value and expectancy beliefs, and self-beliefs are expected to co-develop, reflecting how children evaluate and value their experiences in particular domains (Denissen et al., 2007; Eccles & Wigfield, 2024).

In contrast, vocational psychology typically conceptualizes interests as relatively stable, trait-like preferences for activities that help individuals select and function well in environments that match their orientations, often framed in terms of person-environment fit (Hoff et al., 2020; Holland, 1997). The most influential structural representation within this tradition is Holland's RIASEC model, which distinguishes six interest domains-Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, Conventional-and assumes that these domains form a systematic circumplex configuration, such that adjacent domains are more similar whereas opposing domains are more dissimilar (Holland, 1997; Prediger, 1982). This circumplex space is commonly summarized by two orthogonal core dimensions, People versus Things and Data versus Ideas (Lippa, 1998; Prediger, 1982; Su et al., 2009), and recent latent-variable work supports its internal and external validity, including its links to constructs such as self-concepts (Etzel et al., 2021). Beyond its structural properties, longitudinal and meta-analytic evidence indicates that interest fit predicts satisfaction and career success indicators over time, and that vocational interests are related to consequential later outcomes (Hanna et al., 2021; Hoff et al., 2020; Stoll et al., 2017), and that RIASEC profiles become increasingly differentiated and stable as students approach educational and occupational decisions (Tracey, 2002; Tracey et al., 2006).

The two traditions are complementary in elementary school, where children's engagement extends well beyond formal instruction. From a RIASEC-model perspective, children more broadly engage in activities such as building and tinkering (Realistic), exploring and experimenting (Investigative), drawing and performing (Artistic), helping and caring (Social), persuading and leading (Enterprising), and sorting and organizing (Conventional). Such activities cut across school subjects and occur in multiple contexts, including classrooms, homes, sports clubs, and settings of extracurricular enrichment. Despite not being tied to specific curricular domains, they are consequential for educational outcomes because they afford repeated practice and skill development. Thus, they provide opportunities for knowledge acquisition in line with investment accounts that describe how general capacities such as fluid intelligence are translated into domain-specific competencies over time (Cattell, 1987; Cunha & Heckman, 2007). A RIASEC-based perspective is therefore well placed to describe children's broader activity preferences as a structured map of what they are repeatedly drawn to, and can complement educational research that views interest as becoming increasingly subject-specific with growing domain experience (Gogol et al., 2016; Weich et al., 2024) by capturing activity-based preferences that show meaningful links to learning outcomes (Höft & Bernholt, 2021).

However, whether elementary-school children already show a coherent organization of such broader activity preferences and how it should be measured remains an open question.

Although standardized measurement tools are largely lacking in childhood, evidence suggests that vocationally flavored interests can be meaningfully assessed through age-appropriate activity descriptions (Pässler & Hell, 2020; Pässler & Schneider, 2018; Tracey & Ward, 1998). For example, younger children may respond more reliably to concrete activity descriptions than to abstract occupational labels (Tracey & Caulum, 2015; Tracey & Ward, 1998). Consistent with this, studies using the Inventory of Children's Activities show that younger children's evaluations of activities are often organized around concrete and socially salient features, including gender-typed meanings and situational context (Tracey & Ward, 1998). More generally, research on vocational interests in childhood rarely includes samples below the age of ten or prior to fourth grade (Pässler & Hell, 2020; Phipps, 1995; Rohlfing et al., 2012). When younger children are included, assessments often rely on career aspirations or narrow interest indicators instead of developmentally appropriate, comprehensive RIASEC-based measures (Ayriza et al., 2020; Oliveira et al., 2020; Schiefer et al., 2020; Trice et al., 1995). Consequently, evidence on the structural organization of vocational-like interests before late elementary school remains limited, leaving open when a RIASEC-like circumplex begins to develop.

1.2. Gendered Pathways and Early Interest Differences

Understanding how interests become structured in childhood is particularly important for explaining gendered educational pathways. In educational psychology, gender differences are most consistently discussed with respect to subject-specific motivation (e.g., interest, intrinsic value), self-concept, and participation, especially in STEM domains (Ganley & Lubienski, 2016; Jacobs et al., 2002; Wang et al., 2013). Empirically, a recurring pattern is that gender differences in motivational variables can be noticeable even when gender differences in achievement are comparatively small (e.g., Sewasew et al., 2018). This divergence matters because motivational beliefs are robust predictors of later engagement and educational choices (e.g., elective course taking), above and beyond prior performance (Eccles & Wigfield, 2024; Marsh et al., 2005). At the same time, gendered motivational patterns are not uniform across broad labels such as "STEM". Recent work suggests that stereotypes, perceived fit, and interest can vary substantially across specific STEM fields, implying that early interest development may already be differentiated at a finer-grained level than typical STEM versus non-STEM distinctions (Master et al., 2025). Overall, the educational-psychological literature commonly reports a stereotypical configuration, with girls tending to show higher motivation in language-related domains and boys tending to show higher motivation in science-related domains (Lv et al., 2022; Yeung et al., 2011). These early differences are not typically viewed as purely inherent preferences, but also as motivational outcomes that may be shaped by socialization and meanings attached to domains and activities. For example, expectancy value perspectives emphasize that children's interests develop from what they think they can do (expectancies) and what they perceive as valuable or fitting for them (values), and both components are sensitive to cues from parents, teachers, peers, and broader cultural stereotypes (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020 , 2024; Jacobs et al., 2002). Gender role expectations can influence which activities are encouraged, modeled, and socially rewarded, thereby affecting children's opportunities for practice and the feedback they receive. Importantly, such influences can operate very early. For example, teachers' traditional gender role attitudes have been shown to undermine boys' reading-related motivation and skills already in preschool, illustrating that gendered beliefs in the environment can translate into differential motivational development long before children make formal educational choices (Wolter et al., 2015). Together, these findings suggest that gendered interest patterns may emerge through repeated, cumulative experiences in which activities become associated with gender-typed meanings.

In the vocational tradition, gender differences regarding interests in older samples are often summarized by the People versus Things dimension. Meta-analytic evidence shows robust differences, with men tending to report higher Realistic interests and women tending to report higher Social interests (Su et al., 2009). Longitudinal findings further suggest that these differences are already detectable by late childhood (Hoff et al., 2018; Pässler & Hell, 2020), with girls' vocational interest profiles being more clearly differentiated across domains than boys' (Fouad & Mohler, 2004; Xu & Tracey, 2016). Recent research in Brazil also found higher Realistic interests among boys and higher Artistic and Social interests among girls (De Fruyt et al., 2025). This extends the evidence beyond European and North American samples, although the Brazilian participants were adolescents and thus older than the third graders studied here. At the same time, the development of a coherent circumplex structure remains elusive. Early work suggests that younger children's preferences may be organized around familiarity and enjoyment ("fun" activities), situational and contextual features (where and with whom an activity occurs), and socially salient meanings, including gender stereotypes, rather than around abstract dimensions such as the People-Things and Data-Ideas axes (Tracey & Ward, 1998). In this age range, preferences may therefore remain comparatively concrete and anchored in observable activity features and contexts, which could limit the emergence of broad-dimensional profile differences such as People-Things. Consequently, gender differences that are robust on the People-Things dimension in adolescence and adulthood may be attenuated or not yet detectable in elementary school. Longitudinal work in older samples supports the idea that structural coherence increases across adolescence (Tracey, 2002; Tracey et al., 2006). However, the question remains at what age a circumplex structure begins to take shape.

1.3. The Present Study

Research on vocational-like-or activity-based-interests in young children is still limited and measurement instruments for this age group are scarce. In the present study, we examine whether gender-related interest patterns are already observable in third grade and how they relate to subject-specific academic interests and school grades. Because no comprehensive RIASEC-based self-report measure exists for children of this age, we developed an age-appropriate instrument that captures the six RIASEC domains through concrete activity descriptions suitable for children at this age, with the goal of representing broad activity preferences without relying on abstract occupational titles. Third graders were assessed at two time points approximately six months apart.

We addressed four research aims. First, in developing a new self-report instrument for elementary school children, we examined the fit of children's interest profiles to a RIASEC-like circumplex. We selected a short form using T1 data and evaluated its circumplex fit at T2 for temporal cross-validation. Second, we examined gender differences in interest profiles.

Based on previous findings in older samples, we expected emerging gender-typed patterns aligned with the People versus Things dimension, with boys showing relatively stronger things-oriented interests and girls showing relatively stronger people-oriented interests (Pässler & Hell, 2020; Su et al., 2009). However, we also considered the alternative possibility that such broad-dimensional differences are weak or absent at this age, because primary-school children's activity preferences may still be relatively concrete and contextbound rather than organized along abstract People-Things and Data-Ideas orientations.

Accordingly, in addition to mean differences in the six interest domains, we examined gender differences in profile orientations along the broader People-Things and Data-Ideas dimensions. Third, we investigated stability of the assessed interests over approximately six months. Specifically, we examined mean-level change in profile coordinates and rank-order stability of the six domain scores over this period. Fourth, we studied the relationship of interests with academic and subject-specific interests, and school grades. The research objectives of the present study were not preregistered.

Data were collected at two In total, N = 2,883 third graders from 189 classes at 111 elementary schools in Baden-Württemberg participated in the original study (Golle et al., 2018). 2.2. Measures

>Measures

2.2.1. Vocational-Like Interests.

Building on Holland's (1997) RIASEC model and established interest measures for adolescents and adults (e.g., Bergmann & Eder, 1992; Tracey & Ward, 1998), we newly developed an age-appropriate self-report inventory to assess Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, and Conventional interests. Each domain was measured with seven items, totaling 42 items. Items were presented to students by providing them with the stem "How much are you interested in …?", followed by specific activity statements. Responses were given on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very much). Example items include "building a tree house" (Realistic), "mixing liquids" (Investigative), "drawing pictures" (Artistic), "listening to other children's problems" (Social), "selling something to others" (Enterprising), and "sorting things" (Conventional).

The full questionnaire (German original and English translation of items), including descriptive statistics for each item for both measurement points, can be found in the supplementary materials (SM Table 1).

2.2.2. Academic Interests.

Students' academic interests in core school domains were assessed via self-reports at both measurement points. Children indicated how much they liked school-related activities in German (reading, writing, dictation), mathematics (mental arithmetic, written arithmetic, word problems), cultural/science-related topics (e.g., learning about the local environment, arts-and-crafts/creative activities, learning about nature), and foreign languages (e.g., speaking, writing, and learning vocabulary in English or French).

Items were answered on the same 5-point response scale as the RIASEC-like interest inventory. For each domain, responses were aggregated to form mean scale scores. In the present subsample, academic-interest data were available for n = 1,070 students at T1 and for n = 854 students at T2; missingness was negligible among those with available data (0.4% partially missing at T1; 0% at T2). All items (German original and English translation), including descriptive statistics for each item for both measurement points, can be found in the supplementary materials (SM Table 2).

2.2.3. Scholastic Achievement.

School grades were used as indicators of students' scholastic achievement in German and mathematics. Information was obtained from the participating schools via teachers. For the present analyses, we used midterm grades (T2), ranging from 1 (very good) to 6 (insufficient). Grades at the end of elementary school are highly consequential in the German school system, as they inform promotion decisions and the transition to secondary education tracks. Grade information was available for n = 995 students (0.2% partially missing).

2.3. Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted with R version 4.5.2 (R Core Team, 2025). We provide all materials necessary to reproduce the analyses in an online repository: https://dataverse.harvard.edu/previewurl.xhtml?token=c21d6fec-c4e8-4f79-940b-6a04a0b44c1b Structural Circumplex Analyses. We employed the Structural Summary Method (SSM) via the circumplex R package (Zimmermann & Wright, 2017) to evaluate the fit of boys' and girls' RIASEC profiles. The SSM fitted a cosine curve to the six mean domain scores at the theoretical angular positions (R = 0°, I = 60°, A = 120°, S = 180°, E = 240°, C = 300°). We estimated elevation, representing the mean interest level across domains; amplitude, indicating the degree of profile differentiation; and angular displacement, denoting the peak orientation of the profile within the RIASEC space. Model fit was assessed using R², with values above .80 indicating good fit to the cosine function and values above .70 considered adequate (Wright et al., 2009). The SSM used complete sets of the six domain scores separately at each wave; children were not required to have complete profiles at both waves.

Short Scale Construction Using Ant Colony Optimization.

The original 42-item questionnaire was developed on theoretical grounds to represent the six RIASEC domains through age-appropriate activity descriptions. Because the present study was the first administration of the newly developed instrument, the initial item pool was subject to empirical evaluation and refinement, an integral step in the iterative process of questionnaire development (Clark & Watson, 2019). The full questionnaire showed limited circumplex fit, particularly for boys. We therefore decided to construct a short form with satisfactory circumplex fit for both boys and girls while retaining representation of all six theoretically defined domains. This decision was made post hoc and was not preregistered. As a positive side effect, a shorter questionnaire may also reduce response burden in future applications with young children. To identify a suitable item combination, we applied Ant Colony Optimization (ACO; Olaru et al., 2019; Schroeders et al., 2016), a metaheuristic optimization algorithm inspired by the foraging behavior of ants. Each artificial ant constructs a candidate item set, and items from better-performing sets become more likely to be selected in subsequent iterations, much as pheromone trails guide ants toward shorter routes. The optimization procedure selected four of the original seven items per domain by maximizing the lower of the two gender-specific circumplex fit values, yielding a 24-item version.

Importantly, we used only T1 data to construct the short form and subsequently evaluated the same 24 items at T2, approximately six months later, providing a form of temporal crossvalidation. We computed available-item domain means and applied the SSM separately to boys and girls at the theoretical RIASEC angles. The search used 120 ants per iteration, an evaporation parameter of 0.90, and seed 10, and stopped after 60 consecutive iterations without improvement in the best solution. Each candidate SSM analysis used 100 bootstrap resamples to estimate uncertainty. SM Table 1 identifies the retained items. All six domains remained represented, although selecting four of seven items narrowed their activity content.

Person-Level Circumplex Coordinates (People-Things; Data-Ideas).

After selecting the final short form via Ant Colony Optimization (ACO), we derived individual circumplex coordinates for each child to describe where each individual's interest profile is located within the RIASEC space. First, we z-standardized the six short-form RIASEC scale scores (R, I, A, S, E, C). We then computed profile elevation (PE) as the mean of the six standardized domain scores, representing the individual's overall interest level across domains. To represent profile orientation, we calculated two orthogonal axis coordinates (Gurtman, 2009; Junkuhn & Nagy, 2022): People-Things (PT) and Data-Ideas (DI); PT and DI were computed as weighted linear composites based on the cosine and sine of the theoretical circumplex angles, respectively (cf. Supplementary Materials Method section). These coordinates use standardized individual domain scores and should not be equated with the displacement angles of the raw-score group-mean profiles reported in Table 1.

Higher absolute values of PT and DI indicate a stronger orientation along the respective axis. For PT, positive values indicate a stronger Things-oriented profile (greater relative emphasis on domains located on the Things side of the circumplex, e.g., Realistic), whereas negative values indicate a stronger People-oriented profile (greater emphasis on domains located on the People side, e.g., Social). For DI, positive values indicate a stronger Ideas-oriented profile (greater emphasis on domains located on the Ideas side, e.g., Artistic), whereas negative values indicate a stronger Data-oriented profile (greater emphasis on domains located on the Data side, e.g., Conventional). Values close to zero reflect a more balanced profile with little net orientation on that axis. We used the PT, DI, and PE values as person-related variables to correlate with the covariates of academic interests and school grades.

Missing Data.

The original study used a booklet design in which questionnaire booklets were randomly assigned to classes, resulting in planned missingness (Golle et al., 2018). The T1 analytical sample was restricted to children with at least 32 of 42 RIASEC item responses and an available girl/boy grouping value. Within this sample, item nonresponse rates were 0.9% at T1 and 1.0% at T2. RIASEC scale scores were calculated as available-item means. Correlations were calculated using pairwise available observations, whereas the mixed-effects models included all available coordinate observations (1,150 at T1 and 834 at T2). In sensitivity analyses, we recalculated correlations using alternative T1 response thresholds and complete scores on all Table 3 variables, and refitted the mixed-effects models for children with complete coordinates at both waves. These analyses used the same item set and score definitions as the main analyses. Random booklet allocation is an established planned-missing design (Little & Rhemtulla, 2013). FIML or multiple imputation could in principle use auxiliary variables to improve estimation efficiency. However, children without RIASEC responses provide no direct information about their activity interests. We therefore restricted the analyses to children with sufficient observed RIASEC responses rather than estimating their interest scores entirely from auxiliary variables and modeling assumptions.

Short-Term Mean-Level Change and Rank-Order Stability.

We used these personlevel coordinates to examine (a) gender differences in circumplex orientation and (b) shortterm mean-level change from T1 to T2. To evaluate the short-term temporal stability and potential gender-differential change, we estimated linear mixed-effects models with repeated observations nested within children and children clustered in classes (random intercepts for child and class, respectively). Each model included Time, Gender, and the Gender × Time interaction as fixed effects; the interaction term tested whether boys and girls differed in their change in PT, DI, and PE over approximately six months. Children with data only at T1 were retained in the mixed models, which do not require complete data at both waves (1,150 at T1 and 834 at T2; 1,984 observations in total). They were fitted by restricted maximum likelihood, with inference depending on the missing-at-random assumption conditional on the model variables and on a correctly specified model. We also calculated Pearson retest correlations for each of the six short-form domain scores, using children with available scores at both occasions. As a supplementary analysis, we estimated latent retest correlations based on longitudinal one-factor models for each domain (see Supplementary Materials).

Correlation Intervals and Group Comparisons.

We obtained pointwise 95% percentile confidence intervals for correlations using 5,000 nonparametric school-level (cluster) bootstrap resamples, retaining all classes and children within each selected school to preserve within-school dependence (Field & Welsh, 2007). To test whether the correlations reported in Table 3 differed between boys and girls, we used Steiger's (1980) omnibus test, which compares the Fisher-z-transformed correlations across all 50 pairs simultaneously. We obtained the p value from 5,000 permutations of gender within schools, after standardizing all variables within gender groups, so that the dependence among the correlations and the clustering of children in schools are taken into account. Correlations and confidence intervals are reported in SM Table 7.

3.1. The Circumplex Structure of Vocational-Like Interests

We first evaluated the fit of the circumplex models using the theoretically developed 42-item RIASEC questionnaire. Fit was good for girls (R² = .919) but insufficient for boys (R² = .374) at T1. Given that this was the first empirical application of the questionnaire, we used the data of T1 to inform the construction of a short scale. Specifically, we applied the metaheuristic ACO to select four items for each RIASEC domain, while simultaneously aiming for satisfactory model fit for both gender groups. The resulting 24-item version showed higher fit at T1, with R² = .928 for boys and .934 for girls. Importantly, the same item set was evaluated at T2 without further selection as a kind of temporal cross-validation. Fit of the circumplex model remained satisfactory at this second measurement occasion, with R² = .888 for boys and .945 for girls (Table 1). However, since we used data from the same sample at both measurement time points, the psychometric evaluation of the measure is preliminary, awaiting independent validation. Internal consistency estimates were satisfactory for the original seven-item scales (ω = .81 to .87). For the selected four-item scales, estimates ranged from .64 to .80 at T1 and from .60 to .81 at T2. The lower values, particularly for Enterprising interests, indicate limited reliability and warrant caution when interpreting individual profile coordinates. 4. Discussion

To connect activity-based and subject-specific accounts of interest, we developed a RIASEC-based self-report for elementary school children and examined gender-related profiles, change over six months, and associations with academic interests and grades. The 24-item version selected at T1 showed good circumplex fit for boys and girls at both measurement points. T2 provided temporal cross-validation of the selected item set.

Children's preferences for concrete activities showed a systematic organization beyond school subjects and clear gender-related differences. Below, we discuss the measurement results, gender differences, stability, and academic correlates before considering the study's limitations.

4.1. Dimensional Structure and Gender Differences in Vocational-Like Interests

Structural analyses of the full item pool yielded better circumplex fit for girls than for boys. This difference may reflect the activities described by the items and how children interpret them. For example, the Realistic item "helping with repairs" shares the verb "helping" with Social items such as "helping sick people" and "helping others feel comfortable." A child may endorse this item because they like repairing things or because they like helping an adult, so it may carry social meaning in addition to its intended Realistic content. More generally, younger children may group activities by surface features, such as a shared verb or the people involved, and by whether an activity seems typical for boys or girls, and item selection may affect gender differences in assessed interests (Tracey & Caulum, 2015; Tracey & Ward, 1998). If such interpretations differ between boys and girls, the resulting pattern of correlations may also differ from the expected circumplex structure. Another possible explanation concerns the reading demands of the self-report questionnaire.

Given average gender differences in reading achievement during primary school (Mullis et al., 2017), some boys may have found particular item formulations more difficult to understand or interpret. Finally, familiarity with the specific activities may also play a role (Borgers et al., 2000). However, these explanations remain speculative because we did not assess item comprehension or interpretation.

Another question is whether the selected items adequately cover the six RIASEC domains. Content coverage refers to how well the items represent the range of activities associated with each domain (Haynes et al., 1995; Sireci, 1998). The original questionnaire was developed with reference to Holland's model and established interest inventories. The short form retains four of seven items in each domain, leaving some activities underrepresented. For example, all four retained Investigative items involve experimenting or observing (e.g., conducting experiments in a laboratory, looking through a microscope), whereas the three omitted items concerned studying the behavior of animals or plants, reading newspaper articles about difficult topics, and listening to or watching the news. Because the universe of relevant activities is broad and cannot be sampled exhaustively, some loss of coverage is unavoidable (Markus & Borsboom, 2013). Future work should therefore examine the extent to which the circumplex structure depends on the specific item set and how children interpret the described activities.

Beyond content coverage, the short form also needs to be evaluated in terms of its psychometric properties. Shortening a questionnaire can affect its psychometric properties (Kruyen et al., 2013; Smith et al., 2000). The 24 items selected at T1 also showed good circumplex fit at T2, approximately six months later. However, because both assessments involved the same cohort, the short form still needs to be evaluated in an independent sample. The present study revealed consistent gender differences in the circumplex framework: boys reported higher interests in Realistic and Enterprising, whereas girls reported higher Artistic and Social interests. On the coordinate level of the RIASEC space, boys were more Things-oriented on the People-Things axis, whereas girls were more Ideasoriented on the Data-Ideas axis. Effect sizes on the People-Things dimension were substantial (d = 0.77), comparable in magnitude to those reported in adult samples (Su et al., 2009). For Data-Ideas, the gender difference was smaller (d = -0.37) but in the same direction as in older cohorts. These mean differences resemble gender-related preferences documented in older samples (Su et al., 2009). It thus extends prior findings that gendered RIASEC interest profiles can be observed in later school years (Pässler & Hell, 2020). The gender difference on the People-Things dimension is compatible with accounts emphasizing early gender-typed meanings and differential encouragement for activities (e.g., Jacobs et al., 2002; Wang et al., 2013).

The present observational data do not identify mechanisms. The same pattern could reflect activity opportunities, social feedback, perceived competence, or different interpretations of the items. It is compatible with accounts in which children use gender-typed meanings to judge which activities fit them (Hannover & Wolter, 2019). Related findings concern gender stereotypes about intellectual ability at age six (Bian et al., 2017) and associations of adults' gender role attitudes with children's motivation in preschool (Wolter et al., 2015). Our findings show gender-related preferences beyond particular school subjects in this third-grade sample. They do not identify when these differences first arose or show that early interventions would change later participation. The two-wave design permits an assessment of short-term change and stability over six months. Generalization also requires studies in other cultural and socioeconomic contexts, given evidence that gender differences in preferences vary between societies (Falk & Hermle, 2018; Stoet & Geary, 2018).

4.2. Temporal Stability of Vocational Interests and Their Relations to Academic Interests

The mixed-effects models showed little change in the average People-Things gender difference over six months. Data-Ideas scores increased slightly, somewhat more for girls, and profile elevation declined slightly, more for boys. These are findings about mean-level change. Despite relatively small changes in the group-level profiles, the six RIASEC dimensions showed only moderate rank-order stability over six months (manifest rs = .38-.52; latent rs = .51-.64). Both forms of stability need to be distinguished; an unchanged group mean does not imply that children retain the same relative positions. The six-month interval is too short to determine whether gender differences increase with age or contribute to later educational and occupational choices. Longer follow-ups could examine these questions in relation to earlier longitudinal work (Hoff et al., 2018; Morgan et al., 2013; Stoll et al., 2017).

The correlations between academic interests and RIASEC-based activity preferences were small to moderate. Interest in a school subject is therefore a weak indicator of a child's broader activity preferences, although it may be the most readily available information when children's educational pathways are discussed. A child's interest in mathematics or German says little about whether that child prefers building, investigating, helping, or organizing.

When these preferences are relevant to educational guidance or later career counseling, practitioners should assess them directly using measures with age-appropriate item wording.

Moreover, some scales have limited reliability, which can attenuate correlations and reduce the precision of individual profile coordinates. We also did not test incremental validity, so the present results do not show that activity-based interests predict grades beyond academic interests. The partial overlap may reflect different experiences: academic interests are closely tied to instruction, and activity preferences may also develop through family routines, hobbies, and extracurricular activities (Bryant et al., 2006). Earlier reviews and longitudinal research describe gradual development and differentiation of interests across childhood and adolescence (Gfrörer et al., 2022; Hartung et al., 2005). The small and sometimes unspecific associations in our study are consistent with a need to assess both kinds of preferences directly, rather than substituting one for the other.

4.3. Limitations and Desiderata for Future Research

Several limitations must be taken into account when interpreting the results. First, the 24-item short form was developed through exploratory item selection using T1 data. Datadriven item selection can capitalize on chance characteristics of a sample (MacCallum et al., 1992), a concern that has also been raised for ACO-based measure development (Dong & Dumas, 2025). Because temporal cross-validation at T2 involved the same cohort, it does not replace validation in an independent sample (Smith et al., 2000). Some of the shortened scales also had low reliability, which limits measurement precision. Second, our reliance on child self-reports introduces its own concerns regarding response styles, reading comprehension, and the cognitive demands of self-assessment at this age (Goecke et al., 2022). Future multiinformant designs combining child, parent, and/or teacher reports could help separate trait variance from method effects (Eid et al., 2003 (Eid et al., , 2008)).

Third, the six-month interval is too short to draw strong conclusions about the developmental trajectory of interest structures across elementary school; longitudinal followups would be needed to examine whether the circumplex becomes more differentiated across middle childhood and how stability develops into adolescence (Tracey, 2002; Xu & Tracey, 2016). Fourth, the recruitment procedure may limit the generalizability of our findings.

Participating schools were recruited through the HCAP, an extracurricular enrichment program for gifted elementary school children, and the mean HISEI of the analytical sample was somewhat higher than in a representative sample of fourth graders in Germany. Schools with a stronger interest in enrichment programs and families with higher occupational status are therefore overrepresented. Such selection may bias the results if participation is associated with children's activity preferences. Thus, the observed profiles and gender differences have to be replicated in a larger representative sample of primary school children. All schools were located in one German federal state, so generalizability to other educational systems and cultural settings remains to be established. The independent sample needed to validate the short form should therefore be drawn from a broader range of schools and regions. Fifth, most questionnaire-level missingness resulted from the planned booklet design, but incomplete item responses (approximately 1%) and attrition at T2 (27.4%) also occurred. Included and excluded children, as well as T2 respondents and children with T1 data only, differed only slightly in the variables we compared, but differences in variables we did not assess, such as test motivation, cannot be ruled out. The available-case analyses and sensitivity checks cannot eliminate potential bias due to such unplanned missingness (Schafer & Graham, 2002).

Finally, the original item provided only binary girl/boy response options and did not distinguish gender identity from sex assigned at birth. We therefore cannot separate interest patterns associated with gender identity from those associated with sex assigned at birth. Future research should examine how specific socialization experiences, instructional contexts, and opportunity structures contribute to the emergence, maintenance, and intensification of gendered interest profiles in both scholastic and vocational interests (Eccles & Wigfield, 2024; Master et al., 2025). Ideally, such studies should link interest development to concrete experiences in the family, peer group, and classroom.

4.4. Conclusion

The present study suggests that third graders' preferences for concrete activities can be assessed with an age-appropriate self-report instrument and that gender-typed profiles along the People-Things dimension are already visible in middle childhood. The 24-item version selected at T1 showed good circumplex fit for boys and girls in temporal cross-validation at T2. Nonetheless, this item selection and dimensional structure should be replicated in an independent sample. For educational psychology, these findings suggest that interest research in elementary school need not be limited to curricular domains. For vocational psychology, they indicate that circumplex-based interest models may be examined at younger ages than has typically been done, provided that the measures are validated for this age group.

Integrating these two perspectives may help clarify how early motivational orientations evolve through school-based and out-of-school experiences and how they accumulate into the consequential interest profiles observed in adolescence and adulthood. In this sense, education may either foster or constrain a gendered pathway of interest development.
