To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

description Environmental Exposures and Their Impact on Brain Aging and Health
Environmental exposures can cause acute and long-term impacts on human health (Shetty et al., 2023a). Exposure to concentrations of pollutants such as particulate matter less than 2.5 micrometres (PM2.5), particulate matter less than 10 micrometres (PM10), nitrogen oxides, carbon monoxide, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), and polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) can affect key cellular processes, leading to unfavourable health outcomes (Shetty et al., 2023b). A lack of exposure to greenspace and a greater exposure to urban factors (e.g., noise and heat) has also been continuously associated with poorer health outcomes (Cacciatore et al., 2025; Kondo et al., 2018).

The environmental exposome—one’s cumulative environmental exposure over a lifetime—has particular implications for the brain, and can trigger harmful neurobiological effects such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and cellular death (Aggarwal et al., 2022). Furthermore, recent evidence suggests environmental exposures are associated with neurological conditions that arise in older adults such as dementia and Parkinson’s disease (Killin et al., 2016; Ray Dorsey & Bloem, 2024). Older adult populations are especially vulnerable to the harmful effects of environmental exposures due to both the long-term accumulation of negative effects across the life course, and the physiological susceptibility that arises in later life (Risher et al., 2010). Thus, the investigation of the established unfavourable neural outcomes associated with adverse environmental exposures is important for all individuals across the life course, but is especially critical for older adult populations that are most vulnerable to such outcomes.

The Role of MRI in Evaluating Brain Health in the Context of the Exposome
Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a type of neuroimaging that captures various complex and informative measures of the brain, including functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), and quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM)—all of which reflect different neural properties including brain function, white matter microstructure, and tissue properties, respectively (Hagiwara et al., 2025). The extensive modes in which the brain can be measured allows for detailed analyses of brain changes that can be standardized across aging populations (Hagiwara et al., 2025). Oxidative stress from exposure to environmental toxins can lead to downstream cellular death and brain atrophy, and structural MRI measures of tissue properties can serve as an in vivo proxy for this type of damage (Cannon & Greenamyre, 2011). These neurobiological consequences can also manifest functionally, affecting the connectivity between brain regions (Aggarwal et al., 2022). A measure such as fMRI can be used to measure the correlations between active brain regions, which can in turn give researchers insight into how environmental exposures are associated with outcomes of brain function (Biswal & Uddin, 2025).

Rationale for Systematic Review
The population of older adults (≥ 65 years of age) is expected to reach 1.6 billion by the year 2050—more than double the 761 million in 2021 (United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs [UN DESA], 2023). Because of the growth of the older adult population and the unique neurobiological susceptibility of older adults to harmful environmental exposures, it is critical to establish a comprehensive understanding of these associations (Page et al., 2021; Risher et al., 2010). Literature has demonstrated associations with environmental exposures and brain aging (Lin et al., 2024). However, there is a need for a more rigorous synthesis of the relations between exposures and neuroimaging outcomes in cognitively healthy older adult samples. In fact, federal funding agencies such as the National Institutes of Health have published highlighted topics on “Neural Exposome Factors that Affect Brain Health and Neurological Disorders” (Neural Exposome Factors That Affect Brain Health and Neurological Disorders | Grants & Funding, 2025). Therefore, this systematic review is necessary to evaluate the growing number of research studies on these topics to determine trends in outcomes and study methodology, informing future directions of research in this discipline.

data_exclusion_criteria Participants
This review will include studies that enrolled human participants ≥ 65 years of age who are cognitively healthy (i.e., no current signs of cognitive impairment or dementia) to address the topic of healthy aging. The review will focus on human samples who are exposed to one or more environmental exposure, as well as those who underwent MRI brain imaging of some mode. Studies exclusively involving participants without healthy cognition will be excluded to ensure the observed effects correspond to the presence of environmental exposures.

Concept
The core concept of this review is the relation between adverse environmental exposures experienced by adults aged 65 and over and the neuroimaging outcomes associated with them. This includes both evaluating the exposures analysed and the neuroimaging technique employed. Studies that involve a range or variable number of exposure conditions are included to understand the main environmental exposures and the amounts of exposure that have the largest impact on brain imaging outcomes in healthy older adult populations.

Context
The context is defined by exposure to adverse environmental exposures including but not limited to PM2.5, PM10, nitrogen oxides, urbanicity, black carbon, carbon monoxide, persistent organic pollutants, heat, and volatile organic compounds. The review will focus on humans who are exposed to one or multiple environmental exposures No cultural or geographic restrictions exist for this review. This ensures the synthesis and representation of clinical and real-world settings. Studies will be excluded if they focus explicitly and exclusively on occupational exposures, such as factory lead poisoning.

Types of sources
This systematic review will consider all study designs, including randomized controlled trials, longitudinal, non-randomized controlled trials, before and after studies, and interrupted time-series studies. In addition, analytical observational studies including prospective and retrospective cohort studies, case-control studies, and analytical cross-sectional studies will be considered for inclusion. This review will also consider descriptive observational study designs including case series and descriptive cross-sectional studies for inclusion.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Air pollution and built environment features are increasingly associated with negative health outcomes, yet their relation to brain health in cognitively healthy older adults remains unclear.

Methods: To synthesise the extant literature, we conducted a systematic review, searching MEDLINE (PubMed) and Web of Science (Core Collection) from inception to February 23, 2026. Eligible studies assessed a natural or built environmental exposure against brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) measures in cognitively healthy older adults (ages 65+). Reviewers screened and extracted summary data from published reports in Covidence; no individual patient data were sought. The main outcome was brain health; findings were synthesised narratively using an effect-direction approach, as heterogeneous exposures, outcomes, and effect metrics precluded pooling.

Findings: Of 2,496 records screened, 27 studies met eligibility, comprising >84,000 older adults across 10 countries: United States, adults.

[div-01]

Specifically, we sought to determine which brain structures and functions are most associated with environmental exposures, which exposures exert the greatest apparent impact on imaging outcomes, and how findings vary across study design, exposure window, and methodological quality-thereby mapping the current evidence base and identifying priorities to guide future research and equitable brain health policy for an ageing world.

Methods

We developed and registered a protocol for this systematic review with the Open Science Framework (ID# 10.17605/OSF.IO/T453R) and did not make any changes to the published protocol. We used Covidence software to manage this review (http://www.covidence.org/) and report our review in accordance with PRISMA guidelines.

Search strategy and selection criteria

Searches were designed, validated, and conducted by the authorship team. The original search strategy was developed in MEDLINE (PubMed) using the following concepts: neuroimaging, ageing, and environmental exposures. Once the authorship team reviewed and approved the search strategy, it was translated into search syntax for Web of Science (Core Collection). We implemented the search strategy on February 23, 2026, and aimed to locate published peer-reviewed studies. The text words contained in the titles and abstracts of relevant articles, and the index terms used to describe the articles, were used to develop a full search strategy for each database searched (Appendix 2, pages 5-6).

After the full-text review stage, we screened the reference lists of all included studies and relevant reviews for additional eligible studies, which were assessed against our inclusion criteria. Studies published in any language were included if they had English-language translated versions available. Following the search, all identified citations were collated and uploaded into Covidence, with duplicates removed. We screened articles in two phases. Following a pilot test, titles and abstracts were screened by two independent reviewers for assessment against predefined inclusion criteria. We resolved conflicts by discussion. If an article was found to be relevant, it was moved to full-text review and further assessed in detail against inclusion criteria (again by two independent reviewers). We recorded reasons for exclusion of sources of evidence at full text that did not meet inclusion criteria.

Studies were included if they met three main criteria. (1) The study target population must include adult participants who were 65 years of age or older and cognitively healthy at the time of the study (i.e., no signs of cognitive impairment or dementia based on screening tools or formal clinical evaluations). Our registered protocol specified that studies must include adults ≥65 years of age in the sample; as long as this criterion was met, we did not restrict the minimum or maximum age range of the study. This means that our review retained cohorts whose samples comprised cognitively healthy older adults, but whose age ranges extended into midlife (study mean or median age generally ≥56 years). Adopting this criterion is important because much of the ageing neuroimaging literature, which were large population and longitudinal cohorts, enrolled participants from midlife onward, and excluding these studies would have omitted valuable data on brain ageing. (2) The study must include data about one or more physical environmental exposure such as particulate matter (e.g., PM2.5), nitrogen oxides (e.g., NO2, and ozone), greenspace, bluespace, environmental noise, and extreme heat. (3) Finally, the study must report an MRI brain imaging modality postexposure as the outcome measure. Studies were excluded if they restricted the study sample to people with cognitive impairment or dementia; focused solely on occupational exposures; or were commentaries and other opinion pieces, editorials, reports, dissertations, conference abstracts, review articles, or grey literature.

Data analysis

One independent reviewer extracted data from each included study, conducting a pilot extraction (i.e., "training" stage) and reliability verification. A second independent reviewer conducted quality assurance and quality control of 20% of all extracted studies. We extracted data from each included study that comprised fields such as details of the study sample, environmental exposures measured, their concentration and duration exposed (if any), and neuroimaging outcomes, among other fields. Data fields collected are noted in Appendix 4 (pages 9-16).

We did not conduct any data conversions or other methods to prepare data for synthesis, including a meta-analysis. Robust quantitative pooling requires a cluster of independent studies assessing the same exposure against the same outcome using effect estimates convertible to a common metric-conditions this evidence base did not meet. Although PM2.5 was the most frequently examined exposure (14 studies), the associated neuroimaging outcomes were widely dispersed-spanning medial temporal lobe (MTL), whole brain, white matter, and locus coeruleus volumes, cortical thickness, white matter hyperintensities, and white matter microstructure-such that no single exposure-outcome pairing was reported by more than two studies; the remaining exposures (e.g., PM10, NO2, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, greenspace, and noise) were each examined in only one or two studies. Effect measures were also not commensurable across studies, having been reported as standardised and unstandardised beta coefficients, odds ratios, absolute volumetric differences (cm 3 and mm 3 ), cortical thickness (µm), voxel-based morphometry cluster sizes, correlation coefficients, and per-interquartile-range increments defined by study-specific interquartile ranges. Given this heterogeneity in both exposure-outcome pairings and effect metrics, quantitative synthesis was not feasible, and we instead synthesised findings narratively using an effect-direction approach. For the effect-direction plot, each study contributed a single representative association. Because studies did not uniformly designate a primary exposure or outcome, the review team selected for each study the association corresponding to its main exposure of interest and its primary brain structure finding. Where a study examined multiple exposures or reported outcomes in differing directions, we coded the predominant direction of association across the reported brain structure outcomes and noted mixed findings. This selection was made during synthesis and applied consistently across studies; all study-level associations are reported in Tables 1 2 3.

Risk of bias

We employed the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklists appropriate to each study design. Each checklist was composed of design-specific questions that ranged from eight to nine items rated as Yes, No, Unclear, or Not applicable. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion. No studies were excluded solely based on methodological quality. Methodological quality was assessed using an adapted version of the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, modified by Shan et al,

Stephan et al, and Sánchez-Sánchez et al (Appendix 6 pages 12-13).

The quality of each included study was independently assessed by BMW and a second reviewer (KEP or JLM). The maximum score was seven stars for cross-sectional and nine for longitudinal studies. Studies scoring 1-3 stars were classified as low quality, 4-6 stars as moderate quality, and 7 stars or more as high quality. Item-level definitions and the thresholds applied to each domain of the adapted Newcastle-Ottawa Scale are provided in Appendix 6 (pages 20-21). We report domain-level ratings alongside overall classifications. We did not perform a formal certainty-of-evidence assessment (e.g., GRADE), because the heterogeneity of exposures, outcomes, and effect metrics precluded the pooled estimates such approaches presuppose; this uncertainty is reflected in our cautious interpretation of study results.

Excluding these two large cohorts and a study with 23 participants, 39 most studies (23 of 26) analysed between approximately 200 and 4,000 participants, with a median analytic sample of 854 participants (IQR 400-1,375). Baseline ages spanned approximately 40 to 96 years across the included studies. Half the studies (n=13) restricted enrolment to older adults aged 65 years or older; four enrolled adults from age 60; one enrolled adults aged 50 and older; and a smaller group of studies enrolled middle-aged adults from ages 40 or 45 onward; ages 40-69; ages 40-75; or ages 45-96. Repeated MRI measurements were uncommon and only acquired by five studies, with intervals of approximately 3 to 6 years (Table 1), while the remaining 21 studies assessed neuroimaging at a single timepoint (Tables 2 and 3).

Brain regions analysed using any MRI modality primarily included cortex/lobar areas, followed by white matter, hippocampus and MTL, and basal ganglia/subcortical regions (Figure 2). T1-weighted structural imaging was used in all studies, primarily to derive volumetric measures. T2-weighted and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences were also common (used in 11 studies), employed primarily to quantify white matter hyperintensities. Two studies used diffusion MRI to characterise white matter microstructure-diffusion-weighted imaging and diffusion tensor imaging (fractional anisotropy). Only one study performed resting-state functional MRI, indexed by the fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations. Magnet strength was predominantly 3T or 1.5T; the WHIMS analyses used 1.5T scanners across 14 sites, The MRI outcomes included whole-brain volume; grey and white matter volume; white matter hyperintensities; cortical thickness and surface area; hippocampal and MTL volumes (including amygdala, parahippocampal, and entorhinal regions); subcortical and basal ganglia structures (thalamus, putamen, globus pallidus, nucleus accumbens, caudate); ventricular and cerebrospinal fluid volume; and region-specific targets such as the locus coeruleus, anterior cingulate cortex, insula, and medial orbitofrontal cortex. Additionally, one study assessed covert brain infarcts. Several studies analysed composite measures related to Alzheimer's disease (AD), such as the AD pattern similarity score in Younan (2020) and AD cortical signature thickness in Crous-Bou (2020) and Lee (2020).

Most studies confirmed cognitive health and the absence of dementia by screening instruments or comprehensive neuropsychological batteries.

Screening tools included the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), the Modified Mini-Mental State examination (3MS/3MSE), the Korean MMSE, and the Clinical Dementia Rating. More extensive batteries were used by Puckett (e.g., memory, attention, executive function, language, processing speed, and visuospatial ability), Soeterboek (e.g., Verbal Learning Test, Stroop Colour-Word Test, Concept Shifting Test, and Letter-Digit Substitution Test), Lefèvre-Arbogast (e.g., Benton Visual Retention Test, Trail Making Test Part B, and Isaacs' Set Test), Crous-Bou (e.g., semantic fluency, WAIS-IV, and the Memory Binding Test), Cho (e.g., Seoul Neuropsychological Screening Battery), and Lee (e.g., CERAD-K battery). Beyond neuroimaging and cognitive assessment, additional measures included exposure biomarkers (urinary PAH metabolites in Cho (2020); serum PCBs in Lefèvre-Arbogast (2025), APOE ε4 genotyping, the erythrocyte omega-3 index, depressive symptoms assessed with the 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale, and subjective ratings of neighbourhood environment.

Across these cognitively healthy samples, higher exposure to ambient air pollution -most commonly PM2.5 and to a lesser extent NO2 and PM10-was associated with adverse brain structure (Figure 3). Reported associations included smaller total, grey matter, and white matter volumes, greater white matter hyperintensity burden, cortical thinning, atrophy of the MTL and hippocampus, reduced volumes in AD-vulnerable and subcortical regions, and altered white matter microstructure. These patterns were broadly concordant across independent cohorts spanning North America, Europe, and East Asia.

Beyond PM2.5, however, findings were not uniformly negative: PM10 was not associated with white matter hyperintensities or cortical thickness, persistent organic pollutants showed no association with medial temporal lobe atrophy, and residential noise was largely null except for white matter and cerebrospinal fluid volume. Similarly, residential proximity to major roadways demonstrated no clear pattern, with greater distance from a major road counterintuitively associated with higher white matter hyperintensity volume and no consistent association across other measures. Regarding risk of bias (Appendix 6, pages 18-19), all 27 studies showed generally low risk of bias, meeting Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal criteria, though ratings were not uniform across domains. On the Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal, most studies met the majority of criteria, but domain specific "No" and "Unclear" ratings were common, particularly for MRI (outcome) assessment and in longitudinal studies for follow-up adequacy. The five longitudinal studies were of moderate quality in exposure ascertainment and statistical analysis, with risk of bias in outcome assessment and in establishing temporal precedence of the exposure.

The nine observational cohort studies showed consistently low risk of bias, except for variability in MRI outcome assessment.

Similarly, the 13 cross-sectional studies showed low risk of bias, with minor concerns in outcome assessment and participant selection. Additionally, we assessed methodological quality using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, adapted for cross-sectional and longitudinal studies (Appendix 7, pages 20-21).

13 studies met high-quality criteria: three of them longitudinal, two of them observational cohort, and eight of them cross-sectional. The remaining 13 studies met moderate-quality criteria. Notably, four of the five longitudinal studies did not meet criteria for adequacy of cohort follow-up and quality of outcome assessment varied across all three study types, with 12 of the 27 studies not meeting this criterion . This uniformity in overall judgements may partly reflect the substantial overlap in samples across studies.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesises emerging observational evidence that greater exposure to adverse physical environmental conditions is associated with differences inf brain structure in cognitively healthy ageing. The recurrent involvement of the MTL, hippocampus, and white matter-regions vulnerable to AD-related pathology and vascular burden-is consistent with the hypothesis that air pollution (and fine particulate matter, in particular) may act along pathways that increase the risk of pathological aging processes. Greenspace was more often associated with favourable brain structure though this evidence is weaker and largely cross-sectional. Finding that these associations were present even among older adults with healthy cognition suggests that environmental exposures may alter the neurobiological processes underlying normal ageing or contribute to subclinical structural brain changes that precede overt cognitive impairment.

In general, the studies synthesised here had limited variability in the types of environmental exposures examined, with the majority focusing on different types of toxic air pollutants followed by access to greenspace. Exposure to extreme heat was not examined in any of the present studies, even though disrupted thermoregulation can trigger negative effects on cerebrovascular health, and in turn, affect neurocognitive ageing. Source-specific disaggregation of fine particulate matter may also be informative, as PM2.5 concentrations from wildfire smoke, agriculture, and traffic, for example, have different chemical characteristics that may uniquely impact brain structure and function among older adults. Previous work has already shown that these sources uniquely contribute to risk of dementia in the U.S. compared to India, but the underlying neural correlates of PM2.5 concentrations in healthy ageing have yet to be assessed beyond the studies synthesised in the current systematic review.

Determining whether the current body of literature generalises to low-and middleincome countries will also be an important future direction as extant studies have overrepresented high-income countries, such as those in North America, Europe, and East Asia. Not only did participants recruited from these countries have higher socioeconomic status for their respective countries, but there was also a greater representation of female, relative to male, older adults (primarily from the WHIMS cohort). Beyond this imbalance, evidence suggests environmental exposures may relate to brain ageing differently in females and males-air and water quality more strongly in females, and heat and neighbourhood disadvantage in males -yet because only two of the 27 studies reported sex-stratified outcomes, these differences remain largely unresolved. Moreover, no included study reported outcomes stratified by race or ethnicity, despite well-documented disparities in dementia burden across racial and ethnic groups.

Replication in more diverse cohorts is desired, ideally with environmental exposures measured over longer windows of time. No study to date has looked at cumulative effects beyond 10 years. The most common exposure window was an interval of three years, used in six of the 27 studies, whereas eight studies assessed exposure at a single cross-sectional timepoint and the remaining 12 used windows of one to five years. Simultaneously, longitudinal assessment of both brain health and exposure will be invaluable for identifying inflection points when the brain becomes most susceptible to certain levels and types of exposure. Short, recent exposure windows also overlook susceptibility shaped across the entire life course, including sensitive developmental periods. Prenatal exposure to elevated maternal inflammation, for instance, has been linked to lower hippocampal neurite density decades later in midlife, and exposome frameworks emphasise vulnerable age windows from the prenatal period onward.

With respect to the neuroimaging outcomes examined, all studies but one were limited to structural MRI measures (i.e., regional and total volumes, cortical thickness, white matter hyperintensities) and did not assess relations between environmental exposures and fMRI measures of brain function. Studying different aspects of brain health in future studies alongside multiple environmental factors in the same sample is important, because recent work (published after the current search period ended) suggests that physical environmental exposures accelerated structural brain ageing whereas social environmental exposures accelerated functional brain ageing. In general, macroscopic measurements of volumetry and cortical thickness can encompass numerous neurobiological properties, such as decreases in cell density, neuroinflammation, and shrinking of nuclei. Using MRI techniques that are more sensitive to specific neural processes, such as the microstructure of grey and white matter tissue or brain iron content, are necessary to identify specify biomarkers that can be targeted in future neural or pharmacological intervention work.

The behavioural relevance of environment-related alterations in brain health will benefit from future investigations that directly assess how the alterations help explain age-related cognitive decline, especially those that acquire fMRI measures of brain function while participants perform cognitive tasks. Here, relations with cognitive outcomes were rarely assessed, and few studies specifically measured memory 12 13,5 7 5 8 5 2 31,32,34,35,37,5 5 28,30,42,45,48,49,53,5 4 5 9 7 5 3 1 3 6 0 6 1 performance and speed of processing-despite these domains showing the most substantial decline among healthy older adults.

Physiological health behaviours (e.g., sleep health, physical activity, sedentary time) and lifestyle (e.g., tobacco usage, alcohol consumption, dietary intake) have also received little attention in the current literature. Examining these variables is vital as they may help mitigate or exacerbate the negative impacts of adverse environmental exposures on brain health in older adults and act as helpful intervention targets. Beyond individual risk, these findings may have implications for promoting cognitive resilience and informing public policy. Because the environmental exposures examined here appear to act at the neurobiological level-producing differences in brain structure detectable on MRI-improving these conditions may strengthen the biological substrate of cognitive resilience rather than simply mitigating downstream risk. Plausible mechanisms include attenuation of chronic neuroinflammation and related vascular and oxidative pathways, through which reduced air pollution and greater greenspace could help preserve brain regions vulnerable to age-related and neurodegenerative change. Framed this way, environmental improvement could be one lever at the population level: measures such as lowering ambient pollution and expanding neighbourhood greenspace may complement individual-level strategies and shift emphasis toward community-and policy-level action. In the longer term, exposome-informed approaches that target upstream determinants such as air quality and social inequality could inform policy, reframing brain health protection as a shared responsibility of individuals and governments.

Because only five of the 27 studies acquired repeated MRI-the remaining 21 assessing brain structure at a single timepoint-this review is limited in the ability to make inferences about temporal sequence and within-person change. Exposures were unevenly distributed across study designs: evidence for PM2.5 drew substantially on observational and longitudinal cohorts (11 of 15 studies), whereas greenspace was examined almost exclusively in cross-sectional designs (7 of 8 studies). Two of these greenspace studies reported null associations, and greenspace exposure is strongly correlated with neighbourhood socioeconomic status, so residual confounding by socioeconomic and related factors is a particular concern; thus, greenspace findings should be interpreted with greater caution. Furthermore, effect measures and outcome definitions were highly heterogeneous, precluding quantitative synthesis; and a substantial share of the supportive evidence derives from shared samples-most notably the six WHIMS analyses and the UK Biobank-based studies-so concordant results should not be interpreted as fully independent replication. Importantly, however, the PM2.5 brain volume association was also observed in the Framingham Offspring Study, an independent cohort outside the WHIMS and UK Biobank samples, as well as the other smaller independent samples noted in our review. Because almost all included studies were cross-sectional or measured MRI at a single timepoint, associations should not be interpreted as causal effects, and the cross-sectional volume differences we summarise should not be equated with within-person atrophy or neurodegeneration. More precise characterization of the underlying tissue alterations will benefit from studies using quantitative MRI approaches, such as diffusion MRI or quantitative susceptibility mapping of brain iron and myelin content. Residual confounding and exposure misclassification are further important limitations. The 6 2 2 0 3 2 2 1 3 3 8 6 1 6 3 included studies adjusted for heterogeneous covariate sets: most accounted for age and sex, education or income, and some for smoking, alcohol use, physical activity, APOE ε4 status, or vascular risk factors, but no covariate was adjusted for consistently across all studies (per-study covariate adjustment is summarised in Appendix 9).

This heterogeneity is especially consequential for greenspace and air pollution associations, which are strongly patterned by neighbourhood socioeconomic position and may mutually confound one another. In addition, exposure was almost always assigned from a single residential address, typically without accounting for residential mobility, time spent away from home, or indoor-outdoor differences in exposure, any of which can bias estimates. These factors reinforce an association-based, rather than causal, interpretation of the synthesised evidence.

Conclusion

This review points to a consistent, though methodologically constrained, association between air pollution and adverse brain structure, and more provisionally between greenspace and more favourable brain structure, during healthy cognitive ageing. These conclusions stem from an evidence base that was predominantly cross-sectional or otherwise single timepoint in design, heterogeneous in its outcomes, and drawn from a small number of overlapping cohorts-features that restrict causal inference. Confirming these relations and clarifying their implications for cognitive decline and dementia risk will require longitudinal, multi-exposure studies in independent, diverse populations. Combined with such evidence, the findings synthesised here could inform practical, upstream strategies for clinicians and policymakers seeking to protect brain health across an ageing global population by attenuating exposure to adverse environmental conditions.
