What we found

We found 22 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Nature connectedness (NC) refers to an individual's subjective sense of connection with nature and has a well-established link to overall health and well-being. NC shows potential for being incorporated into future climate anxiety (CA) management interventions. However, no study has reviewed the literature investigating the association between NC and CA. Therefore, we conducted a systematic literature review to: 1) synthesise the empirical research and assess the evidence for associations between NC and CA using meta-analytic methods; and 2) identify factors that may influence this relationship, and the nature of their influence. Following a systematic search of eight electronic databases from inception to April 2026, fifty studies met inclusion criteria. Substantial heterogeneity was identified in NC and CA operationalisations. We conducted random-effects meta-analyses using restricted maximum likelihood estimation (REML), using correlation coefficients (r) as the effect size metric and pooled across studies using inverse-variance weighting. Meta-analysis indicated a significant positive moderate mean association between NC and CA. NC and CA measurement instruments/items were selected as categorical moderators. Subgroup analyses revealed 15 CA instruments significantly influenced the relationship between NC and CA. Based on our findings we recommend: (a) the development and refinement of CA instruments and their conceptual alignment, (b) more diverse population contexts in this research area, and (c) the development of a clear theoretical framework for defining and conceptualising NC and its relationship with CA. This review was registered on the PROSPERO International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (Registration ID = CRD420251063755).

1. Introduction

Anthropogenic climate change has negative consequences for planetary and human health (Balunde et al., 2019; Cahill et al., 2012; Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change [IPCC], 2018; Mouro et al., 2021). Attention has been given to the adverse impacts' climate change has on people's overall mental well-being. The threat and experience of climate change is contributing to climate anxiety (CA), which has been found to be largely associated with poorer mental health outcomes, such as symptoms of anxiety and depression (Cosh et al., 2024). Nature connectedness (NC) has a well-established protective effect on mental health and overall well-being and may be relevant for managing the effects CA has on psychological health (Barragan-Jason et al., 2023; Pitt et al., 2024). However, much of the existing literature has found NC is associated with increased CA, suggesting those who are more connected to nature are more likely to experience heightened anxiety about climate change (e.g., Cameron & Kagee, 2025; Grénman et al., 2025; Jalin et al., 2024; Whitmarsh et al., 2022). This systematic literature review and meta-analysis assesses whether the positive relationship between NC and CA is consistently present, and examines whether it is moderated by measurement instrument for NC and CA.

1.1 Climate anxiety

CA can be considered as a continuum of heightened emotional, thought-based and behavioural responses (e.g., catastrophising, difficulties concentrating, engaging in work and study, socialising, and sleeping) to the consequences of anthropogenic climate change (Clayton et al., 2017; Kessler et al., 2008; North et al., 2004). In the literature, constructs that are related to, and often used interchangeably with CA, include eco-anxiety (e.g., Coffey et al., 2021), environmental concern (e.g., Helm et al., 2018), and climate worry (e.g., Stewart, 2021). Notably, the way CA is measured is not only highly variable, but the different measures used often vary in scope; either targeting aspects of CA directly (e.g., Climate Change Anxiety Scale; Clayton & Karazsia, 2020), or some broader ecological concern (e.g., Environmental Concern Scale; Schultz, 2001).

CA occurs across the lifespan and diverse populations, although the literature indicates high prevalence rates among younger populations, due to the increased likelihood of climate impacts occurring in their future (Crandon et al., 2022). A global survey of 10,000 children and young people (aged 16 -25) across 10 countries found 59% of respondents were very or extremely worried about climate change, and 84% were at least moderately worried (Hickman et al., 2021).

It is important to emphasise that feeling anxious about climate change is not inherently disordered. CA is a rational response to a genuine threat, and it does not, in itself, indicate an individual-level pathology or dysfunction (Bhullar et al., 2022). Some scholars have even argued that a lack of concern may be more maladaptive, given the severity of the current climate crisis (Dodds, 2021). Additionally, recent research suggests that moderate levels of CA can lead to adaptive behaviours such as pro-environmental behaviours (PEBs; Balaskas, 2024; Becht et al., 2024), as climate issues motivate personal accountability and urgency towards acting to address CA (Reese et al., 2023; Zeier & Wessa, 2024).

Although CA is not something to be pathologised, a recent systematic review has shown that greater CA is consistently associated with poorer mental health outcomes such as depression, anxiety, psychological distress and symptoms of stress (Cosh et al., 2024).

Therefore, CA can be understood as a logical and reasonable experience that places strain on mental health. Thus, effective strategies are needed to successfully manage CA's risk to mental well-being.

1.2 Climate anxiety management

Currently, there is no well-established evidence-based intervention for managing CA.

Approaches currently being used in practice utilise problem-focused and emotion-focused strategies such as encouraging activism (Budziszewska & Jonsson, 2021; Clayton & Parnes, 2025; Hickman, 2020; Sanson et al., 2019) and emotion management (Doherty & Clayton, 2011; Verlie et al., 2021). Interventions that encourage activism (Budziszewska & Jonsson, 2021; Clayton & Parnes, 2025; Hickman, 2020), volunteering (Sanson et al., 2019), and collective action (Ojala, 2013; Pihkala, 2020) have been shown to improve individual and environmental outcomes in climate-anxious individuals. There is also evidence suggesting that emotion-focused strategies can be effective in reducing individual CA (Fraser et al., 2013; Verlie et al., 2021). Although the efficacy of these strategies has yet to be examined, healthcare professionals and academics are concerned there may be unintentional side effects of current strategies (Bingley et al., 2022; Pitt et al., 2024). Bingley et al.'s (2022) systematic review of CA interventions found that interventions that are centred only on individual outcomes (e.g., reducing climate concern and promoting individual well-being) are unlikely to address the complexity of CA. Bingley and colleagues (2022) called for a multiple needs' framework, suggesting the effectiveness of CA interventions should be determined based on individual, social and environmental well-being outcomes. For example, an intervention that promoted engagement in PEB and conservation activities could serve to protect both individual well-being, including the management of CA, as well as planetary health (Kerse & Kerse, 2025; Ogunbode et al., 2022; Reese et al., 2023; Zeier & Wessa, 2024). Such recommendations regarding engaging with nature and restoring nature as part of a well-being strategy prompt consideration of the NC literature.

1.3 Nature connectedness

For the purposes of this review, NC will be defined as an individual's experiences of or connections with nature, and these experiences may be physical, experiential, cognitive, and emotional, and includes being one with or close to nature (Barrable et al., 2021; Ives et al., 2018; Mayer & Frantz, 2004). NC conceptualisations vary widely within the extant literature due to the multiple disciplines that reference it including psychology, sociology, ecological sciences and public health; therefore, our definition captures common characteristics of being connected with nature (Tiscareno-Osorno et al., 2023; Zylstra et al., 2014).

In the literature, NC is used interchangeably with several terms, including nature connection, connection to nature, and nature relatedness (e.g., Brambilla et al., 2024; Lumber et al., 2017; Nisbet et al., 2009). While theoretical distinctions are sometimes finely drawn between these different terms, they are not always treated as separate constructs in the literature (Tam, 2013). Like CA, NC is measured in many ways (e.g., Nature Relatedness Scale [NRS]; Nisbet et al., 2009; Inclusion of Nature in Self Measure [INS]; Schultz, 2002).

1.4 Climate anxiety and nature connectedness

An international interdisciplinary study that surveyed 230 participants who selfidentified as having a professional interest in CA (e.g., mental health practitioners, climate activists, academics, scientists), found 90% of participants supported the inclusion of NC in future strategies to manage CA (Pitt et al., 2024). However, the existing literature indicates that NC cannot be treated as a simple panacea for CA. Many high-quality studies indicate a positive association between NC and CA (e.g., Cameron & Kagee, 2025; Grénman et al., 2025; Jalin et al., 2024; Whitmarsh et al., 2022), presumably because greater connection to nature also means greater concern for nature. Additionally, research has indicated that NC can indirectly affect mental health via CA; Curll et al.'s (2022) Australian study found that NC was positively associated with psychological distress through climate worry. This has since been replicated in a Canadian context, which also found NC to indirectly increase mental illhealth via CA (Thomson & Roach, 2023). Taken together, this literature indicates that greater NC co-occurs with poorer mental health outcomes via increased CA.

However, other work suggests the relationship between NC and CA is more complex.

Academics have begun to conceptualise NC within a resilience framework, identifying it as a complex psychosocial determinant of mental health (Liu et al., 2016; Wells, 2021), due to NC's well-established link to better physical, mental, and social health outcomes. NC and nature exposure is associated with fewer symptoms of poor mental health and decreased physiological arousal (e.g., Bakir-Demir et al., 2021; Bezold et al., 2018; Cai & Zhan, 2025).

Connection with nature is also linked to increased eudaimonic and overall well-being, happiness, life satisfaction, and quality of life (Capaldi et al., 2014; Pritchard et al., 2020; Wu & Jones, 2022). Thus, although NC may increase CA, it is also beneficial for overall wellbeing. Notably, other research highlights that the NC-well-being relationship is one with multiple mediators, and these likely also have implications for CA. For example, NC facilitates social connections, which is in turn associated with increased psychological resilience, well-being, and prosocial behaviours (Henderson et al., 2024; Ly & Vella-Brodrick, 2024; Sitnik-Warchulska et al., 2025). Some research has found social support to be associated with increased eco-anxiety (Nieminen et al., 2025), while others find loneliness and social isolation to be associated with greater CA (Hajek & König, 2022). Additionally, the literature indicates a consistent positive association between NC and PEBs (Barragan-Jason et al., 2022; Guazzini et al., 2025; Whitburn et al., 2020), while also indicating PEBs can serve as an effective coping strategy for individuals with climate-related distress (Kerse & Kerse, 2025). This complexity highlights the need for further research into the NC-CAwell-being relationship.

Before NC can be included in future CA management interventions, the nature of the relationship between NC and CA needs to be untangled. While NC corresponds with higher levels of CA, it is also linked to several factors that may moderate the impact CA has on wellbeing through emotional, physiological, and social pathways. Therefore, further investigation is warranted, and the first step is to identify the robustness of the simple association between NC and CA. Despite the amount of research conducted involving NC and CA, as well as the significant variance in conceptualisation and measurement, no review examining their specific relationship with each other has been conducted. Therefore, a systematic literature review and meta-analysis was conducted to examine the association between NC and CA.

2. The Present Study

We aim to systematically synthesise the empirical research and assess the evidence for, and direction of, associations between NC and CA using meta-analytic methods. Previous research has indicated NC predicts CA (e.g., Cameron & Kagee, 2025; Grénman et al., 2025; Jalin et al., 2024; Whitmarsh et al., 2022), therefore we hypothesised NC will be positively associated with CA. Due to the complexity of this relationship, we aim to identify factors that may influence this and the nature of the effect. In addition to identifying any moderators in the literature, we also conducted moderator analyses to investigate how different operationalisations of NC and CA may influence this relationship across studies.

3. Method

The conduct and reporting of this systematic literature review is based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P; Moher et al., 2015) guidelines. This review was registered on PROSPERO (June, 2025; CRD420251063755) alongside a research protocol which was designed in accordance with the PRISMA-P guidelines and checklist (Moher et al., 2015).

3.1 Search Strategy

Search terms used included the various definitions of NC and CA, using key words and phrases with proximity operators and truncation (see Table 1). Information sources that were searched were Scopus, PubMed, APA PsycInfo, Web of Science Core Collection, Wiley Online Library, Cinahl, MedLine, and ProQuest. Databases were searched individually, and search syntaxes were appropriately modified for databases. Corresponding authors were contacted via email if additional information was required. The initial search was completed in June 2025, and an updated search was conducted in April 2026, using the same databases, search strategy and eligibility criteria as the original search to identify studies published since the last search date.

3.2 Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Due to the constraints of the research team, only English language studies were included in the search. Search dates were limited between 1990 and 2026; "ecopsychology" research was introduced in the 1990's (see Roszak et al., 1995), and has steadily grown, with most of the research about anxiety in relation to the climate taking place after 2007 (Pihkala, 2020). Studies that incorporated a quantitative measure that is used to report on the relationship between NC and CA were included. CA occurs across the lifespan and diverse populations and studies that investigated participants of any age, gender, or cultural and socioeconomic backgrounds were included. Samples across multiple settings, therefore community and/or clinical samples, were included. Any study design with quantitative measures of interest, including primary research peer-reviewed studies, longitudinal, crosssectional, case-control studies, cohort studies, randomized controlled trials, and quasiexperimental design studies were included. Studies for which sufficient information could not be obtained to be included in meta-analytic methods, but still met inclusion criteria, were included in this review, but excluded from the quantitative synthesis.

Non-English language studies and those outside the time frame (1990-2026) were excluded. Studies that did not report on the relationship between NC and CA using quantitative measures were also excluded. Additionally, studies that were non-peer reviewed, non-randomized intervention studies, literature reviews, meta-analyses, editorials or case reports were excluded.

3.3 Data extraction and screening

Our searches identified 3584 journal articles for uploading into Covidence. A total of 776 duplicates were automatically removed by Covidence, with an additional 40 duplicates manually removed. Title and abstract screening were conducted by the primary author (A.F.), full text review was conducted by two independent co-authors separately (A.F., L. O'B.). The primary author resolved any discrepancies during the full text review after consultation with other research team members. A total 50 articles met the inclusion criteria (see Figure 1).

A standardised data extraction table was utilised to manage any outcome prioritisation needs and blind data extraction protocols were implemented. The following information was extracted: general information (study ID, article title, authors, year of publication, journal, study objectives / main outcome variable, conclusions / general findings, location of study), participants (description, age, males (%), females (%), other (%), study design, recruitment and sampling -missing data etc), variables / interventions / outcomes (NC scale, CA scale, variables, nature exposure), data analysis (data management, statistical analysis), and results (outcome results, composite correlation scores, conclusions, notes).

3.4 Assessment of bias and robustness

As per PRISMA-P guideline (Moher et al., 2015) individual studies were assessed for risk of bias and quality by three co-authors (A.F., L. O'B., T.H.) with discrepancies resolved by the primary author (A.F.) after consultation with the research team. Included studies were assessed for bias and quality using a version of the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale (NOS; Wells et al., 2011) as it has been widely used in research for its established content validity, interobserver reliability, and it is adaptable to suit research context (Hootman et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2021). This review used the NOS-xs (Carra et al., 2025) which is adapted for cross-sectional correlational studies. Consistent with the original NOS, the NOSxs features a nine-star rating system which assesses six targeted items across three domains: study sample selection, assessment of exposure and outcomes, and confounding factors (Carra et al., 2025). This allows for low, medium, and high risk of bias to be determined. The three researchers independently assessed study quality, with any conflicts being resolved via discussion. Statistical analyses for assessing publication bias were not appropriate due to high heterogeneity, so publication bias was assessed via visual inspection of funnel plots. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using leave-one-out analyses and meta-regression including study risk of bias as a moderator to assess the robustness of findings. 3.5 Meta-analytic methods

Included articles demonstrated sufficient data compatibility, sharing similar outcome measurements, accessible correlational data (zero-order correlation coefficients; Pearson's r), and methodological quality. It was therefore deemed appropriate to conduct a meta-analysis of random effects to provide a summary estimate of the relationship between NC and CA. All meta-analytic calculations were conducted using R Statistical Software (v4.5.2; R Core Team 2025) and the package metafor (Viechtbauer, 2010). Random-effects procedures were followed using Hunter and Schmidt's (2015) guidelines to statistically integrate the data.

Several studies reported multiple effect sizes contributing to the same overall effect size estimate, which violated the non-dependency of effect sizes (Borenstein et al., 2021) and would threaten the validity of meta-analytic results (Scammacca et al., 2014). Statistical dependence was resolved following Hunter and Schmidt's (2015, p. 442) formula for creating composite scores, allowing studies to contribute a single independent effect size to the metaanalysis, where it was appropriate. For example, for studies that reported correlations for the separate subscales of the Hogg Eco-Anxiety Scale (affective symptoms, rumination, behavioural symptoms, anxiety about personal impact; Hogg et al., 2021) we computed a single composite correlation. Similarly, after collapsing scores within scales, studies that reported multiple correlations using more than one measurement of the quantitative variables of interest (e.g., NC measured by NRS and CNS), were used to create a composite correlation, in accordance with Hunter and Schmidt's (2015) guidelines, to represent the overall relationship. Due to the way we define NC in this meta-analysis, studies that examined physical time spent outdoors (e.g., time spent in nature), were calculated together with NC measures (e.g., CNS) to create composite correlations. A total of 16 new composite correlations were calculated; eleven new composites were created (across different measures), and five composites were calculated through collapsing subscales within measures (which we subsequently refer to as their overall scale name e.g., HEAS).

Sensitivity analysis assumptions were used for three studies (Clayton et al., 2013; Kenstler & Shelley, 2024; Smith et al., 2023) as no inter-item correlations were reported between subscales, which were required to calculate composite scores. Sensitivity analyses were conducted assuming r = 0, 0.3, 0.5. Each study reported a consistent low-to-moderate positive correlation across assumptions, indicating the conclusions are robust to plausible assumptions regarding the unknown inter-item correlations. The results for the assumption r = 0.3 between items were used for each calculation. A single study (Eichholtzer et al., 2024) investigated the effects of a nature relatedness intervention on various well-being outcomes in mixed method experimental design, however investigating intervention effects is outside the scope of this review. Therefore, a correlation consisting of all participants data preintervention was used to examine the baseline association between NC and CA as the control and intervention groups are conceptually equivalent.

Two studies (Duffy & Verges, 2010; Gkargkavouzi et al., 2019) were deemed inappropriate to create composite scores because subscales were negatively correlated with each other. Hunter and Schmidt (2015) generally assume positive manifold when forming composite scores and computing a composite score of negatively intercorrelated items would potentially create distortion of results. Instead, each subscale outcome was retained and modelled as dependent effect sizes. Therefore, a multilevel random-effects model was used to account for statistical dependence among estimates from the same sample.

Once statistical dependence was accounted for, a multilevel random-effects metaanalysis was conducted using restricted maximum likelihood estimation (REML).

Correlations coefficients (r) were utilised as the effect size metric and pooled across studies using inverse-variance weighting. Effect sizes were interpreted according to Cohen's (1988) conventions, with correlations of approximately .10, .30, and .50 representing small, moderate, and large effects, respectively. Statistical significance was calculated through twotailed tests with a significance level of .05. Pooled estimates were reported using 95% confidence intervals. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed and identified through Cochran's Q and I² statistic. Publication bias was assessed through visual inspection of funnel plots, as statistical tests were not appropriate due to substantial heterogeneity detected.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted, using a leave-one-out analysis, to assess the individual studies influence on pooled effect size estimates.

Categorial moderator analyses were conducted to assess the influence of individual study characteristics on the overall pooled effect size estimate. NC and CA measurement instruments/items were selected as categorical moderators, due to the significant variance in conceptualisation and operationalisation. A joint moderator model was conducted first, before separate analyses were conducted. Next, subgroup analyses were conducted to examine individual measurement instruments/items effects, using 95% confidence intervals and a significance alpha of .05. We additionally tested the moderating effects of risk of bias, another categorical moderator, on the overall pooled effect size estimate of the relationship between NC and CA.

Correlations extracted from the included studies were transformed to Fisher's z prior to analysis to stabilize sampling variances. All models were estimated using Fisher's z values.

Overall pooled effects and associated intervals were back-transformed Pearson's r for interpretation and presentation, whereas moderator coefficients were reported on the Fisher's z scale.

4.3 Overall association between NC and CA

A multilevel random-effects meta-analysis including 57 effect sizes and 40,356 participants across included studies, revealed a moderate significant mean association between NC and CA (r = .32, 95% CI [.26, .38], p < .001). Substantial residual heterogeneity was identified by Cochran's Q test (Q(df = 56) = 1451.20, p < .001) indicating substantial between-study variance ( = 0.05) and significant heterogeneity (I² = 92.20%). The 80% credibility interval ranged from approximately 0.05 to 0.53, indicating that most effects were positive. The 95% prediction interval ranged from r = -.10 to .63, indicating a significant amount of heterogeneity in true effects, suggesting the strength of the association depends on individual study characteristics (Hunter & Schmidt, 2015).

Sensitivity analyses were then conducted by removing a study that had an outlying effect size and had a large influence on subgroup estimates and was identified as being of low quality and high risk of bias. The removal of this study resulted in a minor reduction on the overall pooled effect size (r = .30, 95% CI [.25, .36], p <.001), representing a 6.25% reduction in the correlation coefficient and a 12.11% reduction in explained variance compared to the full dataset. The effect remained statistically significant and of similar magnitude, supporting the robustness of the NC-CA association.

4.4 Moderator analyses

Joint moderator analyses examining operationalisations of NC and CA measures explained a significant portion of heterogeneity (QM(df = 36) = 74.45, p <.001), however even after controlling for how NC and CA were measured, substantial between-study variance remained (QE(df = 20) = 186.34, p <.001;  = 0.02, I² = 85.53%). This suggests that while measurement difference contributed to heterogeneity, other study characteristics are likely responsible for a significant amount of the remaining variance. Risk of bias was also tested separately as a moderator as reported below in subsection 4.6. grouped into broad conceptual categories accounted for approximately 40.22% of the between-study variance, however substantial heterogeneity remained (QE(df = 42) = 668.93, p < .001; I² = 87.58%).

4.5 CA measurement instrument type as a moderator for the NC-CA relationship

Following on from grouping CA measures into broad conceptual categories, subgroup analysis was conducted to examine individual differences in CA scale variations in the whole sample (see Table 3 for all CA variations and Figure 2 for significant CA variations). CA as a moderator was categorised into 26 levels. Significant associations from subgroup analyses are discussed below and grouped according to measurement type. All significant associations were positive.

Climate Change Anxiety Scale.

A total of twelve studies included in our review used the Climate Change Anxiety Scale (CCAS; Clayton & Karazsia, 2020), or variations of it. Nine studies used the CCAS-13 (one study used the CCAS-13 across two different populations and therefore contributed two effect sizes to the meta-analysis), with pooled correlations approaching a moderate significant association (r = .29, 95% CI [.09, .48]). Another study used the CCAS-8 and showed a stronger significant association (r = .44, 95% CI [.08, .70]).

Two studies respectively utilised the CCAS-22 and an adapted CCAS-12, which found nonsignificant associations.

Single-item measures. Four studies used single items to measure CA; pooled correlations showed a small significant association (r = .28, 95% CI [.11, .43]).

Calculated CA composite scores. Six studies used multiple measures and/or items to measure CA, resulting in composites being calculated (See Table 3). Four composite measures indicated significant associations. A composite created using the CCAS-13, 3-items measuring climate-anxious appraisal, and 7-items measuring climate-anxious affect based on the State Trait Anxiety Inventory (Spielberger, 1983), showed a large significant association Calculated single-item CA composites. One study used multiple single items to measure CA resulting in a calculated composite score (Clayton et al., 2013), revealing a moderate significant association (r = .37, 95% [.01, .64]).

Inventory of Climate Emotions.

One study (contributed two effect sizes due to crosscultural study design) used the Inventory of Climate Emotions (ICE, 32-items; Marczak et al., 2023) with 4-items assigned to measure CA, finding a moderate significant association (r = .36, 95% [.00, .64]), however the lower confidence limit was extremely close to zero.

4.6 Risk of Bias as a Moderator

Informed by these results, a follow-up exploratory meta-regression including all studies was conducted using risk of bias as a moderator of the NC-CA relationship to examine whether the quality of the study contributed to heterogeneity or moderated effect sizes. The omnibus test of moderation was not significant (QM(df = 2) = 0.99, p = .609), indicating effect sizes did not differ significantly across risk of bias levels. Relative to studies rated as having low risk of bias, neither moderate-risk studies ( = -0.07, 95% [-.21, .08], p = .346) nor high-risk studies ( = -0.02, 95% [-0.22, 0.19], p = .875) demonstrated significantly different effect sizes. These findings suggest that the influence of the outlying study is unlikely to be attributable to study quality alone and instead may reflect unique characteristics relating to that study or effect size.

Next, we examined whether risk of bias influenced the moderating effect of CA instruments on the NC-CA relationship. The overall model was significant (QM(df = 27) = 53.62, p = .002). However, neither moderate nor high risk of bias significantly differed from the low-risk reference category, and the contrast between moderate and high-risk studies was also not significant (moderate risk of bias vs low risk of bias:  = -0.10, p = .252; high risk of bias vs low risk of bias:  = -0.23, p = .085; high risk of bias vs moderate risk of bias:  = -0.13, p = .257). Substantial heterogeneity remained (QE(df = 29) = 401.96, p < .001; I² =87.67).

Sensitivity analyses were conducted by the removal of the outlying effect size and high risk of bias study. In contrast to the main-effect model, risk of bias as a moderator of CA instruments on the NC-CA relationship was significantly influenced by the exclusion of this study (QM(df = 26) = 31.62, p = .206). The loss of significance following the removal of the influential high-risk study further suggests that risk of bias does not consistently explain the variation in the NC-CA relationship across studies and appeared to be largely driven by this single study.

4.7 Assessment of Publication Bias

Visual inspection of the funnel plot (see Supplementary materials) indicated effect sizes were generally symmetrically distributed around the estimated pooled effect size produced by the meta-analysis. Studies with smaller sample sizes show greater variability in effect sizes, however there was no clear pattern of asymmetry to suggest substantial publication bias was present. Although steps were taken to account for statistical dependence, these results should be interpreted with caution given the substantial heterogeneity detected and the inclusion of multiple effect sizes per study.

5. Discussion

We contribute to the extant literature with a systematic review and meta-analysis to determine the nature of, and the variability within, the relationship between NC and CA.

Meta-analysis revealed a positive moderate significant association between NC and CA.

Results suggested CA instrument type could moderate the NC-CA relationship, whereas NC instrument type, and individual study risk of bias did not.

The positive association between NC and CA identified in our meta-analysis, indicates that individuals who feel more connected to nature also tend to report elevated anxiety about climate change. This finding is conceptually consistent with previous research.

Forming a strong connection or bond with something often increases concern for its wellbeing; thus, greater connection to nature may lead to stronger feelings of worry and apprehension about climate change and its impacts (Kersten et al., 2022).

However, we wish to reaffirm that the positive association between NC and CA does not preclude the inclusion of NC in interventions designed to address CA. Research evidence identifies NC as an important psychosocial determinant of mental health, associated with greater overall well-being and resilience (Liu et al., 2016; Wells, 2021). finding high climate knowledge significantly strengthened the positive association between NC and CA. This suggests that those individuals with less climate knowledge may be more able to enjoy their connection to nature without the fear of global climate issues dampening their experience (Thomson & Roach, 2023). However, other research shows climate knowledge is associated with less CA, suggesting that when correctly tailored, education on climate issues may be protective for CA (Zacher & Rudolph, 2023). This highlights the need for further investigation of other variables related to the NC-CA relationship, as discussed below in subsection 5.2.

5.1 Influence of measurement instrument and risk of bias

Within our meta-analysis we examined whether NC and CA measurement-type, as well as risk of bias level, acted as moderators for the NC-CA relationship. Risk of bias and NC instrument did not significantly influence the relationship between NC and CA. Analyses using the whole sample indicated CA measurement type explained substantial heterogeneity in effect sizes, accounting for almost 40% of the between-study variance. However, it should be noted that sensitivity analyses demonstrated this moderation effect was not robust to the removal of a high risk of bias study with an outlying effect size. Therefore, while CA measures may contribute to the variability in the observed relationship between NC and CA, our findings should be interpreted with caution.

The focus of the CA instruments varied. Most incorporated affective components, many covered behavioural, cognitive-emotional, and functional impairment, and some emphasised personal experience, and value orientations (e.g., biospheric concerns). This review systematically prioritised identifying overall associations between NC and CA measures, using a standard quantitative approach of collapsing within and across scales.

Nonetheless, patterns in effect size and significance did emerge for different CA measures, suggesting a promising avenue for future research to qualitatively explore CA experiences in the context of NC. Measures that included action-oriented cognitive items within a climate anxiety framing (e.g., "I am willing to control my consumption to realise the sustainable development") showed the strongest NC-CA associations, indicating these aspects may be more strongly related to aspects of NC. In contrast, CA measures that were broader or diffused in approach, demonstrated weaker or non-significant associations (e.g., "I worry about climate change more than other people"). It is possible those instruments showing weaker or non-significant associations may be capturing aspects of CA that are less related to NC, as well as potential differences in measurement sensitivity, specificity and/or precision.

Our findings also suggest that conceptual alignment between NC and CA instruments can also influence the observed NC-CA relationship. For example, in our analyses the strongest significant association between NC and CA was found in a study by Chen et al. (2023), which used the Environmental Awareness Scale (EAS; Despotovic et al., 2021) and the Connectedness to Nature Scale (CNS; Mayer & Frantz, 2004). It is worth noting the EAS is not commonly used to measure CA, and the included three out of the four items do not seem to be closely related to CA definitions; however, this paper was included in this review as it met eligibility criteria; in their paper, the authors define environmental awareness as "individuals' apprehension/concern about the various facets of environmental problems" (Chen et al., 2023, p. 169). Additionally, the first item in the EAS is closely related to CA ("I am extremely worried about the world's environment") and sensitivity analyses were also conducted to assess its influence on results. However, the other three out of the four items in the EAS strongly reflect action-oriented cognition and awareness (e.g., "I am willing to control my consumption to realise the sustainable development" and "The balance of nature is very delicate and easily upset") and were similar to the aspects captured within the CNS, which conceptualises an individual's relationship with nature as affective, experiential and emotional.

In contrast to the EAS, the HEAS had the weakest significant associations with three different measures of NC (CNS, NRS, and EID) across five studies. These NC instruments focus on aspects of affective, experiential and emotional connections in the human-nature relationship. Although the HEAS incorporates aspects of cognitive awareness and affective experiences including in relation to one's impact on the planet, it is primarily focused on affective arousal, persistent thought patterns, and behavioural impacts, rather than aspects of emotional and experiential connections to nature, and engagement within the climate change context. This highlights the importance of considering the best operationalisation of concepts and alignment of measures when designing studies to answer specific research questions.

Relatedly, the CA instruments identified in our review had a negative cognitive, emotional and/or experiential orientation. NC measurement instruments identified in this review are all positively oriented, aligning it with the way well-being is often measured, but not CA. Gago et al. (2024) found the orientation of the well-being instruments identified in their meta-analysis significantly moderated the relationship between CA and well-being, finding correlations to be stronger for negatively oriented instruments compared to those that were positively oriented. This underscores the need to consider the orientation of concepts/measures that best suits researchers' aims.

5.2 Limitations and directions for future research

Regarding the studies included in this review, there were several limitations identified across the literature which should be addressed in future research. Firstly, all the studies This meta-analysis showed that NC was associated with CA. Other meta-analyses have variously shown that CA is associated with worse well-being (Cosh et al., 2024) and that NC is associated with better well-being (Barragan-Jason et al., 2023; Bettman et al., 2025; Capaldi et al., 2014). It remains to be seen the extent to which CA may ameliorate the positive NC-wellbeing relationship, and how key factors like climate knowledge, social connection and PEB may add a moderating resilience to the stress of experiencing CA.

Recent literature suggests this will be a fruitful area of research. For example, multiple studies show that both NC and CA are related to increased PEB and climate action (Ogunbode et al., 2022; Tam et al., 2023; Whitmarsh et al., 2022). Recent meta-analyses indicate a consistent significant positive association between PEB and subjective well-being (Krumm, 2024; Zawadzki et al., 2020). PEBs can serve as an effective coping strategy for individuals with CA, helping to initiate urgency towards acting to address the environmental crisis which increases self-efficacy and alleviates feelings of helplessness in the face of climate change and poor mental health outcomes (Innocenti et al., 2023; Kerse & Kerse, 2025; Schwartz et al., 2023). Additionally, behaviours such as engagement in collective environmental activism can significantly attenuate the association between CA and psychological distress, such as depressive symptoms, and improve well-being (Becht et al., 2024; Kerse & Kerse, 2025; Schwartz et al., 2022).

In terms of measure refinement, based on their apparent sensitivity for detecting significant associations in the current analysis, future research may benefit from specific validation work to identify a single-item measure of CA that has optimal utility in research examining relationships with NC and well-being. Single-item measures are pragmatic and 57 cost-effective, and in our review, all single-item CA measures were significantly associated with NC, perhaps due to their ability to be easily adapted to distinct populations, good internal reliability, and licence for participants to effectively summarise their experiences (Allen et al., 2022; Zhang, 2020). However, this is not to suggest that a single-item measure approach to CA should always be utilised in research. As discussed, a nuanced understanding of the NC-CA relationship will require consideration of how the complex structures of NC and CA relate both to each other and to modifying factors.

A final limitation is that several studies in this review contributed multiple effect sizes in the meta-analyses. Hunter and Schmidt's (2015, p.442) formula for creating composite scores generally assume positive manifold when calculating composite scores. Creating a composite score with negative inter-item correlations would potentially distort results. While appropriate measures were taken to account for statistical dependence (utilising a multilevel random-effects model), the nesting of multiple results within studies may have increased the risk of biased estimates, and reduced generalisability and variance.

6. Conclusion

This review provides insights into the relationship between NC and CA, as well as the diverse ways in which these constructs are measured. Overall, our findings indicate a positive association between NC and CA. However, this relationship does not preclude the inclusion of NC in future health interventions, particularly where CA poses a risk for mental health.

Rather, our findings provide a foundation for future research to investigate variables that may mediate and/or moderate relationships between NC, CA and wellbeing.

While our findings suggest that NC is not a simple universal solution for problems with CA, NC may play a broader role in positively shaping people's well-being. Future research should therefore build on our work to examine factors that may mediate or moderate the NC-CA-well-being relationship, as this work may have important implications for inclusion of NC in interventions targeting populations affected by CA. Like the NC-CA relationship, the association between NC and well-being is only one component of a more complex system of relationships at play. With a view to future research, a secondary aim of this review was to identify any factors that influence the relationship between NC and CA, and the direction of their 52 influence. Factors influencing the relationship between NC and CA have not yet been a research focus in the extant literature; the available data used for this meta-analysis was largely salvaged from descriptive correlation tables or via email correspondence from authors. Only one study included in our search investigated variables that may influence the NC-CA relationship. Thomson and Roach (2023) tested climate knowledge as a moderator, 55 included in this review utilised a correlational research design, disallowing causal inferences to be made.

Additionally, nearly all of the included studies collected data through selfreported measures, which increases the risk of distortion effects such as response bias and method variance. This emphasises the need for experimental and longitudinal research that incorporates objective measurement to further the understanding of the NC-CA-well-being relationship. Secondly, most studies (75 -85%) investigating NC and CA were conducted in Western contexts, indicating population bias in the literature. Climate change disproportionately affects developing nations, as they are more vulnerable to climate threats and possess fewer resources to combat climate change (IPCC, 2022; United States Global Leadership Coalition, 2021). Future research should examine NC-CA-well-being relationships within more diverse populations to better understand the implications of concepts such as solastalgia and cultural variations in NC, thereby advancing understanding of NC and CA in relation to well-being.Thirdly, there were many inconsistencies in conceptualisation and measurement for both CA and NC. Although the current study did not find NC to be a significant moderator of the NC-CA relationship, the conceptualisation and measurement of NC requires further development. In this review, NC was categorised into 22 different operationalisations and while many of the studies included NC measures with similar qualities and characteristics, a universal definition of NC is still lacking (seeTiscareno-Osorno et al., 2023). As previously discussed, CA conceptualisation and measurement also require further development. Future research should continue to examine different CA instruments within the same sample population to clarify observed differences, determining if there is true conceptual variability between instruments or if measurement artefacts are present (seeHempel et al., 2025). Given the complexity involved, both quantitative and qualitative research is needed to examine how different aspects of NC relate to different aspects of CA. This research direction is situated within a broader research agenda needed to further the understanding of the NC-CA-wellbeing relationship, including the investigation of potential key moderators and mediators.
