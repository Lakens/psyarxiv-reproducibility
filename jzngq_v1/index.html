What we found

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

Helping professions are adopting artificial intelligence (AI) to automate documentation, monitoring, and coordination on the premise that offloading routine tasks frees workers for meaningful care. That premise assumes managers know which tasks are routine and which meaningful. We argue instead for a task-contingent AI paradox: meaning in helping work concentrates in identity-central tasks that are increasingly automatable. Study 1, a two-wave critical-incident panel of 307 helping-profession workers (144 longitudinal cases one year later), tested six pre-specified predictions that Time 1 human-coded meaning dimensions forecast Time 2 felt meaningfulness, exhaustion, or turnover intentions; estimates were near zero, and equivalence at the |β| = .30 sensitivity band held for three of five. One exploratory association survived multiplicity screening: narratives grounding meaning in competence were associated with higher later turnover intentions and exhaustion (β = .29 and .30, adjusting for six survey measures). Automated coding did not reach the human-agreement standard; humans coded all predictors. Study 2 coded 7,695 nursing-forum posts captured 2016-2026 for meaning events.

Nurses named AI in seven events; about one post in twenty per period instead named an optimization or implementation object (ratios, metrics, dashboards, documentation load), and the facet at issue was control over means. Study 3 found that share unchanged across the decade; the one strain marker that rose, lacking control, is confounded with a site template change. No study measured individual AI exposure. Against the automation frontier, the results locate the risk of optimization in the tasks workers name as meaningful, not in AI as such.

The AI Paradox: How Optimization May Undermine Meaningful Work in Helping Professions

Organizations in helping professions increasingly treat artificial intelligence (AI) as a way to shift routine work away from clinicians so they can focus on high-value care. Ambient AI documentation tools are associated with lower documentation burden, note-writing time, and after-hours charting, and their adoption with lower burnout and more undivided attention to patients (Albrecht et al., 2025; Lukac et al., 2025; Olson et al., 2025; Shah et al., 2025; Tierney et al., 2024; You et al., 2025). Health systems adopt ambient documentation, predictive monitoring, and automated care coordination under this banner. They frame the result as restoring the human side of care (Olson et al., 2025; Tierney et al., 2024). The premise is intuitive. It rests on an untested assumption: that managers already know which tasks are routine, which are meaningful, and that the two can be cleanly separated.

Field evidence shows that the routine-meaningful boundary can cut through tasks labeled as efficient targets for automation. When UC Davis Health piloted a wearable biosensor system to automate continuous patient monitoring in an oncology unit, it discontinued the device after roughly a year (Schellmann, 2026). Nurses reported detecting deterioration faster than the algorithm in the sickest patients, using cues the sensors could not capture, and the hospital concluded that the alerts offered no clear advantage over existing monitoring (Schellmann, 2026). Frontline accounts of clinical AI report a similar pattern elsewhere: when algorithmic alerts misfire or cannot explain themselves, nurses carry residual risk and close the judgment gap (Schellmann, 2026). What optimization had classified as automatable monitoring proved to be professional judgment central to nursing identity. The episode is what Siggelkow (2007) called a "talking pig": a single instance establishing that a routine-meaningful boundary can run through a task automated in the name of efficiency. If that boundary is misdrawn, optimization saves time and removes occasions for meaning. In helping professions, meaning is part of the psychological infrastructure that sustains the workforce rather than a mere perquisite of the job (Bunderson & Thompson, 2009).

Automation may have an iatrogenic effect in helping professions if it is implemented without knowing which tasks carry meaning. Those occupations already face chronic vacancies and attrition. The pipeline depends on intrinsic rewards to offset job costs. Federal projections anticipate national shortages of registered nurses through at least 2038. The same horizon includes shortfalls of roughly 100,000 mental health counselors, 77,000 addiction counselors, and 44,000 psychiatrists (National Center for Health Workforce Analysis, 2025a, 2025b).

Exposure to robots and AI is associated with job insecurity and maladaptive workplace behavior (Yam et al., 2023). Insecurity about the automation of occupational content, as distinct from job loss, is associated with burnout and health complaints (Roll et al., 2025). Insecurity from human-AI collaboration spills into work-life outcomes (Wu et al., 2024). Comparing oneself with AI coworkers is one reported burnout path (Ma & Li, 2026). Adoption framed as substitution rather than augmentation is associated with lower willingness to stay among caregivers (Jiang et al., 2026). If the tasks automated to reduce burnout are the tasks through which workers experience meaning, then efficiency optimization can become self-defeating. We name that possibility a task-contingent AI paradox. The proposed mechanism is a diseconomy of scale in meaning: efficiency gains from automating meaning-critical tasks may be offset by losses in the psychological resources that sustain the workforce. The question this article addresses is not whether AI can make helping work faster but which tasks are meaning-critical and what follows when those tasks are automated. Dominant scholarly and practitioner accounts rarely pose that question; they answer it by assumption. Reviews of AI across helping professions converge on a division of labor. AI absorbs documentation, monitoring, triage, decision support, and administrative coordination.

Professionals are then freed for direct care, complex judgment, and compassionate interaction (Balan & Gumpel, 2025; Garkisch & Goldkind, 2024; Guo et al., 2024; Han et al., 2026; Hobensack et al., 2024). Empirical evidence for this narrative concentrates on the clerical side.

Automating documentation is associated with lower burden and burnout (Albrecht et al., 2025; Olson et al., 2025; Shah et al., 2025; You et al., 2025). Reviews of AI workplace outcomes give little attention to meaning and purpose (Bankins et al., 2024; Pereira et al., 2023). Conceptual treatments often position automation as a net enabler of meaning when deployment is well governed (Bankins & Formosa, 2023; Smids et al., 2020). The few studies that measure experienced meaningfulness under AI-related job change are recent, largely cross-sectional, and outside flagship industrial-organizational journals (Callari & Puppione, 2025; Gemmano et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026). This literature rests on a shared assumption: that the routine-meaningful partition of tasks is known, stable, and aligned with what can be automated. That assumption is asserted more often than examined.

Two lines of evidence convert the efficiency story into a theoretical tension. Meaning at work is task-contingent and identity-central. It concentrates in particular activities rather than spreading evenly across a job, a reading that follows from the pathway structure Rosso et al. (2010) describe. Automation effects show the same granularity. Generative AI produces large gains on tasks inside its capability frontier and degrades performance on adjacent tasks just outside it (Dell'Acqua et al., 2026). Several tasks now targeted for automation plausibly carry meaning. Automating monitoring, documentation, and analysis can shrink the task identity and problem-solving that underpin clinicians' intrinsic motivation (Bienefeld et al., 2025). Diagnostic AI intersects the autonomous judgment that professionals defend as central to responsibility (Faulconbridge et al., 2025; Lebovitz et al., 2022). Expecting one's work to be robotized is associated with lower meaningfulness and more burnout symptoms (Turja et al., 2022).

Philosophers of technology describe related "achievement gaps" when machines absorb the accomplishments through which workers earn meaning (Danaher & Nyholm, 2021; Parmer, 2024). The relationship between meaning and well-being is not monotonic. Deeply meaningful, calling-infused work can bind workers to self-sacrifice and exhaustion even as it confers purpose (Bunderson & Thompson, 2009; Cardador & Caza, 2012; Schabram & Maitlis, 2017). The routine-meaningful dichotomy therefore fails on two counts: it mislocates where meaning resides, and it assumes that more meaning is uniformly better. What replaces it is a contingency: the effect of automating a task depends on the task's meaning content and on how densely meaning is already concentrated in the work.

We make that contingency analytically tractable by anchoring in the most influential integrative account of why work becomes meaningful. Rosso et al. (2010) distinguished meaning, the type or content of significance a person attaches to work, from meaningfulness, its amount (see also Pratt & Ashforth, 2003). They synthesized a fragmented literature into four pathways through which meaningful work is created or sustained: Individuation (self-agency), Contribution (other-agency), Self-Connection (self-communion), and Unification (othercommunion). These pathways are organized by two superordinate dimensions, a 2 × 2 of Agency-Communion (Bakan, 1966) crossed with Self-Others, which we adopt as the confirmatory level of our framework (Figure 1; facet definitions in Appendix A). The architecture matters for the paradox. Helping work is disproportionately other-oriented and draws meaning from Contribution and Unification pathways, impact on and connection to patients and community. Those relational tasks are the ones the optimization narrative treats as most valuable. Roadmaps for AI in care now name them as candidates for eventual automation while conceding that relational skills lie outside current system capability (Stade et al., 2024), and conceptual accounts locate the meaning at risk in face-to-face contact and complex judgment (Bankins & Formosa, 2023).

Anchoring in Rosso et al. (2010) also lets us extend a foundational framework to a problem its authors could not have anticipated, and to test conjectures they left open. Rosso and colleagues proposed, but could not examine, that the four pathways might combine additively or interactively. They speculated that "there may even be a curvilinear effect through which too many active pathways to meaning are exhausting and lead to burnout" (p. 116). To our knowledge, that dark-side conjecture has not been tested with longitudinal data. A study built around the 2 × 2 can test those questions directly: whether agentic and communal meaning interact, whether self-and other-oriented meaning forecast different outcomes, and whether the density of activated meaning has a turning point beyond which it predicts exhaustion rather than flourishing (Haans et al., 2016). The contribution is to convert a canonical descriptive framework into falsifiable, outcome-linked propositions about when meaning sustains a workforce and when it consumes it, the kind of extension of prior theory to an emerging problem that Rouse et al. (2025) call for.

We tested the argument in three studies (Figure 2). Study 1 is a two-wave criticalincident panel of helping-profession workers. At Time 1, participants described the meaning of their job and wrote structured critical-incident narratives (Flanagan, 1954) about the episodes they found most meaningful. About one year later, Time 2 measured well-being, work engagement, prosocial motivation, felt meaningfulness, burnout, and turnover. Each narrative was coded for Rosso's pathways. We then modeled Time 1 codes as predictors of Time 2 outcomes. The result is what we term a meaning-criticality map: which pathways of meaning, if any, forecast later well-being, engagement, and retention. The Study 1 data predate the current automation wave. We do not observe AI removing tasks. We establish which meaning pathways are workforce-sustaining and then read the paradox by placing that map against the independently documented automation frontier. That comparison is weaker than an experiment but stronger than inferring causation from adoption that no individual worker can be shown to have experienced. Studies 2 and 3 add out-of-sample discourse and a year-to-year contrast in that discourse.

Coding thousands of narrative cells by hand is slow. Substituting a single automated coder also removes the between-coder check on which confidence in the labels depends. We therefore tested whether a panel of large language models could take over the coding (Gilardi et al., 2023; Törnberg, 2024; Ziems et al., 2024). Each model applied the same instructions; between-model agreement was to supply reliability evidence, with the prior human dual-coding as the reference standard. None of the six pre-specified prompt configurations met the agency acceptance threshold (see Results), so Study 1 analyses use the human composite codes. Designbased supervised learning (Egami et al., 2023) was specified for machine labels and was not applied once those labels were set aside.

The article treats meaningful work as something optimization can destroy as readily as design can create it. The task-contingent AI paradox specifies when an efficiency intervention can harm workers: when it targets meaning-critical, identity-central tasks. Testing Rosso et al.'s (2010) additive-interactive and curvilinear conjectures converts a descriptive map of meaning into outcome-linked propositions about workforce sustainability. A two-wave panel then shows which meaning pathways, if any, sustain helping-profession workers over a year. A pre-specified test of automated narrative coding against a human reference retained the human codes when the prompt configurations did not reach the pre-specified agreement thresholds.

Existing accounts theorize how meaning is constructed; few theorize how it is lost, and none, to our knowledge, locate that loss in the collision between an optimization logic and a meaning architecture. The rest of the article develops the framework and four hypothesis blocks, reports the three studies, and returns to the paradox and its limits.

Meaningful Work as Workforce Sustenance

Meaning, Meaningfulness, and Four Pathways

Meaningful work is a working psychological resource with documented downstream consequences rather than a perquisite of employment. Experienced meaningfulness is associated with job satisfaction and performance in cross-sectional data (Gemmano et al., 2026). It transmits effects of technology-related job change onto career sustainability (Liu et al., 2026). It also constitutes a distinct component of subjective well-being at work alongside enthusiasm and contentment (Monnot & Beehr, 2014). Following Rosso et al. (2010), we distinguish the meaning of work, the kind of significance a person attaches to it, from meaningfulness, the amount of positive significance the work holds (Pratt & Ashforth, 2003). Meaning is a qualitative, content question: significance of what kind, arising from which experiences.

Meaningfulness is a quantitative question: how much. The distinction matters because the AI paradox concerns content. Automation does not reduce global meaningfulness uniformly; it removes specific tasks through which particular kinds of meaning are realized (Bankins & Formosa, 2023; Montefiore et al., 2026). Rosso et al. (2010) integrated a fragmented literature into four pathways to meaningful work by crossing two foundational dimensions of human experience. The first is Bakan's (1966) distinction between agency (self-assertion, differentiation, competence, and control) and communion (contact, connection, and unity with others). The second is the direction of the meaning-conferring action, toward the self or toward others. Crossing these yields four pathways: Individuation (self-agency; for example, competence, autonomy, and self-esteem realized through work), Contribution (other-agency; for example, significance, impact, and transcendence of self-interest in service of others), Self-Connection (self-communion; for example, authenticity and identity coherence), and Unification (other-communion; for example, shared values and belonging). In the present project these pathways are operationalized through 13 binary facets, aggregated to the four pathways and then to the 2 × 2 superordinate dimensions using an inclusive (logical-OR) rule. The full mapping is reported in Appendix A (Table A1).

Why the 2 × 2 Is the Confirmatory Level

Our confirmatory tests are specified at the 2 × 2 dimensional level (Agency-Communion × Self-Others) rather than at the level of individual facets. As a measurement matter, finegrained facets applied to short narratives are individually sparse and noisy, whereas aggregation to pathways and dimensions is more reliable. In our human dual-coding, agreement improves monotonically with aggregation and reaches acceptable levels only at the pathway and dimensional levels (see Method). As a theoretical matter, the 2 × 2 is the level at which Rosso et al.'s (2010) open conjectures are stated, about how agentic and communal, and self-and otheroriented, meaning combine. It is therefore the level at which the framework can be most directly extended and tested. Facet-and pathway-level results are reported as secondary and exploratory.

The Task-Contingent AI Paradox

The paradox follows from two independently documented observations. An automation frontier has already crossed into the daily task structure of helping work. A meaning architecture concentrates significance in specific, identity-central tasks. We document each in turn, then derive the paradox and its diseconomy-of-scale mechanism.

The Automation Frontier in Helping Professions

AI has entered the task structure of every major helping profession as deployed or piloted systems rather than as a speculative future. In medicine, ambient AI scribes draft clinical documentation at health-system scale (Olson et al., 2025; Tierney et al., 2024; You et al., 2025).

In nursing, LLMs are entering documentation, clinical decision support, patient education, and workflow management, with efficiency the most common design aim (Han et al., 2026; Hobensack et al., 2024). In mental health care, reviews report structured screening, detection, and decision-support applications (Balan & Gumpel, 2025; Guo et al., 2024), including suiciderisk evaluation (Omar et al., 2024); one roadmap proposes staged integration toward progressively autonomous therapeutic roles (Stade et al., 2024), although World Health Organization guidance summarized in that review favors augmentation over replacement (Guo et al., 2024). Generative AI now also trains counselors through simulated clients and role-play (Jeong et al., 2025; Louie et al., 2026; Maurya, 2024). Social work scholarship maps AI applications across profession, agency, and client functions, though empirical implementation studies remain scarce and ethical concerns remain prominent (Garkisch & Goldkind, 2024; Rodríguez-Martínez et al., 2024; Social Work England, 2025). Algorithmic management, including scheduling, routing, monitoring, and performance scoring, is likewise spreading beyond gig platforms into standard employment settings, where its prescriptive use remains narrow and uncommon (Keegan & Meijerink, 2025; Parent-Rocheleau & Parker, 2022).

The frontier is jagged. Generative AI capability is task-specific rather than job-level, producing large gains on tasks inside the frontier and degraded performance on adjacent tasks outside it (Dell'Acqua et al., 2026). Effects on service work are mixed rather than uniform (Quan et al., 2025). An optimization logic also governs the frontier: automation and augmentation are interdependent rather than alternatives. Systems marketed as augmentation simultaneously automate, and organizations respond to generative AI coverage by deprioritizing the human skills the technology appears to subsume (Devigili et al., 2026; Raisch & Krakowski, 2021).

Substituting machine output for human decisions can impair the experiential learning through which professional judgment develops (Balasubramanian et al., 2022). Even beneficial AI assistance can reduce felt agency and pride when the AI's role is salient (Tong et al., 2021). The tasks that remain contested at the frontier are those in which professional meaning is most concentrated: complex or atypical cases and the assessment of suicide risk. Large language models used in psychiatry have difficulty with complex cases and can underestimate suicide risk (Omar et al., 2024). Evaluations of generative tools in mental-health settings also report dropoffs where contextual reasoning, cultural sensitivity, and high-stakes judgment are required (L. Wang et al., 2025; X. Wang et al., 2025). Professionals engage in deliberate boundary work to protect autonomous judgment and epistemic responsibility from algorithmic encroachment (Faulconbridge et al., 2025). High performers whose identity is invested in skilled judgment resist substitution most strongly (SimanTov-Nachlieli, 2025). Critical-care nurse leaders treated human-centered care, patient autonomy, and accountability in AI-driven decisions as conditions of integration, not extras (Hassan & El-Ashry, 2024). The frontier has not stopped at the clerical boundary; it is moving through tasks whose meaning content has never been mapped.

Localization: Meaning Is Concentrated in Tasks

Localization is the first property of meaning that generates the paradox. Meaning is realized through specific pathways. It therefore concentrates in specific tasks rather than spreading evenly across a job; this is our inference from the pathway structure Rosso et al. (2010) describe. In helping work, tasks that plausibly carry Contribution and Unification meaning overlap tasks now targeted for automation. Examples include advocating for a patient, coordinating care, being present during a crisis, and exercising judgment when protocols fail (Bienefeld et al., 2025; Schellmann, 2026). The qualifier is deliberate: which tasks carry which meaning is what the meaning-criticality map is designed to establish. The extent of this overlap is a question the studies test rather than a premise we assume. Work-design theory predicts the exposure. Digital technologies act on autonomy, skill use, task identity, and relational contact.

Automation decisions are therefore work-redesign decisions whether or not they are recognized as such (Parent-Rocheleau & Parker, 2022; Parker & Grote, 2022). Qualitative evidence from critical care shows that automating monitoring, documentation, and analysis can shrink task identity and problem-solving. Clinicians become supervisors of systems rather than agents of care (Bienefeld et al., 2025). Algorithmic mediation of work is likewise associated with weakened identity and belonging (van Zoonen et al., 2024), and normative analysis places belonging and self-development among the conditions for meaningful work that algorithmic management most threatens (García-Ruiz & Rocchi, 2026).

Non-Monotonicity: The Dark Side of Deeply Meaningful Work

Non-monotonicity is the second property: the relationship between meaning and wellbeing is not uniformly positive. Deeply meaningful, calling-oriented work is double-edged. It can produce exhaustion, self-sacrifice, and vulnerability to exploitation even as it confers purpose (Bunderson & Thompson, 2009; Cardador & Caza, 2012; Schabram & Maitlis, 2017). Rosso et al. (2010) made the corresponding conjecture explicit: too many simultaneously active pathways to meaning may be exhausting. The conjecture has not, to our knowledge, been tested with longitudinal data. Non-monotonicity supplies a boundary condition and a caution for the paradox. More activated meaning is not uniformly protective. A workforce already dense in meaning-critical tasks may be both most rewarded by those tasks and most vulnerable to their loss.

A Diseconomy of Scale in Meaning

From localization we derive the paradox's sign-reversal. Automating a low-meaning task frees time at little psychological cost. Automating a meaning-critical task removes an occasion for meaning even as it frees the same time. The effect of an efficiency intervention is therefore task-contingent, beneficial or harmful depending on the meaning content of the task it removes.

The contingency is already visible in the empirical record. AI adoption was not associated with well-being directly but only through changes in the work conditions employees value (Valtonen et al., 2025). Whether workers respond to AI adoption by crafting toward or defensively away from meaningful tasks depends on whether they appraise the adoption as challenge or hindrance (Cheng et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2024). Aggregated across a workforce, systematically automating meaning-critical tasks yields what we term a diseconomy of scale in meaning. The per-unit efficiency gain is real, but so is a per-unit erosion of the psychological resources that sustain retention and engagement, and the latter accumulates as the former scales. From nonmonotonicity we draw the complementary caution. Because meaning can also exhaust, the map of what to protect must be established empirically rather than assumed. Some dense concentrations of meaning may mark burnout risk rather than assets to preserve.

We treat the paradox as a bridging proposition rather than a directly tested hypothesis because Study 1 does not measure AI exposure (see Overview of the Research Program). The falsifiable content of Study 1 is the meaning-criticality map: which pathways predict which outcomes over time. That map is summarized in the hypotheses below and depicted in Figure 1.

Studies 2 and 3 then ask whether the same pathways appear in organic discourse and whether their salience moved across the years we could observe.

Hypotheses

We organize predictions into four blocks that move from aggregate to dimensional to pathway-specific effects and, finally, to the curvilinear dark side. Blocks 1-3 concern linear, workforce-sustaining relationships. Block 4 tests Rosso et al.'s (2010) curvilinear conjecture. A compact summary appears in Appendix B (Table B1). Throughout, predictors are Time 1 (T1) meaning coded from critical-incident narratives, and outcomes are Time 2 (T2) measures collected approximately one year later.

Block 1: Aggregate Main Effects

The aggregate proposition that meaning sustains a workforce has converging empirical footing. Experienced meaningfulness is associated with satisfaction and performance (Gemmano et al., 2026). It carries the effects of technology-related job change onto career sustainability (Liu et al., 2026). Expecting one's work to be robotized is associated with lower meaningfulness and more burnout symptoms (Turja et al., 2022), and substitution-framed adoption with reduced willingness to stay (Jiang et al., 2026). What this literature lacks is prospective evidence that the meaning content expressed in workers' own accounts of their work predicts consequential outcomes over time. Block 1 supplies that test at the aggregate level, treating the breadth of activated pathways as the coarsest index of a worker's meaning architecture. Block 1 predictions are stated as first-order, average linear relationships. Whether the linear form holds across the observed range is itself tested in Block 4.

Hypothesis 1d.

Aggregate T1 meaning density positively predicts T2 felt meaningfulness of work. This hypothesis establishes the predictive bridge between coded meaning content and experienced meaningfulness as distinct constructs (Pratt & Ashforth, 2003), using a self-report meaningfulness outcome (Monnot & Beehr, 2014) that shares no method variance with the narrative-coded predictor.

Block 2: Dimensional (2 × 2) Confirmatory Effects

Helping work is disproportionately other-oriented. Its meaning is anchored in impact on, and connection to, beneficiaries (Grant, 2008b; Rosso et al., 2010). Helping professionals themselves locate the non-negotiable core of their occupations in the relational and moral dimensions of care (Bienefeld et al., 2025; Hassan & El-Ashry, 2024). The burnout prediction in this block follows from construct correspondence. Depersonalization is the interpersonal component of burnout, a cynical withdrawal from the relational field of work, whereas emotional exhaustion is its general energetic component (Maslach et al., 2001). Meaning anchored in others should therefore most strongly buffer the burnout component that shares its relational content.

Block 2 specifies confirmatory tests on the contrast-coded Agency-Communion and Self-Others dimensions and their interaction. These tests distinguish Rosso et al.'s (2010) additive account from an interactive one.

Hypothesis 2a.

The Self-Others contrast predicts T2 workforce-sustaining outcomes such that other-oriented T1 meaning is more strongly and positively associated with T2 wellbeing and engagement than self-oriented meaning.

Hypothesis 2b.

Agency and Communion interact rather than combine purely additively in predicting T2 outcomes; the effect of one dimension depends on the level of the other (a nonadditive 2 × 2 interaction).

Hypothesis 2c.

The Self-Others contrast differentially predicts burnout components: other-oriented T1 meaning is more strongly (inversely) associated with T2 depersonalization than with T2 emotional exhaustion; this difference warrants separate treatment of these subscales in this block.

Block 3: Pathway-Specific Linear Effects

Within the other-oriented dimension, the two pathways rest on theoretically distinct mechanisms. Contribution operates through perceived impact and significance, the mechanism by which contact with beneficiaries fuels prosocial motivation and persistence (Grant, 2008a (Grant, , 2008b)). Unification operates through shared values and belonging, the relational resources most directly threatened when algorithmic mediation weakens identity and connection at work (van Zoonen et al., 2024). Outcome assignments follow these mechanisms. Contribution's impact mechanism feeds motivational and affective outcomes. Unification's belonging mechanism is an embedding force: shared values and connection bind workers to the setting that supplies them.

Unification, uniquely among the pathways, therefore carries the turnover and mobility prediction. Block 3 tests whether these pathways carry the workforce-sustaining effects that the paradox would place at risk.

Block 4: Curvilinear, Dark-Side Effects

Block 4 tests the conjecture that "too many active pathways to meaning are exhausting and lead to burnout" (Rosso et al., 2010, p. 116), using the theorizing-and-testing guidelines for U-shaped relationships set out by Haans et al. (2016). Block 4 stands in a specified relation to Block 1 rather than alongside it: Hypotheses 1a and 4c address the same predictor-outcome pair with competing functional forms, and Hypotheses 1a and 4a do so for near-mirror outcomes. We therefore treat Block 1 as stating the average, first-order relationship and Block 4 as testing whether that relationship is genuinely linear or conceals a turning point, with the quadratic specification as the arbiter between them (Haans et al., 2016); a quadratic term estimated with an interval excluding zero would refine rather than complement the aggregate proposition. That places its validity in the pre-turning-point range. The dark-side literature locates the exhaustion risk specifically in other-oriented, calling-infused meaning, where self-sacrifice and overidentification bind workers to the source of their depletion (Bunderson & Thompson, 2009; Cardador & Caza, 2012; Schabram & Maitlis, 2017).

Overview of the Research Program

The argument is tested in three completed studies (Figure 2). Study 1 is the two-wave panel. Study 2 coded a decade of nursing-forum posts for meaning events and the objects they implicate. Study 3 tested whether that talk shifted across the 2016-2026 capture window.

Study 1 identified meaning-critical tasks in a two-wave critical-incident panel of helpingprofession workers. Time 1 meaning pathways were used to predict Time 2 well-being, engagement, prosocial motivation, felt meaningfulness, burnout, and turnover.

Study 1 Method

Helping-profession workers were recruited through Amazon Mechanical Turk and completed measures at two waves about one year apart. Time 1 was collected March 10-16, 2016. Participants described the meaning they associate with their job, wrote structured criticalincident narratives (Flanagan, 1954) about the work episodes they found most meaningful, and completed the Time 1 scales below. Time 2 was fielded February 14 to March 30, 2017, under an IRB modification signed December 27, 2016. Across matched respondents, the interval between wave start dates ranged from 11.1 to 12.6 months (M = 11.58, SD = 0.28). The Time 1 narratives predate public release of contemporary generative-AI systems. The analytic sample is 307 respondents at Time 1 and 144 longitudinal survey cases at Time 2 (pre-specified models n = 136 and 128). The sample is majority health care (roughly 55%). Coding Meaning From Narratives

The central measurement task was to code each critical-incident narrative for the meaning pathways it expressed. This subsection covers the coding framework, the human dual-coding that produced the Study 1 predictors, the agreement metric, and the pre-specified machine-coding test that did not support substitution.

Coding Framework

Narratives were coded on 13 binary (present/absent) facets adapted from Rosso et al. (2010) and specified in the project coding instructions. Facets were aggregated to the four pathways and then to the 2 × 2 dimensions with an inclusive logical-OR rule: a pathway is present if any of its facets is present, and a dimension is present if any of its pathways is present.

Appendix A (Table A1) reports the mapping. We treat the four pathways and the 2 × 2 dimensions as the valid aggregation layers. The seven narrative mechanisms in Rosso et al. (2010) are not a coding level in this scheme.

Human Coding as the Validation Reference

Two coders independently applied the codebook to the Time 1 narratives: the first author and a trained research assistant. Disagreements on a stratified sample of cells were then resolved by the first author, blind to which coder had assigned each label (identity keys stayed sealed until that pass was finished). This is a blinded resolution pass by one of the original coders, not an independent third-rater design. Between-coder agreement was computed on the two original independent passes and is unaffected by the later resolved codes. The composite (agreed cells plus resolved cells) is the human reference used in Study 1. It is not a machine-learning training set. Because joint absence exceeds roughly 95% of cells, reliability is reported as the proportion of specific agreement (PSA; Cicchetti & Feinstein, 1990). PSA equals the F1 score when one coder is treated as the reference and the other as the prediction (van Rijsbergen, 1979; see also Dice, 1945). Unlike kappa, PSA is not deflated by extreme class imbalance (Feinstein & Cicchetti, 1990). Agreement was lowest at the facet level and higher at the pathway and dimensional levels. That pattern is one reason the confirmatory tests are specified at the 2 × 2 level. Cells still unresolved after the resolution pass (9.3%) were coded absent in the primary analysis. An endpoint sensitivity check recoded those cells as present. The two-tier coding process is summarized in Figure 3.

The LLM Panel as Candidate Coders

To test whether coding could be scaled, we treated a panel of large language models as candidate coders. Each model applied the same written instructions and returned a present/absent label per facet. Reliability was estimated as between-model PSA/F1, the same index used for the human pair (Cicchetti & Feinstein, 1990; van Rijsbergen, 1979). The human reference was used to test whether panel labels matched competent human judgment (Gilardi et al., 2023; Törnberg, 2024; Ziems et al., 2024). Substitution was conditional on meeting pre-specified agreement thresholds on the development partition, not on a single validation.

The primary machine-coding arm was a few-shot panel. A small set of human-resolved exemplars anchored each facet definition in the prompt. Zero-shot and one-shot prompting were baseline and ablation conditions. Development and evaluation cases did not overlap. Prompts and examples were labeled by provenance so that no fabricated text was presented as a real narrative and no illustrative label was presented as a resolved human code. Agreement was reported as PSA/F1 and confusion structure (Cicchetti & Feinstein, 1990; van Rijsbergen, 1979), not as a correlation, because the coding target is categorical.

Fine-tuning is discussed only as a conceptual benchmark. Customer fine-tuning is not currently available for the frontier-tier model used here, so a fine-tuned arm was not executable by an external research team and was not run. We mention it to bound expectations about achievable accuracy.

Automated Coding: Pre-Specified Validation Against the Human Benchmark

We tested whether large language model (LLM) coding could substitute for human dualcoding before any substantive analysis. Six prompt configurations were developed exclusively on the training partition and fixed by SHA-256 hash before evaluation: a decision-first instruction and a revised decision-first instruction, a per-facet version that issued thirteen separate calls per narrative, a reason-first variant, a decision-plus-evidence variant, and an alternate-codebook variant. Each prompt was evaluated once on the 55-case development partition under a selection rule fixed in advance: three replicates per prompt on the pre-specified primary model (claude-sonnet-5, run 2026-09-22), selection statistics computed on counts pooled across replicates, and case-level cluster bootstrap (B = 10,000, fixed seed) for all intervals. A second model (gpt-5-2025-08-07) ran a single non-voting pass used only for salience reliability.

Two development-set exposures predated the freeze: an earlier model generation had scored 28 of the 55 cases in July 2026, and the choice of communion precision as a development target traced partly to that exposure. The pass was therefore recorded as the single selection evaluation, with this exposure disclosed, rather than treated as a first evaluation on unexposed data.

Acceptance was calibrated to what human coders achieve. A prompt met the standard set for acceptance only if its dimension-level agreement with the composite gold standard (positive specific agreement; PSA) reached the lower bound of the human-human benchmark's bootstrap 95% confidence interval: ≥ .910 for agency (human PSA = .935 [.910, .956]) and ≥ .805 for others-directed meaning (human .844 [.805, .879]). Communion (human .48 [.34, .60]) and selfdirected meaning (human .52 [.43, .59]) carried no acceptance threshold because human agreement was itself too low to validate against. Disqualifiers, evaluated over pooled replicate cells, were any false positive on communion facets or on sparse facets over cells both human coders had coded absent, and a structured-output error rate above .10. None of the six prompt configurations met the agency threshold. Agency PSA was .750-.798 against ≥ .910; the highest value, .798, fell .11 below the threshold. Two decision-first prompts met the others-directed threshold (≥ .805). Full prompt-level numbers are in Table S1. Neither provider's interface accepted a fixed temperature value for these models, so each provider's default was used for every arm. Default parameters must be specified even when they are defaults, for reproducibility (Törnberg, 2024); we record them here as the providers' defaults. That constraint was one reason for the replicate design. An interruption in the middle of the run, and the resume that followed, meant that a small number of initially malformed outputs were redrawn, so error rates are quantities from the final attempt. Interim aggregate monitoring occurred during the run with no subsequent change to any prompt, freeze, or rule. No model scoring of the test partition occurred before the results above were fixed. After the development pass and after these analyses were complete, one descriptive evaluation of a pre-specified 65case subsplit of the test partition was taken under a hashed protocol that fixed the arms, statistics, and reporting rule before any case was scored; it scored the two highest-ranked disqualified prompts. On those 65 cases agency PSA was .81 (decision-first) and .80 (per-facet), and othersdirected PSA .75 and .77, in the range of the development pass; both prompts again carried false positives over agreed-absent cells. The pre-specified screen for scoring the remaining 128 cases (decision-first agency and others-directed PSA both ≥ .75) was not met (others-directed = .746), so those cases remained sealed under that screen. A single, separately authorized scoring pass over the 128 was underway at the time of writing and is not reported here. Analytic Strategy

Confirmatory 2 × 2 Model

The confirmatory analyses regressed each T2 outcome on contrast-coded Agency-Communion and Self-Others dimensions and their interaction, rather than on four collinear pathway dummies. Contrast coding yielded interpretable main effects of the two superordinate dimensions and a single interaction term that directly tested the additive-versus-interactive conjecture (Block 2). Pathway-specific (Block 3) and facet-level analyses were estimated as secondary and exploratory models.

Estimation, Covariates, and Inference Criteria

Each T2 outcome was modeled with ordinary least squares, with the hypothesized predictors entered simultaneously; no stepwise or best-subset selection was used. Every model carried a fixed covariate set: the T1 baseline of the outcome where one exists (the T1 meaningfulness measure in the felt-meaningfulness model, which makes Hypothesis 1d a test of incremental prediction over self-reported meaning), age and work centrality (the variables on which completers differed from noncompleters), and occupation block. Coefficients are reported unstandardized (Table 2) and standardized (Table 3) with heteroskedasticity-consistent (HC3) standard errors (Long & Ervin, 2000) and 95% confidence intervals, with the complete-case n for each model. Pre-specified directional hypotheses were tested at α = .05, one-tailed in the predicted direction; the non-directional interaction (Hypothesis 2b) and comparisons of functional form (Block 4 against Block 1) were two-tailed. The confirmatory family was deliberately small. All remaining dimension-, pathway-, and facet-level estimates are reported in full as point estimates with confidence intervals and are not treated as hypothesis tests, so no multiplicity adjustment was applied to associations that were never tested. Analyses were run in Python (pandas, statsmodels, SciPy).

Curvilinear Tests

Curvilinear predictions (Block 4) were to be tested following Haans et al. (2016): a quadratic term for meaning density, the significance and sign of the quadratic coefficient, a turning point within the observed data range, and a slope that reverses across that range rather than merely flattening. Where a dark-side effect was predicted to differ by orientation (Hypothesis 4b), curvature was to be compared across other-and self-oriented meaning. Because Block 4 respecifies the functional form of relationships stated as linear in Block 1, direct comparison of the linear and quadratic specifications decides between them; where the quadratic term's interval excludes zero, Block 1 coefficients are interpreted as average effects over the observed range.

Statistical Power and What the Sample Could Test

With 144 complete longitudinal cases, the design detects zero-order associations of |r| ≥ .23 with 80% power (α = .05, two-tailed); effects that must additionally clear an autoregressive baseline need to be larger to be detected. Two consequences were fixed in advance rather than decided after inspection. First, confirmatory tests were specified at the 2 × 2 dimensional level, where human-coded base rates supply usable variance: Agency and Others are present in roughly one half to two thirds of human-coded narratives, whereas the Self-Connection and Unification pathways each appear in fewer than 10%. Pathway-level predictions that depend on those sparse pathways (Hypotheses 3b and 3c) were therefore evaluated as estimation with confidence intervals rather than as formal tests. Second, a hypothesis was withheld from formal testing and reported descriptively when the data could not satisfy the design requirements of its test. Both pre-set conditions were met: only 16 respondents carried both Agency and Communion, so the interaction (Hypothesis 2b) was not estimable, and the density distribution concentrated at 0-2 activated pathways, leaving no interior turning point for the Haans et al. (2016) criteria to certify (Block 4). Declared non-tests are reported with the relevant cell counts and distributions and are not interpreted as null results.

Debiasing Downstream Inference

Any coder, human or machine, measures the Time 1 meaning predictor with error. Naive regression of Time 2 outcomes on imperfect labels yields biased coefficients (Egami et al., 2023). Design-based supervised learning (DSL; Egami et al., 2023) was specified for that problem if machine labels had been used. The pre-specified machine-coding test did not support substitution, so DSL was not applied. The Study 1 models use the human composite codes.

Missing Data and Attrition

Because Time 2 was available for a subset of Time 1 respondents, we compared completers and noncompleters on Time 1 variables (reported above) and entered age and work centrality, the variables on which they differed, as covariates in every model. Longitudinal models use complete cases and report their n; Table 1 correlations use pairwise deletion. No imputation was performed.

Concurrent Convergence With Self-Reports

Although the coded dimensions added no prospective information over Time 1 selfreports, they were not empty. Cross-sectionally (n = 272), others-directed meaning correlated with self-reported meaningfulness (WAMI: r = .24, pBH = .002). So did agency (r = .20, pBH = .011) and the contribution pathway (r = .23, pBH = .005). In an adjusted model with all four dimensions, age, and occupation, the dimension block accounted for ΔR² = .08 in WAMI scores.

The others-directed correlation held with narrative word count partialled (partial r = .21). It strengthened under graded two-coder scoring (r = .34). Random split-half re-estimation reproduced the block in all 200 resampled halves. The confirmatory nulls concern incremental prospective prediction, not basic convergence with self-report.

Location of the Concurrent Association in the Meaningfulness Distribution

Quantile regression (Koenker & Bassett, 1978) located the code-meaningfulness association in the lower tail of the meaning distribution (Figure 6). At the 25th percentile, slopes for agency and others-directed meaning (b ≈ 0.70) were about double their ordinary-least-squares counterparts. They shrank toward the mean effect at the 75th percentile. Narratives coded othersdirected also showed lower meaningfulness variance (SD ratios .77-.79, Brown-Forsythe pBH = .079). The association was asymmetric. Of respondents in the top WAMI quartile, 83% carried at least one codable meaning anchor. Only 30% of respondents with an anchor reached that quartile. A codable anchor was common among high-meaning respondents but was not sufficient for high meaning. WAMI is ceiling-compressed (10.7% at the maximum). We interpret the shape of the association rather than the specific slopes.

Espoused but Not Narrated Meaning

Participants answered both an abstract question ("what makes your work meaningful") and a concrete critical-incident prompt. Codes assigned only from the abstract answer, and never grounded in the story, were unrelated to narrative code density (r = .00). They still predicted selfreported meaningfulness at both waves (Time 1 r = .25; Time 2 r = .30, both pBH < .01).

Espousing the significance of one's work without narrating it correlated positively with Time 2 turnover intentions (b = 0.80 [0.27, 1.33], p = .003, answer length partialled). Cell sizes are modest (48-65). The two prompts differ in format. Whether the pattern reflects claiming meaning one cannot ground is a question for a designed study.

Holding narrative richness constant, the number of facets on which the two coders disagreed was negatively related to concurrent meaningfulness (b = -0.21, p = .004, n = 272).

Narratives from lower-meaning respondents were harder to code consensually. That is consistent with the low human-human agreement on communion facets reported in the Method.

A person-level profile analysis ordered mean meaningfulness across code configurations (η² = .079, pBH = .002; Figure 7): agency + self + others > agency + others > agency + self > no codes. The gap opened where others-directed meaning joined agency. The analysis largely recapitulates the dimension-level results and is reported for completeness.

Screened Associations Not Retained

The breadth of the search bounds the retained results. No moderation of code-outcome associations survived screening (249 tests: 233 dimension × moderator interactions and 16 code × context interactions). The codes did not predict Time 2 retention. They did not predict job satisfaction, affective well-being, or work-life interference or enrichment (0 of 35 tests). They were not reproducible from lexical style markers. An earlier 2017 coding of the same narratives carried no outcome signal. Huber re-estimation reproduced all five confirmatory coefficient estimates. Graded two-coder scoring did not add prospective incremental validity over Time 1 baselines (all b* ≤ .06).

No communion association survived the family-wise screen against Time 2 outcomes.

The baseline-adjusted estimates in the confirmatory section were not robust to the unresolvedcell bounds. We note the directional consistency across four Time 2 outcomes as a pattern worth designed replication. Communion facets were also where human coders agreed least. We draw no conclusion from the pattern here.

Finally, the facet-level analyses were constrained by the codebook's resolution: six of thirteen facets occurred in five or fewer of the 276 narratives and could not be tested at all. The facet taxonomy exceeds what a sample of this size can support, which is itself relevant to the design of future narrative-coding studies.

Block 4's curvilinear predictions were withheld from formal testing before estimation.

Only 4 of 276 respondents narrated three or more pathways (2 of them with Time 2 exhaustion data), so the density distribution cannot support a turning-point test. Figure S1 shows the descriptive quadratic estimates, scored as signs only; the corresponding concurrent quadratic on Time 1 meaningfulness appears in Figure S2. The overall quadratic for density on Time 2 exhaustion was positive as predicted (b2 = 0.21, 95% CI [-0.18, 0.60], p = .288). The quadratics on both Time 2 meaning outcomes were negative as predicted. The comparative prediction (Hypothesis 4b) was uninformative. Both orientation-specific quadratics were negative and imprecise (other-oriented b2 = -0.30, p = .209; self-oriented b2 = -0.11, p = .813). Almost no respondents sat at the density levels where curvature would appear. The quadratic intervals include zero.

Study 2: Meaning Events in Occupational Discourse

Corpus

The corpus is Internet Archive snapshots of allnurses.com captured between 1 January 2016 and 8 September 2026 (2026 is therefore a partial year, 147 posts): 7,695 posts of at least 200 characters, in 1,453 threads, by 4,442 hashed member accounts (1,670 threads had cached HTML; recoverable boards are listed in Appendix D, Table D1). The scoring file held 7,709 successful records; 14 posts had been captured and scored twice, and the first record for each post was kept, leaving 7,695 unique posts. Capture year is not posted date; we use three capture windows (2016-2019, 2020-2022, 2023-2026) as a proxy for period. Posts are nested in threads and in accounts; the 34% of accounts that posted twice or more wrote 62% of posts. All intervals are 95% percentile intervals from a bootstrap that resamples threads (B = 2,000); account-level resampling gave intervals of the same or narrower width for every period contrast reported.

Coding Instrument

Each post was coded by a single large language model (claude-sonnet-5, one pass at the API default sampling temperature; the temperature lock specified in the protocol was not applied in the scoring client, a deviation we record here; prompt frozen and hashed before the run) into zero or more meaning events. An event is a passage in which a Rosso et al. (2010) facet of meaning is enacted, frustrated, or wished for. Each event carries the facet, its polarity, the object implicated (a named AI system; a system implementation or rollout; an optimization practice such as staffing ratios, productivity metrics, dashboards, or documentation load; or none), the writer's stance toward that object, what became of the task involved (taken, added, reconfigured, unchanged), the emotion carried, and to whom the writer attributes the situation (the tool, management, staffing, the patient, the self). Four post-level flags record expressed intent to leave, depressed affect, anxious affect, and a sense of lacking control.

No human coding of forum posts was undertaken, and no second-model rescoring, promptperturbation check, or negative-control set has been run for this instrument. Its agreement with human judgment is known only from Study 1's participant narratives, a different genre in which the same prompt family did not meet the acceptance standard. The false-present rate on forum prose is therefore unknown, and every rate below is a rate of model-assigned codes. We treat this as the principal measurement limitation of Studies 2 and 3.

A closed-form dictionary layer (care, mastery, optimization, AI/automation, leaving or strain) was retained as archive description only. Its stems are occupational nouns: on a nursing board, a hit on the care list mostly records that a post is about nursing. We report it where it bears on coverage (how often AI vocabulary appears at all) and do not read its year rates as facet scores.

Career-Point Indicator

The allnurses author pane displays the credential line members choose to publish (e.g., "BSN, RN," "MSN, APRN, NP," "LPN," "Nursing Student"). We parsed that pane, never the post body, into a seven-level role class (student; aide/technician; LPN/LVN; RN; advanced practice; educator/manager; unclassified). A classifiable badge was present for 48% of posts; coverage varied with the page template the archive happened to serve (69% under the 2016-2018 template, 39% under the later one), so role is analyzed as a stratifier within period rather than as a population share, and role-by-year tables are confounded with markup. The badge reflects the credential at the time the page was captured, not necessarily at the time of posting.

Results

The coder returned 5,429 events (0.71 per post); 66% of posts carried no codable meaning event, which is expected in a forum where many posts are short replies. Events per post fell in 2019 (0.44) and 2021 (0.52), with 2020 at 0.66, versus 0.70-0.93 in the other capture years, the same years in which the site's page template changed and mean post length dropped; that concurrent change conditions every trend statement in Study 3.

Nurses rarely referred to AI. Across ten years and 7,695 posts, the coder found seven events in which a named AI system was the object, all in pages captured in 2020-2021 and none in 2023-2026; AI vocabulary from the dictionary layer appeared in under 1% of posts in any year. The objects nurses did name were the instruments by which their work is measured and reorganized: of 629 object events, 73% concerned an optimization practice (ratios, productivity targets, dashboards, documentation load) and 26% a system implementation or rollout. About one post in twenty carried such an object in every period (Figure S3).

Figure S4 shows events per 100 posts by facet, polarity, and period. Two facets carried most of the agency signal in every period: Competence, the most frequently enacted facet (10.7-11.6 enacted events per 100 posts outside the 2020-2022 trough), and Control/Autonomy, the most frequently frustrated facet. The communion-family facets (Dyad Connectedness, Social Identification, Value Systems, Interconnection, Self-Abnegation) together accounted for 22% of all events, more than Competence, but were distributed across five rows none of which exceeded 4.4 per 100 posts, and they were predominantly enacted (650 enacted, 444 frustrated, 92 wished for). Half of all object events (49%) targeted Control/Autonomy and a further 21% Competence.

Among the 629 object events, about three-quarters were frustrated overall (74%; 66% in 2020-2022) and about three-quarters carried a dissatisfied stance (76%, 71%, and 79% by period) (Figure S5). Attribution went to management in 51% of events and to staffing in 22%; the tool itself was blamed in 13%. These are shifts of roughly ten to thirty events per period cell and are reported as description, not as tested contrasts.

Posts that discussed an optimization or implementation object carried an expressed intent to leave at 13.8%, 14.9%, and 15.9% in the three periods, against 1.6%, 1.7%, and 1.9% among other posts (differences 12.1 pp [5.5, 19.3], 13.2 pp [3.9, 22.2], 13.9 pp [6.2, 22.6]); the lack-ofcontrol flag was set in 64-73% of object posts and 7-11% of others (Figure S6). The reverse conditional probabilities qualify this result: pooled across periods, 30% of quit-flagged posts named an object [95% CI 21, 39], so seven in ten did not; 28% of lack-of-control posts did [24, 33]; and 85% of object posts carried no quit flag. Two features of this contrast limit what it can mean. The codebook permits the lack-of-control flag to co-occur with a frustrated Control/Autonomy event, so the first row is in part one complaint counted twice; and posts that raise an optimization object are, by construction, complaint posts, so the comparison group includes questions, advice, and reminiscence. Restricted to the 1,317 posts with at least one frustrated event, the quit-intent difference between optimization complaints and other complaints shrank to 7.5 pp [1.9, 12.9] pooled (17.6% vs 10.1%) and did not exclude zero within any single period. The association is a same-post co-occurrence coded by one model in every period; it is not a temporal ordering and is not evidence that optimization talk precedes exit.

Dictionary and model layers agreed weakly at the post level: optimization dictionary density with any-object code, rpb = .17; leaving-strain density with the quit-intent flag, .16; AI dictionary hits with named-AI events, φ = .13 (4% of dictionary hits carried a named-AI event).

Event count correlated with post length (r = .33). Communion vocabulary was three times denser in posts carrying any event (0.36-0.41 hits per 100 words) than in posts carrying none (0.13), and about equally dense whether the event was a Control frustration or another frustration; 36% of Control-frustrated posts also carried a communion-family event.

A coding scheme developed on structured survey narratives does not automatically transfer to unstructured, self-selected posts, and no transfer sample exists. We treat Study 2 as a completed field description of model-assigned codes with those limits stated.

Study 3: Change in Meaning Talk Across Capture Years

Study 3 tested whether the talk described in Study 2 moved once generative AI entered the occupational conversation. The time axis remains Wayback capture year, and the 2019-2021 template change conditions every series (Figure S3). Period contrasts (2023-2026 versus 2016-2019, and versus 2020-2022) use thread-cluster percentile bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (B = 2,000) and two-sided thread-level permutation tests of period labels (B = 4,000), with Benjamini-Hochberg screening at q = .10 within each family (post-level shares; facet-by-polarity cells; object-event category shares; badge strata); all are descriptive screens, not pre-specified tests.

The share of posts carrying any optimization or implementation object was flat: 4.8% in 2016-2019 and 5.2% in 2023-2026 (difference 0.4 percentage points, 95% CI [-1.8, 2.5]; linear slope 0.00 pp per year [-0.27, 0.27]; 0.01 [-0.26, 0.28] adjusting for log post length). Named-AI events were zero in the 2023-2026 window. Neither the share of posts attending to optimization nor the presence of AI as a named object changed over the decade.

The change was in expressed control. The lack-of-control flag rose from 10.5% of posts to 14.5% (difference 4.0 pp [1.2, 6.7]; slope 0.49 pp per year [0.13, 0.89]; 0.50 [0.14, 0.85] adjusting for log post length). Frustrated Control/Autonomy was the only facet cell that grew across periods (9.6, 9.8, 12.7 frustrated events per 100 posts; 2023-2026 versus 2016-2019 difference 3.1 [0.5, 6.0], permutation p = .027), but the difference did not survive Benjamini-Hochberg screening across the 20 facet-by-polarity cells (q = .27; Table S3). Posts with at least one frustrated event rose from 16.2% to 19.8% (3.5 pp [0.2, 6.6]), but the year series shows this as recovery from the 2019-2021 trough to a level near 2016's 18.6%, and the linear slope did not exclude zero (0.35 pp per year [-0.11, 0.79]). We therefore describe rising control loss with the template caveat attached, and we do not describe rising frustration. Quit-intent, anxious, and depressed flags showed no trend. Across the eight post-level shares, the lack-of-control flag was the only contrast that survived screening (q = .028). Against 2020-2022, seven enacted facet cells rose (q < .10), which is the recovery from the template-change trough, not a change dated to 2023.

Within object events, self-attribution fell from 16% in 2016-2019 to 6% in 2023-2026, and the share of events in which the task was described as reconfigured (15% to 22%) or added to (5% to 9%) grew while "taken" stayed at 4-5%. These are cell shifts of tens of events: the selfattribution decline was -9.7 percentage points [-19.5, -1.2] (permutation p = .046, q = .46 across 19 category shares), and the task-outcome shifts did not exclude zero. RN-badged posts showed no change in object exposure (5.5% to 5.4%; -0.1 pp [-3.0, 2.7]); advanced-practice posts rose from 3.3% to 6.7% (3.4 pp [-0.2, 7.2], permutation p = .11); LPN/LVN cells were small, and student and aide posts were too few after 2019 to estimate (Figure S7). Because the badge is read at capture rather than at posting, the advanced-practice change is compatible with career progression among long-standing members as well as with rising exposure of that role.

Exposure remains the principal limitation. Capture year is not use of an AI system, and hospitalor community-level adoption would not establish that any particular writer was affected. The cross-period comparison is also among people who chose to post on one professional forum; what changed may be who posts as much as what nurses experience. We report the year contrast as a completed description of the available window.

Discussion

The studies asked which coded kinds of meaning, if any, sustain helping work and whether that map is visible in occupational talk after generative AI arrived. The pre-specified tests placed the year-ahead associations between narrated meaning dimensions and felt meaningfulness, exhaustion, and turnover intentions near zero; for next-year meaningfulness the Bayes factor (BF01 = 481.5) favors the null decisively. The same codes were associated with self-reported meaningfulness in the same wave, which supports the concurrent validity of the coding scheme. One prospective association survived screening: when Time 1 stories grounded meaning in competence or mastery, Time 2 turnover intentions and emotional exhaustion were higher. That last result is exploratory and rests on 17 present-coded cases. It was also the only coded feature that out-predicted the Time 1 survey battery for strain. Two interpretations are consistent with the result. Meaning that must be earned through demonstrated skill can become a contingent investment. Meaning staked on mastery can also convert to restlessness when the job stops supplying growth. Those readings treat the code as an anchor in the story. Whether the underlying need was met is a different question; Martela and Riekki (2018) locate meaningfulness in competence need satisfaction, not in narrative mention of mastery alone.

Codes assigned from the abstract meaning answer but absent from the story were orthogonal to the story codes. They still predicted self-reported meaning. The meaning literature already distinguishes claiming or seeking meaning from its presence; the former often tracks distress rather than adjustment (Steger et al., 2006; Park, 2010). Espousing the significance of one's work while narrating none of it may reflect that dissociation. Prompt format remains an alternative explanation.

The dark-side conjecture that motivated Block 4 (Rosso et al., 2010) could not be tested.

Almost no one narrated more than two pathways. The descriptive quadratics on exhaustion and on the two Time 2 meaning outcomes carried the predicted signs. The comparison of otheroriented and self-oriented curvature was uninformative. Whether the cost of densely invested meaning is communal or agentic remains open.

Objects of Frustrated Meaning in Nurses' Discourse

Studies 2 and 3 asked how a decade of nurses' own talk about their work relates to the account of the AI paradox developed in Study 1. The first result is an informative absence. Nurses rarely referred to artificial intelligence: seven events in 7,695 posts named an AI system, none in the 2023-2026 window, and AI vocabulary appeared in fewer than one post in a hundred in any year, including the years after generative tools entered wide public use. A study that operationalized AI exposure as mention of AI would have detected nothing and would have misattributed the reason.

What nurses discussed, in a steady one post in twenty across ten years, were the instruments that measure and reorganize their tasks: ratios, productivity targets, dashboards, documentation load, and the arrival of new systems. We interpret this as the phenomenon the AI-paradox literature describes, in the terms practitioners use for it. The paradox as formulated here is not about a technology but about a mode of organizing work in which decisions about how a task is done migrate from the person doing it to a system that scores it. Nurses have described that mode since before the current wave of tools, and their vocabulary for it is managerial and task-focused, not technological. The implication for measurement is direct: exposure to algorithmic management in occupational text must be sought through the artefacts and practices themselves, not through the word "AI," which is a researcher's category rather than a worker's. We do not claim from these data that AI is eroding meaning, or that any Rosso facet declined after 2023; the data show neither.

The second result concerns which facet carries the strain. Across periods the cell that grew was frustrated Control/Autonomy, the flag that rose was lack of control, and half of all object events landed on Control/Autonomy. Competence remained the most enacted facet in every period, and the communion-family facets were enacted far more often than frustrated. In Rosso et al.'s (2010) terms, where an optimization object is named, the facet at issue is discretion over means, not skill. Study 1's pre-specified agency paths were estimated near zero, so the prospective evidence available here is the one exploratory association that survived screening, and it runs from competence toward strain, not protection. What Studies 2 and 3 add is descriptive.

Communion deserves a separate comment because it is both pervasive and hard to code, and those facts are related. Communion vocabulary saturates the posts that carry any meaning event, but it rarely appears as a discrete episode with a polarity.

A sentence in which a nurse cannot give patients the care they deserve has care as its object and the blocked action as its marked feature; both human coders in Study 1 and the model here register it as frustrated Control, while the communion it presupposes goes uncoded. We suggest this is why inter-coder agreement on Communion sits near .5 for humans and models alike (Study 1) and why the facet is dispersed across five low-frequency facets here. That reading yields a hypothesis rather than a finding: the complaint arrives framed as autonomy loss because the injury is to communion, and autonomy over one's time is the means to it. Testing it requires a measurement approach that treats communion as a property of the post rather than of the event.

Practical Implications

Because Studies 2 and 3 are same-post co-occurrence designs in a self-selected venue, their implications concern measurement rather than intervention. The relevant exposure in nursing is not the presence of an AI tool but the manner in which any system arrives: as a metric, a new documentation duty, or a management mandate. Screening a deployment for what it does to the task (taken, added, reconfigured) and to discretion over means is consistent with the pattern here in a way that screening for "AI" is not. The sense of lacking control is the only strain marker that moved over the decade; its co-occurrence with optimization talk in the same post is partly a property of the codebook and is not evidence of temporal order, so whether discretion over means is an earlier signal than burnout inventories is a question for a lagged, within-account design that has not been run. Competence and relational connection are the meaning resources these data show still being enacted; deployments that displace skill or compress patient contact would act on the parts of the job that remain intact.

Boundary Conditions

Several boundary conditions delimit the paradox. Meaning is heterogeneous. A task that is meaning-critical for one worker may be routine for another (Tang et al., 2022). The paradox is a claim about distributions of meaning across a workforce, not a universal task ranking.

Economic context also matters. In precarious conditions the most consequential feature of work may simply be having a job. The diseconomy of scale we describe becomes decision-relevant when efficiency and retention are both at stake. Finally, the claim is about meaning-critical tasks.

Where automation removes genuinely low-meaning work, the efficiency case is intact (Albrecht et al., 2025; Olson et al., 2025).

Strengths and Limitations

Study 1 establishes a meaning-criticality map, not a measured AI effect. Its strengths are a year-lagged design, confirmatory tests at the more reliable dimensional level, and an attempt to test previously untested conjectures. Its limitations are equally clear. The Time 1 data predate the automation wave, so the paradox is read by juxtaposition. The sample is an online panel and is majority, but not exclusively, health care. Studies 2 and 3 widen the text base. They do not resolve individual-level exposure. We prefer that claim to a causal one the design cannot support.

Studies 2 and 3 carry their own limits. The instrument is a single model applied once, without human coding of forum text and without second-model, perturbation, or negative-control checks, so the false-present rate on forum prose is unknown. The frustration series is confounded with a page-template change that shortened posts and reduced codable events in 2019-2021; we report rising control loss with that caveat and do not claim rising frustration. Period is capture period, not posting period. The exposure-strain contrast compares complaint posts with all other posts and is partly circular by codebook design; most quit-flagged posts name no object. The careerpoint badge is a capture-time credential with template-dependent coverage; aides and students are sparse in the venue after 2019. The dictionaries are occupational vocabularies, not facet measures, and we draw no year-trend conclusion from them. A within-account design over the 1,494 accounts with two or more posts, using posted rather than captured dates, is the analysis that would move "indicator" from a suggestion to a claim; it has not been run.

The engineered prompts fell short of an acceptance standard tied to human-human agreement, a result about this codebook and prompt set rather than a ceiling on machine coding.

Human agreement on communion and self-directed facets was itself low. Prompts selected to match those labels can transmit that disagreement into the scores. An independent panel that applies the same scheme without being fitted to a particular human pair, then evaluated against later well-being and retention rather than against human PSA, may give more accurate estimates than either the disqualified prompts or the human composite. More accurate estimates may be more predictive. That test is the next measurement study, not a result of this article.

A further limit is that the coding scheme records presence, not salience. Human coders made binary judgments. No graded measure of how central an anchor was to the story exists in these data. The one machine attempt at such a measure did not meet the pre-set reliability bar.

Dichotomization at low base rates attenuates associations. Under a normal liability model, the observed point-biserial correlations of .28 between competence codes and Time 2 strain imply latent continuous associations of .44 to .46 (Figure S8). Those biserial estimates assume latent normality and are illustrative. They suggest that the binary results are more likely to understate than overstate the underlying continuous associations.

The pre-specified tests, the concurrent convergence, and the competence-strain association constitute the findings. The remaining screened tests bound how far those three can be interpreted.

Conclusion

In these data, no global meaning score forecast turnover intentions; the one prospective association involved the kind of meaning narrated, specifically competence, and next-year strain.

The optimization case for automating helping work still assumes a known split between routine and meaningful tasks. The pre-specified dimensional map did not forecast next-year outcomes; its estimates were bounded near zero. A decade of nurses' own talk named the instruments of optimization, not AI, and what rose in it, with the template-change caveat attached, was a sense of lacking control over means. The competence result is the one that carried prospective signal, and it is the result a designed study should attempt to falsify.
