To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 29 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Visual mental imagery is widely theorized to support memory by enhancing the richness and distinctiveness of encoded representations. However, it remains unclear how imagery ability interacts with different encoding strategies to shape memory performance. The present study examined how encoding modality (imagery vs. verbal) and processing focus (item-specific vs. relational) interact with visual imagery ability to influence veridical and false memory in the Deese-Roediger-McDermott (DRM) paradigm. A total of 173 participants (71 aphantasic participants, 12 intermediate, and 90 typical imagers) completed ten DRM lists under one of four encoding conditions in a 2 × 2 between-subjects design. Our results suggest that true recall did not differ reliably between different encoding strategies, nor was it consistently predicted by Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire (VVIQ) scores, indicating that imagery ability does not broadly determine veridical memory accuracy. In contrast, imagery-based encoding reduced false memories (critical lure recall) compared with verbal encoding across the range of imagery abilities.

Notably, individuals with aphantasia, despite reporting greater difficulty implementing imagery instructions, showed comparable benefits from imagery-based encoding. This finding suggests that the mnemonic benefits of imagery-based strategies may not depend exclusively on conscious visual imagery and may involve alternative representational processes. These findings challenge the assumption that imagery vividness predicts memory performance and open new avenues for understanding compensatory encoding mechanisms in aphantasia.

1. Introduction

Imagine you're at the grocery store with a mental list, such as sugar, honey, soda, chocolate, pie, and tart. Some items are sweet, others sour or bitter; some are round, others square or heartshaped. How do you hold onto that list? You may repeat the words over and over to yourself, or you may picture each item vividly in your mind, imagining the golden drip of honey or the texture of a piece of chocolate. You may also take a strategy that integrates each item, such as imagining yourself laying out the items in a dessert spread, or narrating to yourself a recipe incorporating these ingredients. These different approaches reflect distinct encoding strategies, and they can shape not only what you remember, but also what you mistakenly believe was on your list. In fact, you might later confidently recall "candy" as having been there, even though it never was. This phenomenon, false memory, is a topic that has long fascinated both the public and researchers. Broadly defined, it refers to the recollection of an event that did not happen, or one that occurred differently than remembered (Schacter, 1999). It demonstrates that memory is not a passive recording system but a reconstructive process shaped by meaning, context, and prior knowledge. Beyond theoretical importance, false memories carry significant real-world implications across domains such as legal systems, where eyewitness misremembering can lead to wrongful convictions (Loftus & Palmer, 1974); clinical therapy, where suggestive questioning can induce illusory recollections (Loftus, 1993); and education, where memory distortions can shape how learners encode, retrieve, and evaluate their own knowledge and performance (Kornell & Bjork, 2008). Therefore, identifying factors that enhance memory accuracy, rather than merely increasing memory accessibility, is a central goal of memory research.

Given the prevalence and consequences of false memories, researchers have examined how encoding strategies shape the formation and retrieval of memory representations. One line of research has focused on item-specific versus relational processing (which we dub here the type of "processing focus"). Relational processing promotes organization among items by emphasizing shared meaning and associations. Returning to the grocery-list example, this might involve linking the words into a story about the order in which you eat the different snacks or imagining sugar, honey, chocolate, pie, and tart arranged together on a dessert table. Although such connections can facilitate memory for the general theme of the list, they may also increase the accessibility of semantically related but non-presented items, such as candy. In contrast, item-specific processing enhances memory monitoring by strengthening representations of unique item characteristics, allowing individuals to better distinguish studied information from related but non-presented information (Huff & Bodner, 2013; Mccabe et al., 2004). In this case, people might rehearse each word individually or form a separate mental image of each item in isolation. By preserving information that differentiates one item from another, item-specific processing may support more effective memory monitoring and reduce the likelihood of falsely remembering related but unstudied items.

Another line of research has examined how encoding modality, particularly verbal versus visual-imagery-based encoding, influences memory accuracy. An imagery-based strategy might involve mentally visualizing the items, such as the chocolate. In contrast, a verbal strategy might involve rehearsing the words repeatedly or incorporating them into a sentence. According to several studies, generating mental images can reduce false memory rate compared to a nonimagery verbally-focused control condition (e.g., "Describe something that might be done with the object"; Foley et al., 2006; Robin & Mahé, 2015). One potential explanation is that compared with verbal strategies that primarily rely on semantic meaning, imagery-based strategies can create richer perceptual representations for better retrieval. This interpretation aligns with the distinctiveness heuristic framework (Dodson & Schacter, 2001), which proposes that vivid perceptual details (such as perception and imagery) provide distinctive cues at retrieval, helping individuals reject information that lacks those sensory features. Together, these findings suggest that encoding strategies play a critical role in determining memory accuracy.

However, a major assumption underlying the research on encoding modality is that all individuals possess intact mental imagery abilities. Recent research challenges this assumption with the identification of aphantasia, a condition characterized by the inability to voluntarily generate visual mental imagery (Zeman et al., 2015), despite intact visual perception and semantic knowledge about the visual properties of the world. Recent estimates suggest that aphantasia occurs in approximately 3-5% of the population, although prevalence estimates vary depending on assessment methods (Dance et al., 2022; Zeman et al., 2020). Given that imagery-based encoding strategies rely on the generation of internal visual representations, individuals with aphantasia provide a unique opportunity to examine whether the memory benefits of imagery really depend on the ability to generate visual images.

Recent evidence has provided differing perspectives on the extent to which aphantasia affects memory performance across different memory systems and retrieval demands. Although individuals with aphantasia may show relatively preserved performance on some recognition tasks (Milton et al., 2021; Pounder et al., 2022), impairments have been reported on tasks requiring the free recall or reconstruction of detailed visual information (Bainbridge et al., 2021; Monzel et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026). According to Yan and colleagues (2026), despite lacking the ability to voluntarily generate mental images, individuals with aphantasia still demonstrated a robust picture superiority effect in memory, suggesting that conscious access to visual imagery may not be necessary for the mnemonic advantage of pictures. Similarly, Keogh et al. (2021) reported that aphantasic individuals performed comparably to typical imagers on visual working memory tasks, indicating that visual information can be maintained and manipulated through mechanisms that do not depend on conscious visual imagery. However, other studies have also provided evidence that imagery ability contributes to various types of visual memory performance, with individual differences in imagery associated with differences in visual working memory, iconic memory, and number of both accurate and false details recalled from long-term memory (Bainbridge et al., 2021; Monzel et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026). These findings suggest that the relationship between visual imagery ability and memory is complex and may depend on the specific memory processes and strategies involved. 2025) compared memory performance between typical imagers and individuals with aphantasia using lists of semantically related words, with participants in both groups instructed to visualize each word as they listened to it. Across the full sample, higher VVIQ scores were significantly associated with a greater proportion of studied words recalled. However, despite this continuous relationship, participants classified as having aphantasia did not differ significantly from typical imagers in either veridical or false memory performance. These findings suggest that the absence of self-reported voluntary visual imagery does not necessarily impair memory.

One important consideration, however, is that participants with aphantasia may have relied on alternative strategies, such as verbal or semantic encoding, to compensate for their lack of visual imagery. Through everyday experience, individuals with aphantasia may have developed compensatory verbal strategies that offer a similar degree of protection against false memories as visual imagery, allowing them to achieve comparable memory performance despite relying on different encoding processes. Without comparison conditions involving other encoding strategies, it remains unclear what underlying processes account for their preserved memory performance.

Thus, to fill this key gap in the literature, the present study directly compares veridical and false memory performance between typical imagers and individuals with aphantasia across different encoding conditions. Specifically, we examine encoding strategies along two dimensions: encoding modality (verbal vs. imagery-based) and processing focus (item-specific vs. relational).

We aim to investigate how these strategies influence memory accuracy and whether their effects differ as a function of visual imagery ability. This approach gives rise to two central research questions.

First, how do different encoding modalities shape memory formation in typical imagers compared to aphantasics? Based on prior findings that visual imagery instructions reduce false memories relative to verbal instructions (Foley et al., 2006; Olszewska & Ulatowska, 2013), participants in the current study were divided into verbal and imagery modality groups. We aimed to examine whether these effects could be replicated and whether they depend on individual differences in imagery ability. Specifically, we expected an interaction between imagery ability and encoding modality, where typical imagers would show a greater reduction in false memories with imagery strategies, while aphantasic individuals might experience a weaker effect due to their lack of mental imagery ability. Moreover, we expected both groups to perform comparably under verbal encoding, where successful encoding relies less on visual imagery Second, how do different processing foci shape memory formation in typical imagers compared to individuals with aphantasia? To address this question, participants were instructed to either focus on individual item representations (item-specific) or to integrate items through meaningful associations (relational). Although previous research has examined the effects of these processing strategies on memory, their influence on memory performance in individuals with aphantasia remains largely unexplored. Drawing on evidence that associative encoding can strengthen semantic connections and increase susceptibility to false memories (Brainerd & Reyna, 2002), we predicted that participants in the relational processing condition would exhibit higher false memory rates than those in the item-specific condition.

To preview, although mental imagery has traditionally been considered an important mechanism supporting accurate memory, our findings reveal a more nuanced relationship between imagery ability and memory performance. We found that the benefits of imagery-based encoding extended beyond individuals with strong visual imagery abilities, with both typical imagers and individuals with aphantasia showing reduced false memories under imagery-based instructions compared with verbal encoding. These findings suggest that the mnemonic benefits of imagery instructions may extend beyond conscious visual imagery itself and may involve additional mechanisms, such as alternative encoding strategies, differences in interpretation of instructions, or metacognitive processes involved in evaluating one's own imagery abilities. By examining how encoding strategies operate across individuals with different imagery abilities, this study provides new insights into the relationship between mental imagery, memory formation, and the mechanisms that support accurate remembering.

2. Method

>Method

2.1 Design

The study employed a 2 × 2 × 2 between-subjects factorial design, with three independent factors: Group (Aphantasia, Typical Imagers), Encoding Modality (Verbal, Imagery), and Processing Focus (Item-specific, Relational). Participants were recruited from both the aphantasia and typical imagery populations, with each participant randomly assigned to one of the four conditions formed by crossing Modality and Focus (verbal specific, imagery specific, verbal relational, imagery relational).

2.2 Participants

All study procedures were approved by the University of Chicago Institutional Review Board (IRB19-1395), and all participants provided informed consent prior to participation.

Before data collection, we performed an a priori power analysis using G*Power to determine the required sample size. For our 2 × 2 × 2 between-subjects factorial design, we anticipated main effects of group and focus, as well as a group × modality interaction effect for both true and false memory. To select an appropriate effect size estimate, we referenced a recent study employing similar designs. According to Bainbridge et al. (2021; aphantasic N = 61, control N = 52), they reported significantly reduced recall in aphantasic participants relative to controls with a medium effect size of f = 0.295 (converted from ηp² = 0.08). We thus adopted a conservative estimate of f = 0.25, the lower bound of a medium effect and below the previously observed significant effect, to ensure adequate power even if the true effect is smaller than the prior study suggest. Using G*Power, we found that a total sample of 128 participants would be required to detect the expected main effects and interaction with 80 % power.

Participants in this study were primarily recruited from two platforms. Typical imagers were recruited online through Prolific, while participants with aphantasia were mainly recruited from the Brain Bridge Lab's Aphantasia Email List (a database of individuals who had participated in prior studies, been screened for aphantasia, and indicated willingness to participate in future research).

Several exclusion criteria were applied prior to analysis. First, participants who did not complete the entire study were excluded. Second, participants who indicated at the end of the study that their data should be excluded for any reason (e.g., technical issues like unable to hear the recording) were removed from the final sample. Third, we forgot to restrict participation to a single submission per individual, allowing a small number of participants to complete the study more than once. Thus, duplicated participants were identified, and only their first set of responses was retained for analysis. In total, five participants were excluded, yielding a final sample of 173 participants (estimated power = 90.5%), with ages ranging from 18 to 79 (M = 43.6, SD = 14.4).

Of the participants, 100 identified as women, 67 as men, and 6 as non-binary or preferred not to disclose their gender. The sample was predominantly White (N = 139), with smaller numbers identifying as Asian (N = 15), Black or African American (N = 15), Hispanic or Latino (N = 12), or other races or ethnicities (N = 11). Participants were allowed to select multiple racial and ethnic identities.

Group classification was based on scores from the Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire (VVIQ; Marks, 1973), which assesses mental imagery ability with scores ranging from 16 to 80. Following classification based on previous aphantasia research (Bainbridge et al., 2021; Pauly-Takacs et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2026; Zeman et al., 2020), participants with VVIQ scores between 16 and 23 (both inclusive) were categorized as aphantasics, whereas those scoring between 40 and 80 (both inclusive) were classified as controls. Participants with VVIQ scores between 23 and 40 were excluded from the categorical group comparisons but retained in the continuous analyses, where VVIQ score was treated as a continuous predictor to examine its relationship with memory performance across the full range of imagery ability.

Based on the classification criteria described above, the final sample comprised 71 aphantasic participants (45 women), 90 participants with typical imagery ability (49 women), and 12 participants with VVIQ scores in the intermediate range (23-40) (6 women), who were excluded from group comparisons. After exclusion, the current sample size of 161 participants still yields an estimated power of 88.3%. Demographic equivalence between the aphantasia and typical imagery groups was assessed on gender and age. Fisher's exact test indicated no significant difference in gender distribution between the two groups, p = .122. However, a Welch's independent samples t-test revealed a significant age difference, with aphantasic participants (M = 47.8, SD = 14.4, range: 18-74) being on average 6.4 years older than typical imagers (M = 41.4, SD = 13.8, range: 20-79), t(147.) = 317.028, p = .005, 95% CI [1.91, 10.79]. Given this age difference, Age was included as a covariate in all subsequent analyses.

2.3 Materials

2.3.1 DRM lists (Supplementary Methods S1.1).

The Deese-Roediger-McDermott (DRM) paradigm is one of the most widely used tools for studying false memory formation (Deese, 1959; Roediger & McDermott, 1995). In this paradigm, participants study several lists of semantically related items (e.g., bed, rest, tired, dream, wake) and are subsequently asked to recall or recognize them. Consistently, individuals tend to report the critical lure, which is a word strongly associated with the studied items but never actually presented (e.g., sleep), as having been seen or heard.

The likelihood of critical lure activation in the DRM paradigm depends largely on the strength of semantic associations among list items. One measure commonly used to quantify this associative strength is backward associative strength (BAS), which refers to the probability that studied list items will elicit the critical lure in a free association task. Prior research has consistently demonstrated a positive relationship between BAS and false memory occurrence (Knott et al., 2012; Roediger et al., 2001), such that higher BAS values are associated with greater rates of false recall and recognition. Thus, to maximize the likelihood of false memory formation in the present study, ten DRM word lists were selected on the basis of Stadler et al.'s (1999) norms and high BAS values reported by Knott et al. (2012).

Each list contains 15 semantically related words presented in descending associative strength relative to its critical lure, with one critical lure identified for each list. This ordering was intended to facilitate progressive activation of the critical lure in memory across the presentation of each list. All DRM stimuli were presented auditorily rather than visually to minimize potential advantages from visual encoding, particularly for typical imagers who may benefit from visually inspecting and remembering the presented words. Auditory presentation also reduced potential confounds across encoding conditions by ensuring that participants, especially those in the verbal encoding conditions, could engage with the intended encoding strategy without relying on visual word representations. All auditory stimuli were generated using Google Text-to-Speech (gTTS) and combined with 2-second inter-stimulus intervals in Audacity.

2.3.2 VVIQ (Marks, 1973).

The Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire is a widely recognized and well-validated measure of imagery vividness (Campos, 2011). It comprises 16 items grouped into four categories.

Participants were instructed to visualize specific scenes or scenarios and rate the clarity of their mental images using a five-point scale, where1 = "no image at all" and5 = "perfectly clear and as vivid as normal vision." The total VVIQ score was calculated by summing participants' responses, yielding an overall vividness score ranging from 16 to 80, with higher scores corresponding to greater mental imagery experience.

2.4 Procedure

The experiment was implemented in PsychoPy (v2025.1.1) and conducted online via Pavlovia. After accessing the study link, participants were assigned a unique participant ID and asked to provide demographic information. They were then instructed to wear headphones to ensure optimal audio quality and proceed to an audio screening task. During this task, participants clicked a sound button on the screen (which plays the spoken word "test") and were permitted to replay it as many times as needed. On the following page, they were asked to indicate whether they could hear the audio (yes/no) and to type the word they had heard. Participants who reported being unable to hear the audio or who entered an incorrect response were directed to exit the study and were excluded from further participation.

Going into the main task (Figure 1), participants were randomly assigned to one of four encoding strategy groups, asked to read the corresponding instructions and follow the instructed method of encoding, and were informed that their memory for the lists would be tested later (Supplementary Methods S1.2). The four groups are verbal specific, imagery specific, verbal relational, and imagery relational. The verbal specific group was asked to mentally repeat each word until the next word appeared. The imagery specific group was asked to form a picture-like mental image for each word until the next word appeared. The verbal relational group was asked to link all the words by mentally forming a purely verbal story based on the words' meanings.

Finally, the imagery relational group was asked to link all the words by forming a visual scene or movie.

Participants listened to ten DRM word lists in total (study phase), each containing 15 items, presented auditorily with 2-second inter-stimulus intervals between words and no visual display (e.g. Bed, Rest, Awake, Tired, Dream, Wake, Snooze, Blanket, Doze, Slumber, Snore, Nap, Peace, Yawn, Drowsy). After each study phase, participants completed five simple math problems (filler task) and then typed as many recalled words as possible (recall phase). To encourage deliberate recall, a minimum response time of 8 seconds was imposed. If participants attempted to submit their responses before this interval, they received a reminder to take their time and avoid rushing through the task. Participants were given unlimited time to complete the recall phase.

After each recall test, participants completed an encoding strategy check by indicating whether they had successfully followed the assigned instructions for that word list (yes/no). If they indicated that they did not follow the instructions, they were prompted to report the strategy they used for that particular list through a free-response question. Although no minimum response time was imposed for this question, participants were required to provide a response before proceeding.

After completing all study and recall phases, participants were given the opportunity to request exclusion of their data if they experienced any technical difficulties during the experiment.

Then, they filled out the 16-item VVIQ (Marks, 1973) to assess their imagery ability. Imagery Relational). Participants then completed 10 study-recall cycles, each consisting of an auditorily presented list of 15 semantically related words (2-s inter-stimulus interval), a filler task involving five arithmetic problems, and a free-recall task in which they typed all remembered words. The order of the 10 wordlists was randomized across participants. Following each recall task, participants indicated whether they had followed the assigned encoding instructions and, if not, reported the strategy used instead. After completing all 10 cycles, participants reported any issues that might warrant exclusion of their data (e.g., technical difficulties) and completed the Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire (VVIQ).

2.5 Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted in RStudio (version 2026.10.0). Exclusion criteria are described in the Participants section, and raw recall responses were preprocessed prior to scoring as described below.

2.5.1 Recall Response Coding.

Raw free-text recall responses were converted to lowercase and split into individual words by tokenizing on spaces, commas, and periods. To account for spelling errors and typographical variations, each recalled word was compared against the valid words for its corresponding DRM list using the Jaro-Winkler string distance metric (p = 0.1; Jaro, 1989; Winkler, n.d.). Words were automatically corrected to their closest valid match if the distance fell at or below a threshold of 0.161. The threshold of 0.161 was selected through manual inspection of recalled words, proposed corrections, and match scores. Exact matches were retained without correction. Following automated correction, all flagged responses were manually audited, and a small number of additional corrections were applied by hand to address cases of plausible but incorrect matches (e.g., cill → sill). All preprocessing was implemented in R using the stringdist and tidyverse packages.

2.5.2 Analytical Approach.

Three dependent variables were derived from the coded recall responses, each analyzed using a method appropriate to its properties.

True recall proportion was calculated as the number of studied words correctly recalled divided by the total number of studied words, representing a continuous measure of veridical memory performance. Prior to conducting the analysis, assumptions for the ANCOVA were evaluated. Normality of residuals was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene's test. Homogeneity of regression slopes was evaluated by examining interactions between the covariate (age) and experimental factors. As all assumptions were satisfied, a 2 × 2 × 2 ANCOVA was conducted with age included as a covariate.

Lure recall was coded as a binary variable indicating whether a participant recalled the critical lure for a given list. Given its binary nature, a binomial mixed-effects logistic regression was performed with participant included as a random intercept. Fixed effects included continuous predictors (mean-centered age and VVIQ score) and categorical predictors (encoding modality and processing focus), as well as all two-and three-way interactions among these factors.

Intrusion count was operationalized as a count variable reflecting the total number of extralist words, words neither studied nor designated as critical lures, recalled across all lists. Given that intrusion counts were highly zero-inflated and represented count data, a negative binomial regression was conducted with an offset for the number of lists presented to account for differences in exposure.

3.1.1 True recall

True recall provides an index of memory for actual experiences and allows us to determine whether the encoding strategies and imagery ability enhance memory strength. Based on previous research (Foley et al., 2006; Olszewska & Ulatowska, 2013), we hypothesized that encoding modality would interact with imagery ability, such that typical imagers would show enhanced recall for studied words following imagery-based encoding, whereas individuals with aphantasia would experience less benefit from imagery instructions due to their reduced imagery ability. In addition, as relational processing promotes the formation of connections among related items, we expect a main effect of processing focus, with participants in the relational processing condition recalling more studied words than those in the item-specific processing condition.

3.1.2 Critical Lure Recall

Critical lure recall reflects the extent to which participants extract the underlying meaning of the studied information and later mistakenly attribute that semantic representation to a specific studied event. As imagery-based encoding can provide richer perceptual representations and more distinctive retrieval cues, we expected an interaction between imagery ability and encoding modality, such that typical imagers would show a greater reduction in false memories following imagery-based encoding, whereas individuals with aphantasia would benefit less from imagery instructions due to their lack of imagery ability. We further hypothesized that participants in the relational processing condition would show higher rates of critical lure recall than those in the item-specific processing condition, as relational processing promotes the formation of connections among related items.

For critical lure recall (scored as 0 or 1 per trial), a binomial mixed-effects logistic regression was performed, with participant as a random intercept. The model revealed a significant main effect of encoding modality. Compared with verbal encoding, imagery-based encoding significantly decreased the likelihood of recalling critical lures (ꞵ = -.265, SE = 0.092, z = -2.88, p = .004, OR = 0.59, 95% CI [0.41, 0.84]; Figure 3A and Figure 3B). A significant interaction between encoding modality and processing focus was also observed (β = -.216, SE = 0.093, z = -2.33, p = .020; Figure 3C). This interaction indicated that the effect of encoding modality on critical lure recall differed depending on processing focus. No other main effects or interactions reached significance (all ps > .12; Supplementary Results Table 2).

The modality × focus interaction was followed up with an analysis of simple effects.

Imagery-based strategy significantly reduced the likelihood of recalling the critical lure compared with verbal strategy when performing relational processing (OR = 0.38, 95% CI [0.23, 0.65], z = -3.60, p < .001; Figure 3C). In contrast, the two modalities did not differ in item-specific processing (OR = 0.91, 95% CI [0.55, 1.50], z = -0.38, p = .701). Processing focus did not significantly affect lure recall within either modality (imagery: OR = 0.64, 95% CI [0.38, 1.08], z = -1.67, p = .096; verbal: OR = 1.52, 95% CI [0.92, 2.50], z = 1.64, p = .100).

Taken together, these findings indicate that imagery-based strategy can reduce critical lure recall relative to verbal strategy. The significant interaction between encoding modality and processing focus indicates that the benefit of imagery-based strategy was more pronounced under relational encoding, whereas no reliable difference between modalities was observed under itemspecific encoding.

Importantly, although we expected typical imagers to show greater benefits from imagerybased encoding due to their ability to generate mental images, the absence of performance differences between imagery ability groups suggests that the protective effect of imagery instructions may not depend exclusively on conscious visual imagery.

3.1.3 Intrusion Errors

Unlike critical lure recall, intrusion errors reflect broader failures in memory monitoring and source discrimination, including, guessing, weak encoding, less constrained retrieval, or difficulties distinguishing between studied and internally generated information. As intrusion errors and critical lure recall all represent forms of false memory, we hypothesized that intrusion errors would show a pattern similar to critical lure recall across encoding conditions. However, we expect that intrusion errors may exhibit greater variability and a higher proportion of zero responses due to their less systematic and less predictable nature.

Intrusion errors were analyzed as counts using negative binomial regression with an offset for the number of lists presented. A preliminary linear model revealed severely non-normal residuals (Shapiro-Wilk: W = 0.836, p < .001), justifying this approach.

Although most main effects and interactions were not significant (all ps > .11; Supplementary Results Table 3), a significant VVIQ group × processing focus interaction was observed (ꞵ = .205, SE = 0.091, z = 2.24, p = .025, IRR = 2.27, 95% CI [1.03, 1.47]; Figure 4A and Figure 4B), reflecting a crossover pattern whereby aphantasic participants showed relatively more intrusions under relational encoding but fewer under item-specific encoding compared to typical imagers.

3.3.2 Strategy Use in Aphantasia

The significant modality effect on lure recall in aphantasic participants, combined with the absence of a VVIQ group × modality interaction, prompted further exploration of whether aphantasic and typical imaging participants differed in their ability to adhere to the instructed encoding strategy. Specifically, we examined whether aphantasic participants showed greater difficulty implementing imagery-based encoding strategies compared with typical imagers, and whether strategy non-compliance differed between imagery and verbal encoding conditions within the aphantasia group.

Strategy non-compliance was quantified as the number of trials in which participants reported that they failed to successfully implement the instructed encoding strategy. It was examined using a binomial mixed-effects logistic regression with a random intercept for participant. The model revealed significant main effects of VVIQ group (β = 3.985, SE = .850, z = 4.69, p < .001), encoding modality (β = 2.425, SE = .824, z = 2.94, p = .003), processing focus (β = 2.419, SE = .756, z = 3.20, p = .001), as well as a significant interaction between VVIQ group and encoding modality (β = 2.492, SE = .820, z = 2.03, p = .002).

Follow-up pairwise contrasts indicated that this interaction was driven almost entirely by the imagery encoding condition. Aphantasic participants reported substantially higher strategy non-compliance during imagery encoding than typical imagers, with aphantasic participants showing near-ceiling non-compliance and typical imagers showing near-floor non-compliance (OR = 422677.8, 95% CI [2570.07, 69514291], z = 4.98, p < .001). In other words, aphantasic participants reported that they could not use visual imagery to remember these words, consistent with self-reported difficulties in generating voluntary visual imagery among individuals with aphantasia. In contrast, no significant group difference emerged in the verbal condition (OR = 19.8, 95% CI [ 0.33, 1202], z = 1.424, p = .154), suggesting that both groups were similarly able to implement verbal encoding strategies. Within-group comparisons further demonstrated that aphantasic participants showed substantially greater non-compliance during imagery encoding than verbal encoding (OR = 1.87 × 10 4 , 95% CI [88.36, 3950274], z = 3.60, p < .001). On the other hand, typical imagers showed consistently low levels of non-compliance across both imagery and verbal encoding conditions (OR = 87.4, 95% CI [0.02, 32], z = -0.07, p = .942; Figure 9).

Based on these findings, we confirmed that aphantasic participants reported substantial difficulty implementing imagery-based encoding instructions, despite the earlier observation that imagery-based encoding reduced critical lure recall in this group. Therefore, the observed benefit of imagery instructions in aphantasia cannot be fully explained by successful engagement in voluntary visual imagery. Instead, the benefit may reflect the involvement of alternative mechanisms or differences in metacognitive awareness that influence how individuals with aphantasia interpret and implement imagery-based instructions.

4. Discussion

Visual imagery has long been considered an important mechanism supporting accurate memory, including reducing susceptibility to false memories (Foley et al., 2006; Robin & Mahé, 2015). However, the case of aphantasia, a condition characterized by the reported inability to voluntarily generate mental imagery, raises a fundamental question: Are individuals who lack conscious access to visual imagery more vulnerable to false memories? More broadly, if individuals with aphantasia can achieve comparable memory accuracy, does this suggest that visual imagery itself is not necessary for the mnemonic benefits typically attributed to imagerybased encoding? The present study addressed these questions by examining how encoding strategies interact with imagery ability to influence both veridical and false memory.

Based on the role of visual imagery in memory encoding, we hypothesized that aphantasic participants would perform similarly to typical imagers under verbal encoding conditions but would show little or no benefits from visual imagery-based encoding. Specifically, we expected that typical imagers would benefit more from imagery-based encoding because they could generate richer mental representations, whereas individuals with aphantasia would show less differentiation between encoding approaches.

Contrary to expectations, the present study did not find any meaningful main effects and interactions in true recall proportion between aphantasic and typical imaging participants.

However, a notable exception emerged when instead modeling imagery ability as a continuous on other processes, such as building relationships among items or using verbal strategies, the contribution of imagery ability may become less apparent. Thus, differences in voluntary imagery ability may influence memory under specific circumstances rather than producing broad changes in memory performance. These findings contribute to a growing and somewhat mixed literature on the relationship between imagery vividness and correct memory performance. A growing body of evidence suggests that the relationship between VVIQ and object memory performance is weaker than theoretical accounts predict (Keogh et al., 2021; Siena & Simons, 2024; Tabi et al., 2022). The absence of a robust relationship between imagery vividness and true recall in the present study is perhaps unsurprising when considered in a broader context: if aphantasia were associated with severe and pervasive memory deficits, it is unlikely that the condition would have gone largely unrecognized until relatively recently, suggesting that the memory system is capable of compensating for the absence of voluntary mental imagery through alternative encoding mechanisms.

For false memory, we predicted that the effects of encoding strategies would depend on individual differences in imagery ability. Specifically, we expected imagery-based encoding to reduce susceptibility to false memories, particularly among individuals with stronger imagery abilities, whereas individuals with aphantasia would show limited differences between verbal and imagery-based approaches. We also predicted that relational processing would increase false memory susceptibility compared with item-specific processing. This prediction was based on previous research suggesting that relational encoding promotes the formation of shared meanings and associative links among studied items, which can increase activation of related but nonpresented concepts and, consequently, increase the likelihood of falsely recalling critical lures (Huff & Bodner, 2013; Mccabe et al., 2004). Consistent with previous findings, we observed a main effect of encoding modality, such that imagery-based encoding reduced critical lure recall compared with verbal encoding. We also observed an interaction between encoding modality and processing focus. This interaction appeared to be driven primarily by memory performance differences between imagery and verbal strategies when participants engaged in relational processing.

In isolation, this result would be unremarkable, as it replicates well-established findings in the false memory literature (Foley et al., 2006; Olszewska & Ulatowska, 2013). What makes it interesting is who showed the effect: Surprisingly, we did not find evidence that imagery ability moderated the effects of encoding strategies on false memory. Instead, both typical imagers and individuals with aphantasia showed reduced false memory following imagery-based encoding.

Importantly, an exploratory, separate model tested specifically within individuals with aphantasia still revealed the main effect of modality consistently, suggesting that they still somehow benefited from imagery encoding instructions in protecting against false memory to the same extent as typical imagers.

Aphantasic participants, by their own report, are unable to form voluntary mental images.

Thus, they should not be able to perform the imagery instructions provided in the current study.

Our exploratory analysis supported this claim by indicating a significantly higher strategy noncompliance trial count for aphantasic participants compared to typical imaging participants when trying to employ imagery-based strategies. If aphantasic individuals cannot generate the distinctive visual representations that imagery encoding is thought to produce, they should not benefit from such a strategy.

There could be two competing interpretations. One possibility is that aphantasia reflects a deficit in metacognitive access rather than a complete absence of imagery-related representations.

According to this view, individuals with aphantasia may generate some form of visual imagery but lack conscious awareness of these internal experiences, leading them to report an inability to visualize. This is consistent with proposals that aphantasia may reflect a deficit in introspective access rather than in imagery generation (Michel et al., 2025; Siena & Simons, 2024). However, several findings suggest that reduced conscious access to imagery may not fully explain aphantasia.

For example, Kay et al. (2022) demonstrated that typical imagers exhibit pupillary light response to illusory brightness, whereas individuals with aphantasia do not show comparable imageryinduced pupillary responses despite demonstrating intact perceptual pupillary responses. These findings suggest that the impairment in voluntary imagery in aphantasia may reflect a genuine difference in the generation of internally simulated visual experiences rather than solely a failure to introspect on existing imagery.

An alternative possibility is that individuals with aphantasia rely on effective nonvisual strategies that support memory performance despite the absence of voluntary imagery. For example, Keogh et al. (2021) found that individuals with aphantasia performed comparably to typical imagers on visual working memory tasks, suggesting the use of a non-visual, non-verbal strategy. Similarly, qualitative work by Hayes et al. (2026) indicates that individuals with aphantasia often rely on alternative memory strategies. These strategies may provide alternative pathways for encoding and retrieving information when visual imagery is unavailable.

Existing accounts of compensation have primarily emphasized verbal or semantic strategies. However, the present findings raise the possibility that the mechanisms supporting imagery-based encoding in aphantasia may not be fully captured by either visual imagery or traditional verbal encoding. Rather, imagery instructions may encourage aphantasic participants to engage in other forms of representational processing. For example, the relational imagery instruction used in the present study asked participants to "build a visual scene or movie" (Supplementary Methods S1.2). Although this instruction was designed to elicit visual imagery, it also inherently involves spatial organization and conceptual integration among items. According to Bainbridge et al. (2021), aphantasic individuals recall significantly fewer objects than controls but exhibit high spatial accuracy, comparable to controls. This suggests that aphantasics may use a spatial strategy to remember the items on the DRM list, which does not require visual imagery.

Further supporting the possibility that individuals with aphantasia relied on alternative encoding strategies, some participants with aphantasia reported, "While I was not able to visualize these words together, I did conceptualize a room in which these items would be located together," or "No mental image formed by the words. However, I do hold them together conceptually as related."

These additional processes may provide alternative pathways through which imagery-based instructions enhance memory performance in individuals with aphantasia. This hypothesis remains speculative and should be tested in future work.

The interaction between imagery ability and processing focus on intrusion errors requires careful interpretation. Contrary to our initial prediction that relational encoding would generally increase intrusion errors by promoting associative activation, the effect of processing focus depended on imagery ability. Specifically, higher VVIQ scores were associated with a trend toward fewer intrusions under relational encoding but greater intrusions under item-specific encoding.

Examining these patterns descriptively, individuals with aphantasia showed numerically higher intrusion rates under relational encoding than item-specific encoding, consistent with standard DRM predictions that relational processing strengthens associative connections among studied items, thereby increasing activation of related but non-presented concepts (Brainerd & Reyna, 2002; Roediger & McDermott, 1995). In contrast, typical imagers showed the opposite numerical pattern, with higher intrusion rates under item-specific than relational encoding. Although this pattern deviates from traditional DRM predictions, it suggests that the consequences of relational and item-specific encoding may depend on the representational strategies available to individuals with different levels of imagery ability.

One possible explanation for this pattern concerns how relational encoding is implemented across individuals with different imagery abilities. For typical imagers, relational encoding may allow the construction of a rich and coherent scene or narrative in which individual items are embedded within a distinctive episodic context. Such structured representations may enhance source monitoring during retrieval by allowing individuals to distinguish between items that were actually experienced within the imagined context and related but non-presented concepts (Johnson et al., 1993). In contrast, item-specific encoding requires participants to generate separate representations for individual items without integrating them into a broader context. Although these representations may remain vivid, they may provide fewer contextual cues to support source discrimination. At the same time, the semantic relationships among items remain available and can activate related concepts, increasing susceptibility to intrusion errors. Thus, item-specific encoding may produce strong memory traces for individual items while offering less protection against semantically related false memories.

For individuals with aphantasia, however, relational encoding may operate differently.

Without access to voluntary visual imagery, instructions to construct scenes or narratives may not produce the same type of distinctive episodic representation. Instead, aphantasic individuals may rely more heavily on conceptual, spatial, or semantic associations when engaging in relational encoding. While these strategies may support organization and integration, they may also strengthen connections among related concepts, increasing activation of non-presented lures during recall. Conversely, item-specific encoding may reduce opportunities for such associative activation by encouraging attention to individual items rather than their relationships with other concepts.

However, this interpretation remains speculative because the observed trends did not yield significant conditional effects. The relationship between imagery ability and intrusion errors did not reach statistical significance within either relational or item-specific encoding. Therefore, these findings should be considered a hypothesis-generating observation rather than a definitive explanation. Future studies directly assessing the strategies used during different forms of encoding will be necessary to determine whether individuals with and without imagery rely on different representational mechanisms during memory formation.

The current study does have some limitations, particularly regarding the nature of the encoding instructions. The verbal relational condition, which required participants to form a verbal story, may have inadvertently elicited mental imagery in typical imagers. Narrative construction naturally engages imagery-like processing (Zwaan & Radvansky, 1998), suggesting that the modality manipulation may not have created a clear distinction between imagery and verbal encoding strategies. However, the significant effects observed in the current study across modality conditions suggest that encoding strategy did influence memory performance, and future research should examine more carefully what drives differential engagement with each modality and how cross-strategy contamination can be minimized. Moreover, the current study relied heavily on participants' self-reported VVIQ and strategy compliance, which is susceptible to metacognitive inaccuracy and social desirability. Future studies should seek more objective methods to limit participants to specific encoding strategies, ensuring a more accurate assessment of the main effects and interactions.

In summary, the present findings contribute to a broader understanding of how individual differences in mental imagery shape memory encoding and false memory susceptibility. The absence of group differences in true recall suggests that imagery vividness does not straightforwardly translate into memory performance advantages under controlled encoding conditions, consistent with a growing body of evidence challenging simple imagery-memory relationships. Moreover, by demonstrating that imagery encoding reduces critical lure recall regardless of imagery ability, the current study raises questions about the mechanisms underlying imagery encoding effects in the DRM paradigm, and opens the possibility that spatial or other nonvisual, non-verbal representational strategies may serve as functional equivalents to visual imagery in memory. Practically, the results also suggest that imagery encoding instructions may benefit a broader population than previously assumed, extending even to individuals who report being unable to form mental images, though the mechanisms through which this occurs remain to be established. Future research should employ objective measures of encoding strategy compliance, more precisely controlled encoding conditions, and larger samples to test these possibilities directly and to determine whether the tentative VVIQ × processing focus interaction for intrusion errors reflects a genuine and theoretically meaningful boundary condition on the protective effects of elaborative encoding.
