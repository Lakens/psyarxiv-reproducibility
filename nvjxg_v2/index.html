Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module retrieves information from repositories.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

Studies examining whether knowing the color of an upcoming distractor (i.e., a negative cue) benefits performance in a subsequent search task have reported inconsistent findings. A central question is whether negatively cued features can be preemptively suppressed (i.e., before distractors capture attention) or whether they initially capture attention and are then handled reactively (i.e., after capture). Negative cueing benefits tend to emerge in difficult search, but neural evidence is rare. To examine the boundary of such benefits and their neural basis, we conducted two experiments. Experiment 1 manipulated task difficulty through the number of task-relevant items while keeping the overall set size constant. Results showed that negative cues speeded up search responses in difficult search (8 task-relevant items) but not in easy search (4 items). Experiment 2 recorded EEG during difficult search to reveal how negatively cued distractors are processed. Preemptive suppression predicts a distractor positivity (P D ), without a distractor N2pc. In contrast, reactive suppression predicts an initial distractor N2pc, followed by a P D . Behavioral results showed that negative cues neither speeded response, nor yielded any cost. ERP results showed that negatively cued distractor elicited a weak N2pc, and no later P D followed it. In addition, alpha activities before the search display did not differ between negative and neutral cues. We therefore found no evidence for either preemptive or reactive suppression of the negatively cued distractors, leaving open whether benefits of negative cues rely on suppression.

1 Introduction

Visual search is often guided by explicit goals held in working memory (Folk et al., 1992; Gaspelin & Luck, 2018; Wolfe, 2021; Yu et al., 2023). When target features are specified beforehand, individuals can develop target templates that selectively direct attention toward items that match the target features, thereby enhancing search efficiency (Geng & Witkowski, 2019; Grubert et al., 2016; Hollingworth & Beck, 2016; Woodman & Luck, 2007). A growing body of research examines whether negative cues, which provide advance knowledge of distractor features, facilitate the formation of templates for rejection (also termed negative templates) and thus guide attention away from distracting items. However, empirical findings on this topic remain inconsistent, and its underlying mechanisms require further clarification (Carlisle, 2023; Chelazzi et al., 2019).

1.1 Hypotheses about distractor handling Two major hypotheses have been proposed to account for cue-guided rejection. The "search and destroy" hypothesis posits that attention first goes to cued distractor features before they can be successfully ignored (Moher & Egeth, 2012), leading to a two-stage process (search and destroy). Evidence supporting this view derives from several studies. Moher and Egeth (2012), for instance, combined a cued search task with a probe-dot detection task that had to be performed on some trials. In this task, participants were asked to report a briefly presented probe at the location of one of the previously presented search items. By varying the time interval between the search display and the onset of the probe, the authors were able to track attentional allocation over time. With short intervals, participants responded faster to probes presented at locations that matched cued distractor features, indicating initial attention deployment to those features, even when they characterized the distractors participants were instructed to ignore. With longer intervals, however, probes at these locations produced slower responses, indicating successful ignoring at a later period. Follow-up eye-tracking studies revealed that participants' initial fixations landed more often on items matching the cued distractor features, whereas subsequent fixations were less likely than expected by chance (Beck et al., 2018). These findings provide direct support for the "search and destroy" hypothesis. Conversely, the "preemptive suppression" hypothesis posits that cued distractor features can be suppressed without initially attending to them (Carlisle, 2023; Carlisle & Nitka, 2019; Zhang et al., 2020). This view aligns with the "signal suppression" hypothesis, which proposes that a salient distractor singleton can be suppressed by learning to avoid capture (Gaspelin & Luck, 2018; Gaspelin et al., 2025; Sawaki & Luck, 2010). For instance, Arita and colleagues (2012) employed a negative cue to indicate the upcoming distractor color and instructed participants to search for a target with a gap on its top or bottom. Their results showed that negative cues led to faster response times (RTs) compared to neutral, uninformative cues, prompting the authors to propose that participants generated a template for rejection to guide attention away from distractors matching the cued color. In a follow-up study, Zhang and colleagues (2020) integrated a letterprobe task (on 1/3 of trials) with a cued search task (on 2/3 of trials) and varied the interval between search display and probes. Probe responses enabled them to examine the attentional allocation among search items at different time points.

At short intervals, there was no evidence for an increase in probe responses for probes presented at locations matching the cued distractor features (Zhang et al., 2020). These findings stand in stark contrast to the "search and destroy" hypothesis, which predicts increased letter reports at those items with short intervals, followed by fewer reports with longer time intervals-a pattern not seen in the data (see also Zhang & Carlisle, 2023). In the meantime, no decreases were found for those probe responses, a finding not directly supporting the "preemptive suppression" hypothesis.

Beyond these contrasting hypotheses, alternative suggestions have been made to account for the observed negative cueing benefits in the literature (Beck & Hollingworth, 2015; Becker et al., 2015). Becker and colleagues (2015), for instance, argued that with two-colored search displays, participants can employ a color recoding strategy to facilitate the search process. Specifically, participants can wait until the search display is presented, identify the color that does not match the negative cue, and then focus their search on items of the non-cued color. In other words, the negative cue is used to generate a positive color template for identifying the target. Similarly, a spatial recoding strategy can be applied when target-colored and distractor-colored items are presented in spatially separated locations (Beck & Hollingworth, 2015).

In a recent study, Zhang and Carlisle (2023) randomly intermixed a small portion of multi-color search displays (featuring either spatially separated (MS) or intermixed (MI) items) with a majority of two-color spatially separated (2S) displays.

The authors hypothesized that since the majority of trials consisted of 2S display, the attentional control settings established for these displays would generalize to the few multi-color displays. According to the color or spatial recoding hypothesis, negative cues should facilitate search in 2S displays because participants can set up a positive template, based on either color or location, for the non-cued items. This strategy, however, should not be transferable to MI displays, where participants would be unable to use the negative cue to generate a positive color or spatial template. Contrary to this prediction, their results revealed a negative cueing benefit even in the multi-color displays (MS and MI), leading the authors to conclude that neither color nor spatial recoding can fully explain the observed negative cueing benefits.

1.2 Search-locked ERPs preceded by negative cues

Alongside the debate, a clear consensus on the neural mechanisms remains elusive. Some studies have utilized the N2pc to examine whether negative cues affect attentional processes in visual search (Berggren & Eimer, 2021; Carlisle & Nitka, 2019; Kerzel & Huynh Cong, 2023; Redding & Fiebelkorn, 2024; Salahub & Emrich, 2021; van Zoest et al., 2021) . The N2pc is a lateralized negativity, observed around 200 ms post-stimulus at posterior electrodes, and is considered to reflect attention allocation toward an item in the contralateral hemifield. It has been used to track either target selection (Eimer, 1996; Luck & Hillyard, 1994) or attentional capture by distractors (Balke et al., 2021; Feldmann-Wüstefeld et al., 2021; Gaspelin & Luck, 2019; Liesefeld et al., 2017). This second use is central to the "search and destroy" hypothesis (Moher & Egeth, 2012), as a distractor N2pc would index its capture stage. For instance, Carlisle and Nitka (2019) tested this prediction with a search task in which target and distractor items appeared in two different colors on opposite sides of the display. Each search display was preceded by either a positive cue (indicating the target color), a negative cue (indicating the distractor color), or a neutral cue (indicating a color not appearing in the subsequent search display). When the search task was preceded by a negative cue, there was no evidence for a distractor N2pc; instead, the results showed an N2pc toward the side of the target items (Carlisle & Nitka, 2019). These findings suggest no attentional capture by negatively cued distractors. Yet, given that the target and distractor items appeared on opposite sides in Carlisle and Nitka's study (2019), the data contained contributions from both target and distractor processing, which are difficult to disentangle (Wöstmann et al., 2022).

Other ERP studies have sought to disentangle distractor processing from target processing by presenting search items not only laterally (to the left and right of fixation), but also vertically (above and below fixation; e.g., Berggren & Eimer, 2021; Kerzel & Huynh Cong, 2023; Redding & Fiebelkorn, 2024; Salahub & Emrich, 2021; van Zoest et al., 2021), thereby facilitating the measurement of distractor suppression through P D components. The P D is a positive deflection observed at posterior electrodes contralateral to a lateralized distractor (Gaspar et al., 2016; Gaspar & McDonald, 2014; Hickey et al., 2009; Liesefeld et al., 2020). Notably, P D latency varies substantially across studies, ranging from approximately 147 ms to 364 ms post-stimulus (Gaspelin et al., 2023). An early P D (100-200 ms), occurring before the N2pc, has been interpreted as marker of preemptive suppression, a mechanism preventing attention from being captured by a salient distractor (Gaspelin & Luck, 2018; Liesefeld et al., 2024; Sawaki & Luck, 2010; Stilwell et al., 2022). However, McDonald et al. (2026) have reported this early positivity regardless of whether a salient singleton was ignored or actively searched for, directly challenging its interpretation as the suppression signal. Instead, this early positivity was suggested to simply reflect a general marker of visual salience (termed "Ppc" or contralateral posterior positivity) that can be elicited by any salient item, for instance, also by lateral targets (Corriveau et al., 2012; Fortier-Gauthier et al., 2012; Kerzel & Huynh Cong, 2023; Oxner et al., 2024 Oxner et al., , 2025)). The P D is more typically observed at around 200-400 ms, often following the N2pc, reflecting reactive distractor suppression after initial attentional capture (Feldmann-Wüstefeld et al., 2015; Liesefeld et al., 2017 Liesefeld et al., , 2022 Liesefeld et al., , 2024;; van Moorselaar & Slagter, 2019). In a study by Berggren and Eimer (2021), search displays contained four items in two different colors: one target-colored item appeared laterally and another vertically, while two distractor-colored items filled the remaining locations. Cues (positive, negative, or neutral) were presented before each search display, following the design of Arita et al. (2012; see also Carlisle & Nitka, 2019). Their results showed no significant N2pc contralateral to the negatively cued distractor (Berggren & Eimer, 2021), again indicating no capture by cued distractors. However, negatively cued distractors also failed to elicit a significant P D when cues varied on a trial-by-trial basis, leading the authors to conclude that there was no evidence in favor of "preemptive suppression" (Berggren & Eimer, 2021). Similarly, Salahub and Emrich (2021) used search displays with one targetcolored and one distractor-colored circle, accompanied by eight task-irrelevant gray circles. EEG results again showed no distractor N2pc for the negatively cued item, nor was there any difference in the EEG pattern between distractors preceded by negative cues and those preceded by neutral cues.

1.3 Increasing task demands promotes negative cue use

Notably, the lack of RT benefits for negative cues in some studies (Berggren & Eimer, 2021; Salahub & Emrich, 2021) casts doubt on whether these cues were actually used in these tasks. Therefore, it is essential to reconsider the conditions under which negative cues become effective. A seminal study by Arita and colleagues (2012) reported significant negative cueing benefits when participants searched through displays containing eight or more items, but not when smaller set sizes were used. Similarly, Berggren and Eimer (2021) found no such benefits when their search displays included only four items. Thus, participants seem more likely to adopt a voluntary strategy when the set size increases. Yet the relationship between set size and negative cueing benefit is not straightforward. For example, Salahub and Emrich (2021) used 10 items but still failed to find negative cueing benefits. Notably, only two items in their displays were color-defined and thus task-relevant; the remaining eight gray items were task-irrelevant and could be easily ignored. This may have made the search task perceptually simple, reducing the incentive to use negative cues. Similarly, Conci and colleagues (2019) showed that even with 8 task-relevant items, negative cueing benefits emerged only when distractors were perceptually similar to the target-making the search more difficult. Together, these findings suggest that the emergence of negative cueing benefits depends more on task demands than on the sheer number of items in the display.

1.4 Alpha-band activity for distractor preparation

Beyond the role of cues in search-locked processing, another important yet unresolved question is whether negative cues can modulate neural activities prior to the onset of the search display. Preparatory alpha-band activity (8-13 Hz), particularly over posterior regions, has emerged as a neural marker of anticipatory attention. A growing body of research suggests that increased alpha power reflects the active inhibition of task-irrelevant information (Bonnefond & Jensen, 2012; Jensen & Mazaheri, 2010; Sauseng et al., 2009). For example, van Zoest and colleagues (2021, Exp. 2) found that cues signaling the location of an upcoming distractor (i.e., a negative cue) led to an increase in alpha activity over the hemisphere contralateral to the cued distractor location (van Zoest et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2023; but see Noonan et al., 2016; Redding & Fiebelkorn, 2024). This supports the influential view that alpha oscillations serve a top-down gating function, filtering out irrelevant input to optimize attentional selection (Bonnefond & Jensen, 2025; Wöstmann et al., 2019). However, how exactly alpha-band activity responds to feature cues remains mixed. While some studies have failed to observe changes in alpha lateralization when comparing negative against positive cues (e.g., de Vries et al., 2019), others report more supportive results (e.g., van Zoest et al., 2021; Wen et al., 2022). For instance, van Zoest et al. (2021, Exp. 3) found greater bilateral alpha power following centrally presented negative cues relative to neutral cues when using one single distractor feature. Wen et al. (2022) observed a reduced alpha lateralization when laterally presented negative cues were fixed rather than varied across trials. Because both studies employed fixed distractor features, the observed effects are likely confounded by learned suppression, making it difficult to isolate the specific contribution of cue-guided effects. 1.5 Rationale Existing EEG studies on negative cues face two key limitations that constrain our understanding of the mechanisms underlying cue-guided distractor handling. First, too simple search task reduced participants' incentive to use negative cues. Second, negative cueing benefits are often confounded by learned suppression, making it difficult to isolate the unique contribution of negative cues during visual search. To resolve these limits, the search displays used in the present study contained either 4 (easy) or 8 (difficult) task-relevant (colored) items, presented alongside several task-irrelevant gray items, keeping the overall set size constant while varying the difficulty of the search task. Each search display was preceded by either a positive, a negative or a neutral cue. Crucially, the color cues were laterally presented and the cued colors varied on a trial-by-trial basis rather than being fixed, thereby minimizing the possibility of participants carrying over the information from previous trials. Based on this design, we hypothesized that if participants can effectively use negative cues, their responses should be faster in trials preceded by negative cues compared to neutral cues. Conversely, if they cannot use negative cues effectively, responses should be comparable to those following neutral cues (replicating the findings of Arita and colleagues, 2012, with small set sizes) or even slower (as observed by Berggren and Eimer, 2021). Furthermore, we predicted that participants would be more likely to rely on negative cues in the difficult search (8 task-relevant items) than in the easy search (4 task-relevant items), because the increased attentional demands would make negative cue use more valuable.

To preview the results from Experiment 1, we observed that negative cues led to faster responses than neutral cues, but only in difficult search. Experiment 2 therefore focused on the difficult task and recorded EEG to ask whether neural signals provide evidence for preemptive or reactive suppression. To effectively dissociate attentional processing of targets from distractors, the experimental stimuli were presented in spatially segregated locations (see Fig. 1A). Two key ERP components (i.e., distractor N2pc and P D ) were used to reflect attentional selection processes following search display onset. The "preemptive suppression" hypothesis predicts that negative cues should directly trigger distractor suppression (Carlisle, 2023; Zhang & Carlisle, 2023), reflected by a significant P D , without a distractor N2pc. Conversely, the "search and destroy" hypothesis (Moher & Egeth, 2012) assumes that the cued distractor first captures attention, this should be reflected by a distractor N2pc (Berggren & Eimer, 2021; Carlisle & Nitka, 2019; Salahub & Emrich, 2021), which should then be followed by a P D , reflecting reactive suppression. Furthermore, the alternative assumption that negative cues are functionally recoded into positive ones (Becker et al., 2015; Beck & Hollingworth, 2015), predicts a significant target N2pc with negative cues, but this N2pc is expected to emerge later than that elicited with positive cues. Finally, presenting the color cue at a lateral position allowed us to analyze alpha lateralization relative to the cued side as an index of the processing triggered by the cue. As the position of the cue was unrelated to where targets and distractors would later appear, this lateralization cannot reflect preparation for an expected location. Higher alpha power over a hemisphere is taken to reflect inhibition of processing in that hemisphere (Foxe & Snyder, 2011), so more positive lateralization, defined as relatively higher alpha power contralateral to the cue, would indicate that the cue and the color it carries were processed less. To isolate the processing of the information given by the informative cue, we compared negative and positive cues with neutral cues. If cued distractor colors were suppressed before search display onset, alpha lateralization in the cuesearch task interval should be more positive after negative than after neutral cues. In contrast, if cued target colors were selected in advance, alpha lateralization should be more negative after positive than after neutral cues.

2 Experiment 1 2.1 Methods

2.1.1 Participants

The sample size for this study was determined a priori via G*power 3.1 (Faul et al., 2009), targeting the RT difference between negative and neutral cues in the difficult search condition, tested via a two-tailed paired t test. Adopting a conservative effect size (Cohen's d =.60) for this specific comparison, based on the negative cueing benefit reported by Zhang and Carlisle (2023), this analysis indicated that 24 participants were required to detect the negative cueing benefit with 80% power (alpha level = .050). Accordingly, we recruited 24 volunteers (16 female, 8 male; age: 23.46±3.05 years).

All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, as verified by visual acuity and color vision screening with an OCULUS Binoptometer 3 (OCULUS Optikgeräte GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany). All participants provided written informed consent prior to the experiment and were compensated with course credits or 9 € per hour. The experimental procedure was approved by the ethics committee of the Department of Psychology at Philipps-Universität Marburg (No. 2021-56k), and conducted in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki.

2.1.2 Apparatus and stimuli

The experiment took place in a dimly lit, electricity-shielding room. Participants were seated in front of a 22-inch VPixx VIEWPixx monitor (Vpixx Technologies Inc.) at a viewing distance of approximately 100 cm. The experiment was controlled by a Windows 7 computer (1680*1050 pixel screen resolution, 60-Hz refresh rate) using E-prime 2.0 software.

All stimuli were presented on a black (0.28cd/m2) background. Gabor patches (with a radius of 0.81° visual angle, cosine envelope, frequency of 0.05 and deviation of 12 pixels) were generated via the online Gabor-patch generator (https://www.cogsci.nl/gabor-generator). They were displayed in one of four isoluminant colors (red [R:199, G:0, B:0], 18.83cd/m2; green [R:0, G:103, B:0], 18.45cd/m2; blue [R:0, G:84, B:159], 18.99cd/m2; gray [R:80, G:80, B:80], 19.09cd/m2) and tilted to one of four orientations (0°, 90° and ±15° relative to vertical midline). Each search display consisted of gray Gabor patches and patches of another two colors that were randomly chosen for each trial. Gabor patches with gray color were always task-irrelevant; they could be any of the four orientations. The Gabor patches with non-gray colors were task-relevant; only one of them contained a target orientation that was tilted to the left (-15° relative to vertical midline) or right (+15° relative to vertical midline), whereas the others were oriented either 0° or 90° to prevent the target orientation from popping out. The proportion of vertical Gabor patches was matched across task difficulties at 50% for both target-and distractor-colored items in each condition. Each display contained 24 Gabor patches with 4, 8 and 12 Gabor patches on an inner (radius: 2.42°), middle (radius: 4.84°) and outer (radius: 7.26°) imaginary circle; Gabor patches were spaced equally on each imaginary circle. The target was never presented on the outer circle, but at every other candidate location with equal probability, independent of task difficulty. Task difficulty was manipulated by increasing the number of colored Gabor patches from 4 to 8 items (see Fig. 1A for an example), based on the finding that negative cues demonstrated a RT benefit at set size 8 but not at set size 4 (Arita et al., 2012). which the task-relevant color cue appeared. After a short delay, either a positive, a negative or a neutral cue was presented for 250 ms. After another delay, the search display was shown, which required participants to detect an orientation target (highlighted with a dashed white circle) and report whether its orientation was tilted to the left or right.

2.1.3 Procedure and design

As shown in Fig. 1C, each trial started with a central fixation point and two arrow cues, indicating the side on which the task-relevant color cue appeared. After a randomized delay for 300-500 ms, one of three lateralized cues (positive, negative or neutral) was presented for another 250 ms. Left/right cue positions and three task-relevant colors (red, green, blue) were fully counterbalanced within each condition and presented in a random order across the whole experiment.

After another randomized delay period for 800-1000 ms, the search display was shown. Participants were asked to search through either four (easy search) or eight (difficult search) colored Gabor patches to find the target. The target was a tilted non-gray Gabor patch; participants reported whether it was tilted to the left or right. Responses were collected via a gamepad with two buttons labeling left and right. Participants used their index or middle fingers of the right hand to press one of the buttons. They were instructed to respond as quickly and accurately as possible. In case of an erroneous response or a slow response (>4000 ms), a white text with "incorrect" or "too late" would appear for 500 ms on the screen. Each trial was followed by a blank interval for 1000 ms.

The experiment consisted of a total of 18 blocks with 64 trials each. Participants had the chance to take a rest after having finished half of the block or the entire block. They also got feedback about mean accuracy and RTs after each block, lasting for 3000 ms.

The three cue types were presented in separate blocks. Their order followed a Latin-square design and was counterbalanced across participants. Easy and difficult search trials were randomly intermixed within the block.

Participants practiced at least one block for each cue type prior to the experimental blocks. The experiment started when mean accuracy was above 65% and mean RTs below 4000 ms.

2.3 Discussion

Results clearly revealed the differential effects of positive and negative cues across task difficulties. The beneficial effects of positive cues were general, appearing in both easy and difficult searches. In contrast, the benefits from negative cues were conditional, emerging only in the difficult search, where participants had to search through 8 colored items. This finding aligns well with previous findings, suggesting that the effectiveness of negative cues increases with search difficulty (Arita et al., 2012; Conci et al., 2019). Crucially, a key feature of our design was that the total number of items (the overall set size) was held constant across conditions; only the number of task-relevant (colored) items varied.

Therefore, our findings provide compelling evidence that task difficulty is determined not by the overall set size, but by the number of potential target and distractor items that must be searched, at least when irrelevant items share a common feature allowing group-level rejection (Duncan & Humphreys, 1989).

3 Experiment 2

Experiment 2 focused on the difficult search and recorded EEG to examine the neural signals that accompany the processing of cued distractors. We analyzed three markers: the Pᴅ and the N2pc after search display onset, and alphaband lateralization in the cue-search task interval. We tested two predictions coming from the preemptive suppression account which assumes that participants can use negative cues to suppress distractor features before they capture attention.

First, if suppression is prepared before the search display appears, alpha lateralization in the cue-search interval should be more positive after a negative cue than after a neutral cue. Second, the cued distractor should elicit a P D (Gaspelin & Luck, 2018; Sawaki & Luck, 2010), and no distractor N2pc is expected. The "search and destroy" account, in contrast, (Moher & Egeth, 2012), assumes that the cued distractor initially captures attention before it can be suppressed, which should be reflected in a distractor N2pc, indicating attention capture, followed by a P D , reflecting reactive suppression.

3.1 Methods

3.1.1 Participants

A new group of thirty naive participants (21 female, 9 male, age: 22.70 ± 2.68 years) was recruited for this experiment.

All had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Visual acuity and color vision were tested with the OCULUS Binoptometer 3 (OCULUS Optikgeräte GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany). All participants provided written informed consent and were compensated with course credits or payment of €10 per hour. The experimental procedure was approved by the ethics committee of the Department of Psychology at Philipps-Universität Marburg (No. 2021-56k), and conducted in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki.

3.1.2 Apparatus, stimuli, procedure and design

Apparatus, stimuli and procedure were largely identical to Experiment 1, with three modifications. To keep the number of trials doable, we included only the difficult search task; this adjustment was based on the finding from Experiment 1 where negative cueing benefits were observed exclusively in this condition. Second, to increase the statistical power for the negative cue condition, we allocated it a proportionally larger number of blocks. Specifically, participants completed eight blocks with negative cues and seven blocks each for positive and neutral cues, with block order randomized across participants. Lastly, the delay periods before and after color cue presentation were fixed at 400 ms and 900 ms respectively.

These durations were standardized to the mean values of randomized intervals used in Experiment 1. This approach ensured identical timing across all three cue types, avoiding chance differences in realized delay that could arise under jittering with finite trials, at the cost of increased temporal predictability.

3.1.3 Behavioral analysis

The criteria of trial exclusion were identical to those used in Experiment 1. A one-way repeated measure ANOVA with the factor cue type (positive, negative, neutral) was conducted on RT results. Degrees of freedom and p values were Greenhouse-Geisser corrected for violations of sphericity. Follow-up contrasts compared positive and negative cues with neutral cues using paired t tests, and p values were Holm-corrected among two tests. Bayes factors (BF01 ) were computed as described in Section 2.1.4. Error rates are reported in Table 1.

3.1.4 EEG recording and pre-processing

The EEG was recorded with a BrainAmp amplifier from 64 Ag/AgCI active electrodes (actiCAP, Brain Products, Munich, Germany) placed according to the international 10-10 system. Electrode impedances were kept below 5 kΩ. The sampling rate was set at 1000 Hz (high cutoff: 250 Hz, low cutoff: 0.016 Hz), electrodes were online referenced to FCz. Blinks and eye movements were monitored by deriving bipolar recordings from electrodes placed below the left eye and FP1 (vertical electrooculogram, vEOG), and from electrodes (FT9 and FT10) placed near the outer canthi of each eye (horizontal EOG, hEOG).

EEG data were processed offline, using the Fieldtrip toolbox (Oostenveld et al., 2011) and custom Matlab scripts for preprocessing. The EEG signal was re-referenced to the average of the left and right mastoid, and bandpass-filtered between 1 and 35 Hz (zero-phase, two-pass 4th-order Butterworth IIR filter; effective cutoffs at half-amplitude; slope ≈ 48 dB/octave). Continuous data was segmented into epochs ranging from 2.85 s before search display onset (equivalent to 1.05 s before the arrow cue onset) to 1.15 s after search display onset. Trials removed in the behavioral analyses were also excluded from EEG processing. Additional trials were discarded if they contained eye blinks (vEOG > ±75μV), or horizontal eye movements (hEOG > ±35μV) within the time window from 200 ms before to 400 ms after the search display onset. ERP analyses focused on electrodes PO7/PO8, which are standard for examining the N2pc and the P D components (e.g., Berggren & Eimer, 2021; Hickey et al., 2009; Stilwell et al., 2022; van Moorselaar et al., 2023).

Trials were also rejected if the voltages at PO7/PO8 exceeded ±75μV (absolute amplitude threshold) at any sample between 200 ms before and 400 ms after search display onset. All participants retained sufficient data for analysis, with fewer than 30% of trials rejected. On average, 89.07% of trials were artifact-free and included in the final analysis.

3.1.5 Search-locked ERP analysis

To analyze event-related potentials evoked by the search display, we focused on epochs ranging from 200 ms before to 500 ms after search display onset. Epochs were baseline-corrected using a 200 ms pre-search interval. To isolate hemisphere-specific attentional activity, we computed contralateral-minus-ipsilateral difference waveforms, separately for trials in which the target or distractor appeared in the lateral visual field. We employed a data-driven yet theoryinformed approach to define the analysis time windows for the N2pc and Pᴅ components, following the procedure established by van Moorselaar et al. (2023). We first identified component peaks on a grand-average difference waveform collapsed across all cue conditions (positive, negative, and neutral) and across distractor-lateral and targetlateral trials. For the Pᴅ component, we used an 110 ms window centered on the grand-average positive peak; for the N2pc component, we used a 75 ms window centered on the grand-average negative peak (typically occurring between 200-300 ms) and calculated its mean amplitude (Berggren & Eimer, 2021; Salahub & Emrich, 2021). These window widths follow the same approach used by Stilwell et al. (2022) and van Moorselaar et al. (2023), consistent with the greater temporal variability reported for the Pᴅ relative to the N2pc across studies (Gaspelin et al., 2023). To assess whether any component is distractor-specific or reflects a shared, non-specific response, we directly compared the distractor-elicited activities against the target-elicited activities. Specifically mean amplitudes of original waveforms, extracted from the time windows defined above, were submitted to repeated-measure ANOVAs with the within-subject factors of cue type (positive, negative, neutral), display configuration (target lateral, distractor lateral), and laterality (contralateral, ipsilateral) to test for main effects and interactions. Follow-up contrasts were Holm-corrected among planned comparisons. Bayes factors (BF01 ) were reported as described in Section 2.1.4.

Next, we tested the lateralization against zero specified by our hypotheses, with p values Holm-corrected across three tests. A negativity was present for targets with positive cues, t(29) = -2.30, p Holm = .040, dz = -0.42, and for distractors with negative cues, t(29) = -2.46, p Holm = .040, dz = -0.45, but the Bayes factors provided only anecdotal evidence for an effect (BF01 = 0.53 and 0.40, respectively). For targets with negative cues, the lateralization was a positive deflection and not an N2pc, t(29) = 2.83, p Holm = .028, dz = 0.52, and the Bayes factor provided moderate evidence for an effect (BF01 = 0.19).

The early positivity already differed between display configurations even under neutral cues, being larger for target-lateral displays, and this difference may persist into the later window. The raw N2pc amplitudes may therefore also contain lateralized responses that do not relate to the cue. To isolate the effect of the cue, we further tested whether attentional enhancement (relative to neutral cues within the same display configuration) can be triggered by informative cues using cluster-based permutation tests (10,000 permutations over 0 -500 ms, one-tailed in the direction of a negativity, clusterlevel α =.05). These tests do not assume a predefined time window, so that effects are not missed when the conditions peak at different latencies. Results showed a significant N2pc enhancement for target-lateral displays with positive cues (positive minus neutral) at 218 -374 ms (cluster p <.001), consistent with selection of the cued target, and a significant N2pc enhancement for distractor-lateral displays with negative cues (negative minus neutral) at 206-247 ms (cluster p = .028), consistent with capture by the cued distractor. To describe how long such distractor capture lasted, we determined when it had fallen to 50% of its peak amplitude (offset) within the N2pc window (228-303 ms), using the jackknife method (i.e., leave-one-participant-out averages). The enhancement of the N2pc for distractors with negative cues (negative minus neutral) peaked at 240 ms and had fallen to half of its peak by 250 ms in all 30 averages, indicating that it was brief. For reference, the enhancement of the N2pc for targets with positive cues (positive minus neutral) peaked at 230 ms and had not fallen to half of its peak by the end of the window in 29 of the 30 averages.

The brief enhancement of distractor N2pc is compatible with a short capture of attention by the negatively cued distractors.

Reactive suppression predicts that such capture should be followed by suppression, which could be indexed by P D . To test this, we examined the cue-related lateralization for distractors (negative minus neutral) across 250-500 ms (after the enhancement had declined), using a cluster-based permutation test (10,000 permutations, one-tailed in the direction of a positivity, cluster-level α = .05). No positive cluster was found (see Fig. 4B), indicating no later distractor positivity.

3.3 Discussion

Experiment 2 replicated the behavioral benefit of positive cues relative to neutral cues under difficult search tasks. In contrast, negative cues did not speed responses, unlike the benefit observed in Experiment 1.

Possible reasons for this discrepancy, along with how our EEG data inform existing hypotheses, are discussed in the next section. 4 General discussion The current study investigated how prior knowledge of a distractor's color shapes visual search and how the distractor is handled. In Experiment 1, positive cues speeded responses in both easy and difficult search, whereas negative cues speeded responses only in difficult search (with eight task-relevant items), and not in easy search (with four task-relevant items), although the total number of items was constant. Experiment 2 replicated the benefit of positive cues but not that of negative cues in difficult search, where RT did not differ from performance seen with neutral cues (BF01 = 4.23, moderate evidence). The EEG data provided no evidence for either preemptive or reactive suppression of negatively cued distractors. Alpha lateralization before the search display did not differ between negative and neutral cues. The early positivity was elicited by both lateral distractors and lateral targets. This positivity was changed by the cue to a similar degree when the lateralized set carried the cued color, whether this set was the target or the distractor. It therefore cannot be taken as a suppression signal. After display onset, negatively cued distractors showed a weak N2pc, which is in line with a short capture of attention, but it was not followed by a P D . 4.1 What can the lateralized ERPs tell us about distractor handling?

In the literature, an early positivity elicited by a salient distractor has been thought to reflect preemptive suppression (Gaspelin & Luck, 2018; Sawaki & Luck, 2010). In our study, negatively cued distractors also elicited an early positivity.

If this reflected preemptive suppression of a negatively cued distractor, it should be specific to distractor-lateral displays, whereas target-lateral displays should elicit little or none. The results did not support this prediction. Target-lateral displays elicited the early positivity as well, and it was larger than distractor-lateral displays even with neutral cues. We thus compared the cue-related modulation, defined as the enhancement of early positivity with negative relative to neutral cues, between display configurations. This enhancement was not significantly larger for distractor-lateral than for targetlateral displays, although this evidence was inconclusive (BF01 = 2.04). Moreover, the enhancement of early positivity was similar for cued targets and cued distractors (BF01 = 5.00), suggesting that the positivity did not serve a function specific to distractors. Therefore, this positivity is more likely to reflect a general priority or salience signal (Corriveau et al., 2012; Kerzel & Huynh Cong, 2023; Oxner et al., 2024 Oxner et al., , 2025)), rather than a preemptive suppression signal. This agrees with the recent finding that a salient singleton elicits early positivity irrespective of whether it is ignored or searched for (McDonald et al., 2026). Our evidence for equivalence is moderate, earlier inferences of preemptive suppression from this component are not refuted but should be treated with caution.

If negatively cued distractors captured attention, as the first stage of the "search and destroy" account predicts (Moher & Egeth, 2012), they should elicit an N2pc. In the N2pc time window (228-303 ms), distractors with negative cues elicited only a weak negativity, supported by anecdotal evidence (BF01 = 0.40). Considering that this raw N2pc may contain lateralized responses that do not relate to the cue, we tested the enhancement of distractor N2pc by negative cues (relative to neutral cues) using a cluster-based approach. This test revealed a negative cluster at 206-247 ms. Such a short effect is compatible with a brief capture of attention by the cued distractor, as a "search and destroy" account (Moher & Egeth, 2012) would predict. This account further requires that capture should be followed by suppression, but no distractor positivity followed this negativity (250-500 ms; cluster-based test). Taken together, these results provide limited evidence that the cued distractor captured attention, and none that capture was followed by suppression. This is consistent with views that capture is not necessarily followed by suppression (e.g., Beck et al., 2018; Hickey et al., 2006).

Therefore, these findings provide no evidence for either preemptive suppression (Gaspelin & Luck, 2018; Sawaki & Luck, 2010) or reactive suppression (Moher & Egeth, 2012), as both require a suppression signal (P D ), and no such signal was found here. One possible reason is that the search display did not require suppression. In our display, target-and distractor-colored items occupied separate regions and were surrounded by uniform gray fillers, so the two groups stood out as clumps. This may allow a clump-scanning strategy, in which attention is first directed to one group and the target is then searched for within it (Liesefeld & Müller, 2020). Under this strategy, distractors do not compete for attention and can be ignored passively. This would account for the absence of a distractor N2pc with positive cues ( BF01 = 3.69), and no P D specific to distractors.

Another possibility is that negative cues were recoded into a positive template (Becker et al., 2015), which would make suppression of the distractor unnecessary (e.g., Rashal et al., 2022). Because a negative cue does not tell observers which color to look for, such a template can be formed only after the search display appears, once the remaining color has been inferred. If negative cues were recoded in this way, targets should still elicit an N2pc, but later than after positive cues. In the N2pc window, negatively cued targets did not elicit an N2pc. Considering that the N2pc may fall outside this window due to its delayed nature, we also checked for a later negativity. A later negativity was present for negatively cued targets, but it was also present for targets with positive and neutral cues, for which no recoding is needed, so this late component cannot be interpreted as recoding. We therefore found no evidence that target selection was delayed by negative cues, and our data provide no support for recoding.

One may propose that identifying the cue color on every trial automatically primes that color, irrespective of whether it later serves as the target or the distractor color (Maljkovic & Nakayama, 1994; Theeuwes, 2013). Priming could therefore contribute to the effects of both positive and negative cues. For positive cues, it makes the same prediction as strategic control, so only negative cues can be diagnostic. Priming predicts that cues change the responses to the lateralized set carrying the cued color to the same extent for positive and negative cues, and that negative cues slow rather than speed responses, because attention would be drawn to the color that is to be ignored. The early positivity fits the first prediction, as the enhancement of early positivity was similar for the cued target and for the cued distractor. However, this result does not exclude strategic control, because it would produce the same pattern. The N2pc and the behavioral results fit priming less well. The enhancement of N2pc for distractors with negative cues was brief and had fallen to half of its peak by about 250 ms, whereas that for targets with positive cues had not by the end of the common N2pc window. This suggests that the cue had a more lasting effect on the cued target than on the cued distractor, which is difficult to explain by a boost that is independent of the role of the cued color. Moreover, negative cues did not slow responses in Experiment 2 ( BF01 = 4.23), and they speeded responses in Experiment 1. A cost of priming could in principle be offset by a benefit of strategic control, in which case both mechanisms would contribute. Therefore, priming is unlikely to be the only factor, although it may contribute to the current findings, and our data cannot separate it from strategic control.

4.2 Is there preparatory suppression on distractor features?

Before the search display appeared, we asked whether the cued color was processed differently after negative cues than after neutral cues. The color cue appeared at a lateral position that was unrelated to the positions of the distractors in the search display, so lateralized alpha could not reflect suppression of an expected distractor location. We used the position of the color cue as a handle on its processing, because alpha lateralization is sensitive to the processing of a lateral stimulus and the cues were matched in position, size, and color distribution. A difference from the neutral cue should therefore reflect how the color of the cue and its central symbol were processed. Higher alpha power is taken to reflect inhibition, and lower alpha power facilitation, of processing in the corresponding hemisphere (de Vries et al., 2019; Foxe & Snyder, 2011). If a cued distractor color was suppressed in preparation for the search, alpha lateralization should therefore have been higher after negative than after neutral cues, whereas selection of the cued target color should have lowered it after positive cues. The data showed neither pattern. Negative cues did not differ from neutral cues at any time point, and the decrease after positive cues, although in the predicted direction, did not reach significance (430-620 ms). These results provide no evidence for suppression of the cued color during the interval between the cue and the search display.

The absence of modulation on alpha lateralization by negative cues can be related to recent evidence on how a cue to ignore a color affects processing of that color. Using steady-state potentials to track feature-selective processing in early visual cortex, Jänig et al. (2026) found that the cued color was rapidly facilitated after the cue, irrespective of whether participants were instructed to attend to it or to ignore it. The authors attributed this to stimulus-driven selection of the cued color. Such facilitation would predict lower alpha lateralization after negative than after positive cues, which we did not observe. The paradigms differ in ways that may explain why. In that study, three colored dot fields were superimposed at the same location and the cue matched one of them, so facilitation of the cued color could act on a stimulus that was present at the same time, and competition occurred between colors within the same receptive fields. In our experiment, no stimulus in the cued color was present during the cue period other than the cue itself, so there was no concurrent stimulus on which such facilitation could act, and our measure indexed the processing of the cue at its position. The two results are therefore not directly comparable.

4.3 How robust is the benefit of negative cues?

Some studies in which the distractor color changed from trial to trial have reported benefits of negative cues, and have suggested that these benefits increase with search difficulty (Arita et al., 2012; Conci et al., 2019; Zhang & Carlisle, 2023; Zhang et al., 2022). Experiment 1 agrees with this pattern, as negative cues speeded responses more in the difficult search, which had eight task-relevant items, than in the easy search, which had four, although the total number of items was constant. This also suggests that negative cueing benefits depends on the number of task-relevant items. Experiment 2, however, which contained only the difficult search, did not reproduce the benefit of negative cues (BF01 = 4.23).

The two experiments differed in whether negative cues speeded responses in the difficult search, but this difference was not statistically significant (F(1,52) = 2.34, p = .132, ηp² = 0.043) and may reflect sampling variability.

Combining the two experiments, the effect size of negative cueing benefits in the difficult search was dz = 0.30 (95% CI [0.02, 0.57]), which is smaller than in Experiment 1 alone. For an effect of this size, a sample of 30 would detect it with a power of only about 36%, so a non-significant result in Experiment 2 is not surprising. Besides, behavioral responses in Experiment 2 were numerically slower than in Experiment 1 by a similar amount with positive cues (22 ms) and neutral cues (25 ms), but by about twice as much with negative cues (52 ms). The missing cueing benefit thus reflected relatively slower responses with negative cues, and not faster responses with neutral cues. The experiments also differed in context, as easy and difficult trials were intermixed in Experiment 1 whereas Experiment 2 contained only difficult trials, and we cannot determine whether this difference contributed. Whatever its source, our EEG results describe how the cues were processed in a sample in which negative cues did not speed responses, and they cannot show how the benefit observed in Experiment 1 arises. 4.4 Conclusion Knowing in advance which color to ignore can make visual search faster, but the benefit appears small and dependent on search difficulty. In the experiment with EEG, in which negative cues did not speed responses, we found no evidence for preemptive suppression, as alpha lateralization before the search display did not differ between negative and neutral cues, and the early positivity was modulated similarly for positively cued targets and negatively cued distractors.

We also found no evidence for reactive suppression, because the weak, brief enhancement of N2pc by negatively cued distractors, which is compatible with a short capture of attention, was not followed by a distractor positivity.
