Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Eye-tracking correlations with cognitive and emotional states vary across studies, likely because they depend on specific task demands. We investigated the consistency and specificity of such associations between wearable eye-tracking data and self-reported measures of stress, cognitive workload, and fatigue within assessment-centre-style tasks.

Methods: Participants performed tasks involving social evaluation, reasoning, planning, comprehension, and presentation while wearing Meta Aria Gen 1 glasses. Data from 97 participants were analysed using linear mixed-effects models to examine associations between 104 wearable eyetracking and head-movement metrics and each self-reported state, while accounting for task effects. Metric-by-task interactions assessed task dependence, and sensitivity analyses examined the effects of elapsed session time and shared variance among states.

Results: After false-discovery-rate correction, significant metric-by-task interactions were observed in 53 stress, 61 workload, and 12 fatigue models, especially for saccade amplitude, speed, and duration. Across tasks, stress was linked to increased gaze variability, while workload and fatigue were linked to greater fixation angular distance. Fatigue associations weakened after adjusting for session time, and no fatigue metric-by-task interactions remained significant after adjustment for elapsed session time. After controlling for overlap among self-reported states, associations remained significant for stress, but not workload or fatigue.

Conclusion: Ocular associations varied by task and by the shared variance across subjective states. Measures of fixation distribution appeared to capture a more general subjective-state component rather than exclusively distinguishing workload or fatigue. Several gaze and saccade associations with stress remained significant after adjustment for workload and fatigue. Predictive modelling should distinguish shared from state-specific information and be validated across diverse activities.

1. Introduction

>Introduction

1.1 Eye-tracking for real-time cognitive-state monitoring

Real-time monitoring of cognitive and affective states has considerable value across applied domains, including healthcare, education, psychology, and occupational contexts. Of particular scholarly interest are the variables of stress, cognitive workload, and fatigue, as optimal performance in dynamic settings requires the allocation of adequate attentional and cognitive resources to sustain situation awareness and support decision-making (Endsley, 1995; Kahneman, 1973). More specifically, stress has been linked to difficulties in attentional control and emotional regulation. Excessive cognitive load may exceed the resources needed for effective task performance, while fatigue can impair vigilance and sustained attention (Bafna & Hansen, 2021; Eysenck et al., 2007; Hopstaken et al., 2016). Consequently, accurate, real-time evaluation of these states may enable early detection of emerging challenges and facilitate prompt intervention. These capabilities hold significant potential for applications in adaptive learning systems, clinical diagnostics, workplace safety procedures, and preventing deterioration in performance or well-being.

Multiple methodologies have been established for evaluating cognitive states; however, each has limitations for continuous monitoring in real-world contexts. Self-report instruments, such as the NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX), are widely used to measure cognitive workload for their costeffectiveness. Nonetheless, these tools are inherently retrospective, may interfere with ongoing activities, and are vulnerable to response bias (Hart & Staveland, 1988; Taylor & Bolton, 2026). Despite these constraints, self-report measures provide direct insight into participants' subjective experiences. Brief, repeated single-item assessments capture intra-individual fluctuations, even if they may hinder inter-individual comparisons because interpretation and scale use vary. Consequently, the present investigation employs repeated self-report ratings primarily as a subjective reference for cognitive states, rather than as an objective standard.

Beyond self-report, cognitive states can be assessed using behavioural performance measures and physiological signals. Behavioural measures, however, are often task-dependent and may detect performance deterioration only after it has occurred. Electrophysiological techniques such as electroencephalography (EEG) provide objective, temporally precise data, but typically require specialised equipment, sensor preparation, signal preprocessing, and controlled recording conditions. In a systematic review of physiological measures of mental workload, Tao et al. (2019) examined cardiovascular activity, eye movements, EEG, respiration, electromyography, and skin conductance. They found that sensitivity varied across tasks and research domains, suggesting that no single physiological signal provides a universally reliable workload index. Alongside this variability, the financial and logistical demands of many recording techniques can limit their use in everyday settings. These limitations highlight the need for signals that can be recorded continuously with minimal disruption to ongoing activity.

Among the physiological and behavioural signals considered for this purpose, eye-tracking offers a promising approach. Patterns of eye behaviour provide observable indices of underlying cognitive and emotional processes because eye movements are closely associated with neural systems involved in visual processing, attention, and cognitive control, and may therefore offer a behavioural window into these processes (Duchowski, 2002; Mahanama et al., 2022). Distinct eye-tracking metrics capture different aspects of visual and cognitive functioning: fixations indicate the location and duration of sustained visual attention; saccades reflect rapid shifts in gaze; blinks may provide information regarding attentional state and ocular fatigue; and pupil diameter is sensitive to cognitive effort and autonomic arousal (Beatty, 1982; Bradley et al., 2008; Mahanama et al., 2022; Skaramagkas et al., 2023). As these features can be recorded continuously during task performance, eye-tracking provides a non-invasive behavioural measure that can complement retrospective self-report. Notably, eyetracking measures are also influenced by factors such as task demands, visual context, illumination, and recording conditions, all of which must be considered when interpreting the resulting data. Despite these challenges, their sensitivity to ongoing cognitive and physiological processes has prompted growing interest in identifying ocular features associated with specific states, including stress, cognitive workload, and fatigue.

1.2 Ocular correlates of stress, cognitive workload, and fatigue

Research links stress, cognitive workload, and fatigue to changes in fixation, saccade, blink, pupil, and gaze-distribution measures. However, these effects vary across experimental paradigms and recording contexts. The following sections review evidence for each state and explore possible sources of this variability.

1.2.1 Stress and anxiety

Research on eye-tracking in the context of anxiety has predominantly concentrated on attentional biases towards threatening stimuli. A meta-analytic review of 33 experiments across anxiety and depression conducted by Armstrong and Olatunji (2012) demonstrated that individuals with heightened anxiety levels tend to orient more swiftly towards threatening cues during free-viewing and visual-search paradigms. Additionally, these individuals tend to fixate longer on threatening distractors in visual search tasks, although evidence of difficulty disengaging from threat was not consistently observed during free viewing. These results imply that gaze behaviour associated with anxiety is partially dependent on whether the task explicitly requires disengagement from threatening stimuli.

Studies of experimentally induced acute stress have also reported changes in the spatial extent of visual exploration. Herten et al. (2017) found that participants exposed to psychosocial stress spent more time fixating on objects central to the stressful episode than control participants. Similarly, Guy et al. (2023) reported that acutely stressed participants made fewer saccades and inspected a smaller proportion of presented images than participants in the control condition. However, stress did not significantly affect fixation duration in that study. These findings indicate that acute stress may, under controlled conditions, produce a more spatially restricted pattern of visual exploration.

Interestingly, evidence from naturalistic settings is less consistent with this attentional-narrowing pattern. Ahmadi et al. (2024) recorded eye movements continuously from intensive-care nurses during routine clinical work and found that higher self-reported stress was associated with more fixations and higher gaze entropy, but shorter saccade durations and smaller pupil diameter. Thus, in a complex environment requiring continuous monitoring of spatially distributed information, stress appears to be associated with increased, less predictable visual sampling rather than restricted exploration.

Evidence from mobile eye-tracking in real-world social settings further underscores the importance of context. Konovalova et al. (2021) examined gaze behaviour during a real-life social situation in which participants were in the same room with an unfamiliar confederate. Participants with higher social anxiety showed shorter initial fixations on the confederate, made fewer fixations and saccades overall, and exhibited shorter scanpaths. Rather than the hypervigilance sometimes reported in controlled paradigms, higher social anxiety was associated with greater visual avoidance in real-world interactions. These findings show that anxiety-related gaze patterns observed under laboratory conditions may not generalise directly to naturalistic social environments and reinforce the importance of studying ocular behaviour within the context in which it occurs.

Collectively, these studies indicate that stress and anxiety are associated with alterations in gaze allocation, but do not provide evidence for a consistent direction of effect. Attentional Control Theory offers one theoretical framework for interpreting this variability, positing that anxiety impairs the efficiency of goal-directed attentional control while amplifying the influence of stimulus-driven processing (Eysenck et al., 2007). Accordingly, the manifestation of anxiety-related effects in gaze behaviour may depend, in part, on task structure and the spatial distribution of salient or task-relevant information. More concentrated gaze patterns have primarily been observed when salient or stressrelevant information is spatially localised, whereas greater dispersion and less structured scanning may arise when attentional demands are distributed across a complex environment.

1.2.2 Cognitive workload

Studies examining cognitive workload have reported changes across several aspects of eye behaviour, including fixations, saccades, blinks, and pupil diameter. In their review, Marquart et al. (2015) identified these measures as commonly investigated workload indicators but noted substantial variation in the direction of reported effects, particularly for fixation-based measures. This variability suggests that the relationship between eye behaviour and workload depends partly on task demands.

Fixation behaviour may be a particularly valuable indicator in scenarios that require accumulating and retaining information during visual exploration. Meghanathan et al. (2015) found that fixation duration increased with the number of targets retained in memory, both within and beyond estimated workingmemory capacity. Successive target fixations lengthened as information accumulated, whereas distractor fixations did not show the same pattern. These findings support using fixation duration as an indicator of memory-related demands during visual exploration.

Evidence from blink measures further illustrates the importance of distinguishing cognitive from visual demands. Veltman and Gaillard (1998) examined pilots performing flight-simulator and concurrent memory tasks. Greater visual-processing demands were associated with longer blink intervals and shorter blinks, whereas increasing memory-task difficulty shortened blink intervals. Similarly, Recarte et al. (2008) found that blink rate increased with the mental demands of cognitive tasks but decreased when a concurrent visual-detection task was introduced. The findings suggest that blink behaviour reflects the balance between cognitive and visual demands, rather than a single dimension of workload.

Additionally, pupillary responses have shown a more consistent association with cognitive demand under controlled illumination. An early study by Kahneman and Beatty (1966) observed progressively greater pupil dilation as memory load increased, providing early evidence that task-evoked pupillary responses track cognitive effort. Subsequent research has repeatedly shown that pupil diameter increases with task difficulty and processing demand (Beatty, 1982; van der Wel & van Steenbergen, 2018). Nevertheless, pupil diameter is also affected by luminance, emotional arousal, and autonomic activity, limiting the measure's specificity and complicating its use in naturalistic environments.

Overall, ocular measures are sensitive to cognitive workload, but their interpretation depends on the nature of the task and its visual demands. For fixation and saccade measures, this means that observed changes may reflect both the visual strategies used to perform a task and the workload experienced while doing so.

1.2.3 Fatigue

Mental fatigue from prolonged cognitive activity has been linked to changes across multiple ocular feature families, particularly blink, saccadic, and pupillary measures. For instance, Zargari Marandi et al. (2018) investigated younger and older adults performing a sustained, cognitively demanding computer task and reported that increases in subjective fatigue were accompanied by longer and more frequent blinks, reduced saccade duration and peak velocity, as well as alterations in fixation duration and pupil-dilation range. Although several ocular measures differed between age groups, their trajectories over time on task were broadly comparable. This suggests that fatigue-related ocular changes may follow similar temporal patterns across age groups, despite age-related differences in some measures.

In addition, evidence from both laboratory and operational contexts suggests that saccade velocity may represent a relatively reliable correlate of mental fatigue. Di Stasi et al. (2013) reported reductions in microsaccade peak velocity and mean saccadic velocity as fatigue progressed during a prolonged visual-search task, irrespective of task complexity. Consistent with these findings, reviews have identified saccadic slowing across prolonged task performance and in operational settings (Bafna & Hansen, 2021; Schleicher et al., 2008). Nevertheless, saccade velocity should not be regarded as a state-specific marker, as it is also influenced by arousal and cognitive workload.

Other studies have found that pupillary responses may index reduced task engagement, a state frequently associated with mental fatigue. Hopstaken et al. (2016) concurrently assessed pupil diameter, gaze allocation, EEG activity, task performance, and subjective fatigue during prolonged cognitive activity. As time on task increased, both task-evoked pupillary responses and task performance declined, whereas the proportion of gaze directed towards task-relevant stimuli remained relatively stable. Subsequently, increasing rewards for high performance restored both pupillary responses and task performance, despite persistently elevated subjective fatigue. These findings suggest that diminished pupillary responsivity during mental fatigue may reflect motivational disengagement rather than an irreversible depletion of cognitive resources.

Findings concerning fixation behaviour remain inconsistent. Studies have variously reported longer fixations, shorter fixations, or no systematic changes as mental fatigue develops, whereas comparatively little research has examined the spatial dispersion of fixations. Time on task has itself been found to alter fixation-duration distributions (Friedman & Komogortsev, 2024), thereby further complicating their interpretation as specific markers of fatigue. This heterogeneity may reflect differences in task demands, time on task, participant characteristics, and the distinction between mental fatigue and related states, such as ocular fatigue, sleepiness, and reduced alertness. Consequently, fixation measures may capture changes in information processing, attentional engagement, gaze stability, or visual-search strategy rather than mental fatigue specifically.

Overall, stress, cognitive workload, and mental fatigue affect multiple families of ocular features; however, no single measure demonstrates a consistent, state-specific association. These relationships seem to be modulated by individual characteristics, task demands, and contextual factors. This heterogeneity challenges monitoring cognitive and affective states in naturalistic settings, where these conditions often co-occur with varying visual and cognitive demands. Therefore, establishing whether ocular associations generalise across tasks or are contingent on task context is critical to explaining inconsistencies in the literature and identifying features suitable for predictive modelling.

1.3 Ocular features as candidate inputs for predictive models

Machine-learning models increasingly use eye-tracking features to estimate stress, cognitive workload, fatigue, and related cognitive-affective states. Common inputs include pupil diameter, fixation duration and frequency, saccade amplitude and velocity, blink rate and duration, and measures of gaze dispersion and entropy (Aksu et al., 2024). Although these models have demonstrated promising classification performance, predictive accuracy alone does not establish whether the features used provide reliable or state-specific information. Models may also exploit patterns associated with individual participants, task activities, recording devices, or data-processing pipelines, limiting their generalisability beyond the conditions in which they were developed (Kaczorowska et al., 2021; Mahanama et al., 2022).

Task context poses a particular challenge because eye movements reflect both psychological state and the visual and behavioural demands of an activity. A feature associated with workload during reading, for example, may show a different association-or none-during visuospatial planning or verbal interaction. Models developed within a single task may therefore learn relationships that do not generalise to other activities. Distinguishing between states is another challenge: stress, cognitive workload, and fatigue frequently co-occur, so ocular features that predict one state may reflect information shared with the others rather than information specific to the intended target.

Personalised and context-aware approaches offer potential ways to address these challenges. For example, GazeMind, a gaze-informed LLM agent framework for real-time cognitive load estimation using smart glasses (Wang et al., 2026), uses per-user calibration informed by individual characteristics and historical observations to assess cognitive load. Such approaches may help account for stable differences between individuals and changes in feature distributions over time. Complementing personalisation, activity context can be inferred from wearable signals such as egocentric video, gaze, and head pose, potentially supporting models that account explicitly for task demands (Yang et al., 2026). However, these approaches alone do not establish which ocular features retain informative associations across activities or distinguish between correlated states.

Developing generalisable models therefore requires characterising how candidate features relate to cognitive states across heterogeneous tasks, including which associations are shared across activities and which depend on task context. The present study contributes to this feature-characterisation stage by examining overall and task-dependent associations between wearable eye-tracking metrics and self-reported stress, cognitive workload, and fatigue.

1.4 Wearable eye-tracking in non-laboratory settings

Alongside advances in machine learning, wearable eye-tracking technology has enabled researchers to explore these relationships beyond controlled experimental tasks. Wearable systems allow participants to navigate and interact within their environment while continuously monitoring eye movements, thereby enhancing the study of attention and cognitive states under more ecologically valid conditions (Franchak & Yu, 2022). These devices have been employed to evaluate workload, fatigue, and engagement across various research and operational contexts (Gorin et al., 2024; Martinez-Marquez et al., 2021).

Nonetheless, the increased ecological validity associated with wearable eye-tracking introduces additional sources of variability, such as fluctuations in visual input, task behaviours, and environmental factors. Naturalistic fixation behaviour is also shaped by coordinated eye and head movements and the associated costs of gaze reorientation, with the balance between these constraints and information-seeking behaviour varying across tasks (Burlingham et al., 2024). These complexities can obscure relationships that occur in robust laboratory conditions, raising concerns about the generalisation of findings derived from highly standardised experimental designs. Much existing work still relies on small samples, simplified tasks, or simulated experimental environments with limited contextual diversity (Gorin et al., 2024; Ono et al., 2023; Rivas-Vidal et al., 2026).

To address these limitations, recent efforts in egocentric multimodal data collection have produced large-scale naturalistic datasets designed to support machine learning research on human behaviour. Meta's Project Aria provides a wearable multimodal sensor platform that captures egocentric video, audio, inertial, and eye-tracking data and supports derived spatial-mapping output (Engel et al., 2023). This platform supports large-scale data collection in unconstrained environments, enabling the study of perception, attention, and behaviour in naturalistic settings. Building on this ecosystem, datasets such as Ego-Exo4D offer extensive recordings of skilled human activities captured simultaneously from first-person and third-person viewpoints, including multimodal streams such as eye gaze, 3D point clouds, and language annotations (Grauman et al., 2024). Related resources have extended naturalistic multimodal recording to everyday social interaction. For example, the RL-R CHAT dataset uses Project Aria to capture extended egocentric multimodal recordings of group conversations in quiet and noisy environments (Miller et al., 2026). Together, these resources exemplify the increasing scale and contextual diversity of naturalistic wearable datasets available for modelling behaviour across activities, environments, and individuals.

Despite these advances, relatively few studies have examined a broad range of fixation, saccade, and gaze-distribution features in relation to stress, cognitive workload, and fatigue within the same repeated-measures wearable eye-tracking dataset (Kovalenko et al., 2023; Rivas-Vidal et al., 2026). As a result, it remains unclear not only which features are most strongly associated with these states, but also which associations remain consistent across naturalistic activities and which depend fundamentally on task.

1.5 The present study

The present study examined whether associations between wearable eye-tracking metrics and selfreported stress, cognitive workload, and fatigue were consistent across diverse naturalistic activities or depended on the task performed. The broader analysis included 104 metrics characterising fixations, saccades, gaze distribution, and head motion; the present paper focuses on the ocular metrics. Data were collected using a repeated-measures, assessment-centre-style task battery involving social evaluation, written reasoning, visuospatial planning, video comprehension, and verbal presentation.

The first aim was to identify the feature families and individual metrics most strongly associated with each self-reported state overall, after accounting for mean differences between tasks. Because the existing literature has produced mixed and sometimes contradictory findings, we did not specify directional hypotheses about whether individual metrics would be positively or negatively associated with each state. Accordingly, the analysis of overall feature-state associations was exploratory.

The second aim was to test the a priori hypothesis that task context would moderate feature-state associations. Specifically, we hypothesised that the magnitude, and potentially the direction, of associations between eye-tracking and head-movement metrics and self-reported states would differ across tasks. This hypothesis was motivated by evidence that visual-attention allocation changes systematically with task goals and behavioural context (Hadnett-Hunter et al., 2019), and that visualsearch strategies may be stable within an activity but show limited consistency across different activities (Clarke et al., 2022). We used metric-by-task interactions to test this hypothesis. Distinguishing comparatively task-general from task-dependent associations may help explain inconsistencies in the existing literature and inform the selection and interpretation of candidate features for future machine-learning models.

Additional exploratory analyses examined factors relevant to interpreting and potentially using these features for prediction. We assessed correlations among the three self-reported states and used conditional models to examine whether feature associations remained after accounting for the other concurrent states. Because fatigue and ocular behaviour may both change over a session, sensitivity analyses also tested whether fatigue-related associations remained after adjustment for elapsed session time. Finally, we examined correlations among the eye-tracking metrics to characterise redundancy among features.

2 Materials and Methods

2.2 Participants

A total of 103 adults participated in the main study (Mage = 34.8 years, SDage = 13.5 years, 46 males, 56 females, 1 preferred not to say). One recording was unavailable because the file was missing, leaving 102 participants with available recordings. We excluded five additional participants from the featurestate analyses because their data did not meet completeness requirements due to missing personalised eye-gaze data or misaligned timestamps across event, self-report, and eye-gaze records. This resulted in a primary analytical sample of 97 participants.

Participants were recruited through advertisements at Exeter Science Park, social media, and personal referrals. Eligibility was restricted to adults aged 18 years or older with normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Individuals who typically wear glasses were required to wear contact lenses during the session. All participants provided informed consent before taking part and received compensation after completing the study.

An ethics review board approved the study before it began. The board included members with doctoral-level and professorial research expertise and relevant experience. It reviewed the study procedures, informed consent, participant welfare, data protection, and debriefing arrangements.

2.3 Design

The study employed a naturalistic repeated-measures design to examine within-participant variation in self-reported stress, cognitive workload, and fatigue across a series of assessment-centre-style tasks. To balance ecological relevance with procedural control, the protocol incorporated standardised task administration, repeated self-report assessments, and counterbalancing. Participants completed four tasks according to one of four predefined sequences (A-D), with stress, cognitive workload, and fatigue assessed repeatedly throughout the session using separate self-report scales. Task order was counterbalanced using a fixed A-B-C-D rotation rather than random assignment, producing an approximately even distribution across sequences (25-26 participants per order).

2.4 Apparatus and materials

2.4.3 Eye-tracking apparatus

Eye-tracking data were recorded using Meta Project Aria Gen 1 glasses connected to the Aria companion application (Engel et al., 2023). Recordings were acquired using profile 28, which determines the device recording specifications such as capture rate and image resolution. Profile 28 specifically sets the sampling rate of inward-facing eye cameras to 60 Hz. Participants completed an in-session calibration through the companion application before the task battery began (Mansour et al., 2025).

2.4.4 Experimental software

Task presentation, self-report collection, and event marking were implemented in PsychoPy (version 2025.2.4; Peirce et al., 2019). Informed consent and demographic questionnaires were administered through Gorilla Experiment Builder (http://(www.gorilla.sc; Anwyl-Irvine et al., 2020).

2.4.5 Task battery

The task battery comprised four time-limited activities selected to sample a range of cognitive and social demands encountered in non-laboratory or applied contexts, such as the workplace. These included reading and information integration, planning and problem-solving, verbal communication, and social evaluation. The tasks were not intended as independent or equivalent experimental inductions of stress, cognitive workload, or fatigue. Rather, they were designed to generate natural variation in these states across activities with different perceptual and behavioural demands.

Two activities included substantial reading components because processing written information and formulating responses are common features of many everyday and workplace activities. The battery also included visuospatial planning, video comprehension, and spoken presentation tasks to broaden the range of viewing behaviours sampled. This heterogeneity was intentional: reading tends to produce relatively structured scanpaths, whereas planning, video viewing, and spoken or socially evaluative activities permit different visual-search and attentional strategies (Rayner, 1998). Consequently, task was included as a fixed effect in the statistical models to account for mean differences between activities. Nevertheless, the resulting associations should be interpreted as relationships observed within an ecologically varied task battery, rather than as evidence that specific eye-tracking features are entirely independent of task-specific viewing strategies.

1)

Structured Interview Task -Participants read a standardised job description and verbally answered two competency-based questions, with up to 60 seconds allowed for each response. The activity incorporated elements of socially evaluative jobinterview and public-speaking paradigms, which are commonly used to elicit stress responses under controlled conditions (Kirschbaum et al., 1993; Dickerson & Kemeny, 2004). 2)

Email and Reasoning task -Participants read a realistic scheduling email, interpreted the constraints it contained, and provided a written solution. They were given up to 120 seconds to read the email and a further 120 seconds to compose their response. 3) Tower of London task -Participants completed two trials of the Tower of London, a wellestablished measure of planning and executive functioning in which coloured objects are rearranged to reproduce specified target configurations (Shallice, 1982). Participants had up to 120 seconds for each trial. 4)

Comprehension and Presentation task -Participants viewed approximately three to four minutes of instructional material and then delivered a one-minute presentation within a simulated videoconference. This activity combined audiovisual comprehension and information recall with verbal communication and potential social-evaluative demands.

2.4.6 Self-report measures

Participants rated their current stress, cognitive workload, and fatigue using separate 11-point scales ranging from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating greater intensity. All three states were assessed on nine occasions: immediately before and after each of the four tasks, with one additional assessment during the video component of the Comprehension and Presentation task. Visual thermometer scales with descriptive anchors were presented at the beginning of the session to support consistent interpretation of the response scales.

2.5 Procedure

Sessions were conducted individually in meeting rooms at Exeter Science Park by one of two trained researchers and lasted approximately 45 min. Only the participant and researcher were present, preventing the recording of any non-involved persons. Participants were informed that they would complete a series of simulated workplace exercises while wearing eye-tracking glasses. These exercises were modelled on activities commonly used in employment assessment centres, which are structured selection processes in which candidates complete multiple tasks, such as interviews, written exercises, problem-solving activities, and presentations, while their performance is evaluated. This context was selected to provide a realistic combination of cognitive, communicative, and social demands and to create mild social-evaluative pressure that could contribute to natural variation in participants' stress, cognitive workload, and fatigue. To maintain this element, participants were told that their performance would be evaluated and compared with that of other participants. Participation was voluntary, and participants could withdraw at any time without consequence. The purpose and nature of the evaluative framing were fully explained during debriefing.

After providing informed consent, participants completed questionnaires assessing demographic characteristics and voluntarily self-identifying disability. They also reported whether they had participated in a preceding eye-tracking study. Afterwards, the experimental session began (see Figure 1 for a procedure overview). First, participants received the full session instructions and were presented with the visual thermometers. Next, we fitted and adjusted the Aria glasses for each participant. Recording was initiated using the designated recording profile, after which in-session eyetracking calibration was performed through the Aria companion application. The calibration procedure is a structured, device-guided process in which the phone is held at eye level, aligned precisely during a "leveller" step. Once aligned, the participant completes ten calibration rounds by maintaining visual focus on the displayed number while slowly moving their head in the arrow's direction until each countdown finishes. Calibration was repeated as needed until completed successfully.

Participants then completed the four tasks in their assigned counterbalanced order. They reported their ratings of stress, cognitive workload, and fatigue before and after each task stage, with an additional rating occasion during the instructional video. The researcher monitored recording and device status throughout the session and documented any procedural or technical issues that could affect data quality. At the end of the session, participants were thoroughly debriefed and told that the activities were not an actual employment assessment centre, and that their performance was not formally assessed or compared to others. They then received £50 as compensation for their participation.

2.6 Data handling, validation, and processing

At the end of each testing day, the researchers transferred the recordings from the Aria glasses and uploaded them to Aria Studio for storage and processing. Each recording was processed in Aria Studio using Machine Perception Services to generate eye-gaze and simultaneous localisation and mapping outputs.

The resulting files were evaluated using a specialised validation script that examined file availability, integrity, and temporal coverage. Additionally, diagnostic plots were generated for inspection to identify signal discontinuities, implausible values, calibration issues, or other data anomalies.

The output data from the eye-tracking hardware were processed using Cineon's proprietary eyetracking pipeline. The pipeline takes eye-tracking data as input and produces standardised, temporally aligned gaze, pupillometry and eye openness data synchronised with task information and selfreports.

The processing workflow incorporates hardware-specific adaptors, such as the Aria adaptor in this case, which transform raw eye-tracking recordings from different devices into Cineon's standardised data format. These adaptors perform coordinate transformations between the distinct components of the eye-tracking hardware, including the scene and eye-tracking cameras, as well as resampling and synchronisation with events and self-reports. The adaptor output conforms to a common Cineon schema, thereby enabling subsequent processing to be conducted consistently irrespective of the hardware used to acquire the recordings. Following conversion, the standardised Cineon data can undergo the same validation, preprocessing, feature-extraction, and analysis procedures across different recording systems.

Following standardisation, the resulting signals were processed using a signal-processing pipeline comprising filtering and resampling. The signals were resampled to exactly 60 Hz to correct residual jitter. Subsequently, the feature-extraction pipeline segmented the data into fixed-length windows of 60 s, with a 30 s overlap between consecutive windows. Each self-report was paired with the 60 s of eye-tracking data immediately preceding the corresponding response. Within each window, features related to fixations, saccades, gaze dispersion, and gaze entropy, along with summary statistics characterising gaze behaviour, were computed. Fixations and saccades were identified using the I-VT (Velocity Threshold Identification) method applied to the gaze signal.

Features were organised at three levels. Feature family referred to the broad signal class: gaze, fixation, saccade, and head movement. Feature group referred to the construct represented within a family, such as duration, angular amplitude, angular speed, or dispersion. A metric referred to the summary statistic calculated for that construct, such as the mean, median, upper quartile, or standard deviation. Descriptions of eye-tracking feature groups are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

2.7 Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in R using RStudio (version 2023.12.0; Posit Team, 2023, R Core Team, 2025). Tests were two-sided, with statistical significance determined using an FDR-adjusted threshold of p < .05.

Pre-post changes in stress, cognitive workload, and fatigue were examined using paired-samples ttests. Differences were calculated as post-task minus pre-task ratings. Effect sizes were reported as Cohen's dz with 95% confidence intervals, and the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied across the 12 comparisons (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). Within-participant associations among selfreported states were examined using repeated-measures correlations implemented in the rmcorr package (Bakdash & Marusich, 2017). Between-participant associations were examined using Pearson correlations between participant-level mean ratings. Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied separately across the three correlations at each level. Feature redundancy was assessed using pooled Spearman correlations and Pearson correlations after participant-mean centring.

Feature-state associations were estimated using separate linear mixed-effects models for each of the 104 eye-tracking and head-movement metrics and each self-report outcome (Bates, 2005). Outcomes and metrics were standardised before fitting the additive task-adjusted models. For the taskinteraction models and their task-specific slopes, only metrics were standardised; task-specific coefficients therefore represent changes in outcome rating points per one-standard-deviation increase in the metric. Each model included the metric and task as fixed effects, a participant random intercept, and a first-order autoregressive residual structure based on the sequential ordering of observations within participants. Models were fitted by maximum likelihood using the lme function from the nlme package (Pinheiro et al., 2026). Metric coefficients therefore represented standardised associations averaged across the task battery after accounting for mean differences between tasks.

Task dependence was tested by adding a metric-by-task interaction and comparing the interaction model with the corresponding additive model using a likelihood-ratio test. Task-specific coefficients were estimated from the interaction models; representative coefficients for metrics with FDRsignificant interactions are reported. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied separately across the 104 feature tests for each outcome and separately across the corresponding 104 interaction tests. For task-specific slopes, Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied across metrics separately within each outcome-task combination.

Sensitivity analyses repeated the fatigue models with standardised elapsed session time included as a covariate. Elapsed time was calculated from the start timestamp of each participant's first retained task segment. Conditional models additionally controlled for the other two self-reported states; fatigue models also retained elapsed time. These analyses were interpreted as estimates of conditionally unique associations rather than as tests of state-specific biomarkers. FDR correction was applied separately across the 104 tests within each outcome and analysis family.

3 Results

3.2 Analysis samples and changes in self-reported states

Of the 103 participants, 98 had complete paired self-report data for all task-stage comparisons, whereas 97 contributed sufficient matched eye-tracking and self-report observations for the mixedeffects analyses.

Across the task battery, mean ratings were highest for stress (M = 2.96, SD = 2.17), followed by cognitive workload (M = 2.78, SD = 2.21) and fatigue (M = 1.62, SD = 1.75). Median ratings were 3 for cognitive workload and stress and 1 for fatigue.

All task stages showed significant increases in stress, cognitive workload, and fatigue. Differences were calculated as post-task minus pre-task ratings, such that positive test statistics and effect sizes indicate an increase. FDR correction was applied across the 12 comparisons.

Self-reported stress, cognitive workload, and fatigue increased significantly across all four tasks following FDR correction.

3.3 Correlations among self-reported states and eye-tracking features

The three self-reported states showed substantial overlap (Table 1). Within participants, stress was strongly correlated with cognitive workload, r = .733, 95% CI [.698, .764], and moderately correlated with fatigue, r = .590, 95% CI [.542, .635]. Cognitive workload and fatigue were also moderately correlated, r = .601, 95% CI [.554, .645]. Between-participant correlations based on participant-level mean ratings ranged from .633 to .773. All correlations remained significant after FDR correction, FDRadjusted p < .001. The eye-tracking metrics were also substantially redundant. The 104 metrics generated 5,356 unique feature pairs. In the pooled analysis, 341 pairs had absolute Spearman correlations of at least .70, including 202 at or above .80, 84 at or above .90, and 46 at or above .95. After removing stable between-participant differences by centring features within participants, 298 pairs remained correlated at |r| ≥ .70 and 59 at |r| ≥ .90.

Several pairs were mathematically or nearly perfectly related, including gaze-vector concentration and standard deviation, whose relationship follows from their definitions in circular statistics; gaze entropy and gaze-entropy efficiency, fixation-time and saccade-time percentages, and event counts and their corresponding rates. Accordingly, counts of significant metrics should not be interpreted as counts of independent ocular effects. The results below emphasise representative metrics and feature groups; Supplementary Tables S2 and S3 report the complete feature-correlation matrices.

3.4 Task dependence of feature-state associations 3.4.3 Prevalence of metric-by-task interactions

After FDR correction, we identified significant metric-by-task interactions in 53 of the 104 stress models (51.0%) and 61 of the 104 cognitive-workload models (58.7%). Twelve fatigue models (11.5%) also showed significant interactions in the primary analysis.

Task dependence was particularly common among measures of saccade amplitude, angular speed, and duration. Likelihood ratio tests showed that the strongest stress interaction involved the upper quartile of curved saccade amplitude, χ²(3) = 36.30, FDR-adjusted p < .001. The strongest workload interaction involved median straight saccade amplitude, χ²(3) = 37.43, FDR-adjusted p < .001. In the primary fatigue analysis, the strongest interaction involved median straight saccade amplitude, χ²(3) = 19.95, FDR-adjusted p = .012.

3.5 Average feature-state associations across tasks

Task-adjusted models estimated the average association between each metric and each self-reported state while controlling for differences in mean state ratings across tasks. Because significant interactions were common, these coefficients should be interpreted as summaries across the task battery rather than as associations expected to hold within every activity. Table 3 presents representative significant associations from each feature group.

3.5.3 Stress

Of the 104 metrics, 63 were associated with stress before correction, and 61 remained significant following FDR correction. The strongest association involved gaze-vector standard deviation, with greater gaze variability associated with higher stress, β = .170, SE = .025, t(747) = 6.71, FDR-adjusted p < .001. Gaze-vector concentration showed an equivalent association in the opposite direction because it was nearly perfectly inversely related to gaze-vector standard deviation and was therefore not interpreted as a separate effect.

Stress was also positively associated with mean saccade duration, β = 0.150, SE = 0.024, t(747) = 6.18, FDR-adjusted p < .001, and mean fixation yaw dispersion, β = 0.146, SE = 0.025, t(747) = 5.79, FDRadjusted p < .001. The average stress associations therefore involved greater gaze variability, broader horizontal fixation dispersion, and longer saccade duration.

3.5.4 Cognitive workload

Of the 104 metrics, 47 were associated with cognitive workload before correction, and 40 remained significant following FDR correction. The strongest association involved mean fixation angular distance, β = 0.139, SE = 0.027, t(747) = 5.08, FDR-adjusted p < .001. Workload was also positively associated with mean fixation yaw dispersion, β = 0.125, SE = 0.027, t(747) = 4.68, FDR-adjusted p < .001, and mean fixation pitch dispersion, β = 0.123, SE = 0.028, t(747) = 4.43, FDR-adjusted p < .001.

Gaze-vector concentration was negatively associated with cognitive workload, β = -0.110, SE = 0.028, t(747) = -4.00, FDR-adjusted p < .001, while mean saccade duration showed a smaller positive association, β = 0.094, SE = 0.026, t(747) = 3.61, FDR-adjusted p = .002. Thus, the average workload associations were concentrated primarily among measures of fixation displacement and spatial dispersion.

3.5.5 Fatigue

Of the 104 metrics, 34 were associated with fatigue before correction, and 22 remained significant following FDR correction. The strongest association involved median fixation angular distance, β = 0.085, SE = 0.021, t(747) = 4.13, FDR-adjusted p = .002. Comparable associations were observed for mean fixation pitch dispersion, β = 0.085, SE = 0.021, t(747) = 4.10, FDR-adjusted p = .002; mean fixation angular distance, β = 0.082, SE = 0.020, t(747) = 4.03, FDR-adjusted p = .002; and mean fixation yaw dispersion, β = 0.076, SE = 0.020, t(747) = 3.84, FDR-adjusted p = .002.

Unlike stress and workload, fatigue was not significantly associated with gaze-vector variability or mean saccade duration after FDR correction. Its average associations were concentrated among measures of fixation displacement and spatial dispersion.

3.6.4 Associations conditional on the other self-reported states

Conditional models examined whether the ocular associations remained after accounting for correlations among the three self-reported states. These analyses were interpreted as estimates of conditionally unique statistical associations rather than as tests of definitive state-specific biomarkers.

After including cognitive workload and fatigue as covariates, 37 of the 61 metrics originally associated with stress remained significant after FDR correction. The largest conditional association involved gaze-vector standard deviation, β = 0.091, SE = 0.018, t(745) = 4.97, FDR-adjusted p < .001, 95% CI [0.055, 0.127]. Mean saccade duration also remained positively associated with stress, β = 0.085, SE = 0.017, t(745) = 4.89, FDR-adjusted p < .001, 95% CI [0.051, 0.120]. These coefficients were smaller than those obtained in the primary models, indicating that part, but not all, of the stress associations reflected variance shared with workload and fatigue.

Two fixation-duration metrics that were not significant in the primary stress models became significant after covariate adjustment. Mean fixation duration was negatively associated with residual stress, β = -0.053, SE = 0.019, t(745) = -2.76, FDR-adjusted p = .018, 95% CI [-0.090, -0.015], as was the upper quartile of fixation duration, β = -0.051, SE = 0.019, t(745) = -2.73, FDR-adjusted p = .019, 95% CI [-0.087, -0.014]. Because these effects emerged only after adjustment for correlated states, they may represent suppression effects.

None of the 40 metrics originally associated with cognitive workload remained significant after we included stress and fatigue as covariates. The largest conditional coefficient involved mean fixation angular distance, β = 0.059, FDR-adjusted p = .156. Similarly, none of the 10 time-adjusted fatigue associations remained significant after stress and cognitive workload were included; the largest coefficient involved median fixation angular distance, β = 0.044, FDR-adjusted p = .219.

These findings suggest that the observed associations between workload and fatigue predominantly reflected variance shared across the subjective state measures. By contrast, several gaze and saccade metrics remained associated with the component of stress variance not shared with concurrent workload and fatigue. Nevertheless, these conditional associations should not be construed as uniquely diagnostic biomarkers, as adjusting for highly correlated ratings may produce both attenuation and suppression effects.

4 Discussion

This study examined whether associations between wearable eye-tracking and head-movement metrics and self-reported stress, cognitive workload, and fatigue were consistent across assessment-centrestyle activities. Our results supported the hypothesis that these associations would depend on task context, particularly for stress and cognitive workload. In the task-adjusted analyses, stress was most strongly associated with gaze variability, whereas workload and fatigue were most strongly associated with fixation spatial distribution. Further analyses showed that these relationships needed to be interpreted in light of temporal progression and overlap among the subjective states. Several fatigue associations attenuated after adjustment for elapsed session time, and none of the workload or timeadjusted fatigue associations remained significant after accounting for the other subjective states. Several stress associations persisted following this adjustment, although this does not establish their specificity to stress.

Our pre-and post-test comparisons indicated that the task battery elicited significant increases in stress, workload, and fatigue across the reported stages. Workload generally showed greater change than stress or fatigue, with the largest raw increase during the Tower of London task and the largest standardised effect during the Comprehension and Presentation task. These results support the conclusion that the battery generated within-participant variability in subjective demand. However, since the three states increased concurrently and the tasks were not intentionally designed to manipulate each state independently, we cannot infer that specific activities selectively induced particular states. Furthermore, the observed differences in changes among activities were descriptive rather than directly tested statistically.

The most compelling evidence of task dependency comes from significant metric-by-task interactions in 53 of 104 stress models and 61 of 104 workload models. Associations involving saccade amplitude, angular speed, and duration varied in both magnitude and, in some cases, direction across activities. This finding aligns with existing research showing that task goals influence fixation locations and eyemovement patterns (Mills et al., 2011; Hadnett-Hunter et al., 2019), and that viewing strategies are more consistent within specific activities than across diverse tasks (Clarke et al., 2022). Activities such as reading, planning, video viewing, and verbal interaction require distinct visual sampling strategies; thus, these differing requirements may explain the inconsistent associations with subjective states reported across studies using varied activities.

The contrast between Comprehension and Presentation and Tower of London illustrates this point. Task-specific associations were more prominent during the former, despite the substantial pre-post workload increase during Tower of London. Greater subjective change therefore did not necessarily coincide with more widespread ocular associations. The combination of visual processing, memory retrieval, speech planning, and social communication during Comprehension and Presentation may have permitted greater variation in gaze behaviour, whereas the constrained layout of Tower of London may have restricted it. However, because we did not independently manipulate these properties, we cannot determine which accounted for the differences. Where significant interactions were present, the task-adjusted coefficients should consequently be interpreted as pooled estimates rather than relationships that held consistently across activities.

Alongside the task-specific findings, our task-adjusted analyses identified associations across the battery. Stress was associated with greater gaze-vector variability and lower gaze-vector concentration, suggesting more dispersed gaze at higher stress ratings. This pattern may reflect less stable attentional allocation or increased environmental monitoring, consistent with previous observations under stress (Ahmadi et al., 2024). It is also compatible with Attentional Control Theory, which proposes that anxiety reduces goal-directed attentional control (Eysenck et al., 2007), although we did not directly test this mechanism. Stress was also associated with longer saccade durations, in contrast to the shorter saccades reported by Ahmadi et al. (2024). Together, these findings suggest that stress-related changes in visual behaviour may differ across task settings.

By comparison, workload and fatigue were most strongly associated with greater fixation angular distance and dispersion, indicating a wider spatial distribution of fixations at higher ratings. This could reflect more extensive visual sampling as participants experienced greater demand or tiredness. Previous research has linked fixation behaviour to memory and processing demands (Meghanathan et al., 2015), although it does not directly explain the spatial pattern observed here. Because fixation distribution also depends on the arrangement of relevant information and the costs of acquiring it (Burlingham et al., 2024), these associations cannot distinguish increased subjective demand from changes in the visual strategies used to perform the tasks.

For fatigue, our exploratory sensitivity analysis showed that session progression was an additional consideration. Fatigue increased systematically over time after accounting for mean task differences.

Including elapsed time reduced the number of significant fatigue associations from 22 to 10, with all retained associations involving fixation spatial distribution. None of the 12 initial fatigue metric-by-task interactions remained significant. This sensitivity to temporal adjustment is consistent with evidence that oculomotor behaviour changes with time on task (Friedman & Komogortsev, 2024). Because task order and elapsed time were related, however, the disappearance of significant interactions does not establish task independence. Counterbalanced task order and concurrent measures of sleepiness and ocular discomfort would help distinguish activity-related effects from accumulated mental fatigue and visual strain (Bafna & Hansen, 2021).

The conditional analyses further examined whether the observed associations included information beyond what was shared among stress, workload, and fatigue. Following adjustments for the other ratings, 37 of the original 61 stress-related associations remained statistically significant, including those pertaining to gaze variability, gaze concentration, and mean saccade duration. The lack of significant associations with workload and fatigue after conditioning aligns with the notion that these features capture overlapping information; however, the disappearance of significance alone does not definitively demonstrate that shared variance entirely accounts for the initial relationships. Additionally, controlling for correlated ratings changes how we interpret the remaining variance and may induce attenuation or suppression effects (Lynam et al., 2006). The persistence of certain stress-related associations suggests that concurrent workload and fatigue do not fully explain these relationships, nor do they provide evidence of exclusively stress-specific mechanisms.

Our findings bear significant implications for predictive modelling. Although we did not directly assess predictive performance, the identified interactions highlight relationships that require explicit validation to assess transferability across activities. Employing participant-independent and task-held-out evaluation methods, along with activity-specific performance reporting and explicit modelling of task context, would be instrumental in determining the generalisability of candidate features. Concurrent modelling of stress, workload, and fatigue may further elucidate shared versus residual information. Furthermore, given the substantial correlation among numerous metrics, the count of significant associations should not be interpreted as indicative of independent, informative signals. Techniques such as feature selection, regularisation, or aggregating related measures are essential to discern their unique contributions. Consequently, these findings support evaluating ocular features across diverse activities and correlated subjective states before deploying them as generalisable indicators of specific cognitive states.

4.1 Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, the study was observational, and the tasks did not independently manipulate stress, workload, or fatigue. These states co-occurred and were assessed using brief self-report ratings, which reflected subjective experience rather than an objective criterion. Accordingly, causal conclusions and strong claims of construct specificity are unwarranted. Because the feature-state models did not explicitly distinguish within-participant from between-participant associations, their coefficients may reflect both sources of variation.

Second, although the activities were more naturalistic than conventional fixed-screen paradigms, they remained structured assessment-centre-style tasks. Moreover, they differed in several ways, including content, visual layouts, response requirements, social demands, and temporal positions. These differences capture relevant aspects of applied task context but prevent identification of the specific properties responsible for each interaction. Task order also limited the separation of activity-related effects from temporal progression, particularly for fatigue.

Furthermore, the 60-Hz sampling rate limited the temporal resolution required for detailed analyses of saccadic dynamics (Mack et al., 2017). Additionally, our processing pipeline did not produce validated measures of pupil size and blinks, thereby limiting the range of ocular parameters analysed.

It is important to note that these exclusions are attributable to the specific pipeline used for this dataset rather than to inherent limitations of the device family.

Finally, the feature-level analyses were exploratory and examined each metric independently despite substantial redundancy among features. They characterised associations within the present dataset rather than out-of-sample predictive performance. Independent replication and predictive validation are required before these findings can support claims about reliable state estimation.

4.2 Conclusion

Task context substantially shaped associations between wearable eye-tracking features and selfreported stress, cognitive workload, and fatigue. Saccadic associations were particularly taskdependent, whereas fixation-distribution measures showed broader associations qualified by temporal progression and overlap among the subjective states. Several stress associations persisted after adjustment for workload and fatigue but did not establish stress-specific biomarkers. These findings support characterising candidate features across heterogeneous activities and evaluating task context, elapsed time, and correlated subjective states when developing wearable state-estimation models.
