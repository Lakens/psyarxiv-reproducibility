Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

• Early adversity biases precision in specific channels, not as a global gain.

• Precision-only and maladaptive-prior accounts make dissociable predictions.

• Sensitive-period brakes and self-confirming sampling can stabilize calibration.

• The ACC is proposed as a hub within a distributed precision circuit.

• Supportive input predicts faster precision re-tuning, not a mirror image of harm.

1. Introduction: The Predicting Brain and the Developing Brain

>Introduction: The Predicting Brain and the Developing Brain

1.1 The Brain as a Prediction Machine

One increasingly influential perspective in cognitive neuroscience conceptualizes the brain not as a passive, stimulus-driven processor of external information, but as a proactive, hypothesis-testing organ that continuously generates and updates predictions about the world (Clark, 2013; Friston, 2010). This view is most formally articulated within predictive coding and predictive processing frameworks, which have been applied to perception, action, cognition, and psychopathology (Hohwy, 2013; Rao and Ballard, 1999). These frameworks are neither new nor uncontested (Section 3.3), and they should not be treated as universally applicable explanatory models. Rather, they provide an integrative lens through which specific developmental processes may be re-examined, including how early experiences shape expectations, regulatory systems, and adaptive responses to environmental demands.

We use predictive processing (PP) as the general term for this family of theories, and reserve predictive coding for the specific hierarchical, message-passing implementation in which each cortical level predicts the activity of the level below and forwards only the residual prediction error (Rao and Ballard, 1999). At its core, PP holds that the brain constructs and continuously refines a generative model of the world to infer the causes of its sensory input, minimizing prediction error, the discrepancy between what is predicted and what is sensed (Friston, 2010). Perception is then not a bottom-up reconstruction of reality from raw sense data, but a model-driven process in which sensory input primarily serves to correct predictions (Clark, 2013).

1.2 Early Life as a Period of Calibration

If the brain is a prediction machine, its foundational models are not fixed at birth but are calibrated by experience. Early life, particularly the period from conception to two years of age, is marked by unparalleled neuroplasticity, during which environmental exposures shape emerging structural and functional architecture (Van den Bergh et al., 2020; Van den Bergh et al., 2024). This plasticity is not uniform in time: it is organized into critical and sensitive periods that open and close on different schedules in different neural systems, and whose closure is actively regulated (Hensch, 2005; Knudsen, 2004). From a dynamicsystems perspective, development is an emergent property of the reciprocal coupling of organism and environment; early caregiver-infant interactions establish stable yet flexible "attractor states" that canalize long-term trajectories (Montirosso et al., 2020; Provenzi et al., 2018). The statistical regularities, affective tone, and predictability of the early environment provide the foundational "training data" for the brain's generative models. Calibration is not limited to adversity: the developing brain is experience-expectant and depends on structured, supportive input to organize adaptively (Greenough et al., 1987; Nelson and Gabard-Durnam, 2020). Section 2 sets out these developmental foundations before the computational account is introduced, because they determine why early calibration matters disproportionately and why it can persist.

1.3 Thesis Statement

This article bridges PP and developmental psychopathology by proposing a framework for the lasting impact of ELA, a broad construct spanning prenatal stress and perinatal complications through neglectful or abusive caregiving (Chertoff et al., 2026). Although our title concerns adversity, we treat ELA as one pole of a broader early-life environment that ranges from depriving to supportive, because the same predictive machinery is calibrated across this whole range. We argue that ELA does not simply inflict increased risk; it calibrates a predictive architecture toward survival in an environment experienced as hostile, unpredictable, or unsupportive. The framework makes two claims of graded strength: 1.

Core (parsimonious) claim: altered precision weighting. ELA primarily recalibrates how much each prediction error counts: assigning high precision (gain) to potentialthreat signals and to predictable input that would normally be attenuated, while assigning low precision to evidence that disconfirms expectations of threat or signals benign novelty. This claim requires no commitment to the content of highlevel beliefs.

2. Stronger (optional) claim: maladaptive priors. Under sustained adversity, these precision biases can become entrenched as rigid, pessimistic content priors about the self, others, and the world. This claim is more explanatorily ambitious and correspondingly more demanding empirically, because it attributes specific belief content to the developing system.

PP accounts have been applied to psychopathology and, in particular, to autism, where atypical precision has been proposed as a core computational feature (Lawson et al., 2014; Van de Cruys et al., 2014). What we add is fourfold. First, we argue that ELA-related precision dysregulation is channel-specific and biased rather than global, and we commit to this specificity in an explicit mapping from dimensions of early experience to predicted precision channels (Section 4.4, Table 2). Second, we specify behavioral, computational, and neural signatures that would distinguish altered precision from altered priors (Section 4.3, Table 1). Third, we anchor the calibration in developmental mechanisms (sensitive periods, plasticity brakes, and individual differences in susceptibility; Section 2) and in a distributed circuit in which the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) is proposed as a hub for allocating gain across channels (Section 5). Fourth, we hold the account to a symmetry constraint, in which the same machinery is calibrated by supportive as well as adverse input, with an asymmetric prediction of its own (Section 7.2).

Two scope statements frame what follows. The framework's primary domain is threat vigilance and affective learning and decision-making under uncertainty; extensions to other outcomes are secondary and are flagged as such, and Section 7.3 states the processes the framework does not claim to explain. Its evidential basis is the published literature reviewed here. Prenatal-anxiety findings from two longitudinal cohorts, published individually and synthesized through the present lens in a companion paper (Van den Bergh and Frasch, 2026), are used as illustrations of compatibility, not as independent tests, because the same findings partly motivated the framework.

2. Developmental Foundations: Why Early Calibration Matters and Why It Persists

>Developmental Foundations: Why Early Calibration Matters and Why It Persists

2.1 Critical and Sensitive Periods, and Two Kinds of Experience

The influence of experience on the developing brain is not constant over time but concentrated in windows of heightened plasticity. In a critical period, a circuit needs a specific, species-typical input to develop typically, and missing that input has lasting consequences; a sensitive period is a wider window in which experience shapes development strongly but does not dictate it (Gabard-Durnam and McLaughlin, 2020; Knudsen, 2004; Nelson and Gabard-Durnam, 2020). Greenough et al. (1987) linked such windows to two kinds of plasticity. Experience-expectant plasticity relies on input that every member of the species can count on, such as patterned sensory stimulation and contingent social interaction, and for which the brain is prepared in advance. Experiencedependent plasticity stores what is particular to an individual's environment: how sensitive the caregiving is, and how much threat, deprivation, or unpredictability the child meets (Chertoff et al., 2026; Teicher and Samson, 2016).

This distinction matters for the present account. An input counts as experience-expectant only if the brain is prepared for it as a normal part of development, and most forms of adversity fail this test. Adversity therefore acts mainly as an experience-dependent input to calibration rather than as an expected signal shaping circuits through canonical criticalperiod mechanisms (Chertoff et al., 2026; Gabard-Durnam and McLaughlin, 2020). Deprivation is the partial exception: it is the absence of expected input, a violation of the expectable environment (Nelson and Gabard-Durnam, 2020), and we accordingly predict a different precision profile for deprivation than for threat (Table 2). In PP terms, experienceexpectant input supplies the structured statistics from which usable generative models and adaptive precision estimates are built; experience-dependent input tunes those estimates to the particular statistics of an individual's environment.

Windows differ between systems: sensory systems have early windows, whereas amygdala-prefrontal circuitry, the ACC, and association cortex mature later and remain plastic into adolescence, a second sensitive period for threat, reward, and control (Chertoff et al., 2026; Gee et al., 2013; Larsen and Luna, 2018). Prenatally, maternal signals reach the fetus through biological channels (maternal cortisol and placental corticotropin-releasing hormone, maternal heart rate and circadian rhythms, uterine blood flow, and maternal movement and voice), and fetal responses to changes in maternal psychophysiological state are measurable from mid-gestation (DiPietro et al., 2008; Van den Bergh et al., 2020). Timing within gestation also matters: in the Leuven cohort, maternal anxiety at 12 to 22 weeks of gestation, rather than later in pregnancy, was associated with impulsivity and poorer endogenous cognitive control in adolescence (Mennes et al., 2006; Van den Bergh et al., 2005), a period of intense neuronal migration and early cortical organization. We therefore expect different precision channels to be calibrated in different windows, a commitment made explicit in the window column of Table 2.

2.2 Plasticity Gates and Their Relation to Precision

The opening and closure of plasticity windows are regulated by identifiable mechanisms: the maturation of parvalbumin-positive inhibitory interneurons and the resulting excitation-inhibition balance, the formation of perineuronal nets that stabilize synapses, and epigenetic and neuro-glial "brakes" that restrict later remodeling (Hensch, 2005; Takesian and Hensch, 2013; see Chertoff et al., 2026, for a review). Two observations link these gates directly to precision. First, the neuromodulators proposed to set precision (Section 5.4) also gate developmental plasticity: catecholamine depletion prevents experience-dependent ocular-dominance plasticity during the visual critical period (Kasamatsu and Pettigrew, 1976), and the cholinergic brake Lynx1 limits plasticity in adult cortex (Morishita et al., 2010). Second, adversity can shift window timing. Early stress has been associated with accelerated maturation of amygdala-prefrontal connectivity in previously institutionalized children (Gee et al., 2013; see Callaghan and Tottenham, 2016, for the stress-acceleration hypothesis), environmental factors influence the pace of brain development more generally (Tooley et al., 2021), and in mice early-life stress induces precocious critical-period timing with premature perineuronal-net formation (Poplawski et al., 2024).

Together these findings suggest a specific developmental hypothesis: precision set-points are learned from the statistics of the environment while the relevant window is open, under the control of neuromodulatory systems that are themselves gates on plasticity, and are stabilized when the brakes engage. If adversity also accelerates closure, the set-point would be fixed earlier, from a shorter and more adverse sample of experience, and with less opportunity for later correction.

2.3 Biological Embedding

Experience becomes biologically embedded through lasting molecular and physiological changes (Berens et al., 2017; Lupien et al., 2009). ELA alters DNA methylation of stressregulatory genes such as the glucocorticoid receptor gene NR3C1, a molecular memory of the early environment (Berens et al., 2017), and can recalibrate the hypothalamic-pituitaryadrenal (HPA) axis toward hyper-responsive or blunted reactivity, with consequences for glucocorticoid-receptor-dense regions such as the hippocampus, amygdala, and prefrontal cortex (Lupien et al., 2009; McEwen, 2012). Prenatally, embedding begins in utero: maternal stress signals reach the fetus through the maternal-placental-fetal interface, where placental regulation, for example of the glucocorticoid-inactivating enzyme 11β-HSD2, partly determines fetal exposure (Van den Bergh et al., 2020). We speak of developmental "signs" rather than only "scars": the same machinery embeds supportive caregiving, whose marks are as durable but adaptive (Nelson and Gabard-Durnam, 2020; Van den Bergh et al., 2024).

2.4 Why Calibration Persists

Plasticity explains why early experience can change neural function; it does not by itself explain why an adaptation persists after the conditions that elicited it have changed. We propose three complementary mechanisms, the third of which is specific to the predictive account.

1. Structural stabilization. Once plasticity brakes engage (Section 2.2), set-points learned during the window become harder to revise, so a calibration acquired early is carried forward by circuit structure.

2. Molecular and physiological memory. Epigenetic marks and recalibrated HPA setpoints (Section 2.3) maintain an altered internal milieu that continues to bias neuromodulatory tone, and hence precision, in later life.

3. Self-confirming sampling.

A system that assigns low precision to disconfirming evidence discounts exactly the evidence that would retune it. Under active inference, it also selects actions, such as avoidance and reduced exploration, that make such evidence less likely to be sampled at all (Friston, 2010; Lloyd et al., 2022). The calibration thereby protects itself: the information needed to revise it is either down-weighted or never collected.

The third mechanism explains why calibration can persist in objectively safe later environments without assuming explicit negative beliefs, and it locates the developmental mismatch central to the developmental origins of health and disease (DOHaD) framework: calibration that fitted the early environment becomes costly when later environments diverge from it (Chertoff et al., 2026; Ellison, 2005). Persistence is not permanence: adolescence reopens windows for threat, reward, and control systems (Larsen and Luna, 2018), and the self-confirming loop can be broken by interventions that force highprecision disconfirming evidence (Section 7.4).

2.5 Individual Differences in Susceptibility

Individuals exposed to similar early conditions follow markedly different trajectories: some show little lasting impact, some recover after an initial disruption, some develop difficulties only later, and some remain chronically impaired (Bonanno et al., 2023; Rutter, 2012). The framework should therefore not be read deterministically. Differentialsusceptibility and biological-sensitivity-to-context models propose that some individuals are more responsive to environmental input for better and for worse (Belsky et al., 2007; Belsky and Pluess, 2009; Boyce and Ellis, 2005), and that sensitivity may itself be shaped prenatally (Pluess and Belsky, 2011; Hartman et al., 2023). Gene-environment interactions contribute: genotype moderates the association between childhood maltreatment and later antisocial behavior (Byrd and Manuck, 2014; Caspi et al., 2002), dopamine-related genotypes moderate sensitivity to rearing environments (Bakermans-Kranenburg and van IJzendoorn, 2011), and a polygenic score for fasting insulin moderates the association between early adversity and childhood impulsivity (Batra et al., 2021a). Sex, timing, and the availability of buffering relationships further moderate outcomes (Chertoff et al., 2026; Del Giudice et al., 2011).

PP offers a computational translation of susceptibility: a higher plasticity of precision estimates, for example a faster adaptation of estimated volatility and hence of learning rates (Behrens et al., 2007; Mathys et al., 2011), predicts stronger calibration toward threat under adversity and faster calibration toward safety under support, a testable crossover (Section 7.2). Resilience is correspondingly read as preserved flexibility of precision allocation (Bonanno et al., 2023; Kalisch et al., 2024).

3. The Architecture of Prediction: A Primer on Predictive Processing

>The Architecture of Prediction: A Primer on Predictive Processing

3.1 Core Principles

3.1.1 Generative Models and Priors

The brain does not perceive the world directly; it constructs an internal generative model to infer the hidden causes of sensory signals (Friston, 2010). Two terms are often used interchangeably and should be kept apart. The generative model is the full causal model: its structure (which hidden states exist and how they cause one another and the sensory input), the likelihood mapping from states to signals, and the expectations over states. A prior is a narrower object, the expectation over a particular hidden state at a particular level before new evidence arrives, for example that light comes from above, or that danger is imminent. A child can therefore hold an accurate, well-structured generative model of a caregiving environment and still differ from other children in specific priors within it, or in the precision assigned to particular signals. Higher-level social expectations shaped by repeated caregiving experiences resemble what attachment theory describes as internal working models of self, others, and relationships (Bowlby, 1982; Bretherton, 1992; Tottenham and Vannucci, 2025).

3.1.2 Prediction and Prediction Error

The generative model issues top-down predictions that cascade down the hierarchy (Rao and Ballard, 1999). Predictions are compared with bottom-up input; their discrepancy, the prediction error, is propagated upward, and the system minimizes prediction error by updating the model. Because only unpredicted input is passed forward, this is an efficient coding scheme (Friston, 2010).

3.1.3 Precision Weighting

Not all prediction errors are treated equally; their influence on belief updating is scaled by their precision, the brain's estimate of their reliability (formally, the inverse variance) (Feldman and Friston, 2010). Precision acts as a gain control on prediction-error units: a precise error drives rapid updating, whereas an imprecise error is discounted and existing beliefs are preserved. This gain control is widely regarded as a mechanism of attention (Feldman and Friston, 2010), and it lets the brain balance reliance on priors against updating from new evidence. One point of formal discipline should be stated at the outset: precision is not categorically separate from belief, because precision estimates are themselves beliefs about the reliability of signals. The distinction maintained throughout is between content priors (expectations about latent causes and outcomes) and precision expectations (estimates of the reliability of evidence and predictions). Precision weighting is the pivot of our framework: it is the component we argue is most directly recalibrated by early adversity.

3.2 A Hierarchical and Embodied Framework

Low levels of the hierarchy predict simple sensory features; higher levels predict increasingly abstract regularities such as object identities, event sequences, social intentions, and the self (Hohwy, 2013). Under active inference, action is integrated into the predictive cycle: motor commands are predictions about the sensory consequences of movement, and the body moves to fulfill them (Friston, 2010; Seth and Friston, 2016). Action thus also determines which evidence is sampled, which is why avoidance can maintain a calibration (Section 2.4).

3.3 Nuances, Critiques, and Empirical Status

Despite its reach, PP faces serious critiques, the most consequential being potential unfalsifiability (Bowman et al., 2023). Because the content of priors and the settings of precision are not directly observable, a PP model can often be tuned post hoc to explain almost any outcome (Bowman et al., 2023; Kwisthout et al., 2017). Many applications operate in an "as-if" mode, borrowing the mathematics of Bayesian inference to describe behavior without committing to biological implementation (Hodson et al., 2024; Mangalam, 2025).

The empirical status of the framework's core claims is mixed, and the distinction between them matters here. The general claims that the brain is sensitive to prediction and that expectation modulates neural responses are well supported. The specific architectural commitments of predictive coding, distinct prediction and prediction-error units with precision implemented as their gain, have more modest support (Hodson et al., 2024; Walsh et al., 2020). Expectation suppression in sensory cortex is less robust and less clearly separable from repetition suppression and attention than early accounts assumed (Feuerriegel et al., 2021); some findings run opposite to simple prediction-error accounts, for example repetition effects unmodulated by expectation (Song et al., 2025; Taylor et al., 2024); and expected input is sometimes perceived more readily rather than suppressed, a tension PP accounts must resolve (Press et al., 2020). PP is better supported as a computational-level account of learning and inference under uncertainty, where modelbased studies estimate precision-like parameters such as learning rates and volatility (Behrens et al., 2007; Mathys et al., 2014), than as a settled account of cortical microcircuitry.

Our framework is built to rest on the better-supported level: its central constructs (channel-specific precision, volatility-dependent learning rates, prediction-error gain) are estimable from behavior with hierarchical Bayesian models, and its neural claims are stated as hypotheses. Grounding PP in ELA research also narrows the falsifiability problem, which bites hardest when priors and precision are free parameters of an adult brain considered without its history. The ELA literature supplies measurable developmental conditions, with documented associations with, and in animal, quasiexperimental, and intervention designs causal influence on, neurodevelopment. This does not vindicate PP; it constrains otherwise free parameters, which allows the framework to commit to the discriminating predictions of Section 4.3, the channel mapping of Section 4.4, and the boundary conditions of Section 7.3.

4. Hypothesis: ELA Recalibrates Precision and, More Strongly, May Instantiate Maladaptive Priors

Supplementary Table S1 summarizes the framework by PP component, including the experience-expectant or positive-input interpretation of each component, which captures the symmetry principle that the same component is calibrated by all early input.

4.1 The Core Claim: Dysregulation of Precision Weighting

ELA disrupts the capacity to allocate precision flexibly and adaptively. We distinguish two facets: • Over-precision of potential-threat and predictable input (hypervigilance).

Having "learned" that the environment is dangerous or hard to predict, the system assigns high precision to any signal that might indicate threat, and fails to attenuate precision for repetitive, predictable input that should become irrelevant. Threataligned prediction errors are amplified, capturing attention and driving updating in a self-reinforcing loop (Adams et al., 2013; Sterzer et al., 2018).

• Under-precision of disconfirming or positive evidence (inflexible updating). The system assigns low precision to prediction errors that would contradict a threatbiased model; unexpected positive events (kindness, success, safety) are treated as noise (Sterzer et al., 2018).

A finding that appears to contradict the hypervigilance profile sharpens it. Model-based fMRI in a birth cohort followed to adulthood links ELA to reduced expected-value and reward-prediction-error signaling in striatum, ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), and ACC (Sacu et al., 2024), and adults with more adverse childhood experiences explore less and underweight recent reward feedback in a foraging task (Lloyd et al., 2022). Adolescents exposed to early stress likewise learn stimulus-outcome contingencies more slowly and update them less well after a reversal (Harms et al., 2018). The evidence is not one-sided: in a probabilistic learning task, maltreated adolescents showed not only greater choice variability but also biased beliefs about the likelihood of reward (Hanson et al., 2017), a priors-type effect, which indicates that the two mechanisms distinguished in Section 4.3 can co-occur. Reduced reward-prediction-error encoding is not what a globally hyper-responsive system would show. We therefore make the reconciliation explicit: under a limited precision budget, chronic over-weighting of threat-aligned channels is purchased at the cost of under-weighting reward-and value-related channels. ELA neither raises nor lowers responsiveness globally; it reallocates gain across channels. This is a testable commitment: within individuals, threat-channel gain and reward-channel encoding should be inversely related, and interventions that reduce threat-channel gain should partially restore reward-prediction-error encoding. A globally elevated or globally blunted gain profile in the same individuals would count against it.

This dual bias is a form of maladaptive meta-learning: the system has learned a biased rule for how to learn from future evidence (Nussenbaum and Hartley, 2024). Precision in this sense is signal-and level-specific, not one global parameter: sensory precision for repeated input, affective-salience precision for threat-related cues, attentional precision for neutral or ambiguous stimuli, and interoceptive precision for bodily signals. The ELA profile we propose is biased toward particular signals rather than globally elevated, which distinguishes it from the global over-weighting proposed for autism (Section 7.1).

4.2 The Stronger Claim: Formation of Maladaptive Priors

Under sustained adversity, precision biases may consolidate into rigid, pessimistic content priors: exteroceptive priors (social interactions predicted as rejecting; ambiguity predicted to resolve negatively), interoceptive priors (a baseline expectation of high arousal and distress; Barrett et al., 2016), and priors about the self (ineffective, unworthy, or culpable, particularly where a child infers their own inadequacy as the cause of a caregiver's behavior). A high-confidence prior resists updating and can manufacture confirming evidence: a person who strongly predicts rejection may perceive a neutral expression as scornful. Physically abused children, for example, show broadened perceptual categories for anger (Pollak and Kistler, 2002), a finding that could reflect either a shifted prior or altered precision on ambiguous facial cues, and is therefore exactly the kind of result the tests in Section 4.3 are designed to adjudicate.

We flag explicitly that the priors claim is the framework's strong form. Published ERP studies of infants and children prenatally exposed to maternal anxiety report enhanced processing of repeated standard tones (van den Heuvel et al., 2015), of fearful relative to happy vocalizations (Otte et al., 2015), and of neutral relative to valenced pictures (van den Heuvel et al., 2018), and related work reports altered auditory and attentional responses after prenatal anxiety or depression (De Asis-Cruz et al., 2020; Harvison et al., 2009; Hennessey et al., 2024; Hunter et al., 2012; Luotonen et al., 2022; Maria et al., 2020). These studies did not test PP; read through a PP lens, they can be accommodated by altered precision without requiring explicit negative priors (see Van den Bergh and Frasch, 2026, for a synthesis with adult follow-up data). The priors claim should therefore be advanced only where data warrant the additional commitment, and tested against precision-only alternatives rather than assumed. A recent computational study supports this developmental specificity: using a Bayesian foraging model, Zhang et al. (2026, preprint) found that early-life unpredictability, but not lifelong traumatic experience, predicted altered uncertainty-driven decision-making in adulthood.

4.3 Distinguishing Precision from Priors

Behaviorally, a child with high precision on threat and low precision on safety and a child with pessimistic priors about safety will both show threat-biased decisions. The two accounts are nonetheless distinguishable, because they locate the bias in different terms of the belief update. In a simple Gaussian update, the change in a belief μ after a prediction error δ is Δμ = [πδ / (πδ + πμ)] δ, where πδ is the precision of the evidence and πμ the precision of the current belief. The precision-only account places the ELA effect in π δ , separately for particular channels and for evidence of particular valence; the priors account places it in the starting value of μ (and, in its rigid form, in a high πμ). These locations make different quantitative predictions, which are summarized in Table 1 and can be estimated trial by trial with hierarchical Bayesian or reinforcement-learning models (Mathys et al., 2011 (Mathys et al., , 2014)). The last row answers a question central to any clinical use of the framework. Under the precision-only account, retuning precision should improve inference even in a person whose early expectations were negative, because those expectations are continuously reestimated from evidence once evidence is weighted appropriately. Under the priors account, retuning precision leaves pessimistic beliefs intact until enough disconfirming evidence has accumulated. Existing findings are suggestive but not decisive: reduced value and reward-prediction-error signaling (Sacu et al., 2024) and underweighting of reward feedback (Lloyd et al., 2022) are effects on evidence weighting, as the precision account predicts, whereas biased beliefs about reward likelihood in maltreated adolescents (Hanson et al., 2017) are a priors-type effect; none of these studies compared the two accounts on the same data. The prenatal-anxiety literature supplies a second illustration from adolescence and adulthood. In the Leuven prenatal cohort, higher maternal anxiety at 12 to 22 weeks of gestation was associated with impulsive responding on an encoding task but not with differences in stopping an ongoing response at age 14 to 15 ( Van den Bergh et al., 2005), with poorer performance at age 17 on tasks requiring endogenous integration and control of task parameters but not on prepotent-response inhibition or visual orienting (Mennes et al., 2006), with less efficient decision making and an enlarged frontal P2a component on a gambling task requiring endogenous control, but not on a Go/Nogo task requiring exogenous control, in 17-year-old boys (Mennes et al., 2009), and with altered BOLD responses during endogenous cognitive control at age 20 (Mennes et al., 2020). Externally cued control is scaffolded by precise prediction errors supplied by the task, whereas endogenous control depends on the system's own precision estimates over goals and outcomes; a selective difference in the latter is what a channelspecific precision bias predicts (Van den Bergh and Frasch, 2026). Neither illustration was designed as a test; the decisive studies would fit both accounts to the same trial-by-trial data.

4.4 From Adversity Dimensions to Precision Channels

A falsifiable framework must also state which dimensions of early experience bias which channels, in which direction. Treating "ELA" as one exposure would purchase explanatory coverage at the price of falsifiability, because prenatal maternal anxiety, physical threat, deprivation, environmental unpredictability, and rigidly invariant input differ in timing, severity, controllability, sensory statistics, and social content. Dimensional models distinguish threat from deprivation as separable exposures with distinct neurodevelopmental consequences (McLaughlin et al., 2020; Sheridan and McLaughlin, 2014), and the unpredictability of parental and environmental signals has emerged as a third, separable calibrator of developing circuits (Davis and Glynn, 2024; Glynn and Baram, 2019). Volatility estimation supplies the computational bridge: the brain tracks how quickly contingencies change and adjusts learning rates and precision accordingly (Behrens et al., 2007), a process estimable at the individual level with the Hierarchical Gaussian Filter (Mathys et al., 2011 (Mathys et al., , 2014)). 5. Neural Implementation: A Distributed Precision Circuit with the ACC as a Hub

Precision dysregulation is unlikely to reside in one region. We propose that it is implemented by a distributed circuit and that the ACC is a hub within it, and we separate two claims that differ in evidential strength. The first, well supported, is that the ACC performs computations central to precision: it tracks surprise and volatility, signals errors, and computes the expected value of control (Behrens et al., 2007; Shenhav et al., 2013).

The second, more specific and so far untested, is that ELA-related alterations of the ACC mediate the precision changes proposed here. Developmental evidence that ACC structure and function are altered after adversity exists (Cohen et al., 2006; Sacu et al., 2024; Teicher and Samson, 2016), but mediation has not been demonstrated, and it is stated below as a hypothesis with a test.

We privilege the ACC for three reasons. It integrates the variables that a gain-allocation decision requires (volatility, value, and the cost of control) (Behrens et al., 2007; Shenhav et al., 2013); together with the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) it provides prominent top-down input to the locus coeruleus, an anatomical route by which cortical estimates of uncertainty and value can set noradrenergic gain (Aston-Jones and Cohen, 2005); and it matures late, giving it a long window for calibration (Section 2.1). Its divisions also differ: the dorsal ACC and anterior midcingulate cortex (aMCC) track volatility, surprise, and the expected value of control, whereas subgenual and perigenual divisions, with the vmPFC, track affective value and autonomic outflow. Sections 5.1 and 5.2 concern primarily the dorsal division, and predictions for the two should not be interchanged.

5.1 The ACC in Error Monitoring and Bayesian Inference

The dorsal ACC (dACC) responds robustly to errors, whether detected internally or signaled by negative feedback (Holroyd et al., 2004), positioning it to compute and broadcast prediction errors. Its activity tracks trial-by-trial Bayesian surprise (unsigned prediction error) independently of valence, implicating it in detecting volatility and signaling when the generative model is failing (Behrens et al., 2007; Hayden et al., 2011). ACC error signals are modulated by value: single neurons in primates fire more strongly when a larger predicted reward is lost (Amiez et al., 2006; Kennerley et al., 2006), and the dACC integrates reward over longer timescales, providing a filtered history of outcomes needed to update value-based expectations (Seo and Lee, 2007).

5.2 Hypothesized ACC Dysfunction in ELA: Biased Gain Allocation

We hypothesize biased ACC gain allocation rather than global hyper-responsiveness: chronic early stress and unpredictability re-allocate the ACC's limited gain toward unsigned surprise and threat-aligned error signaling, and away from signed reward prediction errors and expected value. A persistently amplified surprise signal, reporting that the world model is inadequate even in safe contexts, could drive rumination; the same reallocation predicts the reduced expected-value and reward-prediction-error encoding in ACC, vmPFC, and striatum observed after ELA (Sacu et al., 2024). The claim requires distinguishing what a bare "hyper-responsive ACC" conflates (signed versus unsigned errors, threat versus reward domains, task-evoked reactivity versus model-derived parameters, tonic versus phasic activity, developmental stage), and it is falsified by uniformly elevated or uniformly blunted ACC responses across these dimensions in the same individuals. Mediation can be tested longitudinally, by measuring early exposure, later ACC volatility and gain-allocation signals, and later precision parameters in the same individuals, and causally, by manipulating ACC activity in animal models during and after the relevant window.

5.4 The Noradrenergic and Cholinergic Substrate of Precision

Precision weighting, gain control on prediction-error units, is widely proposed to be set by noradrenaline from the locus coeruleus (LC-NE) and acetylcholine from the basal forebrain (Feldman and Friston, 2010). A computational division of labor has been proposed in which acetylcholine signals expected uncertainty and noradrenaline unexpected uncertainty (Yu and Dayan, 2005), and human pharmacology supports distinct roles: noradrenergic antagonism alters learning about unexpected changes, cholinergic antagonism alters the attribution of uncertainty to chance versus contextual change (Marshall et al., 2016), and the β-adrenergic antagonist propranolol reduces learning rates and the influence of volatility on belief updating and pupil-linked arousal (Lawson et al., 2021). These systems mature across the early sensitive periods during which ELA exerts its effects, gate developmental plasticity (Section 2.2), and are engaged by chronic early stress (Lupien et al., 2009). We therefore propose a concrete chain: ELA alters LC-NE and basal-forebrain cholinergic development, which biases precision set-points while plasticity windows are open, producing the hypervigilant, poorly attenuating phenotype of Section 4.1. To our knowledge, developmental neurochemistry has not yet been tied to computational precision estimates in ELA cohorts, which is itself a testable gap.

5.5 Dopamine: Prediction Errors, Precision over Policies, and Early Adversity

Dopamine requires separate treatment, because dopaminergic signaling and precision weighting are distinct constructs that interact. Phasic dopamine activity encodes reward prediction errors (Schultz et al., 1997), a signal that precision then scales. Within active inference, dopamine has additionally been proposed to encode the precision of beliefs about policies, that is, the confidence with which actions are expected to achieve preferred outcomes (FitzGerald et al., 2015; Friston et al., 2012), and dopaminergic midbrain responses in humans track the expected certainty of desired outcomes (Schwartenbeck et al., 2015). Dopaminergic antagonism also impairs the use of uncertainty estimates for fast, adaptive responding (Marshall et al., 2016). Dopamine thus enters the framework at two points: as a generator of the reward-prediction-error signals whose gain we propose ELA reallocates, and as a candidate substrate of policy precision, which governs exploration and avoidance.

Early adversity alters mesocorticolimbic dopamine in ways that fit the gain-reallocation claim. In humans, low reported early parental care was associated with greater stressinduced ventral striatal dopamine release in a small PET study (Pruessner et al., 2004), childhood adversity has been associated with greater amphetamine-induced ventral striatal dopamine release (Oswald et al., 2014) and with elevated presynaptic striatal dopamine function in young adulthood (Egerton et al., 2016), and emotional neglect predicts blunted ventral striatal development in adolescence (Hanson et al., 2015; see Novick et al., 2018, for a review of reward processing after early stress). In animals, prenatal restraint stress alters the development of the dopaminergic system (Baier et al., 2012; Pallarés and Antonelli, 2017); early-life stress primes the ventral tegmental area toward lifelong stress susceptibility through the developmental transcription factor Otx2, an effect mimicked by juvenile but not adult Otx2 knockdown and reversed by its overexpression (Peña et al., 2017); and adversity-induced anhedonia in rats depends on aberrant engagement of amygdala corticotropin-releasing-hormone neurons by rewarding experience (Bolton et al., 2018). Read together, these findings suggest sensitized dopaminergic responses to stress and threat alongside blunted reward-related responses, the dopaminergic counterpart of the reallocation in Section 4.1, rather than a uniform increase or decrease.

Two predictions follow. Computationally, ELA should lower policy precision for rewardseeking actions, reducing exploration and favoring avoidance (consistent with Lloyd et al., 2022), a parameter separable from perceptual learning rates (Table 3). Causally, the animal manipulations above can be combined with computational phenotyping: juvenile Otx2 knockdown in the ventral tegmental area, or partial silencing of amygdala corticotropin-releasing hormone, should shift learning-rate asymmetries and exploration parameters in probabilistic reversal and foraging tasks in the predicted directions, and their reversal should normalize them. Such experiments would also test whether dopaminergic and noradrenergic contributions to the ELA phenotype dissociate, as pharmacological work in healthy adults suggests (Marshall et al., 2016).

6. A Network-Level Perspective: Disrupted Functional Gradients After Adversity

Functional connectivity gradients characterize the brain's macroscale organization: continuous axes that reduce the connectome to a low-dimensional, functionally meaningful space (Huntenburg et al., 2018; Margulies et al., 2016). The "model-error" and "model-precision" labels used below are interpretive constructions of this framework, not established identities of the gradients.

• The "model-error" gradient (G1): a hierarchy from transmodal association cortex (e.g., the default mode network, DMN) to unimodal sensory and motor cortex. Its transmodal apex corresponds to regions initiating abstract top-down predictions, its unimodal end to regions processing bottom-up input and generating prediction errors (Huntenburg et al., 2018; Margulies et al., 2016).

• A "model-precision" axis (relating to G2/G3): distinguishes networks that represent the content of predictions and errors (DMN, sensory networks) from attentional networks, salience and frontoparietal control, hypothesized to modulate precision (Huntenburg et al., 2018).

We hypothesize that ELA perturbs the normative development of these gradients, because a brain adapting to chronic threat must prioritize reactive sensory monitoring over abstract, long-range prediction. ELA may compress the model-error gradient, operationalized as reduced G1 range and dispersion, reduced eccentricity of transmodal regions, reduced alignment with a normative template, and delayed developmental expansion, each computable and anchored in documented normative maturation of cortical gradients (Dong et al., 2021; Paquola et al., 2019). On the model-precision axis, ELA is predicted to shift the balance toward the salience network at the expense of the frontoparietal control network, a systems-level mechanism for hypervigilance, and to compromise coordination between isocortex and the subcortical nodes of Table 3.

Because the most costly elements of brain networks are the most vulnerable to abnormal development (Bullmore and Sporns, 2012), a tilt toward a chronically engaged salience system at the expense of long-range integration is also consistent with network economy. Direct evidence from prenatal-exposure cohorts so far concerns connectivity rather than gradients: in 28-year-old offspring from the Leuven cohort, higher maternal anxiety at 12 to 22 weeks of gestation was associated with weaker functional connectivity of the medial prefrontal cortex with the left inferior frontal gyrus, without differences in global network measures (Turk et al., 2023). Gradient analyses of such longitudinal data would test the present hypothesis directly.

7.1 Reframing Psychopathology through a PP Lens

The framework supports a transdiagnostic reframing stated in terms of precision first; the profiles below differ in which signals are over-or under-weighted, and the ELA profile we propose is the biased, threat-confirming variant.

• Depression: over-precise negative expectations about self, world, and future, such that positive prediction errors are assigned low precision and ignored (Badcock et al., 2017; Kube et al., 2020).

• Anxiety disorders: chronically high uncertainty with imprecise priors about safety, compensated by up-regulated precision on bottom-up prediction errors, yielding hypervigilance, arousal, and worry (Adams et al., 2013; Sterzer et al., 2018).

• Autism spectrum disorder (ASD): an informative contrast rather than an ELA outcome. Early accounts proposed a global, inflexible over-weighting of predictionerror precision (Lawson et al., 2014; Van de Cruys et al., 2014), later reframed around the balance between priors and sensory evidence (Pellicano and Burr, 2012; Van de Cruys et al., 2017). Recent computational work found no chronic distortion in how priors or sensory evidence were weighted, but a reduced ability to adjust learning to a volatile environment (Arthur et al., 2023). This differs from the ELA model, in which precision is selectively biased toward threat-relevant channels.

7.2 Testable Predictions

The framework yields falsifiable predictions designed to discriminate the precision-only account from the priors account (Table 1), to test the channel mapping (Table 2), and to locate the bias in the circuit (Table 3). They extend paradigms already used to test PP (Hodson et al., 2024; Walsh et al., 2020).

• Behavioral and computational: in probabilistic-learning tasks, individuals with ELA histories should show faster learning from negative and slower learning from positive feedback, with the asymmetry persisting in stable environments (precision account) rather than decaying from a shifted baseline (priors account). A priorsspecific signature would be valenced bias in baseline expectations before evidence arrives.

• Neuroimaging: ELA should predict greater ACC and insula responses to unexpected negative outcomes and smaller ventral striatal and ACC responses to unexpected positive outcomes. Because the key quantity is the precision-weighted prediction error, not raw activation, the sharpest tests use model-based fMRI to estimate precision and prediction-error signals directly; resting-state data should show the gradient alterations of Section 6.

• Electrophysiology: prediction-error indices (MMN, FRN) should be modulated by ELA history, with enhanced amplitudes to threatening deviants. The prenatalanxiety findings reviewed in Section 4.2 motivate a sharper, pre-registered prediction: reduced repetition suppression for predictable standards and enhanced responses to threat-related auditory input, tested against non-predictive (attention or arousal) alternatives (Song et al., 2025; Taylor et al., 2024).

• Individual differences: a measure of precision plasticity (Section 2.5) should moderate the association between early experience and later precision parameters in opposite directions for adverse and supportive environments, a crossover rather than a uniform vulnerability effect.

• Symmetry and supportive input: a "mirror signature" for supportive input would be unfalsifiable, because any inverse of any adversity effect would confirm it, so we specify an asymmetry. Supportive, structured early environments are predicted to confer faster precision re-tuning when volatility changes: after a contingency reversal, supportively reared individuals should re-normalize learning rates more quickly than both adversity-exposed and average-environment comparisons (Nelson and Gabard-Durnam, 2020). The one positive-valence datum in the prenatal literature points the same way: higher maternal mindfulness during pregnancy was associated with smaller infant N250 responses to repeated standards and larger P150 amplitudes, the opposite of the association with maternal anxiety on the same stimuli (van den Heuvel et al., 2015).

7.3 Scope and Boundary Conditions

A framework that could accommodate every consequence of ELA would not be falsifiable.

We therefore state what this one does not explain and what would count against it.

Primary and secondary domains. The core domain is threat vigilance and affective learning and decision-making under uncertainty, where precision-like parameters are estimable and the cited evidence is concentrated. Applications to mood, conduct, and risk-taking (Section 7.1) are secondary and depend on that core.

Processes outside the framework. (i) Effects of biologically proximal insults that act directly on neural tissue or on the plasticity machinery itself (malnutrition, teratogens, hypoxic or preterm brain injury) are predicted to produce general reductions in processing capacity rather than channel-specific biases; recent developmental reviews distinguish these biologically proximal exposures from psychological adversity (Chertoff et al., 2026).

(ii) Global intellectual impairment after severe deprivation is not a precision bias and is not explained here, although the framework predicts that deprivation-related differences in social and linguistic channels should be dissociable from it. (iii) Somatic outcomes of ELA, such as cardiometabolic disease, are outside the framework except where they feed back on neural computation (Section 7.5).

Observations that would count against the framework. The proposals below are hypotheses about mechanism, not claims of efficacy.

Exposure-based CBT. The inhibitory-learning account casts the violation of fearful expectancies as the active ingredient of exposure (Craske et al., 2014); in precision terms, its techniques make disconfirming evidence precise. Maximizing expectancy violation increases the prediction error to be weighted; removing safety behaviors removes the avoidance policies that prevent disconfirming evidence from being sampled (Section 2.4); and varying stimuli and contexts prevents the new learning from being assigned precision only in the treatment context. Cognitive techniques that target expectations address the content priors of Section 4.2 (Kube et al., 2020). The framework predicts that patients whose profile is dominated by evidence weighting (Table 1) should benefit most from exposure, whereas those with shifted baseline expectations should require additional work on content, and that successful treatment should normalize learning-rate asymmetries, a mechanistic outcome measure that can be added to symptom measures (Huys et al., 2016).

Pharmacological augmentation.

The framework links candidate agents to the precision mechanisms of Section 5.4. D-cycloserine, an NMDA-receptor partial agonist given to enhance consolidation of new learning, produces a small augmentation of exposurebased therapy in an individual-participant meta-analysis (Mataix-Cols et al., 2017). βadrenergic blockade with propranolol reduces learning rates and the influence of volatility on updating in healthy adults (Lawson et al., 2021), and given after reactivation of a fear memory it attenuated later fear responses in a proof-of-concept study (Kindt et al., 2009). Because noradrenergic, cholinergic, and dopaminergic agents leave distinct computational fingerprints (Marshall et al., 2016), pharmacological effects can be assessed with the same computational assays proposed for diagnosis, and the framework predicts that noradrenergic agents should act on precision-weighting signatures rather than on baseline expectations. No such trial has yet been conducted in ELA-exposed patients with computational endpoints.

Caregiving and dyadic interventions.

For young children, the most direct recalibration is a change in the caregiving signal itself. Attachment and Biobehavioral Catch-up, which trains caregivers to respond contingently and predictably, improved attachment organization among maltreated children in a randomized trial (Bernard et al., 2012), and Chertoff et al. (2026) identify parent-child synchrony and co-regulation as modifiable routes by which a supportive environment can reset regulatory systems during sensitive periods. The framework predicts that such interventions should act through the predictability channel of Table 2 and should be most effective while the relevant windows remain open.

Other approaches. Real-time fMRI neurofeedback targeting the amygdala has been piloted in combat-related PTSD (Zotev et al., 2018) and could be adapted to train precision-linked ACC or amygdala signals; no ELA-specific trial exists. Mindfulness-based training of precision on distressing interoceptive signals is a possibility we note but do not rest any claim on, because it has not been tested as a mechanism in ELA populations.

7.5 A Future Direction: Energetic and Metabolic Constraints on Precision

The framework is stated at the information level. An energy-level reading is worth developing separately, keeping two levels of description apart. The first is the energetic cost of neural computation: signaling and wiring are shaped by energetic economy (Attwell and Laughlin, 2001; Bullmore and Sporns, 2012), most of the brain's energy budget is committed to ongoing signaling (Jamadar et al., 2025), and chronic over-precision may be a costly operating point. The second is the physiological regulation of energy availability. The HPA axis is one regulator (Section 2.3); insulin is another, and it also acts as a neuromodulator in the central nervous system, influencing neuronal signaling, synaptic plasticity, and reward-related behavior (Kullmann et al., 2016). Insulin enhances striatal dopamine release through cholinergic interneurons (Stouffer et al., 2015), and brain insulin resistance alters dopamine turnover and produces anxiety-and depression-like behavior in mice (Kleinridders et al., 2015). Developmental adversity is linked to this system: prenatal psychosocial stress has been associated with insulin resistance in young adults (Entringer et al., 2008), and a polygenic score for fasting insulin moderates the association between early adversity and childhood impulsivity, with enrichment for dopamine D2 receptor signaling (Batra et al., 2021a). Early adversity also programs insulin signaling, a neuroendocrine pathway beyond the glucocorticoid system (Batra et al., 2021b), and an expression-based polygenic score for the mesocorticolimbic insulin-receptor gene network moderates the association between early adversity and metabolic syndrome in adult women (Jaramillo-Ospina et al., 2025), which points to brain insulin signaling as a source of individual differences in the long-term effects of adversity. Insulin signaling therefore offers an experimentally tractable link between early conditions, metabolic regulation, and the reward computations of Section 5.5. The first testable question is whether metabolic state (e.g., insulin sensitivity) moderates precision and reward-learning parameters in ELA-exposed individuals.

Any energetic extension must also be discriminated from accounts that already formalize cost-constrained inference: the Expected Value of Control theory, which places a costbenefit computation over control signals in the ACC (Shenhav et al., 2013); resourcerational analysis (Lieder and Griffiths, 2020); and an energy-information trade-off derived within predictive coding (Huang et al., 2024). Because no biologically serious account assumes unlimited energy, raw metabolic cost or allostatic load would not discriminate an energetic account from a precision-only one; the discriminating evidence would be a specific trade-off in which inferential accuracy is sacrificed to conserve energy under metabolic constraint. A network-level formalization of this direction has been proposed by the first author (Frasch, 2026); it is not developed here, and none of the present claims depends on it.

8. Conclusion

We have argued that early experience calibrates the developing brain's predictive architecture, and that the most parsimonious and testable locus of that calibration is precision weighting, the gain placed on different classes of prediction error. Calibration occurs within sensitive periods whose brakes, together with self-confirming sampling of evidence, can stabilize it beyond the conditions that produced it, and susceptibility to it varies between individuals. Under adversity the system over-weights potential threat and predictable input and under-weights disconfirming evidence; that these biases consolidate into pessimistic priors is a stronger, optional claim, and we have specified the signatures that would distinguish the two. The ACC is proposed as a hub allocating gain across channels within a distributed circuit whose other nodes make distinct, testable contributions. The framework states its boundaries, treats supportive input as calibrating the same machinery, and points to interventions aimed not merely at changing beliefs but at recalibrating the processes that weight the evidence for them.

Figure 1

Figure1places these dimensions on a single hypothesized continuum of environmental predictability. Its middle region, structured variability with learnable statistics, is the condition under which precision is expected to be calibrated most flexibly; chaotic input and rigidly invariant input form the two extremes. The figure is a conceptual hypothesis. The chaotic arm is supported by the unpredictability literature reviewed above(Davis and Glynn, 2024; Glynn and Baram, 2019); the rigid arm, in which over-predictable input yields over-precise priors and reduced exploration, is a theoretical extrapolation that has not been tested directly in human development.

Figure 1.

Figure 1. Early-life calibration of precision along a hypothesized continuum of environmental predictability.The horizontal axis orders early environments by the statistical structure of their signals, from chaotic (high entropy, no learnable regularity) through structured variability (fluctuation with rhythm and repair, learnable variance) to rigid invariance (low variance, over-predictable). The curve depicts the hypothesized flexibility of precision calibration, highest for structured variability. Prenatally, the relevant signals are maternal physiological signals that reach the fetus (e.g., cortisol and placental corticotropin-releasing hormone, heart rate and circadian rhythms, movement and voice); postnatally, they are features of caregiving (sensitivity, contingency, rhythm, and repair). Chaotic input is predicted to bias precision toward potential threat with persistently high learning rates, and, only as a possible downstream consequence under sustained adversity, rigid threat-related priors (Section 4.2). Rigidly invariant input is predicted to yield over-precise priors and reduced exploration. The figure is a conceptual hypothesis, not a summary of established findings; the rigid arm in particular is a theoretical extrapolation (Section 4.4).
