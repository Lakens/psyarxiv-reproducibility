To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 4 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Partial least squares correlation (PLSC) is a multivariate technique used in neuroscience to capture patterns of relationships between neural data and covariates of interest. Conventionally, PLSC captures between-subjects correlations (e.g., between average neural activation and average task performance). However, within-subject correlations (e.g., trial-by-trial relationships between neural activation and subjective ratings or stimulus attributes) are often of interest as well. Thus, we introduce an extension of PLSC to capture within-subject correlations. We validate this approach against related parametric methods and demonstrate that it detects known effects in simulated data and reproduces published findings in open fMRI and EEG datasets. This method is freely available as part of the pyplsc and mne-plsc Python packages.

1 Introduction

Partial least squares correlation (PLSC) is a multivariate analytic technique used in chemometrics (for a review, see Mehmood and Ahmed, 2016) and neuroscience (via the PLS software package; McIntosh et al., 1996; McIntosh and Lobaugh, 2004; Krishnan et al., 2011). It uses singular value decomposition (SVD) to explain relationships among a large number of observed variables using a small number of latent variables. In this way it is conceptually similar to principal component analysis (PCA): whereas PCA captures the total variance in a dataset, PLSC captures the covariance between a main set of multivariate observations and a smaller set of covariates.

Existing PLSC methods are applicable when the unit of observation is coarser than individual trials. That is, they capture covariance between participant-level multivariate neural data (averaged over trials) and participant-level covariates (e.g., questionnaire scores or average task performance). However, research questions can often pertain to within-subject correlations. For example, we may be interested in the trialby-trial relationship between neural data and subjective ratings or stimulus attributes. Importantly, trialby-trial relationships can disappear or even reverse when trial-level measurements are averaged, as they are in conventional PLSC. This phenomenon, known as "Simpson's paradox", has been demonstrated in for functional connectivity estimates, which often differ at the within-versus between-subjects level (Roberts et al., 2016). Thus, evaluating within-subjects correlations requires extending existing PLSC methods.

In this paper, we introduce and validate a novel extension of PLSC for within-subjects correlations (henceforth wsPLSC), which we have made freely available as part of the pyplsc Python package. This method is applicable to multivariate data in general (and pyplsc is modality-agnostic, operating directly on matrices and data frames), but in this paper we will focus on neuroscience using both the M/EEG-specific Python package mne-plsc and the more general pyplsc for fMRI. Before describing wsPLSC, we first review the mathematics of the conventional between-subjects PLSC method.

1.1 Between-subjects PLSC

Consider a set of N obs observations of N var neural data variables (e.g., voltage at a specific time and electrode, BOLD signal at a specific time and voxel) organized in a matrix X and a set of N obs observations of N cov covariates (e.g., behavioural measures, where typically N cov < N var ) organized in a matrix Y . X has dimensions (N obs , N var ) and Y has dimensions (N obs , N cov ). The cross-correlation R between X and Y can be computed by first normalizing each column (subtracting the mean and dividing by the L2 norm) and then taking the matrix product: R = Y T X (1) yielding a matrix with dimensions (N cov , N var ). R can then be submitted to compact SVD, which yields a triplet of matrices U , S, and V : R = U T SV where the columns of U and V are the left and right singular vectors, respectively (analogous to the eigenvectors of PCA), and S is a diagonal matrix containing the singular values (analogous to the eigenvalues of PCA).

Within neuroscience applications of PLSC, the left singular vectors in U are referred to as the "design saliences", and the right singular vectors in V as the "brain saliences" for reasons that will become clear shortly. SVD can be understood as identifying a set of latent variables (LVs) that explain the correlations between neural variables and covariates. The number of latent variables is equal to the rank of R, and is thus typically equal to N cov . Each latent variable is represented by a triplet of (1) a set of brain saliences, (2) a set of design saliences, and (3) a singular value. The brain and design saliences represent weights that can be used to compute linear combinations of neural variables and covariates, and the strength of the correlation between these linear combinations is captured by the singular value. As in PCA, singular values are ordered by magnitude such that the first latent variable captures the most cross-correlation and subsequent latent variables capture less and less.

In PLSC, statistical inference occurs via permutation testing, in which the rows of Y are repeatedly shuffled at random with respect to the rows of X and the SVD of their cross-correlation is re-computed. This breaks any systematic relationship between the neural variables and covariates and yields a null distribution of singular values for each latent variable. If the observed singular value for a given latent variable is above the 95th percentile of its null distribution, that latent variable is considered to explain statistically significant amount of the cross correlation between the neural variables and the covariates.

Having identified a singificant latent variable, we often want to characterize the associated brain saliences to determine which specific neural variables contribute strongly to a multivariate pattern related to one or more covariates. To do so, we can use bootstrap resampling to estimate the standard deviation of the brain saliences and compute z scores by dividing each observed brain salience by its standard deviation. Conventionally, brain saliences with |z| > 2 are considered stable and important contributors to a latent variable. It is also possible to simplify interpretation by performing cluster analysis on the brain saliences, such that only large groups of stable saliences that are adjacent in space, time, or frequency are interpreted (see, for example, Wiebels et al., 2016).

When observations are stratified by between-or within-subjects conditions, cross-correlation matrices are computed for each condition and "stacked" vertically to form a matrix R with N obs × N conds rows and N cov columns (where N conds is the number of experimental conditions). Note that the "direction" of stacking is a matter of convention and is mathematically arbitrary. By decomposing this stacked matrix, PLSC can capture both overall patterns of cross-correlation and their modulation by experimental condition.

1.2 Within-subjects PLSC

Conventional PLSC can be extended to within-subjects correlations by computing cross-correlation matrices over trials within participants (stratifying by within-subjects conditions as applicable), averaging these participant-specific matrices (stratifying by between-subjects conditions as applicable), and performing SVD on the resulting final matrix, which has N obs × N conds rows and N cov columns. Participant-specific correlation coefficients can be normalized via an arctanh transform prior to averaging (Fisher, 1915) and re-scaled afterward via a tanh transform.

Formally, the rows of X and Y correspond to individual trials in wsPLSC. Let X subj and Y subj be the trial-level neural data and covariates for a given participant. As in conventional PLSC, the statistical significance of latent variables can be assessed via permutation testing and the stability of brain saliences can be assessed by bootstrap resampling. Both permutation and bootstrapping must be stratified, accounting for the clustered nature of the data (i.e., participants are nested in between-subjects conditions; within-subjects conditions are nested in participants; trials are nested in within-subjects conditions). This is already the case in conventional PLSC, and is extended to the level of individual trials for wsPLSC. For example, individual trial-level covariates (i.e., rows of Y ) would be shuffled within participants or within-subject condition labels, as applicable.

The product of the brain saliences for a latent variable k (i.e., the kth column of V denoted by V * k ) with the neural data X is referred to as the "brain scores" B k for that latent variable: which summarizes how strongly the multivariate patterns captured by the brain saliences are expressed for each observation.

The correlations between these brain scores and the covariates are useful for characterizing the latent variable and are often visualized (with quantile-based confidence intervals from bootstrap resampling) in conventional PLSC analyses. In wsPLSC, we can similarly visualize the within-subjects correlations between the brain scores and the covariates.

In the remainder of this paper, we will attempt to validate and demonstrate wsPLSC. We will aim to show that wsPLSC (1) produces similar results to a conceptually similar parametric method for univariate data, (2) has appropriate type-I error rates, (3) detects simulated effects, and (4) reproduces established results for a public dataset.

2.2 Comparison with parametric methods

SVD can be applied to scalar values and column vectors, meaning that PLSC can be applied to unvariate data (with a single outcome variable and a single covariate). Conceptually, univariate wsPLSC aims to detect the same effects as a linear mixed effects model (LMM) that models a single continuous outcome variable, a single continuous predictor, possibly several categorical predictors, and a participant-level random intercept. Thus, if the stratified permutation approach used to assess significance in wsPLSC is valid, univariate wsPLSC should produce results similar to those of LMMs. To evaluate whether this is the case, we simulated a series of experiments for analysis via both methods.

We first performed a set of simulations in which observations were not stratified by any experimental condition. 2.3 Evaluating type-I error rates

In multivariate data, with many observed variables and relatively few observations, spurious cross-correlations will inevitably appear by chance even in the absence of true underlying relationships. Because SVD systematically identifies the pairs of observed variables and covariates that are maximally correlated with one another, a potential concern is that wsPLSC might identify spurious cross-correlations as significant and therefore suffer from an inflated type-I error rate. If wsPLSC is statistically valid (i.e., if it has an appropriate type-1 error rate), then under the null hypothesis of no systematic relationship between any covariate and any observed variable, the p value distribution should be uniform (i.e., p < 0.01 1% of the time, p < 0.05 5% of the time, etc.). Thus we again simulated a set of experiments, both with and without stratification by experimental condition.

We first simulated multivariate data for 1000 experiments in which 5 "participants" each completed 20 "trials". 10,000 observed variables and 12 covariates were simulated. As in the univariate simulations, each observed variable had a participant-level random intercept simulated as a standard normal random variate. Observations were not stratified by any experimental conditions. Thus, the cross-correlation matrix submitted to SVD had 12 rows and 10,000 columns, yielding 12 singular values and thus 12 p values per experiment. We collected these p values to determine whether, for each of the 12 pairs of latent variables, the p values followed a uniform distribution.

We next stimulated a second set of 1000 experiments with 5 participants per each of two betweensubjects conditions, who each completed 20 trials per each of two within-subjects conditions. For ease of visualization (to avoid producing a large number of p values per experiment), 10,000 observed variables but only 4 covariates were simulated. As in the univariate case, observed variables included participant-level random intercepts as well as fixed effects of within-and between-subjects conditions. Each experiment yielded 16 p values (4 covariates × 2 within-subjects conditions × 2 between-subjects conditions).

For both sets of experiments, we used Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests to determine whether the observed distribution of p values differed significantly from a uniform distribution. We applied FDR correction to the results of these distributional tests (i.e., to the p values from the Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests, not to the p values directly obtained from wsPLSC), given that there were 12 tests in the first set of simulations and 16 tests in the second set of simulations.

2.5 Analysis of real data

To demonstrate the utility of wsPLSC with real data, we analyzed an open fMRI dataset and an open EEG dataset. The goal of this analysis was to demonstrate that wsPLSC is able to reproduce established experimental effects. Although these results were possible to obtain in the original datasets via alternative methods, wsPLSC offers a conceptually elegant method that is already familiar to many neuroscience researchers (McIntosh and Lobaugh, 2004). Note, however, that our goal is not to recommend wsPLSC over other methods; instead, we merely aim to validate it in terms of its ability to match the results of other methods. For both our EEG and our fMRI analyses, we used datasets from experiments on physical pain. The advantage of this type of study for the current purpose is that pain is a unipolar continuous variable that can be correlated with neural responses. For the fMRI dataset, we used subjective pain ratings as a covariate for wsPLSC, whereas for the EEG dataset we used the energy level of the stimulus. Although PLSC analysis that includes covariates is sometimes referred to as "behaviour PLSC" (Krishnan et al., 2011), in principle any numerical measure can be used as a covariate, whether it is behavioural (e.g., subjective ratings) or not (e.g., stimulus energy). Use of this data was approved by the Baycrest Research Ethics Board (#26-24).

2.5.1 Real fMRI data

To demonstrate the utility of wsPLSC for fMRI data, we used OpenNeuro dataset ds000140 (study 2 from Wager et al., 2013). Briefly, 33 participants underwent thermal stimulation of their left forearms at varying temperatures over 7 fMRI runs for 12.5 seconds per trial. After each stimulation, participants rated whether or not the stimulus was painful. If they rated the stimulus as painful, they then rated their degree of pain using a 100-point visual analog scale.

Data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep 25.1.3 (Esteban et al. (2019); Esteban et al. (2018); RRID:SCR 016216), which is based on Nipype 1.10.0 (Gorgolewski et al. (2011); Gorgolewski et al. (2018); RRID:SCR 002502). See the appendix for the preprocessing details.

Data were modeled using a beta series approach (Rissman et al., 2004) as implemented in the nilearn Python library (Abraham et al., 2014a). That is, within each run, each trial was modeled using a canonical HRF convolved with the 12.5-second duration of the thermal stimulation. Importantly, pain ratings were not used to modulate the amplitudes of HRFs, since this modulation was meant to be later captured by wsPLSC. 6 motion parameters (rotation and translation across 3 axes), signal in CSF and WM, linear drift, and a constant term were also included as nuisance regressors. Single-trial beta images were extracted only from trials where the stimulus was rated as painful. wsPLSC was used to extract the spatial pattern from these single-trial beta images that was maximally correlated with pain ratings within subjects. Because this dataset included only a single covariate, the analysis yielded a single latent variable.

We computed z-scores for the brain saliences via bootstrap resampling to identify regions where the brain saliences for the latent variable were stable. We compared these results against those from a conventional GLM analysis as well as to regions associated with the term "pain" in the meta-analytic database NeuroSynth (Yarkoni et al., 2011). For the conventional GLM analysis, we used the same parameters as in the beta series analysis, but included pain ratings as a modulator. Data were concatenated across runs and any run where no trial was rated as painful was not included.

2.5.2 Real EEG data

To demonstrate the utility of wsPLSC for EEG data, we used OpenNeuro dataset ds005437 (Xiangyue et al., 2024) from Hu et al. (2014). Briefly, 29 participants underwent laser stimulation of nociceptors at a range of levels of energy. Such stimulation evokes an ERP component within 500ms whose amplitude increases with the energy of the stimulus. Each participant received 5 stimuli at each of a range of energy levels, beginning from 1 J and increasing in increments of 0.25 J up to 4.5 J (this upper limit varied per participant according to pain tolerance, with the lowest upper limit being 3.75 J).

EEG data were bandpass filtered between 1 and 100 Hz and epoched from 0.2 s prior to stimulus onset until 0.5 s after. Data were then downsampled from 1000 Hz to 200 Hz and cleaned via the "autoreject" algorithm pipeline recommended by Jas et al. (2017): autoreject was first used to reject noisy epochs and independent component analysis was then applied to un-rejected epochs using the extended infomax algorithm (Bell and Sejnowski, 1995). The minimum number of components explaining 95% of the variance were extracted and labeled using the "ICLabel" method (Pion-Tonachini et al., 2019) as implemented in the MNE-ICAlabel library (Li et al., 2022). Components labeled "eye blink" or "heart beat" were removed from all epochs (including initially rejected epochs). Finally, autoreject was applied to the ICA-cleaned data to interpolate noisy channels on an epoch-wise basis and to remove any remaining noisy epochs. wsPLSC was used to extract the spatiotemporal pattern maximally correlated with stimulus energy within subjects. Because the analysis included only a single covariate (stimulus energy), wsPLSC yielded a single latent variable.

Traditional ERP analysis involves analyzing amplitude at some channel or set of channels of interest, at some latency or within some temporal window of interest. In PLSC, we can perform a similar analysis via spatiotemporal clustering: after defining an adjacency matrix over channels and times, clusters of adjacent brain saliences with z-scores above some threshold (conventionally |z| > 2, but higher thresholds can be used to avoid overly broad clusters; for the current analysis, we used a threshold of |z| > 5). Our clustering approach treated ERP components as dipoles, i.e., patterns across multiple (possibly non-contiguous) channels that include both positive and negative changes in amplitude: we treated all channels as adjacent (thereby clustering only with respect to time) and considered positive and negative saliences to be part of the same cluster. After clustering, we identified cluster-specific peaks (i.e., specific channels and latencies where |z| values were maximal) and extracted data across trials and participants at these peaks for further analysis.

3.1 wsPLSC vs conventional PLSC

Both wsPLSC and conventional PLSC analyses yielded significant latent variables (p < 0.001 and p = 0.012, respectively). Moreover, in both analyses there was a significant correlation between the true weights w used to simulate the data and the brain saliences (R(98) = 0.97, p < 0.001 for wPLSC; R(98) = 0.85, p < 0.001 for conventional PLSC). However, the two analyses implied opposite correlations between the covariate and data scores (figure 3). Conventional PLSC yielded a straightforward positive correlation (left panel), whereas wsPLSC yielded a set of negative correlations within subjects that are not visible when the data are averaged within participants (right panel). It is important to note that neither level of analysis is necessarily more correct or accurate; rather, wsPLSC simply answers a different question (which correlations exist within subjects?) from conventional between-subjects PLSC (which correlations exist across subjects?). The results of these two types of analyses may be aligned, unrelated, or, as in this simulation, opposite to one another (i.e., an instance of Simpson's paradox).

4 Discussion

We have introduced a novel extension to PLSC to capture within-subject correlations, which we refer to as wsPLSC. We have demonstrated that wsPLSC yields comparable results to conceptually similar parametric methods for univariate data; exhibits an appropriate type-I error rate for multivariate data; and can detect both known effects in simulated data and published results in a public dataset. wsPLSC is available as part of the open-source pyplsc package and can be applied to M/EEG data via the mne-plsc package.

A major advantage of within-subjects analysis is its specificity. Multivariate methods exist for analyzing condition-wise differences (e.g., "mean-centred" PLSC or barycentric discriminant analysis; Krishnan et al., 2011; Abdi et al., 2018), and condition-wise differences are often interpreted in terms of the presence versus absence of some underlying variable that could also be treated as a covariate. For example, a contrast between painful vs neutral stimuli conditions can be interpreted as a signature of subjective pain (Wager et al., 2013). Thus, an analysis that is able to compare condition-wise differences against correlations with an underlying covariate can provide valuable insight into the nature of the construct being studied. For example, in a study of mental imagery, is the difference between an imagery versus a perception condition similar to the pattern associated with subjective vividness ratings? In a study of memory, is the pattern associated with successful recognition similar to the pattern associated with subjective familiarity? In a study of pain, is the difference between painful versus neutral stimuli similar to the pattern correlated with subjective pain ratings? By addressing questions such as these, wsPLSC can be a useful complement to existing methods.

It is important to note that we do not claim wsPLSC is necessarily superior to other analytic techniques for examining within-subjects correlations. Nonetheless, PLSC more broadly does offer advantages as an open-ended ombnibus technique that does not require the researcher to pre-specify the variables where an effect is likely to be found (i.e., ROIs or times/frequency bands of interest). It is thus particularly wellsuited to exploratory analyses that cannot be constrained by the existing literature, and wsPLSC inherits these advantages. We therefore believe that wsPLSC will be a useful extension to PLSC for researchers familiar with this analytic approach and its conventions, and for those investigating more exploratory research questions.

Appendix A: fMRI preprocessing 4.1 Anatomical data preprocessing T1-weighted (T1w) images (1 per participant) were corrected for intensity non-uniformity (INU) with N4BiasFieldCorrection (Tustison et al., 2010), distributed with ANTs 2.6.2 (Avants et al., 2008, RRID:SCR 004757), and used as T1w-reference throughout the workflow. Brain tissue segmentation of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), white-matter (WM) and gray-matter (GM) was performed on the brain-extracted T1w using fast (FSL (version unknown), RRID:SCR 002823, Zhang et al., 2001). Brain surfaces were reconstructed using recon-all (FreeSurfer 7.3.2, RRID:SCR 001847, Dale et al., 1999), and the brain mask estimated previously was refined with a custom variation of the method to reconcile ANTs-derived and FreeSurfer-derived segmentations of the cortical gray-matter of Mindboggle (RRID:SCR 002438, Klein et al., 2017). (2009), RRID:SCR 008796; TemplateFlow ID: MNI152NLin2009cAsym].

4.2 Functional data preprocessing

For each of the 7 BOLD runs per subject, the following preprocessing was performed. First, a reference volume was generated, using a custom methodology of fMRIPrep, for use in head motion correction. Headmotion parameters with respect to the BOLD reference (transformation matrices, and six corresponding rotation and translation parameters) are estimated before any spatiotemporal filtering using mcflirt (FSL, Jenkinson et al., 2002). The BOLD reference was then co-registered to the T1w reference using bbregister (FreeSurfer) which implements boundary-based registration (Greve and Fischl, 2009). Co-registration was configured with six degrees of freedom. Several confounding time-series were calculated based on the preprocessed BOLD: framewise displacement (FD), DVARS and three region-wise global signals. FD was computed using two formulations following Power (absolute sum of relative motions, Power et al. (2014)) and Jenkinson (relative root mean square displacement between affines, Jenkinson et al. (2002)). FD and DVARS are calculated for each functional run, both using their implementations in Nipype (following the definitions by Power et al., 2014). The three global signals are extracted within the CSF, the WM, and the whole-brain masks. Additionally, a set of physiological regressors were extracted to allow for component-based noise correction (CompCor, Behzadi et al., 2007). Principal components are estimated after high-pass filtering the preprocessed BOLD time-series (using a discrete cosine filter with 128s cut-off) for the two CompCor variants: temporal (tCompCor) and anatomical (aCompCor). tCompCor components are then calculated from the top 2% variable voxels within the brain mask. For aCompCor, three probabilistic masks (CSF, WM and combined CSF+WM) are generated in anatomical space. The implementation differs from that of Behzadi et al. in that instead of eroding the masks by 2 pixels on BOLD space, a mask of pixels that likely contain a volume fraction of GM is subtracted from the aCompCor masks. This mask is obtained by dilating a GM mask extracted from the FreeSurfer's aseg segmentation, and it ensures components are not extracted from voxels containing a minimal fraction of GM. Finally, these masks are resampled into BOLD space and binarized by thresholding at 0.99 (as in the original implementation). Components are also calculated separately within the WM and CSF masks. For each CompCor decomposition, the k components with the largest singular values are retained, such that the retained components' time series are sufficient to explain 50 percent of variance across the nuisance mask (CSF, WM, combined, or temporal). The remaining components are dropped from consideration. The head-motion estimates calculated in the correction step were also placed within the corresponding confounds file. The confound time series derived from head motion estimates and global signals were expanded with the inclusion of temporal derivatives and quadratic terms for each (Satterthwaite et al., 2013). Frames that exceeded a threshold of 0.5 mm FD or 1.5 standardized DVARS were annotated as motion outliers. Additional nuisance timeseries are calculated by means of principal components analysis of the signal found within a thin band (crown) of voxels around the edge of the brain, as proposed by (Patriat et al., 2017). All resamplings can be performed with a single interpolation step by composing all the pertinent transformations (i.e. head-motion transform matrices, susceptibility distortion correction when available, and co-registrations to anatomical and output spaces). Gridded (volumetric) resamplings were performed using nitransforms, configured with cubic B-spline interpolation.

Many internal operations of fMRIPrep use Nilearn 0.11.1 (Abraham et al., 2014b, RRID:SCR 001362), mostly within the functional processing workflow. For more details of the pipeline, see https://fmriprep.readthedocs.io/en/latest/workflows.html.
