What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

p = .930 Interestingly, they found that the regression coefficient for Investigative interests was not statistically significant (b = 0.01; p = .930) in a model without the EVT constructs, but it was statistically significantly negative (b = -0.24; p = .014) when they controlled for the EVT constructs.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Comparing Logit and Probit Coefficients From Same-Sample Nested Models: Adapting the (Revised) KHB Method to an SEM Framework

Many empirical studies in the social sciences use regression analyses to compare nested models with an increasing number of predictors. Typically, the focus is on investigating how regression coefficients change when additional predictors are added to a given model. For instance, a researcher might be interested in the impact of parental socioeconomic status on children's college graduation and how it changes if student achievement is added as a predictor (see the example in Breen et al., 2013). The interpretation of such differences in regression coefficients is straightforward for comparisons of samesample nested linear models with continuous outcome variables. However, the interpretation is generally more complicated for nonlinear models, such as logistic or probit regressions, for two reasons (Karlson et al., 2012). First, the variance of the assumed underlying latent variable for the categorical outcome is typically not constant across same-sample nested models with different predictors. Second, the shape of the residual distribution usually changes across models. Karlson et al. (2012) suggested a method, often referred to as the KHB method, in which both the variance of the underlying latent variable of the outcome and the shape of the residual distribution are held constant across nested models. The core idea is to hold the predicted part of the underlying latent variable (linear predictor) constant across the models that are being compared. In the revised KHB method, Breen et al. (2021) presented a modified approach that estimates the full model in a first step and generates the linear predictor, which is subsequently predicted by models with fewer predictors. However, in its common implementations (Kohler & Karlson, 2024; Kohler et al., 2011), the KHB method is limited to manifest predictor variables and data sets without missing observations.

It also cannot easily incorporate nested data structures or survey weights.

In the present paper, we introduce the single-step pseudo mediation model (PMM) and show how this model implements the multistep KHB method within a structural equation modeling (SEM) framework. We demonstrate how differences between and ratios of regression coefficients from a sequence of same-sample nested models can be estimated using coefficients from a single full model.

This specification solves typical challenges in the analysis of empirical data, such as (a) missing values, (b) measurement error in the predictors, and (c) complex data structures with clustering and survey weights. In current implementations of the (revised) KHB method, either all observations with any missing values on the variables of interest have to be excluded from the analysis (listwise deletion, see, e.g., Xie & He, 2025) or the data have to be (multiply) imputed before the analysis (see, e.g., Domènech-Abella et al., 2018). Applying the KHB method to a complex data set that includes plausible values and replicate weights (e.g., the PIAAC data used in Braun, 2018) can be challenging (Gurkan et al., 2021). Such issues can easily be handled with the PMM in contemporary SEM software such as Mplus (Muthén & Muthén, 2017). Moreover, the PMM can also easily be realized in Bayesian modeling frameworks, for instance, PROC MCMC (SAS Institute Inc., 2023) or the blavaan package (Merkle et al., 2021; Merkle & Rosseel, 2018). In addition to the SAS macro used in our simulations to estimate model parameters for the revised KHB method, we also provide Mplus code templates for models with up to 10 predictors as well as a SAS macro to generate the respective code for models with even more than 10 predictors (PsychArchives: https://doi.org/10.23668/psycharchives.22695). The Mplus code can also easily be adapted for the blavaan package.

Statistical Inference for Parameter Estimates Using the PMM

Currently, the standard estimation approach in an SEM framework is maximum likelihood (ML) estimation (Jobst et al., 2023). There are also extensions such as full information maximum likelihood (FIML) estimation, which allows for improved estimation under missing data scenarios, and robust full information maximum likelihood (RFIML) estimation, which additionally provides acceptable inferences in cases of nonnormally distributed continuous variables (Jia & Wu, 2023). In generalized SEM frameworks, link functions map ordered-categorical or binary outcomes onto normally distributed underlying latent variables, enabling ML estimation via numerical integration (Rockwood, 2021).

Inferential tests for individual parameter estimates are usually based on Wald statistics, which treat parameter estimates divided by their estimated standard errors as zvalues (Rosseel & Vidal, 2025): A ratio of an estimate to its standard error outside the range of -1.96 to 1.96 is judged as statistically significant at an alpha level of 5%. The differences between coefficients for a given predictor from different models in a PMM are equivalent to indirect effects (at least technically). However, as these effects are products of parameters, the delta method (Sobel, 1982), which relies on normality, provides only a rough approximation.

The (multivariate) delta method is also implemented in SEM software such as lavaan (Rosseel, 2012) or Mplus (Muthén & Muthén, 2017) for user-defined parameters such as indirect effects. However, simulation studies have shown that tests for indirect effects based on the delta method typically suffer from low power (Biesanz et al., 2010; Falk & Biesanz, 2015). Therefore, in line with Breen et al. (2021), we suggest relying on bootstrapping for statistical inference for differences and ratios of estimates in the PMM approach. The percentile bootstrap has been shown to provide acceptable Type I error rates and coverage rates for the 95% confidence limits, for example, for indirect effects in mediation models (Biesanz et al., 2010; Falk & Biesanz, 2015; Tibbe & Montoya, 2022) even in the case of missing or nonnormal data.

For more complex PMMs (e.g., multidimensional latent variable models), however, bootstrapping might not be feasible, as the numerical integration needed for (FI)ML estimation can be computationally cumbersome. In such cases, Sobel's (1982) suggestion to construct normal-distribution-based confidence intervals with the standard errors for indirect effects given in SEM software packages such as Mplus (MacKinnon et al., 2012) may be a reasonable-though potentially less powerful-choice. Furthermore, Bayesian estimation can be a viable alternative for complex models that require numerical integration with (FI)ML estimation (Rockwood, 2021).

Comparing the PMM With the KHB Method

If it includes only observed variables, a PMM is always a saturated structural equation model (i.e., it has zero degrees of freedom), as all associations among variables are specified by specific paths (regressions). Thus, the PMM is an easy one-step procedure by which to obtain all relevant regression coefficients for a series of target models that implement the KHB method from a single model. By defining the KHB coefficients as additional model parameters (under Model Constraint Mplus 8.11; Muthén & Muthén, 2017), the distribution of y * is necessarily constant when estimating these parameters.

The revised KHB method (Breen et al., 2021) introduced a different way of obtaining the unbiased coefficients for a model sequence predicting a binary outcome with increasing numbers of predictors: Here, the distribution of 𝑦𝑦 * is held constant across models by generating the linear predictor 𝑉𝑉 (see Equation 2) for the full model on the basis of the estimated regression coefficients β j from a nonlinear probability model (e.g., a logistic regression model). This generated linear predictor variable 𝑉𝑉 is then used as an outcome variable in a series of linear (OLS) regression models that estimate the regression coefficients with a stepwise inclusion of predictors. Suppose there is a full model with two predictors 𝑥𝑥 1 and 𝑥𝑥 2 . In a first step, the regression coefficients for both predictors are estimated, for instance, in a logistic regression model. Based on the resulting estimates of both regression coefficients, the linear predictor is generated as 𝑉𝑉 = β 1 𝑥𝑥 1 + β 2 𝑥𝑥 2 . The regression coefficient for a model including only 𝑥𝑥 1 is then estimated in a second step by regressing 𝑉𝑉 on 𝑥𝑥 1 in a linear regression. This approach is analogous to the classical mediation approach in linear models where the total effect for 𝑥𝑥 1 is estimated in a model including only 𝑥𝑥 1 as a predictor, and the indirect effect is estimated as the difference between this total effect and the effect of 𝑥𝑥 1 on the outcome in a second model that also includes 𝑥𝑥 2 (MacKinnon, 2008). Although conceptually related, the revised KHB approach is different from the single-step PMM approach, as the revised KHB approach still relies on a series of models to be estimated, whereas all coefficients for different target models are obtained from a single model in the PMM approach.

The PMM approach takes advantage of the flexibility of SEM implementations to easily and appropriately handle challenges commonly encountered in the analysis of realistically complex empirical data. Thus, it is more flexible than the (revised) KHB method.

First, missing data-a common problem in applied data analysis-can be handled by the implementation of FIML estimation in SEM if the missing-at-random (MAR; Rubin, 1976) assumption holds. Analogous Bayesian versions for handling missing data are available as well (Enders, 2025). Second, measurement error can be handled by defining latent variables and respective measurement models-with metric as well as categorical indicators-for the predictors in SEM. Third, complex sampling designs (including cluster sampling and survey weights to account for unequal probability sampling) can be taken into account in SEM software such as Mplus (Muthén & Muthén, 2017) or lavaan (Rosseel, 2012). Mplus (Muthén & Muthén, 2017) also implements bootstrapping procedures that allow users to estimate a nonsymmetric confidence interval (often called percentile bootstrap; Biesanz et al., 2010; Falk & Biesanz, 2015; Tibbe & Montoya, 2022) for coefficients and p values even for clustered data (cluster bootstrapping).

A Simulation Study: Research Questions

We demonstrate the equivalence of our PMM approach with the revised KHB method in a simulation study with three predictors that were either uncorrelated or correlated. With uncorrelated predictors, the regression coefficients for a given predictor were expected to be constant across models including only 𝑥𝑥 1 (Model 1), 𝑥𝑥 1 and 𝑥𝑥 2 (Model 2), or all three predictors (Model 3) when the revised KHB method or the PMM approach was applied.

However, when correlated predictors were specified, we expected changes in the regression coefficients for a given predictor across models. In general, we expected comparable coverage rates for the nonsymmetric bootstrap 95% confidence intervals of the regression coefficients as well as the respective parameter differences (Δs) and ratios (Ρs).

Method

We used the model shown in Figure 2 with three normally distributed predictors 𝑥𝑥 1 , 𝑥𝑥 2 , and 𝑥𝑥 3 as the population model in the simulation. The variance of 𝑥𝑥 1 as well as the residual variances of 𝑥𝑥 2 and 𝑥𝑥 3 were set to 1. First, we applied a population model with uncorrelated predictors (i.e., 𝑏𝑏 21 , 𝑏𝑏 31 , and 𝑏𝑏 32 were set to zero so that the expected Δ and Ρ population parameters were zero and one, respectively). The direct effects of the predictors were set to 𝑏𝑏41 = 0.5, 𝑏𝑏42 = 0.7, and 𝑏𝑏43 = 1 for 𝑥𝑥 1 , 𝑥𝑥 2 , and 𝑥𝑥 3 , respectively.

Second, we used a population model with correlated predictors to generate the data.

We chose regression coefficients (𝑏𝑏21 = 0.258, 𝑏𝑏31 = 0.211, and 𝑏𝑏32 = 0.204) that reflected intercorrelations of r = .25 among all predictors with variances σ 12 = 1, σ 22 = 1.067, and σ 12 = 1.111 for 𝑥𝑥 1 , 𝑥𝑥 2 , and 𝑥𝑥 3 , respectively. We used these variances to adjust the regression coefficients of the predictors of the outcome from the scenario with uncorrelated predictors.

Thus, after the predictors were z-standardized, identical coefficients would be expected. The adjusted coefficients were as follows: 𝑏𝑏41 = 0.5, 𝑏𝑏42 = 0.678, and 𝑏𝑏43 = 0.949.

For each of three different sample sizes (N = 200, 500, 1,000), we generated 1,000 replicates. We used logistic regression models with 1,000 bootstrap samples to analyze the data, and we constructed nonsymmetric 95% confidence intervals as described by Asparouhov and Muthén (2023) for each estimate of a parameter difference or ratio. The revised KHB method was implemented in SAS (SAS Institute Inc., 2023), and the PMM approach in Mplus 8.11 (Muthén & Muthén, 2017). The SAS macro called %revKHB is included in the online materials and can be used to estimate logistic or probit regression coefficients for different models with an increasing number of predictors and differences and ratios of regression coefficients from different models as well as their symmetric and nonsymmetric bootstrap confidence intervals and p values (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2023).

We also estimated a Bayesian version of the PMM. The respective model details and results are reported in the Appendix.

Two Empirical Examples

We ran the models with and without controlling for motivational constructs from expectancyvalue theory (EVT; Eccles et al., 1983). These empirical examples were chosen to demonstrate how model parameters are interpreted in "real-world" applications. Furthermore, we selected the first example (Karlson & Holm, 2011) to check the reproducibility of the published coefficients from the KHB method and to compare the standard errors from the "classical" KHB approach and those from the PMM based on bootstrapping. The second example referred to a study by Wille et al. (2020), who did not originally use a KHB approach. We reanalyzed their data using a PMM, which allowed us to perform a statistical test on a reported finding regarding the "Investigative" vocational orientation. The regression coefficient for "Investigative" was not statistically significant in a model without the EVT constructs, but it was statistically significantly negative after they were included. Additionally, we wanted to explore the impact of different model specifications (e.g., survey weights and latent variables) on the results.

Discussion

Comparing coefficients from same-sample nested models is not as straightforward in linear probability models (e.g., logistic or probit regression) as in linear regression because the regression coefficients in nested models might change even if a predictor is included in a model that is uncorrelated with all the other predictors. To handle this problem, Karlson et al. (2012) suggested a method in which the distribution of the underlying latent variable of the outcome is held constant across nested models (the KHB method). In the present paper, we introduced the PMM and embedded it in an SEM framework. Then we showed in our simulation study and in one of our empirical examples that the PMM produces results that are equivalent to the (revised) KHB method under the same assumptions (observed data from a simple random sample without missing values). The PMM can easily be implemented in Mplus via the code templates or our SAS macro, which generates the necessary Mplus code.

Compared with the (revised) KHB method, we demonstrated in our empirical examples, which were based on Wille et al. (2020), that the PMM has at least three advantages (if implemented in Mplus): First, missing values can be adequately handled under MAR assumptions with FIML instead of listwise deletion or multiple imputation. Second, complex sampling designs (e.g., cluster sampling, survey weights) can be taken into account, for instance, by applying a cluster bootstrap with or without survey weights. Third, latent variables can be included as predictors or covariates that-assuming an adequate specification of the measurement model-may be preferable to point estimates (e.g., factor scores) that contain measurement error and may lead to biased results.

For inferential statistics, the results from a bootstrapping procedure (e.g., the lower and upper limits of a CI) always depend on randomly drawn bootstrap samples. In simulation studies with a rather large number of Monte Carlo samples, even relatively small numbers of bootstrap samples lead to valid results with regard to the coverage of confidence intervals (Gaigall & Gerstenberg, 2026). In a specific statistical application, however, a larger number of bootstrap samples may be recommended for two reasons (Hesterberg, 2011): First, results are more likely to converge across different software implementations. Second, compared with the cost of the data in terms of money, time, and so forth, the cost of the analysis is typically of minor importance. Thus, the gain in the precision of a CI (e.g., due to a larger number of bootstrap samples) is less expensive than increasing the sample size. Hesterberg (2011), for instance, suggested 15,000 bootstrap samples for a bootstrap percentile interval "to have 95% probability that the actual one-sided noncoverage for a 95% bootstrap interval falls within 10% of the desired value, between 0.0225 and 0.0275" (p. 522).

Therefore, for applied researchers, approximately 15,000 bootstrap samples would be advisable for 95% CIs, provided that number of samples is computationally feasible in terms of memory capacity and computation time. Otherwise, it may be difficult for other researchers to closely replicate a given CI when using different software, as the lower and upper limits of the CI are less accurate approximations of the respective percentiles of the theoretical bootstrap distribution.

Limitations and Future Directions

The PMM can be applied to a large variety of different contexts, including scenarios with missing data, complex sampling, and latent variable models. With the bootstrap approach, reliable standard errors and accurate nonsymmetric CIs for the estimated coefficients can be expected in many different scenarios. Bootstrapping, however, might not be feasible for (very) complex (latent variable) models because of the high computational demand, and standard error estimation for (grand) indirect effects based on (robust) maximum likelihood estimation might be preferred in such cases. However, symmetric confidence intervals calculated with this method may fail to achieve the intended 1-α coverage probability. In simulation studies, the first-order or Sobel test (Sobel, 1982) for indirect effects, for instance, showed low power rates and low Type I error rates (MacKinnon et al., 2002). In such cases, Bayesian estimation of the PMM might be a viable alternative to maximum likelihood approaches.

Regarding ordered-categorical outcomes with more than two categories, even though we did not discuss such cases in the present study, the PMM can easily be applied (e.g., in Mplus where such outcomes can be handled as well). In such cases, the outcome is represented by a single latent response variable 𝑦𝑦 * , and all equations that include the regression coefficients for the predictors and covariates are valid. For the thresholds, however, the respective equations must be applied to each threshold in cases with more than two outcome categories. Furthermore, researchers can use our SAS macro %revKHB to estimate coefficients for ordered-categorical outcomes via the bootstrap-based revised KHB method.

Conclusion

In applied research, the specific research questions about categorical outcomes are often addressed by applying logistic or probit regression models. Compared with other approaches, the statistical hypotheses that can be addressed with the KHB method may often be seen as the most adequate for such research questions. We propose that researchers integrate the KHB method's strength in ensuring regression coefficient comparability across models with the capacity of SEM to handle missing data via FIML, accommodate survey weights, and incorporate latent variables. For our simulation, we also estimated a Bayesian version of the PMM that was based on the specification of a Bayesian mediation model proposed by Miočević and MacKinnon (2014) for SAS PROC MCMC (SAS Institute Inc., 2023). We applied moderately informative normal priors (µ = 0, σ2 = 9) for the threshold as well as the regression parameters (including intercepts).

For the residual variances, we specified gamma priors on the precision (i.e., the inverse of the residual variances) with 0.1 for shape and inverse scale, respectively. The equations for the additional model-specific parameters as well as the Δ and Ρ parameters were included in the model during the estimation. This allowed us to monitor convergence during the estimation process also with regard to these additional parameters of interest. We checked for convergence by running PROC MCMC within the automcmc macro (Wagner et al., 2023) with a single chain and the convergence criteria of an effective sample size (ESS) of at least 400 (Zitzmann & Hecht, 2019) and a potential scale reduction (PSR) smaller than 1.01 (Vehtari et al., 2021). We report parameter estimates (posterior mean and median), standard deviations and mean square errors of the estimates, 95% highest posterior density (HPD) intervals, as well as 95% credible intervals based on the 2.5 th and the 97.5 th percentiles of the posterior distribution and their coverage of the parameters in percent as well as the percentage of replications for which the intervals did not include 1 in the case of ratios, or zero otherwise (percentage of a given parameter estimate judged to be significantly different from zero). Bayesian estimation is also possible in Mplus (Muthén & Muthén, 2017) with the ESTIMATOR = BAYES option.
