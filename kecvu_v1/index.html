Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Good science has the power to move and change lives. At its best, motivation science not only deepens the understanding of human behavior but also contributes meaningfully to improving the lives of people and communities. Central to its excellence is the field's capacity to speak to a broad spectrum of contexts and human experiences. In this chapter, we reflected on the current state of motivation science by discussing its historical emphasis on universality and considering how greater attention to specificity can further advance the field. We then outlined three promising directions for future development and offer recommendations to support sustainable and systemic change. By moving toward a more inclusive motivation science, we hope to enhance both the field's knowledge and its community, strengthening a discipline that more accurately reflects and effectively serves the diverse populations it aims to understand.

Toward a More Inclusive Motivation Science

Motivation science, as a field of scientific study, serves two primary aims. First, it advances knowledge by enabling researchers to describe, explain, and predict motivational phenomena with greater precision and depth (Popper, 2005). Second, it empowers the application of this knowledge-whether through personalized guidance, targeted interventions, or policy recommendations-to foster positive change and improve lives (Fishbach, 2021; Science Granting Council Initiative in Sub-Saharan Africa, 2025). At the heart of both aims lies a curiosity about and commitment to understanding people for people, including individuals across diverse identities, lived experiences, and social and cultural contexts.

Human lives and conditions are inseparable from motivational experiences. A person's identity and the circumstances they navigate affect not only their motivation-why they do what they do-but also the effectiveness of efforts to support and influence it. Although important commonalities in human motivation exist, motivation also varies richly in how it is experienced, expressed, and enacted across groups and contexts (Markus, 2016). Recognizing this variation is not just meaningful; it is essential because the relevance and success of our field depend on researchers' ability to engage with this diversity and to develop insights and solutions that are valid and useful across populations and contexts (IJzerman et al., 2020). To fully realize the goals of understanding motivation, motivation scientists must take seriously the people the field seeks to understand and thoughtfully consider who benefits from our work and how. Doing so requires sustained attention to human variability, particularly the experiences of nondominant groups and individuals (e.g., those marginalized by gender, sexuality, language, class, neurodiversity, disability) whose perspectives and needs have too often been underrepresented, unrecognized, or underserved (Silan et al., 2023). This chapter is not the first to argue that attending to specificities and non-dominant experiences is essential for advancing scientific knowledge and improving society. Such calls emerged especially over the past two decades, with scholars highlighting the value of representation and contextualization for producing a more accurate and comprehensive understanding of human behavior. These conversations have emerged in general psychology (Atari et al., 2025; Rad et al., 2018) as well as in subfields such as social, personality, cognitive, developmental, and health psychology (Atherton et al., 2026; DeJesus et al., 2024; Petrutiu et al., 2026; Remedios & Snyder, 2015; Thomas et al., 2023). Although meaningful progress has been made, substantial room for growth remains. A recent analysis of journals published by the British Psychological Society, for instance, found that participants from Latin America, Eastern Europe, the Middle East, and Africa represented only 8.7% of all samples collected between 2021 and 2023 (Petrutiu et al., 2026).

Compared with these broader conversations in psychology, discussions of representation and inclusivity remain limited in motivation science. And yet, questions of specificity are arguably especially consequential for motivation science because motivation processes serve as foundational principles for a wide range of disciplines, including cognitive science, economics, management, neuroscience, and beyond (Motivation Science, 2026). Improving motivation science, therefore, could have far-reaching implications across numerous areas of scholarship. Moreover, motivation theories directly inform decisions and intervention design in high-stakes contexts. Educators' instructional practices, organizations' performance and reward systems, and behavioral change interventions aimed at promoting health and well-being are all, in some ways, grounded in assumptions about how motivation works (e.g., Albarracín et al., 2006; Bryan et al., 2019; Farrar & Papies, 2024; Legate & Weinstein, 2025). Because these decisions can have profound consequences for human lives, motivational theories and interventions must be reliable, valid, and applicable across the diverse populations and contexts they serve.

Against this backdrop, this chapter reflects on the current state of motivation science by examining its historical emphasis on universality and the ongoing challenges that continue to limit its ability to capture the full complexity of human motivation. We then discuss three promising directions for advancing the field: 1) leveraging the specificities of human lives and contexts, 2) intentionally studying and understanding non-dominant populations and issues, and 3) expanding opportunities for current and future scholars in the motivation science community. Finally, we offer concrete recommendations for individuals and institutions with influence in the academic system to support sustainable and systemic change. By identifying both challenges and opportunities, we hope this chapter contributes to developing a more inclusive and impactful motivation science.

Historical Emphasis on Psychological Universals

The roots of motivation science trace back to the early development of psychology, when theorists sought to explain basic cognitive and motivational processes. William James, for example, argued that motivated behaviors arise from instinctive impulses of human nature that function beyond reflective thoughts (James, 1890). Later, Kurt Lewin conceptualized motivation as a force or state of tension generated by an imbalance between a person's need and their current state (Lewin, 1943 (Lewin, , 1951)). This early theorizing, together with subsequent refinements and extensions, laid the foundations for many contemporary principles of motivation (e.g., reinforcement sensitivity theory, expectancy theory, goal systems theory). From its inception, motivation science has been fundamentally theory-driven, emphasizing the development of explanatory models that can be empirically tested through systematic measurement or experimental manipulations. This approach has encouraged researchers to focus on common patterns in motivational processes and establish causal explanations for general human experience (see also Tafreshi et al., 2016; Thomas et al., 2023).

Motivation science emerged alongside the study of basic cognition and physiobiological processes (Lord et al., 2010; Shah et al., 2002) and, like many areas in psychology, has traditionally endorsed a (post-)positivist approach (Syed & McLean, 2022). This perspective emphasizes discovering objective, replicable principles that can be reproduced (exactly) across people and contexts. Combined with the scientific preference for parsimonious explanations, this orientation has encouraged theoretical assumptions that prioritize broad generalization over contextual specificity. Consequently, many influential theories of motivation have sought to identify the fundamental mechanisms underlying human behavior (e.g., Maslow, 1943; McClelland, 1987), often implying that these processes are, or at least assumed to be, universal across populations and settings. This emphasis on universality has generated powerful theoretical insights, but it has also shaped the way the field asks research questions.

Clearly, studying psychological universals, or basic mental processes shared across humans, is instrumental in advancing the scientific objectives of understanding and improving human functioning.

We do not question the value of pursuing universality in motivation science. On the contrary, we argue that advancing knowledge of universals also requires careful attention to variation in motivational phenomena and vigilance against allowing presumed universality to constrain scientific progress. Indeed, findings once assumed to reflect universal psychological processes have often proven to be more contingent on context than previously recognized. The emergence of cultural psychology, for example, has challenged the default assumptions of universality by demonstrating meaningful cultural variation in even basic cognitive processes such as cooperation and visual perception (Henrich et al., 2005; Norenzayan & Heine, 2005), suggesting that motivational processes, too, are shaped by human variability and contexts.

Specificities Are Not the Enemy of Universal Motivation Science

Understanding potentially universal phenomena requires investigating both commonalities and differences in human motivation. Claims of universality can only be evaluated by examining the extent to which motivational processes generalize across populations and situations. Crucially, universality is not a binary attribute but a continuum (see Norenzayan & Heine, 2005). Psychological phenomena can be considered universal at different levels. At the most basic level, a phenomenon may be universal if it is conceptually available across focal groups or contexts; if not, it is nonuniversal. A stronger form of universality is shown when a phenomenon operates similarly across groups, exhibiting similar underlying processes and functions. The strongest form of universality requires a phenomenon to be equally accessible, come to mind with similar ease, and carry comparable relevance. These are also called existential, functional, and accessibility universals, respectively. Taken together, demonstrating that a motivational process exists across groups or contexts often represents only the first step. Researchers must also examine whether that process functions similarly and holds comparable significance across diverse populations and contexts.

While some motivation principles may prove to be highly universal, researchers should not assume universality by default. Rather, because variability is a fundamental feature of human experience, universality should be treated as a strong claim that requires commensurately supporting evidence.

Embracing specificity in the study of motivation, therefore, does not weaken the search for universal principles. In fact, it is essential for establishing the scope, strength, and boundary conditions of universal claims. This perspective also creates opportunities for a richer and more nuanced understanding of motivation, ultimately advancing the field by opening new avenues for scientific discovery (Cheon et al., 2020). With this goal in mind, we next highlight several challenges that may hinder progress in this direction before outlining key priorities and recommendations to advance a more inclusive science of motivation.

One well-documented barrier is sampling bias: reliance on non-diverse, majority-group samples.

Much like many areas of psychology, this tendency can emerge from reliance on convenience samples, such as university students through subject pools or participants on online survey platforms (e.g., Prolific and CloudResearch). Although these sampling methods are usually efficient and cost-effective, one major downside is that they likely overrepresent certain populations, such as U.K. and U.S. White, Englishspeaking, and educated participants without disabilities (e.g., Pew Research Center, 2016). This sampling practice inherently excludes meaningful variability and limits the field's ability to speak to diverse lived experiences, including those of stigmatized individuals (Remedios & Snyder, 2015) and people living in non-dominant global regions (e.g., Global South; Krys et al., 2025). Scholars have characterized this longstanding limitation as a reliance on WEIRD populations-referring to samples drawn disproportionately from Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic regions (Heine et al., 2010), and more recently, critics have revised this characterization to the dependence on samples that are White, English-speaking, normatively Invisible, Racially color-evasive, and socially Dominant class (Thomas et al., 2023).

To be clear, the crux of the issue is not about the use of narrowly focused samples in and of itself.

As will be elaborated below, target sampling can be valuable for addressing specific research goals (e.g., recruiting individuals from the dominant group to understand anti-minority prejudice). The problem exacerbated by normative sampling bias is the tendency to treat dominant-group samples as neutral, prototypical, or broadly generalizable, even when supporting evidence is lacking or nonexistent (Cheon et al., 2020; Roberts & Mortenson, 2023; Yarkoni, 2022). In a U.S.-centric field, this bias results in unequal treatment across dominant and non-dominant group samples. In a study of social psychologists, over 50% from the Global South and Southern and Eastern Europe reported differential standards of editors and reviewers-for instance, student samples in the US but not outside of the US were considered acceptable for publishing (Bou Zeineddine et al., 2022). This bias can also manifest in responses to research taking an indigenous approach or focused on minority populations. Researchers are sometimes asked to recruit additional (comparative) samples from dominant groups, even when there is little theoretical justification, merely to show that the experiences of non-dominant groups differ from those of dominant groups (Roberts & Mortenson, 2023; Stanley, 2007). In doing so, dominant-group experience is treated as the norm, while non-dominant-group experiences are positioned as deviations that require additional evidence or explanation to be validated.

In addition, motivation research typically frames its focus around the self and treats individuals as context-free entities, risking decontextualized knowledge that lacks meaningful specificity. This emphasis on the self likely stems from the discipline's psychological origins and its focus on internal drives, personal agency, and individual traits (Eccles & Wigfield, 2002). With few exceptions, foundational motivation theories-such as self-determination theory, control theory, achievement motivation theory, goal setting theory, and expectancy-value theory-and their research primarily conceptualize motivation as a psychological phenomenon that is emerging from and regulated within or largely by the will of the person (see Urhahne & Wijnia, 2023). As a result, most empirical motivation research has focused on how individual differences affect goals and motivation, and how differences in goals and motivation affect individuals (Austin & Vancouver, 1996). Although this approach is useful, overreliance on it can lead researchers to miss how contexts and systems shape a person's motivation, and vice versa (Destin, 2020; Fitzsimons et al., 2015; Silverman et al., 2023). This individual focus helps explain why many studies to date have operated under presumed neutral contexts and have not yet systematically examined whether motivational constructs are indeed universal. This issue of presumed contextual neutrality can also manifest in study design and measurement when scientists assume standardized methods elicit uniform experiences across participants. For instance, in survey studies, researchers often assume that scale items operate equivalently for all participants, overlooking potential variations in interpretation that may arise from participants' social, educational, or linguistic backgrounds (Bauer, 2017). Survey items may take on different meanings when administered to non-native speakers or when translated into local languages without a rigorous, culturally sensitive translation procedure. In experimental settings, researchers may likewise overlook how contextual factors such as sociocultural background, researcher-participant dynamics, and power relations can differentially color participant interpretation and engagement in the study (e.g., Lin et al., 2021). What appears "neutral" in study design may, in practice, reflect dominant cultural norms, leading non-dominant populations to interpret or respond differently. These differences can introduce systematic bias and potentially lead to flawed conclusions.

Relatedly, normative analytical approaches that assume universality further entrench this issue by masking meaningful group differences. The default practice of aggregating individual data to focus on general patterns and treating majority-group effects as the reference standard risks overlooking unique experiences and dismissing deviations as statistical "noise" or error. A common manifestation of this bias is using demographic variables solely as controls. Although controlling for demographics can be important when grounded in sound theoretical rationale, it may also suppress opportunities to examine meaningful variation across groups that reveal meaningful insights. Further, when group membership moderates outcomes, controlling for these moderators can lead to biased or misleading inferences (see Rohrer, 2018).

The challenges the field faces are multifaceted; however, identifying and addressing them also sheds light on exciting avenues for progress in motivation science.

Opportunities to Broaden and Advance Motivation Science 1. Beyond the Individual Level: Considering Specificities of Groups and Contexts

One clear opportunity for advancing motivation research is embracing the specificities of groups and contexts. To do so, researchers can improve measurement and experimental design practices to account for potential influences of participant backgrounds (e.g., conducting equivalence tests of survey items, consulting subject matter experts and participants during piloting). More broadly, researchers can move beyond conceptualizing motivation as a primarily intrapersonal process-such as the internal regulation of goals, behaviors, and outcomes-and diversify their samples, while more carefully considering how group-and context-specific factors relate to research questions and analyses. These factors may include situational, social, cultural, and historical influences that shape and modulate motivation.

Studying motivation beyond the individual level is not new (Heine, 2007; Hofmann, 2024). What we advocate here is a renewed and deeper commitment to situating research in lived experiences and context, which can help generate new theories and refine existing ones. This approach is relevant even for motivational constructs traditionally centered on the self. To illustrate this, we draw primarily on examples from the literature on self-determination theory-particularly the basic psychological needs of autonomy, competence, and relatedness (Ryan & Deci, 2000)-and on self-control, defined as the process of prioritizing distal over proximal motivations when they conflict (Fujita, 2011). In brief, we highlight theoretical potential and the breadth of research directions that can emerge when specificities and contexts are explicitly considered.

Gender provides a useful illustration of why attention to group and context specificities matters for understanding motivation. A substantial body of research shows that men and women are socialized with different expectations and social roles (Eagly et al., 2000), which can meaningfully shape their motivational experiences (Diekman & Eagly, 2008). For example, women are often expected to adopt a nurturing, caregiving role; correspondingly, they are more likely than men to endorse communal goals and values (e.g., Diekman et al., 2010). Even when pursuing the same goal or attempting to satisfy the same psychological needs, men and women may rely on different motivational pathways and strategies, and it is critical to understand how context shapes gender differences in motivation and achievement (Ryan & Morgenroth, 2024). For instance, Skewes et al. (2018) found that in the context of university faculty promotion and tenure, women tended to fulfill competence needs through relational interactions and affirmations, a pattern the authors called relational competence. Men relied more on informational sources, such as understanding formal tenure guidelines and expectations. In a German sample, autonomy-supportive environments were found to foster greater self-determination in boys than in girls (Großmann et al., 2023), suggesting that the same motivational context may not have identical effects across gender groups.

Women and men can also experience different developmental and social contexts that shape selfregulatory outcomes differently. Although self-control is often conceptualized as an individual-level process, interpersonal and developmental contexts also shape it (e.g., Fitzsimons & Finkel, 2011). Women tend to score higher on self-control than men, a difference associated with outcomes such as stronger academic performance (e.g., Duckworth & Seligman, 2006; Hamama & Hamama-Raz, 2021; Wu et al., 2017), lower alcohol use, and reduced rates of certain forms of delinquency and recidivism (Benda, 2005). At the same time, broader social structures can shape how motivation is evaluated and rewarded.

At work, men reported benefiting more than women from risk-taking behaviors, even though there was no gender difference in the propensity to take the same risk (Morgenroth et al., 2022). In the context of fundraising, women entrepreneurs seeking funding in the United States were disproportionately asked prevention-focused questions about avoiding losses, whereas men were more often asked promotionfocused questions concerning aspirations and potential gains. These differences ultimately contributed to gender disparities in venture capital allocation (Kanze et al., 2018), suggesting that the contexts individuals are in, shaped by sociocultural expectations, can systematically affect their motivational experiences and outcomes.

Another growing body of work highlights the importance of ethnocultural contexts, such as nationality and racial-ethnic identity. Cross-national studies have shown that while the factor structure of psychological needs (competence, relatedness, and autonomy) is largely consistent across cultures, the degree to which these needs are endorsed, practiced, and emphasized varies. For example, countries in the Anglosphere-such as Australia, Canada, and New Zealand-tend to emphasize individual competence more than East Asian countries such as South Korea and Japan, where collectivistic values are emphasized (Nalipay et al., 2020). In a study comparing U.S. and Bulgarian workers, Deci et al. (2001) found that Bulgarian participants reported higher satisfaction of autonomy and greater autonomy support from supervisors-an unexpected finding that invites further exploration of how collectivistic and posttotalitarian contexts shape motivational expectations and experiences.

Motivation also becomes theoretically even richer when examined through the lens of intergroup contexts and power dynamics. For instance, to study the motivation behind learning a new language in a foreign environment requires understanding not only students' motivation to learn but also the social relationships and power structures in the classroom that shape their educational experiences. Autonomysupportive classrooms, for example, have been shown to reduce language anxiety and enhance classroom participation among Asian international students in the U.S. (Cho et al., 2023). An autonomy-supportive environment goes beyond providing information or opportunities for skill development. In educational settings, where teachers often occupy positions of authority, autonomy-supportive practices communicate respect and acknowledge students' perspectives and sense of individuality. Such practices could encourage students to express their interest and develop greater intrinsic motivation, ultimately facilitating learning and performance.

Similarly, for self-control studies, researchers have an opportunity to unpack how the development, expression, and measurement of self-control may be racialized. In the U.S., a scale validation study found that Nonwhite and White individuals responded differently to self-control items, with Nonwhite participants scoring higher overall, except for items related to "temper" (Ward et al., 2018). Findings like this raise important questions about whether commonly used measures capture selfcontrol equivalently across groups and whether observed differences reflect the construct itself or the social and cultural contexts in which it develops (i.e., level of universality). Ignoring these contextual influences is not merely a conceptual oversight; it can have important real-world consequences. Although racial minority groups, particularly Black Americans, may score similarly to or higher than White individuals on measures of self-control, they are often stereotyped as possessing poorer emotional control and self-regulation (Motro et al., 2022). These racialized perceptions can foster dehumanization and justify unequal treatment across social institutions (Bai & Zhao, 2024). For example, false beliefs about Black individuals' pain experiences have been shown to contribute to disparities in pain assessment and treatment within healthcare settings (Hoffman et al., 2016). Further, the benefits and drawbacks of selfcontrol may also vary across racial identities and socialization experiences. For example, low self-control was more strongly associated with negative mental health outcomes among Black students than among Asian American students (Kim et al., 2016). At the same time, greater emotional self-control among Asian American students was associated with less favorable attitudes toward help-seeking (Kim et al., 2016), a potential backfiring effect. Together, these intergroup examples illustrate how motivational processes are shaped not only by individuals' internal states but also by the power dynamics within which motivation unfolds.

Therefore, future research on motivation would benefit from a more comprehensive examination of how motivation operates within systemic constraints and affordances. Recent reviews of the selfcontrol literature, for example, have called for greater attention to situated self-control, highlighting how environmental structures and situational demands afford different expressions and outcomes of selfregulation (Hofmann, 2024; Werner & Berkman, 2024). Educational research echoes a similar point: motivational outcomes are not solely the result of individual effort but are deeply shaped by the systems in which individuals operate (Destin, 2020; Silverman et al., 2023). Whether self-control can be effectively exercised often depends on the opportunities, resources, and constraints afforded by one's environment.

Consider food-related self-regulation. In communities where healthy options are scarce, unattractive, or unaffordable-due to factors such as economic inequalities, geographical limitations, and targeted marketing practices (Balcetis et al., 2026; Vilar-Compte et al., 2021)-framing unhealthy eating primarily as a failure of individual self-control risks overlooking the root causes of the problem.

Understanding self-control, therefore, requires more than examining what individuals choose to resist or pursue and personal strategies. It also requires examining the options available to them, the incentives embedded in their environment, and how social systems reward or constrain self-control behaviors. This shift, from focusing on the individual level to acknowledging structural influences such as cultural expectations and systemic pressures, shows promise for designing more effective motivational interventions across diverse populations and contexts.

The purpose of these examples of quantitative and qualitative differences is to encourage motivation scientists to consider the specificities of groups and contexts more intentionally, thereby refining theories and opening important research directions. Again, specificity is not at odds with universality, and nuance can be an ally rather than an enemy. With data that acknowledge heterogeneity in motivational processes, the science will be better equipped to offer more comprehensive and actionable knowledge that is relevant and useful for serving diverse communities.

2. Theory Building Through Studying Non-Dominant Populations and Issues

Second, motivation science has an opportunity to generate new insights and build novel theories by directly addressing the experiences of minorities, or members of a non-dominant group as a central focus of study (Krys et al., 2025; Roberts et al., 2020; Uskul et al., 2024). Although non-dominant group members are often numeric minorities, individuals are often minoritized when they lack certain power, privilege, and/or status compared to the dominant group. Minoritization is context-specific; individuals can be disadvantaged in some situations or dimensions but not others. For instance, the idea of Majority World refers to the majority populations of Global South countries, and yet these populations remain substantially underrepresented in psychological research (Uskul et al., 2024).

To some, the idea of focusing "more narrowly" on non-dominant group or minority experiences as a way to broaden the understanding of motivation may seem counterintuitive. One reason for this perception is the assumption that studying a more specialized population runs counter to a core goal of science: producing universal knowledge and findings generalizable across a wide range of populations.

However, generalizability is not a static achievement-it is an ongoing process that requires continuous verification and accumulating evidence (Van Lange, 2013). Studying non-dominant populations in motivation science is not a detour from this goal but a necessary step toward it (e.g., understanding majority populations worldwide). As discussed earlier, research on distinct populations also helps test the boundaries of existing theories. Scientifically, studying subpopulations matters because falsifying a hypothesis or identifying a boundary condition requires as little as one reliable counterexample (Popper, 2005). Thus, focusing on minority experiences does not undermine generalizability; rather, it strengthens the scientific process by advancing the understanding of when, where, and for whom motivational principles apply, reaching and improving more lives.

Importantly, history shows that careful examination of non-dominant populations can spark innovations that benefit everyone. In medical science, for example, studying underrepresented groups has been essential for identifying heterogeneity in treatment effects and for making discoveries that improve outcomes across populations. The discovery of proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9), which led to major advances in treating atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, was made possible by identifying a rare mutation in Black individuals in the U.S.-a mutation not commonly found in White samples (Gross et al., 2022). Another example is the "Hispanic paradox"-the observation that some Hispanic populations in the southwestern U.S. exhibit relatively favorable health outcomes despite socioeconomic disadvantages (Markides & Coreil, 1986). Research to understand the "Hispanic paradox" led to breakthroughs in understanding the social determinants of health, including the protective roles of family support and cultural practices (CDC, 2024).

Motivation science can benefit in similar ways. Scholars have long studied high-performing individuals-such as elite athletes, astronauts, and spelling bee champions-to uncover insights about self-discipline, motivational profiles, and techniques like deliberate practice and self-regulation (e.g., Brcic, 2010; Duckworth et al., 2011; Jordalen et al., 2020). These findings have informed broader motivational theories and benefited people beyond those specific domains. Can researchers apply the same logic to other subpopulations? Emerging evidence suggests this strategy is promising.

Take Japanese culture as an example. The concept of ikigai (a multidimensional sense of purpose or "reason for living") has been shown to predict higher well-being and lower depression, suggesting its potential relevance across cultures (Wilkes et al., 2023). Japan also exemplifies a "tight culture," characterized by strong social norms and low tolerance for deviance, which tends to enhance selfregulation but constrain individual creativity (Gelfand et al., 2011). Yet Japan remains one of the most innovative nations. This paradox has inspired researchers to explore how different motivational processes may support both individual and collective innovation. Specifically, while people in tighter cultures performed less well in culturally foreign creativity tasks, they were more engaged and likely to succeed when the tasks were culturally familiar (Chua et al., 2015).

More broadly, studying non-dominant populations offers meaningful pathways to discovery. First, it can broaden our knowledge by illuminating previously unexplored possibilities. For example, studying individuals facing severe adversities, such as poverty or low socioeconomic status (SES), enables a more comprehensive understanding of key motivation processes. For example, research suggests that low-SES individuals' adverse experiences could shape a different set of motivational strategies, given limited resources to sustain health and resilience (Chen & Miller, 2012). Their self-control improved academic grades and psychosocial outcomes, but it also impaired physical health, as evidenced by accelerated cellular aging (Miller et al., 2015).

Second, understanding less privileged, minority populations enhances scientists' ability to identify and respond to a wider range of human needs with greater precision and sensitivity. For example, individuals managing disabilities, such as learning disorders and mild intellectual disabilities, often reported experiencing lower levels of self-determination (Vouyoukas & Tsikouras, 2021). Importantly, these challenges could be mitigated through individualized, need-specific accommodations as well as social support, an intervention that may ultimately benefit everyone, not only those with disabilities (Garrels & Sigstad, 2019). Research on attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) offers another illustration. Students with ADHD often report lower levels of intrinsic and extrinsic motivation (and higher levels of amotivation) when managing slow and monotonous learning tasks (Smith et al., 2020).

For these reasons, traditional lectures and homework assignments may seem less interesting and meaningful, contributing to engagement difficulties and lower academic performance. However, studies examining the first-person experiences of individuals with ADHD have suggested that these patterns do not always simply reflect lower motivation. Rather, they may point to qualitative differences in motivational pathways. In addition to commonly studied motivators such as competence and autonomy, children and adolescents with ADHD report being particularly motivated by immediacy and the passage of time, while finding familiar and predictable activities less rewarding than their peers without ADHD would (Morsink et al., 2017). Had researchers focused solely on identifying universal motivators without examining how motivational processes operate across groups and contexts, it would be easy to conclude that individuals with ADHD are simply less motivated. Instead, attention to specificity reveals a more nuanced reality: motivational processes may differ not only in magnitude but also in how they operate across individuals and contexts. Such insights can refine motivation theories and inform more effective and equitable solutions responsive to diverse needs (e.g., gamification; Morsink et al., 2017).

Further, studying intersectional experiences-beyond single identities in isolation-helps scientists identify how contexts such as power, privilege, and status interact to shape motivational experiences (Remedios & Snyder, 2015). This perspective can reveal meaningful specificities that might otherwise remain invisible and can inform more targeted and equitable interventions (see Crenshaw, 1997). For instance, while racial minorities and individuals with learning or physical disabilities in the U.S. may each report lower levels of self-determination, it is equally important to understand how social structures and institutional contexts create different opportunities and constraints for experiencing autonomy and intrinsic motivation. An intersectional lens highlights the experiences of individuals with multiple marginalized identities, whose needs may not be adequately captured by studying any single category in isolation. A study with Black youths with disabilities illustrates this point. Black youths with disabilities reported lower autonomy relative to their peers with disabilities from other racial groups (Garrett, 2011). To advance our field, we must continue to explore and understand motivation through the lens of non-dominant group experiences. Doing so will allow us to build more comprehensive and precise knowledge, as well as to incubate new discoveries and innovative solutions that truly serve everyone.

3. Strengthening Participation by Building a Broader and More Welcoming Scholarly Community

Third, a meta-scientific opportunity also emerges for motivation science to reflect on strategies to expand access and participation in the field. Science is not produced in social vacuums; its success depends on the people it can attract, support, and retain as contributors to the system. From a systems perspective, how the scientific system is set up directly influences who feels welcome to participate and who is recognized as a legitimate contributor (Aly et al., 2023; Ledgerwood et al., 2022), with direct implications for scholars as people and their communities.

More specifically, what a scientific system values signals to scientists what the system is about and the level of support they would receive to thrive within it (Bretz et al., 1989). These values are often communicated explicitly and implicitly through the rewards and field-wide practices the system elevates, often by powerful constituents (Hamedani & Markus, 2019), such as leaders in scientific societies and editors of academic journals. Consider these questions: Who are celebrated as high performers or significant contributors in professional societies (and what backgrounds do they have in common)? What kinds of research topics or approaches are more likely to be presented at conferences, accepted by journals, and funded by funding agencies? Whether they are unintentional or not, these patterns of rewards and practices are meaningful signals to current and future scholars, influencing their calculus of whether and to what degree they want to take part or stay in the field (i.e., recruitment and retention; Aly et al., 2023; Bretz et al., 1989).

Recognizing this systemic perspective, motivation scientists have a collective opportunity to improve and expand motivation science by reimagining what a thriving field of motivation science might look like and how to increase opportunities for more people to be involved and support those within the system. This expansive approach helps avoid epistemic exclusion (Settles et al., 2021), a phenomenon in which certain scholars and scholarship are systematically devalued or marginalized as irrelevant or unimportant within academic systems. Research suggests this experience is especially common among scholars from non-dominant backgrounds and scholars in the Majority World (Krys et al., 2025; Uskul et al., 2024). Scholars of color, for instance, are often underrepresented in their field of study (Roberts et al., 2020)-a situation that likely applies to motivation science. By default, they are among the few experts when studying topics related to their lived experience. While their work is scientifically critical and informative (see Opportunity 2), they often face challenges finding opportunities for meaningful collaboration and reviewers with sufficient expertise to evaluate their work properly. As a result, their work progress is undermined, and their research is often more scrutinized and less appreciated (Torrez et al., 2024). Scholars from underrepresented backgrounds also often describe similar experiences as "academic outsiders," who have earned their credentials yet still feel like outsiders in their careers because they navigate systems not designed with their lived experiences in mind (Reyes, 2022). These reflections underscore the need for a more inclusive approach to science that supports scholars and values their contributions more holistically-not as exceptions but as essential to the field's advancement.

One effective way to move in this direction is to (re)shape values and incentives within the system to create opportunities to recognize a wider range of valid research approaches and to invite participation by developing, appreciating, and retaining diverse talents. Expanding opportunities does not mean lowering scientific standards. On the contrary, diverse teams often produce higher-impact science, generating research that is more visible, innovative, and highly cited, even though their work may face more scrutiny or resistance (Freeman & Huang, 2015). Although this issue extends well beyond a specific field, meaningful progress in motivation science may have especially far-reaching implications. As mentioned earlier, because the focus on human and contextual specificity remains relatively recent in motivation science, there is considerable opportunity to implement change and strengthen the field's ability to achieve its central mission: understanding and improving human motivational processes. Additionally, motivation theories and findings inform research and practice across numerous disciplines and are routinely applied in high-stakes contexts. As a result, the populations and perspectives represented in motivation science can profoundly shape whose goals, challenges, and pathways to thriving become visible within the scientific literature and broaden participation in the academic ecosystem. It is therefore time to reimagine motivation science as a field that actively values diverse experiences, perspectives, and approaches, recognizing that doing so enhances both the quality of scientific knowledge and the field's longevity and capacity to contribute to human well-being.

Final Thoughts: Actionable Recommendations for Systemic Change

In this final section, we offer actionable recommendations to support and realize the opportunities outlined above. Because individuals in positions of influence often hold greater power-and responsibility-to shape the values, norms, and incentive structures of the field (Hamedani et al., 2024), these recommendations focus on actions that research advisors and mentors, journal reviewers and editors, and leaders of professional societies and funding agencies can take within their respective spheres of influence. These recommendations are organized around three interconnected domains: conducting research and training, publishing, and shaping professional communities. Together, these efforts aim to catalyze and sustain a paradigm shift toward a more inclusive and impactful science of motivation.

Conducting research and training. Mentors and advisors play a pivotal role in shaping the next generation of scholars. As such, they can especially support and encourage more thoughtful incorporation and study of variability across groups and contexts in motivation research, as well as research questions grounded in unique lived experiences (Ghai et al., 2025). Strategies may include inviting thoughtful conversations about research practices to uncover aspects of the hidden curriculum that formal training does not always address (Aly et al., 2024; FORRT, 2025). Moreover, they can design an affirming training experience that allows trainees from varied backgrounds to reflect on their positionality and encourages critical reflection on how these experiences can enrich research (Meral et al., 2025). During study design and data collection, mentors can model inclusive practices by asking: Whom is this research trying to understand? Does our sampling strategy reflect that population? Are we considering the contextual influences-such as identity, culture, or systemic affordances-that may shape motivational processes? How may the research project engage respectfully and reciprocally with participants and benefit communities? (see Levac et al., 2019) These considerations should extend to how scientists analyze and report findings. For example, treat demographic variables as meaningful sources of variation, not merely as control variables. When appropriate, researchers should explore group differences and moderating effects to better understand how motivation operates across contexts, as well as to deepen understanding of a case study or subpopulation for richer insights and scientific advances. Writing should reflect both scientific rigor and care in describing effects, populations, and human conditions. This practice can extend to the knowledge and references authors build on. Inclusive citation practices are essential to ensure that the knowledge base reflects the full spectrum of scholarly contributions (Lawson et al., 2025). Several writing and conceptual guidelines are made available to help researchers achieve these goals (American Psychological Association, 2023; Ghai et al., 2025; Rogers et al., 2024; Veldhuis et al., 2024).

Journals and publishing processes. Reviewers and editors have a unique opportunity to shape what is valued and visible in the field. One persistent barrier to inclusion is the publishing landscape: while general motivation or psychology journals continue to prioritize work grounded in dominant cultural norms, research on diverse motivational experiences is often minimized as "niche" or relegated to "specialty journals" (Ghai et al., 2025). To counter this pattern, editors and reviewers should seek scholarship from non-dominant regions and work that draws on lived experiences of non-dominant groups, which are theoretically rich and innovative (Bou Zeineddine et al., 2022). Academic journals in motivation science can formalize recognition and encourage research and manuscript submissions on understudied populations and related topics. One successful strategy is to update journal submission guidelines and organize special issues that explicitly encourage diverse and indigenous topics as well as global participation (Ledgerwood et al., 2024; Uskul et al., 2024). One example is a special issue in the Personality and Social Psychology Review (Adjei et al., 2025). Relatedly, journals can also expand their reviewer pools by recruiting scholars from underrepresented regions and backgrounds, and they can create editorial fellowships to build capacity globally (Ledgerwood et al., 2024). In a time of reviewer shortages, such efforts can offer win-win solutions that improve review quality, broaden perspectives, and enhance the relevance of published science.

Additionally, editors and reviewers should broaden their appreciation for the contributions of different methodological approaches and recognize the trade-offs in research design. For instance, ecologically valid, descriptive, and generative qualitative methods such as interviews and ethnographic studies are especially valuable for understanding minority experiences and contextual specificities in depth (Saldana, 2011; Syed & McLean, 2022). Whereas experimental research often requires large sample sizes to ensure adequate statistical power, qualitative research is guided by different principles, with sample sizes often determined by factors such as depth of inquiry and theoretical saturation. Rather than estimating population parameters (e.g., how many people are motivated by a particular factor and to what extent), qualitative methods seek to uncover rich meanings and narratives in contextualized lived experiences (e.g., how students with disabilities find motivation and why). In doing so, they often reveal unexpected insights and new questions that the existing literature has yet to explore. Consequently, they can be especially useful for identifying population-and context-specific nuances and generating insights that may be difficult to capture through quantitative methods alone.

Relatedly, motivation science should move beyond uncritically relying on rules of thumb for sample size and more seriously consider sample representativeness and relevance relative to the populations to which researchers seek to generalize. For example, a conventional study may draw on a relatively homogeneous sample of several hundred participants, and yet it is used to support broad claims about universal human motivation. By contrast, a study of an intersectional minority population, such as Black women CEOs in the U.S., may include only a small number of participants yet still represent a more substantial proportion of the population of interest, offering more precise and contextually grounded insights into the population's motivational experiences. Yet current review practices do not always adequately account for these methodological trade-offs. The conventional dominance of controlled experiments and quantitative methods in motivation science can undervalue qualitative work and limit the field's ability to benefit from diverse methods (Syed & McLean, 2022; Tafreshi et al., 2016), a default preference that warrants reconsideration. Furthermore, to develop scholars and enhance the quality of scientific work, editors and reviewers can reframe peer review as a formative process rather than primarily serving a gatekeeping function (Aly et al., 2023). That is, through the review process (even if a rejection), editors and reviewers can offer constructive, developmental feedback that elevates the science and affirms the scientists that they can belong in the field. To improve review practices, journals can offer reviewer and editor training (e.g., constructive critical feedback and a globally inclusive approach to research) and make editorial policies transparent to avoid a hidden curriculum that disadvantages or limits opportunities for certain authors. Several guidelines (Ledgerwood et al., 2024; Puthillam et al., 2024; Uskul et al., 2024) Professional societies, awards, and funding. Leaders of professional societies and funding agencies have the power to shape the field by allocating rewards in the system, such as conference invited talks, paper and scientist awards, and grants. In these contexts, we encourage expanding "theoretical contributions" to highlight research programs that expand knowledge about the complexity of humanity, including work that centers contextual specificities and non-dominant populations and reflects various kinds of scholarly impact. Expanding criteria does not lower standards or contradict meritocratic principles. Rather, when designed and implemented systematically, expansion illuminates and celebrates the diverse ways researchers contribute to the field. For example, how might we recognize the resourcefulness of researchers who conduct rigorous work with limited resources? Or the depth and care involved in studying marginalized communities? These questions matter, as they help motivation science to develop more appropriate standards and increase the ability to qualify diverse meaningful contributions (i.e., criterion deficiency), as well as preventing irrelevant factors-such as institutional prestige or geographic bias-from influencing evaluations (i.e., criterion contamination; Austin & Villanova, 1992).

Further, how intellectual leadership and scholarly achievements are expressed is not uniform.

Who do we celebrate and give opportunities to in our field? Who serves as an editor? Who gives conference addresses? Who receives research awards? (Society for the Science of Motivation, 2025).

Reflecting on these questions may lead to meaningful directions of change. For instance, in award nominations and evaluations, the fields of psychology and motivation science tend to place a premium on individual contributions (e.g., "intellectual independence"; Association for Psychological Science, 2025). This focus on independent contribution is common and likely reflects a conventional approach rooted in norms shaped by dominant Western individualist cultures. However, motivation research shows that aligning incentives with motives is critical to supporting motivation and achievement. Specifically, this individualistic reward approach can demoralize researchers, such as those who are communally oriented and conduct research or primarily work in teams, undermining their intrinsic motivation and sense of belonging to science (e.g., Allen et al., 2018). Moreover, this approach can create tension between "what is best for scientists' careers and what is best for science" because the latter often relies on innovative collaborations rather than a single person (Tiokhin et al., 2024). It can also inadvertently prioritize scholars from better-resourced regions, perpetuating global disparities in support and recognition. These underlying tensions in the system can be challenging to address. But confronting them will allow motivation scientists to better understand and work toward addressing them (e.g., when and how do we need to shift our orientation and award rubrics?). By doing so, we can create new opportunities within our academic system to align incentives more closely and holistically with contributions that move the field forward (see Ledgerwood et al., 2022, for a framework of open and inclusive psychology).

Finally, the motivation science community has a rich opportunity to learn from the Global Majority. Collaborations that center on Majority World perspectives can yield insights that are both locally grounded and globally relevant (Krys et al., 2025). Providing incentives, support, and opportunities for meaningful collaborations with scholars from these regions is essential. Professional organizations can create opportunities, such as conferences and virtual task forces or hackathons (Puthillam et al., 2024), funding mechanisms (Conde, 2025), and networks of local organizations (e.g., ABRIR, https://abrirpsy.org/), that help globally dispersed scholars pool their resources and expertise to undertake large-scale, open research projects that would otherwise be unrealizable (Silan et al., 2023).

Conclusion

Making motivation science more inclusive is imperative for both science and people. By attending to human and contextual specificities, researchers can ask deeper questions-not only about who is motivated and how, but also why, under what conditions, and to what end. These questions are foundational to advancing both the science and its real-world impact. Importantly, inclusion strengthens the field. By building a stronger, broader community of scholars, we enrich collective knowledge and ensure that motivation science truly reflects the people and societies it seeks to understand and serve, shaping a field that is rigorous, relevant, and responsive to the surrounding world.
