Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Aggression in the genus Pan (including bonobos and chimpanzees) remains a central puzzle in primatology and anthropology. Traditionally framed as a dichotomy between "peaceful bonobos" and "violent chimpanzees," recent findings challenge this view by documenting coalitionary aggression, maternal cannibalism, and lethal intergroup encounters in bonobos, alongside evidence that captive chimpanzees and bonobos exhibit comparable aggression levels. These observations highlight the importance of ecological context, demographic composition, and behavioral plasticity in shaping aggression. We propose that species identity is a weaker predictor of aggression in Pan than socioecological, demographic, social, and neuroendocrine context. While captivity offers methodological control, it also introduces confounds such as reduced group size, absence of fission-fusion dynamics, and enforced proximity. Conversely, wild studies reveal the ecological pressures, territorial defense, resource distribution, and interspecies competition that drive variation in aggression. Future research must integrate captive and free-ranging data through hybrid protocols, quantify ecological and demographic drivers, and explore neuroendocrine mechanisms underlying sex-specific aggression. An integrative review on Pan aggression will refine our understanding of adaptive strategies, illuminate the evolutionary roots of human social conflict, and advance comparative models of primate behavior.

1. Introduction: beyond the peaceful bonobo versus violent chimpanzee dichotomy

Aggression is a behavioral trait present across all phyla in the animal kingdom, from nonhuman animals to humans. Georgiev et al. (2013) proposed that aggression can be understood as an adaptive cost-benefit strategy in which the expression of aggression depends on the balance between its fitness benefits, such as access to resources, mates, territory, and social status, and its associated costs, including injury, energetic expenditure, stress, and damage to social relationships. This framework predicts that variation in aggression among species reflects differences in ecological and social conditions rather than fixed behavioral predispositions. Investigating aggression in non-human primates (NHPs) provides a crucial comparative framework for understanding the evolution of human aggressive behavior, particularly with regard to our closest phylogenetic relatives. According to Holekamp & Strauss (2016) "aggression" is defined as "harmful, potentially harmful, or threatening behavior that is directed towards conspecifics, and tends to increase the distance between an attacker and its opponent". For detailed definitions and classifications of the various aggression types included in the Pan ethogram, we direct readers to Nishida et al. (1999); Stevens et al. (2023). Bonobos (Pan paniscus) and chimpanzees (Pan troglodytes) live in multi-male/multi-female social systems characterized by male philopatry, which implies female dispersal in groups with fission-fusion dynamics. They are our closest living relatives, sharing a common ancestor with humans and exhibiting approximately 97-98% genetic similarity, as well as notable physiological parallels (Prüfer et al., 2012). Although they have both similar social systems, the divergence remains significant. Bonobos are expected to align more closely with the "empathizing" trajectory, displaying higher levels of cooperation and affiliation, while chimpanzees would align more with the "systemising" trajectory, showing -among othershigher levels of aggression and competitive behavior (MacLean, 2016). By acting as a natural ecological barrier separating the two species of the genus Pan, the Congo River has facilitated substantial divergence in their morphological and cognitive characteristics, as well as in their social organization.

According to the self-domestication hypothesis (Hare et al., 2012), bonobos have retained a suite of neotenic (juvenile) traits, including morphological characteristics, lower testosterone levels, reduced aggression, and higher levels of social play compared to chimpanzees-even in adulthood. In addition, the authors argued that this behavior may be explained by the fact that the distribution range of bonobos (a species endemic to Democratic Republic of the Congo, DRC) does not overlap with that of any other great ape, because of the Congo River, thereby reducing interspecies competition.

In contrast, chimpanzees inhabit the equatorial belt of Africa north of the Congo River, ranging across habitats from dense equatorial rainforests to seasonal savannas. This natural environmental barrier may have shaped the aggressive traits displayed by chimpanzees relative to gorillas, with whom they share the same distribution range. Interspecific encounters can escalate to lethal attacks, as reported by Southern et al. (2021) in Loango National Park, Gabon, where a coalition of adult male chimpanzees killed a juvenile gorilla during a territorial patrol. Comparative studies of wild populations have demonstrated that male chimpanzees exhibit higher rates of aggression, more frequent coalitionary behavior, and stronger affiliative bonds with other males than bonobos, suggesting important species differences in the social functions of aggression and cooperation (Surbeck et al., 2017).

The present review seeks to move beyond the traditional dichotomy of "peaceful bonobos"

versus "violent chimpanzees" by situating aggression within an integrative evolutionary framework. Specifically, we ask whether aggression in Pan reflects adaptive strategies shaped by ecological constraints, reproductive pressures, social organization, and neuroendocrine mechanisms (Box 1). We hypothesize that interspecific differences in aggression between bonobos and chimpanzees are primarily explained by ecological, demographic, social, and neuroendocrine factors interacting with environmental context rather than by fixed behavioral predispositions.

2. Aggression as a multidimensional trait in Pan

Understanding how NHPs negotiate dynamic socio-ecological contexts by weighing biological costs and advantages is crucial to comprehending the multifaceted nature of aggression and social behavior in the genus Pan (Garcia et al., 2021). Primates manage resource competition, reproductive constraints, and social disputes by using flexible cognitive processes, such as value-based decision-making and long-term planning, rather than strict behavioral reflexes (Garcia et al., 2021). According to this evolutionary framework, offensive aggression functions as a specialized biobehavioral system that has been molded by natural selection to react directly to territorial, resource, and status challenges. Successful outcomes serve to lessen difficulty and improve individual or group control (Blanchard & Blanchard, 2003). Thus, resource-driven competition, coalition building, and mate guarding are examples of behavioral strategies that reflect optimum trade-offs influenced by social structures and ecological limitations (Garcia et al., 2021). Harris (1999) further argued that aggression in primates should be interpreted within a socioecological framework in which competition for mates, resources, and offspring protection may generate different forms of aggressive behavior. Because these functions are not mutually exclusive, aggressive interactions often reflect multiple selective pressures operating simultaneously, highlighting the importance of evaluating aggression in relation to ecological conditions, demographic structure, and reproductive context.

Aggression is increasingly recognized as a multidimensional phenomenon shaped by interactions among developmental, physiological, social, and evolutionary processes. Rather than reflecting a single behavioral trait, aggressive behavior emerges through the interaction of endocrine mechanisms, social experience, dominance relationships, and ecological pressures. Dominance hierarchies resulting from repeated agonistic interactions may further structure access to resources while reducing the frequency of overt conflicts, thereby linking aggression with broader patterns of social organization (Holekamp & Strauss, 2016). Therefore, rather than permanent, species-wide behavioral predispositions, interspecific differences in aggression and social cohesion between chimpanzees and bonobos represent adaptive, context-dependent reactions to conspecific problems (Blanchard & Blanchard, 2003; Garcia et al., 2021). Aggression can be viewed as a context-dependent adaptive strategy in which ecological conditions, social dynamics, and sex-specific reproductive interests influence the balance of costs and benefits, favoring coalitionary aggression and territorial violence when the potential gains in resources and reproductive success exceed the associated risks (Georgiev et al., 2013). Recent evidence provides direct support for this adaptive framework. Wood et al. (2025)'s work has demonstrated that territorial expansion following repeated lethal attacks against neighboring groups was associated with increased female fertility and substantially improved infant survivorship. Females were more than twice as likely to give birth after territorial expansion, while infant mortality declined markedly.

These findings indicate that coalitionary intergroup aggression can generate tangible fitness benefits through increased access to territory and resources, supporting the view that aggressive behavior in Pan may function as an adaptive strategy shaped by ecological and reproductive pressures rather than as a maladaptive expression of violence.

As highlighted by Bernstein & Gordon (1974), aggression in NHPs is not merely a disruptive force stemming from resource competition (e.g., food access and mating partners), but a regulated biological mechanism that functions to preserve social order, enforce behavioral codes, and maintain group cohesion. In the genus Pan, bonobos are well known for being a non-territorial but tolerant species. The occurrence of cannibalism in bonobos (e.g., Tokuyama et al., 2017) without accompanying infanticide, unlike the pattern commonly reported in chimpanzees (reviewed in Wilson et al., 2014), remains an unresolved issue. In particular, extreme manifestations of maternal cannibalism in Pan, such as the consumption and sharing of a deceased offspring by the mother (or another individual), challenge current interpretations of the relationship between empathy, maternal attachment, and aggressive behavior. Such observations challenge straightforward interpretations of empathic capacities in bonobos and other great apes. These are unsolved matters to be answered by future research. While several studies on wild or captive Hominidae, including Pan species as well as other NHPs, have demonstrated that mother-offspring bonds may persist beyond death, as evidenced by continued caregiving behaviors such as carrying, inspecting and grooming deceased infants (e.g., reviewed in: Gonçalves & Carvalho, 2019; de Marco et al., 2022), it is important to note that although empathy is generally considered to entail a prosocial orientation, not all forms of behavior prosocial or necessarily arise from empathic motivations (Yamamoto, 2017). The motivations behind this aggressive trait in bonobos remain poorly understood, apart from hypotheses linked to ecological constraints (i.e., nutritional stress, scarcity of animal protein). Meanwhile, in their chimpanzee counterparts, the motivations are linked to territorial defense and reproductive strategies such as male sexual coercion of females, but this pattern is absent in bonobos. To date, however, no study has related the propensity for cannibalism with prey availability or hunting success in bonobos. The taxon most closely comparable to Pan species, in terms of aggression traits, is cetaceans, such as bottlenose dolphins (Tursiops spp.), where sexual coercion-excluding infanticide-and lethal aggression among conspecifics also occur (Scott et al., 2005). 3. Proposed mechanisms underlying aggression in Pan

>Proposed mechanisms underlying aggression in Pan

3.1 Ecological mechanisms

In both daily foraging situations and macro-level population dynamics, ecological constraints -primarily the spatial distribution, seasonality, and patchiness of food resources-act as a main evolutionary catalyst shaping aggressive behaviors in the genus Pan. At the local scale, the reliability of resources directly influences social unity and intra-group competitive dynamics. While the ubiquitous availability of protein-rich Terrestrial Herbaceous Vegetation (THV) in bonobo habitats buffers against foraging stress and promotes long-term female gregariousness and coalitions by attending mixed-sex parties without incurring large costs from contest and scramble competition, while chimpanzees' dependence on seasonal variation in food availability leads to more flexible fission-fusion social systems have been primary topics in socio-ecological comparisons to address the differences in genus Pan (Furuichi, 2009). These ecological factors regulate the cost-benefit trade-offs of intergroup violence and establish home-range carrying capacity at the landscape scale. Chimpanzees use numerical asymmetries during border patrols to aggressively extend territorial boundaries when resource scarcity imposes energetic costs. This ensures fitness benefits including increased female fertility and infant survivorship (Wood et al., 2025). On the other hand, ecological strain triggers group fission when population expansion surpasses local carrying capacity, transforming former conspecific allies into territorial rivals engaged in protracted, deadly conflict (Sandel et al., 2026). In the end, violent encounters in Pan are dynamically structured by ecological limitations at every level of social organization, from proximate feeding competition to ultimate territoriality at the landscape scale.

3.2 Reproductive strategies and infanticide

Male infanticide in animals has often been interpreted within the framework of sexual selection, specifically as a strategy to shorten the interbirth interval of reproductively active females (reviewed in Hrdy, 1979). In female bonobos, tumescence lasts longer, thereby providing lower-ranking males with increased opportunities for reproductive success. By contrast, in female chimpanzees, the ovulatory cycle is shorter, which facilitates the monopolization of tumescent females by high-ranking males and their allies, often through sexual coercion (Paoli, 2009; Feldblum et al., 2021). Such strategies may involve coalitionary support, affiliative bonds, and political alliances. This coercion can lead to infanticide, whether intra-or inter-group, frequently followed by cannibalism (reviewed in Wilson et al., 2014). Evidence from bonobos suggests that the function of aggression may differ substantially from that observed in chimpanzees. At LuiKotale, Hohmann et al. (2019) found that although male bonobos occasionally directed intense aggression toward immature individuals, the behavior did not conform to predictions of the adaptive infanticide hypothesis. Aggression was directed primarily toward older, weaned immatures rather than dependent infants, males did not preferentially target unrelated offspring, and no lethal outcomes were observed. These findings indicate that male aggression in bonobos may serve functions other than infanticide, including the regulation of dominance relationships, dispersal processes, or competition for future resources. Yet paternal infanticide has not been documented. Walker et al. (2021) described in the Kasekela group at Gombe an attempted case in which an adult male, identified as the father, joined his mother, who initiated the attack on the infant of another adult female. This behavior may reflect the pervasive uncertainty of paternity in chimpanzee societies. By contrast, male infanticide in mammalian societies is well documented, whereas cases committed by females remain very rare among group-living primates. Nevertheless, several hypotheses have been proposed, including exploitation for food and competition over resources or social stress (reviewed in Hrdy, 1979). Walker et al. (2021) re-evaluated adaptive hypotheses concerning female-led infanticide in wild chimpanzees by testing several predictions. They found no correlation between food scarcity and the occurrence of infanticide, and attackers were not in energetically costly reproductive states. However, infanticide was significantly more likely when the female-to-male ratio was higher. Moreover, females were more prone to attack mothers with whom they experienced greater competition, as indicated by core area overlap.

Females occupying higher dominance ranks are more likely to prevail over lower-ranking females when initiating aggression.

3.3 Social mechanisms: maternal support and coalitions

In female-dominated societies such as that of bonobos, male-male aggression does not appear to function to prevent other males from associating with females, given the absence of sexual coercion. A similar pattern is observed in other female-dominated mammals in which females are as aggressive as, or more aggressive than, males, such as spotted hyena, Crocuta crocuta (Curren et al., 2015). Furuichi (2011) proposed that the relatively peaceful nature of bonobo society is largely rooted in female social strategies. Female bonobos maintain strong affiliative bonds, form coalitions, exert influence over group movements, and provide support to their adult sons, while prolonged pseudo-estrus reduces male reproductive competition and sexual coercion. Together, these interacting mechanisms enhance female social influence, promote group cohesion, and contribute to the higher levels of tolerance typically observed in bonobos compared with chimpanzees. In bonobos, females form powerful coalitions among non-relatives to subordinate males and gain priority of access to resources. Evidence from LuiKotale indicates that maternal support plays a central role in male bonobo social dynamics: mothers frequently associate with their adult sons, provide agonistic support during conflicts with other males, and enhance the mating success of low-and mid-ranking sons (Surbeck et al., 2011), suggesting that dominance trajectories and reproductive opportunities are shaped not only by male competitive ability but also by female social leverage and maternal support. Concurrently, female bonobos integrate into new groups through affiliative mechanisms; sexual behavior frequently serves as a social appeasement tool, mitigating intragroup xenophobia and facilitating the peaceful social integration of immigrant females through socio-sexual interactions with central resident females, notably via genito-genital rubbing (G-G rubbing) (reviewed in Wrangham, 1993).

Although chimpanzees and bonobos share fission-fusion dynamics and male philopatry, divergent social structures and physiological traits shape their respective capacities for peaceful coexistence. As Clay et al. (2016) highlight, bonobo society is catalyzed by prolonged female sexual swellings and concealed ovulation, which foster female-female aggregation, secure feeding priority, and reduce inter-male competition. Furthermore, while both species engage in conflict resolution strategies like reconciliation and consolation, bonobos uniquely integrate high levels of socio-sexual behavior as a mechanism for social tension regulation.

In contrast, social mechanisms in chimpanzees are structured around male-male cooperation and intense competition. Comparative evidence from wild populations indicates that male chimpanzees engage more frequently in male-male coalitions and devote a greater proportion of their affiliative interactions to other males through grooming and post-conflict reconciliation, whereas bonobo males form substantially fewer coalitions with one another (Surbeck et al., 2017). Consequently, male-male aggression in chimpanzees is driven primarily by territorial defense and reproductive strategies, including the use of sexual coercion against females. Female immigration in this species is a high-risk process in which young females face marked hostility from resident females competing for territory and resources (Walker et al., 2021), whereas native males exhibit reproductive tolerance. New immigrants receive significantly more aggression from other immigrants than do established immigrants, though new and established immigrants are treated equally by adult natal males.

These contrasting patterns highlight how divergent social strategies-female-centered affiliation and appeasement in bonobos versus male-driven alliances and resource competition in chimpanzees-shape interspecific variation in social cohesion and conflict.

3.4 Neuroendocrine mechanisms

3.4.1 Oxytocin modulates empathy and consolation

Oxytocin (OT) is a neuropeptide hormone involved in a range of functions of sociality in animal systems, especially in affiliative behaviors, such as dyadic relationship qualities. In this section, we will discuss the way OT modulates empathy and consolation.

First, according to using the analogy of a "Russian Doll model", "empathy" has been defined as "any process that emerges from the fact that observers understand others' states by activating personal, neural and mental representations of that state, including the capacity to be affected by and share the emotional state of another; assess the reasons for the other's state; and identify with the other, adopting his or her perspective" (reviewed in de Waal & Preston, 2017).

However, as proposed by Yamamoto's (2017) combination model, empathy is not a linear cognitive trait, but rather a composite of three independent mechanismsmatching with others, understanding of others, and prosociality-which explains why cognitive perspective-taking in primates does not automatically trigger proactive helping behavior.

The neuropeptide hormone OT is critically involved in the establishment of mother-offspring bonds. Yet, in humans and other NHPs, social relationships are embedded within complex social systems where bonds may endure for years beyond lactation and occur between numerous pair-bondings other than parental care. To date, it remains unclear in NHPs whether OT-mediated modulation of hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis activity is primarily associated with: (i) exposure to stress, while substantial evidence has accumulated in rodents, evidence in humans remains equivocal (reviewed in Brown et al., 2016), and the topic has been largely overlooked in NHPs, particularly chimpanzees, for which no evidence is currently available (Samuni et al., 2019a); (ii) social buffering responses to stressors (e.g., allogrooming and consolation); (iii) social support occurring in the absence of stressors during everyday social interactions that promote health and well-being. These three potential pathways have been reviewed in detail by Crockford et al. (2017).

Studies conducted in captive rhesus macaques have demonstrated that aerosolized OT administration enhances prosocial decision-making in prosocial choice tasks. Furthermore, OT selectively reduces attention to negative or threatening stimuli, as revealed by analyses of gaze allocation during the presentation of photographic stimuli (reviewing in Putnam et al., 2018). Current evidence suggests that OT modulates empathy by enhancing sensitivity to socially relevant cues and increasing emotional attunement to others. This is reflected in reduced self-interest, greater prosocial engagement, and enhanced emotional empathy.

Importantly, these effects are highly context-dependent and are shaped by both individual characteristics (e.g., sex, personality, anxiety, and developmental history) and environmental conditions, particularly social and emotional contexts (reviewed in Yao & Kendrick, 2025).

In Pan species, particularly wild bonobos, Cheng et al. (2025) found no association between OT reactivity and intergroup encounters. Specifically, OT levels did not increase during intergroup encounters in either sex. Instead, males exhibited lower OT levels when a neighboring group was present, whereas females showed no detectable changes across contexts. Although males displayed higher OT levels than females in the absence of neighboring groups, OT levels concentrations were similar between the sexes during intergroup encounters. These findings contrast with those reported in humans (de Dreu et al., 2010) and chimpanzees, where urinary OT levels were associated with intergroup encounters but were not influenced by the intensity of the stressor, such as the degree of intergroup risk or hostility (Samuni et al., 2017 (Samuni et al., , 2019a)). Whether OT responses in bonobos are similarly independent of variation in intergroup threat intensity remains unclear and warrants further comparative investigation. The finding that OT promotes outgroup attention in the sex most involved in intergroup relations in both bonobos and chimpanzees aligns with evidence that the OT system in Pan has conserved social functions that are expressed in species-specific ways, such as facilitating social bonding through G-G rubbing in female bonobos and cooperation during group hunting and border patrols in male chimpanzees (Brooks et al., 2022).

Second, "consolation behavior" has been defined as "reassurance behavior directed at a distressed party, such as a victim of aggression. Concern about another's state, and attempts to ameliorate this state" (reviewed in de Waal & Preston, 2017). Consolation is widely distributed across taxa-observed in apes, monkeys, canids, elephants, and corvids-and exhibits a developmental decline, being most prevalent among immature individuals, which points to early-life motivational origins rather than advanced adult cognition alone. While bonobos and chimpanzees both engage in post-conflict consolation as a marker of empathic concern, recent longitudinal research highlights that these behaviors are shaped by distinct developmental and individual dynamics. Webb et al. (2017) demonstrated that individual differences in chimpanzee consolation behavior exhibit relative stability across development.

These individual variations persist even after accounting for baseline affiliation rates, opportunities to console, kinship, and dyadic relationship quality. Additionally, the study revealed a developmental decline in consolation, showing that older chimpanzees are less likely to console than younger individuals. Finally, an individual's tendency to console positively correlates with its Composite Sociality Index, indicating that higher consolers achieve greater social integration and overall social competence. Furthermore, observational data in bonobos demonstrate that victim-directed consolation and prosocial responses are heavily modulated by the signaling strategies (such as paedomorphic or distress signals) deployed by the victim (Clay, 2023).

Notwithstanding the substantial body of research on the subject, it remains difficult to ascertain whether OT plays a direct causal role in affiliative processes or merely reflects an epiphenomenal correlate of underlying mechanisms (reviewed in : Putnam et al., 2018; Leng et al., 2022). Beyond these considerations, OT appears to modulate the balance between affiliation and aggression to stress responses while reducing xenophobic responses toward out-group individuals.

3.4.2 Testosterone

The steroid hormone testosterone (T) is well established as a key modulator of aggressive behavior, as demonstrated by extensive empirical research, in non-human animals while in humans that link is more controversial (e.g., Book et al., 2001; reviewed in Geniole et al., 2020). However, the behavioral effects of T are not uniform across species. Lynn (2008) argued that many vertebrates exhibit behavioral insensitivity to T, indicating that circulating hormone concentrations alone do not reliably predict aggression or other social behaviors.

Instead, species-specific ecological pressures, life-history strategies, and variation in receptor sensitivity may modulate how T influences behavior. This perspective suggests that differences between bonobos and chimpanzees may arise not only from variation in hormone levels, but also from differences in physiological responsiveness to androgens.

Recent evidence further suggests that the relationship between T and aggression is considerably more complex than traditionally assumed. In a large meta-analysis, Geniole et al. (2020) found that baseline T and T reactivity were positively associated with aggression in men, but the observed effects were relatively weak. Moreover, experimental administration of T did not consistently increase aggressive behavior. The authors concluded that T-related effects on aggression are strongly influenced by social context, individual characteristics, and situational factors, highlighting that T should be viewed as a context-dependent modulator rather than a direct biological determinant of aggression.

Evidence from wild chimpanzees further supports a neuroendocrine basis for aggression in reproductive contexts. Muller & Wrangham (2004) found that male chimpanzees exhibited elevated T levels during periods when sexually receptive parous females were present, coinciding with increased rates of male-male aggression. In contrast, T levels did not increase in the presence of nulliparous females, despite similar mating activity. These findings suggest that T is more closely associated with competitive aggression related to reproductive opportunities than with sexual behavior itself, supporting the view that neuroendocrine mechanisms play a central role in mediating context-dependent aggression in Pan. On the other hand, neither in captivity nor in the wild, T levels in bonobos suggest an association with only a limited subset of aggressive behaviors. Most forms of aggression appear to be more strongly influenced by social factors than by endocrine status, although elevated T is linked to increased responsiveness to provocation and certain display behaviors. These findings indicate that aggression in bonobos is primarily socially regulated, with T exerting a more restricted modulatory role (Marshall & Hohmann, 2005; Sannen et al., 2024a,b). In other words, T is correlated with social rank, but not with dominance rank (unlike chimpanzees), suggesting that social status in bonobos depends primarily on maternal support and matrifocal relationships rather than endocrine-driven competitive ability (Furuichi, 1997; Surbeck et al., 2011).

Recent evidence suggests that T should not be viewed solely as a facilitator of aggression.

Experimental work from Kelly et al. (2022), carried out on social Mongolian gerbil (Meriones unguiculatus), a territorial rodent in which occurs sexual coercion, infanticide, and cannibalism (Elwood & Ostermeyer, 1984; Palombit, 2015) as in chimpanzees, has shown that T can rapidly promote context-appropriate social responses, increasing prosocial behavior in affiliative settings while reducing it in competitive contexts. These findings indicate that T may function as a flexible regulator of social behavior whose effects depend on current social conditions, potentially through interactions with OT-mediated social salience mechanisms.

Overall, T appears to exert contrasting social effects across Pan species, amplifying coalitionary aggression and reproductive competition in chimpanzees, while in bonobos its effects are moderated by strong affiliative bonds and mitigates reproductive coercion.

Recent evidence from humans indicates that T and OT interact rather than acting independently in the regulation of competitive and aggression-related behaviors. In an experimental model of intergroup conflict, increases in T reactivity predicted costly competitive behavior toward outgroups in males, whereas OT administration attenuated this relationship. Notably, these effects were sex-specific, with no comparable T-dependent pattern observed in females (Cherki et al., 2024). Although similar mechanisms remain largely unexplored in bonobos and chimpanzees, these findings provide a valuable theoretical framework for understanding how interacting neuroendocrine pathways may contribute to context-dependent variation in aggression across Pan species.

4. Emerging evidence challenging the traditional dichotomy

In African great apes, coalitionary lethal aggression has traditionally been considered unique to chimpanzees (reviewed in Wilson et al., 2014) and gorillas (Rosenbaum et al., 2016; Potel et al., 2024), owing to their classification as territorial species. By contrast, bonobos are generally described as exhibiting higher levels of reconciliation and consolation behaviors (Clay & de Waal, 2015; Brooker et al., 2024), and it was thought that such a degree of aggressiveness was absent from their social organization. This traditional view is consistent with the self-domestication hypothesis, which proposes that natural selection against aggression in bonobos, driven by reduced feeding competition and stronger female coalitions, favoured increased social tolerance and reduced male coercion relative to chimpanzees (Hare et al., 2012). Under this framework, many behavioral, cognitive, and morphological differences between the two Pan species are interpreted as correlated outcomes of selection for reduced aggression rather than as independent adaptations. Over 50-year of research on wild bonobos have yielded only two reports from long-term studies, neither providing evidence of such behavior. First, Wilson et al. (2014) noted the death of an adult male bonobo from Eyengo's group at Lomako in 1997, although the event itself was not directly observed. Second, Pashchevskaya et al. (2025) reported at LuiKotale a suspected case of coalitionary aggression by adult females against a resident adult male within the same group. Following the attack, the perpetrators, along with other group members not involved in the aggression, were observed licking his open wounds, seemingly providing post-conflict support. The adult male was not seen again during 150 consecutive days of group monitoring from the date of the event, suggesting that the attack may have been lethal. Notably, his mother had already disappeared from the group the previous year, around 2010. We suggest that this phenomenon may be explained by the fact that dominance among male bonobos is facilitated by the continued support of their mothers, who remain present within the natal group (Surbeck et al., 2011). In chimpanzees, rough play from an early age is thought to shape stronger dispositions toward aggression in adulthood (Sabbi et al., 2021). Several studies on children, as in other mammals, have established that social life influences the development of aggression, with effects that differ between the sexes.

However, this behavior, which appears to be absent in bonobo society, was recently documented under natural conditions for the first time by Gareta García et al. (2026) at Kokolopori. During an encounter between two neighboring groups, members of the Kokoalongo group attacked an adult female from the Ekalakala group, and in the days following this attack, a 52-day-old infant died. The authors nevertheless argued against classifying the case as infanticide, due to the unclear nature of the observation. Moreover, the infant was cared for by an adult female from the Kokoalongo group until its death, and she even continued to carry the corpse afterward, suggesting an adoption process "care provided by an unrelated adult -i.e., alloparenting -similar to that previously described by Tokuyama et al. (2021) at Wamba. This indicates that adoption is not unique to bonobos; a similar pattern of alloparental care by the dominant sex is also observed in wild chimpanzees, where males tolerate and adopt young conspecifics (or orphans) after the death of their mothers (e.g., at Taï: Samuni et al., 2019b). This further supports the assumption that aggression in bonobos does not conform to predictions of infanticide cases as reported at LuiKotale by Hohmann et al. (2019). However, very little is actually known about this behavioral variation, as long-term studies dedicated to wild bonobos and intersite comparisons remain relatively sparse. In addition, the recent study by Bryon et al. (2026) demonstrated that, in captivity, the levels of aggressiveness observed in chimpanzees are comparable to those in bonobos, thereby challenging the bonobo's reputation as a peaceful great ape-famously described by Frans de Waal, the Dutch-American primatologist and ethologist, as the 'make love, not war' primate. Frans de Waal showed that bonobos are the only species within the genus Pan to employ sexual behavior beyond reproductive purposes, uniquely using it as a communicative strategy to prevent or reduce aggressive interactions (reviewed in Wrangham, 1993).

Importantly, skepticism regarding the traditional bonobo-chimpanzee dichotomy is not new.

More than two decades ago, Stanford (1998) argued that the behavioral differences between the two Pan species had often been overstated and that many apparent contrasts reflected differences in research intensity, sampling history, and ecological context rather than fundamentally distinct behavioral syndromes. He suggested that bonobos and chimpanzees share a broader repertoire of social and aggressive behaviors than commonly assumed, a view that anticipated recent findings emphasizing behavioral plasticity and substantial withinspecies variation.

Comparative analyses across the Pan lineage further suggest that behavioral variation cannot be adequately explained by species membership alone. Kovalaskas et al. (2021) argued that ecological conditions, social environments, and population-specific factors generate substantial behavioral diversity within both bonobos and chimpanzees, limiting the explanatory power of simple species-level contrasts. Their findings support the view that aggression and social behavior in Pan are best interpreted along a continuum of behavioral plasticity rather than as fixed species-specific syndromes. This perspective is supported by recent empirical evidence challenging species-wide generalizations. Using standardized focal-follow data collected from wild populations, Mouginot et al. (2024) found that male bonobos at Kokolopori exhibited higher rates of male-male aggression than male chimpanzees at Gombe, even when analyses were restricted to contact aggression. However, male chimpanzees engaged in coalitionary aggression far more frequently than bonobos.

Moreover, in both species, more aggressive males achieved greater mating success. These findings suggest that the frequency, intensity, and social consequences of aggression do not necessarily co-vary and should be interpreted within the broader context of reproductive competition and coalitionary behavior. These contrasting findings suggest that variation among populations may rival or even exceed average differences between species. Ecological conditions, demographic composition, social relationships, and local selective pressures likely shape the expression of aggression in both bonobos and chimpanzees. Consequently, aggression in Pan may be better understood as a context-dependent and plastic behavioral strategy rather than as a fixed species-specific trait. Recent evidence from the Ngogo chimpanzee group further illustrates the importance of social and demographic context in shaping aggressive behavior. Sandel et al. (2026) documented a rare permanent fission of a previously cohesive chimpanzee group, followed by sustained lethal aggression between former group members. The transition from social cohesion to intergroup violence was associated with increasing social polarization, spatial segregation, and reproductive isolation.

These observations suggest that coalitionary aggression can emerge from shifts in social relationships and group structure, supporting the view that aggression in Pan is a flexible and context-dependent behavioral strategy rather than a fixed species-specific predisposition.

Moving from active aggressive displays to feeding contexts, recent evidence indicates that behavioral differences between bonobos and chimpanzees may be less pronounced than traditionally assumed. Using standardized co-feeding experiments across 16 groups comprising 225 Pan individuals of bonobos (7 groups comprising 82 individuals, sex-ratio 36:46) and chimpanzees (9 groups comprising 143 individuals, sex-ratio 58:85), van Leeuwen et al. (2023) found substantial overlap in social tolerance between the two species and showed that group identity was a stronger predictor of co-feeding behavior than species membership. Some bonobo groups exhibited lower tolerance than chimpanzee groups, whereas others displayed the opposite pattern, highlighting considerable behavioral variation within species. These results challenge species-level generalizations and emphasize the importance of local social dynamics in shaping behavioral expression in Pan.

This pattern of overlapping behavior extends beyond social tolerance and co-feeding. Recent evidence suggests that the limitations of species-level dichotomies extend beyond aggression and social tolerance. Comparing consolation behavior in bonobos and chimpanzees, Brooker et al. (2025) found no significant species differences in the tendency to provide affiliative support to distressed group members. Instead, variation in consolation was primarily explained by within-species factors, including age, kinship, affiliative relationships, and group dynamics. The authors concluded that behavioral variation within species may exceed differences between species, reinforcing the view that socio-emotional traits in Pan are highly flexible and context dependent. Collectively, these findings indicate that behavioral variation within bonobos and chimpanzees may, in some cases, exceed differences between species. Across aggression, social tolerance, and empathy-related behaviors, species membership appears to be a weaker predictor of behavioral expression than the interacting effects of socioecological, demographic, social, and neuroendocrine factors. This review is consistent with the review of Gruber & Clay (2016), who argued that comparisons between bonobos and chimpanzees have historically overstated species differences while underestimating substantial variation within species. The authors emphasized that behavioral diversity across Pan populations reflects a continuum shaped by ecological and social factors rather than a simple opposition between two fixed behavioral syndromes. Consequently, understanding aggression in Pan requires consideration of both interspecific and intrapopulation variation, as well as the ecological conditions under which behavior is expressed.

As emphasized by Pusey (2022), the contrast between "warlike" chimpanzees and "peacemaking" bonobos should not be interpreted as a simple species-level dichotomy. While chimpanzees typically exhibit territorial behavior and hostile intergroup interactions, bonobos may engage in prolonged and tolerant encounters with neighbouring groups. Importantly, evidence suggests that these interacting bonobo groups remain socially distinct, indicating that intergroup tolerance is genuine rather than an artefact of group subdivision. The ecological and demographic factors underlying these differences remain debated, but variation in resource distribution, population density, home-range overlap, and territorial defendability have been proposed as key drivers. Together, these observations support the view that aggression and tolerance in Pan are flexible socioecological strategies rather than fixed species-specific traits.

This review further supports the need to interpret aggression within a broader socioecological framework and highlights the importance of considering environmental context when comparing captive and wild populations.

5. The problem of captivity: limitations and sampling bias Surbeck et al. (2026) challenged the findings of Bryon et al. (2026) in their comment, highlighting that captivity alone would not be enough to explain these variationsparticularly the reduced group size, the absence of fission-fusion dynamics (e.g., lack of territorial defense), birth control, and enforced proximity-all of which alter the expression of behaviors according to constraints linked to resource availability or group composition (e.g., age-sex class or demography), as well as zoo enrichment. In other words, the captive environment itself may constitute a factor underlying the behavioral differences observed between species, when contrasted with those expressed under natural conditions. It remains challenging to compare data from captive environments with those from wild populations due to the absence of hybrid protocols, which can lead to underscore aggression in Pan reduced to a simple dichotomy of 'peaceful bonobos' versus 'violent chimpanzees'. For future directions, the research should quantify ecological drivers such as resource distribution and prey availability, and examine the neuroendocrine bases of sex-specific aggression. Such an integrative approach will clarify whether aggression in Pan reflects adaptive strategies, ecological constraints, or evolutionary plasticity, and in turn, illuminate the roots of human social conflict. Captivity may alter the socioecological conditions that regulate aggression and therefore complicate direct comparisons with wild populations.

It is noteworthy that there is still an imbalance in the sampling effort of current comparisons between chimpanzees and bonobos. While data on chimpanzees come from over twenty longterm locations throughout Africa, long-term behavioral data on bonobos come from just three to four continually investigated sites (e.g., Wamba, Lomako, LuiKotale, and Kokolopori).

Our comprehension of the entire range of male-male violence in Pan may be skewed by this imbalance, especially when it comes to uncommon behaviors like deadly coalitionary aggression. To test the context-dependent model presented here, other bonobo populations and standardized behavioral protocols must be included in future research.

Box 1. Testable predictions and future directions

We propose a series of additional testable predictions that may further inform ongoing debates in primatology and anthropology regarding the extent to which bonobos and chimpanzees differ in their propensity for aggressive behavior. Prediction 1. Socioecological context will explain more variation in aggression than species identity. Prediction 2. Bonobos and chimpanzees exposed to similar ecological conditions will exhibit more similar aggression profiles than predicted by the traditional aggression dichotomy. Prediction 3. Coalition structure and maternal support will predict dominance outcomes and aggressive behavior.

Prediction 4. Variation in oxytocin and testosterone pathways will be associated with context-dependent variation in aggression. Prediction 5. Infanticide, cannibalism, and intergroup violence will be better predicted by ecological and demographic variables than by species membership.

Conclusion

Early studies contributed to the widespread portrayal of bonobos as peaceful and chimpanzees as inherently aggressive. However, accumulating evidence from both captive and wild populations suggests that aggression in Pan is best understood as a flexible and context-dependent behavioral strategy. The marked variation in lethal aggression observed among chimpanzee groups, together with recent findings from bonobos, indicates that differences among populations may equal or even exceed average species differences.

Therefore, the traditional bonobo-chimpanzee aggression dichotomy may partly reflect observer bias generated by uneven field effort and sampling intensity, rather than a fixed species-level difference. Rather than representing opposite ends of a behavioral continuum, bonobos and chimpanzees appear to share a common behavioral repertoire whose expression is shaped by interacting ecological, demographic, social, and neuroendocrine factors. These observations highlight the major role of local socioecological conditions in shaping aggressive behavior and support the view that aggression in Pan is better explained by behavioral plasticity than by fixed species-specific predispositions. Understanding this variation will improve comparative models of primate social evolution and provide new insights into the evolutionary roots of human conflict.
