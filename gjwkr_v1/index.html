What we found

We found 7 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Socioemotional skills are fundamental to children's well-being and development, yet the neurodevelopmental processes through which familial relationships contribute to these skills remain poorly understood. While socioemotional skills are partly heritable, they are also shaped by social learning through interactions with others, including siblings. Sibling relationships represent one of the earliest and most enduring social contexts throughout development and provide a valuable context for investigating how familial relatedness is reflected in children's neural processing of socioemotional information. However, little is known about whether siblings exhibit similarities in these neural responses and how such similarities relate to socioemotional behavior.

In this study, we investigated whether sibling dyads show greater similarity in neural responses during socioemotional processing than unrelated child dyads and whether such neural similarity is associated with socioemotional functioning. To assess this, 120 children (6-14 years), including 39 sibling dyads, completed a naturalistic fMRI movie-watching task designed to elicit socioemotional responses. Results showed that sibling dyads exhibited greater whole-brain functional neural similarity during socioemotional processing than unrelated children across the whole brain. Regionof-interest analyses further revealed greater functional neural similarity among siblings across several prefrontal, temporal, and limbic regions, including the left ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC). Moreover, greater similarity in left vmPFC responses, a region implicated in social cognition, was associated with higher levels of socially competent behavior in both sibling and unrelated dyads, suggesting that neural similarity may be particularly pronounced among children with higher social competence. These findings highlight sibling relationships as an informative context for investigating familial similarities in neural responses to socioemotional information and their associations with socioemotional behavior during childhood.

Introduction

Brain and behavioral development are strongly shaped by the social environment in which a child grows up, with family being one of the earliest and most influential contexts (Bronfenbrenner, 1979).

While developmental research has traditionally emphasized parent-child relationships as a primary source of socialization, increasing attention has been directed toward the role of siblings, whose relationships represent some of the most enduring social bonds across the lifespan, which is particularly salient during childhood (Brody, 2004; Dirks et al., 2015; McHale et al., 2012; Rogers et al., 2018; Venturelli et al., 2016; Yucel & Yuan, 2015). Sibling relationships provide a valuable context for examining developmental processes, as similarities between siblings can reflect genetic relatedness and shared environmental experiences, whereas differences may result from genetic difference and non-shared environmental influences (Burt, 2014; Chorlian et al., 2007; Her et al., 2021; Plomin & Daniels, 2011; Voorpostel et al., 2007). Through frequent and emotionally salient interactions, siblings influence one another through reciprocal processes such as modeling, imitation, reinforcement, scaffolding, cooperative play, communication, and conflict resolution (Bacha-Trams et al., 2024; Bandura, 1977; Voorpostel et al., 2007). As a result, siblings may shape one another's socioemotional development, with effects that may be adaptive or maladaptive depending on relationship quality and broader contextual factors (Dirks et al., 2015; Hou et al., 2020; Hughes et al., 2018; Ningrum, 2024; Stormshak et al., 2009).

Socioemotional functioning encompasses various processes involved in emotion processing and regulation as well as social understanding, including empathy, prosocial behavior, perspective-taking, and social engagement (Ochsner, 2008; Thompson, 1990; Thompson & Goodman, 2009). More adaptive socioemotional functioning is associated with greater psychological well-being, stronger interpersonal relationships, and improved academic achievement (Berger et al., 2011; Cipriano et al., 2024; Durlak et al., 2011; Elias & Haynes, 2008; Tugade & Fredrickson, 2007), whereas difficulties are linked to increased risk for internalizing and externalizing psychopathology (Eadeh et al., 2021; Eisenberg et al., 2001; Fournier et al., 2021).

Importantly, socioemotional functioning reflects both heritable influences and those arising from learning processes and social experiences (Bronfenbrenner, 1979; Eisenberg et al., 1998; Thompson & Virmani, 2012). Given siblings' shared biological backgrounds and frequent interactions, examining similarities in their socioemotional functioning may offer insight into how familial factors are associated with individual differences in these skills.

Neurodevelopmentally, socioemotional functioning relies on the maturation and integration of distributed brain networks, particularly areas of the corticolimbic circuit (Casey et al., 2019). Limbic regions, including the amygdala and hippocampus, are involved in emotion processing, emotional learning, and memory formation and mature relatively early (Gogtay et al., 2004; Phan et al., 2002). In contrast, prefrontal cortical regions, including lateral and medial PFC, continue to develop into young adulthood, supporting the gradual maturation of emotion regulation and other higher-order control processes (Casey et al., 2019; Gee et al., 2013; Mills et al., 2014; Silvers et al., 2017). Functional interactions between prefrontal and limbic systems are considered critical for adaptive socioemotional functioning and emotion regulation (Casey et al., 2019; Kohn et al., 2014; Silvers et al., 2017). Given their ongoing maturation across childhood and adolescence, these corticolimbic systems may be influenced by environmental and social input, suggesting that early social experiences, including sibling relationships, may contribute to their development (Knudsen, 2004; Tottenham, 2013). However, whether siblings exhibit similar neural responses when processing socioemotional information during childhood remains largely unknown.

One approach to investigating how individuals process the same information is through functional neural similarity. In naturalistic neuroimaging paradigms, similarity between specific dyads is often quantified using inter-subject correlation and related approaches (Parkinson et al., 2018). These approaches are based on the premise that individuals who attend to, interpret, or process information in similar ways may exhibit similar temporal patterns of neural activity (Finn et al., 2020). Prior research suggests that individuals in close social relationships may exhibit greater neural similarity, potentially reflecting shared experiences, convergent interpretive frameworks, or similar socioemotional processing styles. For example, greater neural similarity has been observed among friends during naturalistic movie watching (Parkinson et al., 2018), and structural and functional neural similarities have also been reported in parent-child dyads across networks (Takagi et al., 2021). Importantly, intergenerational neural similarity has further been linked to socioemotional functioning, emotion regulation, and mental health (E. Abraham et al., 2020; Dimanova et al., 2023; Fehlbaum et al., 2022; Foland-Ross et al., 2016; Lee et al., 2017; Scatolin, Federici, Dimanova, Saikkonen, et al., 2026; Su et al., 2024; Yamagata et al., 2016). These similarities may capture shared patterns in how family members process and respond to socioemotional information, potentially arising from a combination of shared genetic influences, common environments, and interpersonal experiences. Importantly, functional neural similarity offers a complementary perspective to behavioral measures by capturing the temporal correspondence of brain responses as individuals encounter social and emotional information over time.

Despite growing evidence for neural similarity in friends and parent-child dyads, relatively little research has examined neural similarity between siblings. The few existing studies suggest that siblings exhibit similar neural responses across both task-based and naturalistic paradigms. Among adolescent siblings, neural similarity in regions implicated in valuation and decision-making, including the vmPFC, ventral striatum, and anterior insula, has been observed during risky decisionmaking, particularly when younger siblings model older siblings' behavior, consistent with social learning processes (Rogers et al., 2021). Adult sisters showed greater neural similarity in default mode and sensory regions during naturalistic movie watching than friends or acquaintances (Bacha-Trams et al., 2024). Further evidence from electroencephalography indicates adult sibling similarity in feedback-related neural processing, with greater similarity in loss-related theta activity associated with depression risk and poorer sibling relationship quality (Li et al., 2025). Together, these findings provide initial evidence that siblings can exhibit similar neural responses across decision-making, naturalistic viewing, and affective tasks. However, existing evidence is largely limited to adolescent and adult samples and specific cognitive or affective processes, leaving it unclear whether sibling neural similarity is also evident during childhood when children encounter naturalistic socioemotional information. Furthermore, whether such neural similarity is associated with siblings' socioemotional behavior remains insufficiently understood.

The present study investigates similarity in neural activity during socioemotional processing in siblings compared to unrelated child dyads. To assess this, children completed a naturalistic fMRI movie-watching paradigm designed to evoke socioemotional responses. Neural similarity was examined at both the whole-brain level and within regions previously implicated in socioemotional functioning, including frontal, temporal and limbic areas (Alcalá-López et al., 2018; Buhle et al., 2014). We expected siblings to show greater neural similarity than unrelated children at both the whole-brain level and within regions previously associated with socioemotional functioning (Scatolin, Federici, Dimanova, Saikkonen, et al., 2026). We further investigated whether siblings showed greater similarity in socioemotional behavior, hypothesizing that siblings would exhibit greater behavioral similarity than unrelated children. Finally, we examined whether neural similarity was associated with two metrics of socioemotional functioning within dyads: the degree of behavioral similarity between children and their overall level of socioemotional competence. By integrating neural and behavioral measures, this study aims to establish whether familial resemblance extends to neural responses during socioemotional experiences for siblings and whether these neural similarities are associated with children's socioemotional functioning.

Results

fMRI and behavioral data were collected from 120 children (55 girls; age range: 6-14 years), 69 of whom were siblings. These participants formed 39 sibling dyads, specifically 13 female-female, 13 male-male, and 13 mixed-sex dyads. Of these, 30 families contributed one sibling dyad each, while three families contributed three dyads each. Age differences between siblings ranged from 0 (three twin dyads) to 5.83 years. All participants completed a 12-minute, 29-second naturalistic moviewatching fMRI paradigm (Scatolin, Federici, Dimanova, Borbás, et al., 2026) featuring socioemotional content and promotional material from the film Inside Out and related promotional material (Docter & Del Carmen, 2015; Pixar, 2015a Pixar, , 2015b)). Participants also completed questionnaires assessing emotion regulation and socially competent behavior.

Sibling functional neural similarity across the whole brain

To quantify functional neural similarity, we computed the temporal correspondence of neural responses between children during the full movie-watching paradigm. Mean response time series were extracted for each participant from 126 cortical and subcortical regions. Pearson's correlations between regional time series were computed for all 4696 eligible child dyads. Correlation coefficients were z-scored within each region to ensure cross-regional comparability, and wholebrain functional neural similarity for each dyad was derived as a voxel-weighted average of these zscored regional similarity scores (see Methods).

We then tested whether sibling dyads showed greater whole-brain functional neural similarity relative to unrelated dyads. Dyad-level binary logistic regression models were estimated using cluster-robust standard errors to account for the non-independence of observations arising from children contributing to multiple dyads. Dyad relatedness (siblings vs. unrelated) was modeled as the outcome, with whole-brain neural similarity as the predictor.

Sibling dyads exhibited significantly greater whole-brain functional neural similarity than unrelated dyads (β = 0.53, 95% CI [0.16, 0.90], p = .0054, odds ratio: 1.70, 95% CI [1.17, 2.46]). Fig. 1 shows the distributions of whole-brain functional neural similarity for sibling and unrelated dyads. Boxplots, scatterplots, and density plots show the distributions of whole-brain functional neural similarity for sibling and unrelated dyads, calculated as the voxel-weighted average of z-scored regional correlation coefficients. Siblings showed significantly greater wholebrain functional neural similarity during socioemotional processing than unrelated children.

We next examined whether mean dyadic age and within-dyad age difference were associated with whole-brain functional neural similarity. Cluster-robust linear regressions were estimated with whole-brain functional neural similarity as the outcome and mean dyadic age and within-dyad age difference as predictors. The outcome and predictors were z-scored. Within sibling dyads, whole-brain functional neural similarity tended to decrease with increasing age difference (β = -0.30, 95% CI [-0.61, 0.01], p = .054) and increase with mean dyadic age (β = 0.26, 95% CI [-0.02, 0.53], p = .064), although neither association reached statistical significance. To assess whether these patterns were specific to siblings, we estimated corresponding models across all eligible dyads, including both siblings and unrelated pairs. Neural similarity decreased significantly with increasing age difference (β = -0.09, 95% CI [-0.15, -0.03], p = .005), whereas the positive association with mean dyadic age did not reach significance (β = 0.10, 95% CI [-0.01, 0.22], p = .076).

Considering the effects of age on neural similarity, we conducted a sensitivity analysis including mean dyadic age and age difference within dyads as covariates. Siblings still exhibited greater whole-brain functional neural similarity than unrelated dyads (β = 0.57, 95% CI [0.21, 0.94], p = .0019).

As an exploratory follow-up analysis, we examined whether the difference in whole-brain neural similarity between sibling and unrelated dyads depended on dyad sex composition. Clusterrobust linear regressions were estimated with z-scored whole-brain functional neural similarity as the outcome and dyad relatedness (siblings vs. unrelated), sex composition (same-sex vs. mixedsex), and their interaction as predictors. The interaction between relatedness and sex composition was not significant (β = -0.20, 95% CI [-1.02, 0.63], p = .64). However, given the small number of mixed-sex (n = 13) and same-sex (n = 26) sibling dyads contributing to this contrast, the analysis may have had limited statistical power. The nonsignificant interaction should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of equivalent effects across dyad types.

Sibling functional neural similarity during socioemotional processing in regions of interest

We next examined whether sibling dyads exhibited greater functional neural similarity during socioemotional processing than unrelated dyads within predefined regions of interest (ROI). ROIwise binary logistic regression models with cluster-robust standard errors were estimated for 58 regions implicated in socioemotional processing, encompassing frontal and temporal cortical areas as well as subcortical regions (Buhle et al., 2014; Lindquist et al., 2012; see Fig. 2 for the complete ROI list). In each model, dyad relatedness (sibling vs. unrelated) served as the binary outcome and regional functional neural similarity as the predictor. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied across the 58 ROIs, with an adjusted significance threshold of pFDR < .05. No covariates were included in the models.

At the uncorrected threshold (p < .05), siblings exhibited greater functional neural similarity than unrelated children across several prefrontal, temporal, and limbic regions. However, only three regions survived FDR correction: right supplementary motor area (β = 0.49, 95% CI [0.19, 0.79], p = .0014, pFDR = .0411), left olfactory gyrus (β = 0.54, 95% CI [0.20, 0.88], p = .0018, pFDR = .0411), and the medial orbital part of the left superior frontal gyrus (β = 0.51, 95% CI [0.18, 0.83], p = .0021, pFDR = .0411). Fig 2 displays regional effect estimates and distinguishes findings that survived FDR correction from those reaching only uncorrected significance. Sensitivity analyses accounting for mean dyadic age and age difference within dyads identified the same three regions after FDR correction (see Supplement).

Association between sibling neural and behavioral similarities

We next examined whether the observed similarity in socioemotional neural processing was associated with socioemotional behavior. First, to identify socioemotional behaviors in which siblings showed similarities, we tested the associations between siblings' self-reported emotion regulation and socially competent behavior. As siblings are considered indistinguishable dyads, we used double-entry Spearman's correlations (see Methods). Siblings showed small, positive, but non-significant correlations in emotion regulation (rho = .255, p = .11) and socially competent behavior (rho = .301, p = .060).

We next examined whether neural similarity in the medial orbital part of the left superior frontal gyrus, one of the three regions showing significantly greater similarity among siblings after FDR correction, was associated with socially competent behavior. This region corresponds to the left ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), which has previously been implicated in prosocial behavior (Hiser & Koenigs, 2018; Lockwood et al., 2024), providing a rationale for investigating its association with social competence. Fig. 3A illustrates the relationship between siblings' socially competent behavior and left vmPFC similarity, with a locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (loess) surface overlaid. To statistically test this association, we characterized socially competent behavior within sibling dyads using two complementary metrics: Nearest Neighbors (NN) and Anna Karenina (AK). The NN metric captures similarity between siblings' behavioral scores, irrespective of whether their social competence is high or low, and is calculated from the reversed absolute difference between scores. The AK metric is based on the Anna Karenina hypothesis, named after the opening line of Tolstoy's Anna Karenina: "All happy families are alike; each unhappy family is unhappy in its own way." This hypothesis proposes that individuals with higher levels of a given characteristic exhibit more similar responses, whereas those with lower levels show greater heterogeneity (Finn et al., 2020). In the present study, the metric captures the average level of socially competent behavior within a dyad and is operationalized as the mean of the two siblings' percentile ranks. Both metrics range from 0 to 1. Each metric was entered separately into a clusterrobust linear regression predicting left vmPFC similarity in siblings, with the age difference within each dyad and mean dyadic age as covariates.

The AK model revealed a significant positive association between left vmPFC similarity and higher dyadic average in socially competent behavior within siblings (AK: β = 0.40, SE = 0.16, 95% CI [0.08, 0.71], p = .015, AIC = 112.59, R² = .166), while the NN model revealed no significant association between neural similarity and similarity in socially competent behavior (β = 0.21, SE = 0.12, 95% CI [-0.05, 0.46], p = .107, AIC = 117.14, R² = .063). Moreover, the AK model showed lower AIC (ΔAIC = 4.54) and higher explained variance (ΔR² = .103) relative to the NN model. These findings suggest that greater left vmPFC neural similarity was associated with higher average social competence within sibling dyads, rather than with greater similarity between siblings' behavioral scores. Fig. 3B illustrates this association.

The association between left vmPFC similarity and higher average social competent behavior within dyads, indexed by the AK metric, was also observed across the entire sample of children (β = 0.091, SE = 0.03, 95% CI [0.024, 0.157], p = .008, AIC = 13203.2, R² = .015), although the estimated association was smaller and the model explained less variance than in the sibling-only analyses. The same association shown in two dimensions: greater dyad-average socially competent behavior within sibling dyads, indexed by the Anna Karenina (AK) metric (Finn et al., 2020), was significantly associated with greater left vmPFC neural similarity.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated whether siblings show greater functional neural similarity during socioemotional processing compared to unrelated children and how such similarity relates to socioemotional behavior. Using a naturalistic movie-watching paradigm, we found that sibling dyads exhibited significantly higher whole-brain functional neural similarity than unrelated children, suggesting that familial relatedness is associated with greater similarity in neural responses during socioemotional processing. Greater age differences between dyads were associated with lower neural similarity across sibling and unrelated dyads, suggesting that neural similarity varies with age proximity and that this association is not necessarily specific to sibling relationships. Regionally, increased neural similarity in siblings was observed across several prefrontal, temporal, and limbic areas at the uncorrected threshold, with effects remaining significant after correction for multiple comparisons in the right supplementary motor area, left olfactory gyrus and medial orbital part of the left superior frontal gyrus, which corresponds to the left ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC). Finally, greater neural similarity in the left vmPFC was associated with higher average social competence within siblings and unrelated children's dyads.

Siblings exhibited greater neural similarity than unrelated children, suggesting that they may perceive and interpret socioemotional stimuli in a similar manner (Parkinson et al., 2018). Such similarity has previously been observed between friends and between parents and children (Parkinson et al., 2018; Scatolin, Federici, Dimanova, Saikkonen, et al., 2026). This similarity may reflect the joint contribution of genetic and environmental influences, given that siblings share on average half of their segregating genetic variation, grow up in overlapping family and social environments, and serve as sources of social modelling throughout development (Bralten et al., 2021; Keller et al., 2024; Marek et al., 2026; Plomin et al., 2012; Voorpostel et al., 2007). Within sibling dyads, whole-brain neural similarity tended to be greater among children closer in age and with higher mean dyadic age. Across all dyads, greater age differences were associated with lower neural similarity, suggesting that age-related variation in neural similarity may extend beyond sibling relationships and may reflect the greater correspondence of neural responses in children at similar neurodevelopmental stages (Moraczewski et al., 2018). The sex composition of sibling dyads was not significantly associated with whole-brain neural similarity, although our sample was relatively small and may have had limited statistical power to detect such associations. In the regions of interest analyses, siblings showed greater similarity in several prefrontal, temporal, and limbic areas implicated in socioemotional processing at the uncorrected threshold. After correction for multiple comparisons, three regions remained statistically significant, including the right supplementary motor area (SMA), the left olfactory gyrus, and the medial orbital part of the left superior frontal gyrus, corresponding to the vmPFC. Although traditionally associated with motor planning and action control (Nachev et al., 2008), the SMA has also been implicated in the observation of others' actions (Caspers et al., 2010) and empathy-related processing, particularly in response to others' pain (Jauniaux et al., 2019). The olfactory gyrus is located in close proximity to orbitofrontal regions, which are involved in affective and reward-related processing (Rolls, Cheng, et al., 2020), while the vmPFC is implicated in several processes, such as emotion recognition and regulation, reward processing, and decision making (Hiser & Koenigs, 2018).

In addition to neural similarity, we considered whether siblings also resembled one another in socioemotional functioning, specifically in emotion regulation skills and socially competent behavior. Siblings showed positive but statistically non-significant correlations in both emotion regulation skills and socially competent behavior. Given the relatively small number of sibling dyads and the resulting limited statistical power, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Nevertheless, sibling similarities and differences in socioemotional functioning may reflect a combination of shared and child-specific developmental experiences. While siblings share aspects of their broader family environment, individual differences in socioemotional functioning may be shaped by child-specific factors, including temperament, personal stressors, and unique parentchild or peer relationships, which differ even within the same family (Morris et al., 2007; Plomin & Daniels, 2011; Shewark & Blandon, 2015; Whiteman et al., 2009). Age differences between siblings may further contribute to variability in emotion regulation, as regulatory strategies evolve across childhood and adolescence alongside changes in cognitive abilities, emotional understanding, and social experiences (Zeman et al., 2006; Zimmermann & Iwanski, 2014). At the same time, shared socialization experiences may contribute to similarities in socioemotional behavior. Prosocial and socially competent behaviors are shaped through repeated socialization experiences and are often reinforced across family, school, and peer contexts (Busching & Krahé, 2020; Eisenberg et al., 2015; Wong et al., 2021). This may be particularly relevant during childhood, when siblings are likely to spend substantial time in overlapping family and social environments. Such shared experiences may promote the internalization of similar social norms and contribute to comparable patterns of socially competent behavior (De Vries et al., 2009; Grusec & Hastings, 2015; Hughes et al., 2018; McHale et al., 2012).

Beyond examining neural and behavioral similarity separately, we investigated whether the two were related. Of the three brain regions showing greater neural similarity among siblings, we focused on the left vmPFC given its established role in social cognition and behavior. Meta-analytic evidence highlights the vmPFC's involvement in mentalizing (Molenberghs et al., 2016; Schurz et al., 2014) and morality (Fumagalli & Priori, 2012), while recent findings demonstrate its importance for prosocial behavior (Lockwood et al., 2024). Based on this functional relevance, we examined whether similarity in siblings' vmPFC responses was associated with socially competent behavior, considering both the degree of behavioral similarity between siblings and their average level of social competence. Our findings revealed no significant association between left vmPFC neural similarity and the degree of behavioral resemblance between siblings, as assessed using the Nearest Neighbors (NN) metric. In contrast, greater vmPFC neural similarity was associated with higher average levels of socially competent behavior within sibling dyads, as indexed by the Anna Karenina (AK) metric (Finn et al., 2020). This finding is consistent with the Anna Karenina hypothesis, which proposes that individuals with higher levels of a given characteristic exhibit more similar responses, whereas those with lower levels may show more heterogeneous responses. Although the AK model showed a stronger descriptive fit than the NN model, the analyses did not establish a statistically significant difference between the two associations. Nevertheless, these findings suggest that the average level of social competence within sibling dyads may be relevant to understanding variation in vmPFC neural similarity, beyond the degree to which siblings resemble one another behaviorally.

Importantly, the association between left vmPFC similarity and average social competence was not restricted to sibling dyads. Across the entire sample, unrelated children's dyads with higher average levels of socially competent behavior also exhibited greater similarity in left vmPFC responses, although the estimated association was smaller and the model explained less variance than in the sibling-only analysis. These descriptive differences should be interpreted cautiously, as the analyses did not directly test whether the association differed between sibling and unrelated dyads.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the association between vmPFC neural similarity and socially competent behavior may extend beyond familial relatedness. Given the established role of the vmPFC in social evaluation, mentalizing, moral cognition, and prosocial decision-making (Amodio & Frith, 2006; Lockwood et al., 2024; Molenberghs et al., 2016; Schurz et al., 2014), one possible interpretation is that higher average social competence within a dyad is associated with greater convergence in how children process socioemotional information. However, the present analyses cannot determine whether this neural correspondence reflects similar interpretations, attentional processes, or other aspects of socioemotional information processing.

Moreover, greater neural similarity does not necessarily indicate more adaptive processing, highlighting the importance of considering the behavioral context in which neural similarity occurs.

Future research should therefore investigate the psychological processes underlying these associations and examine whether, and under which conditions, neural similarity relates to adaptive or maladaptive patterns of socioemotional functioning.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, we did not assess the quality of the sibling relationship. Sibling warmth, conflict, emotional closeness, and time spent together may influence socialization and shared experiences and could therefore contribute to neural and behavioral similarity. Incorporating measures of sibling relationship quality in future studies may help determine whether greater relational closeness or more frequent interactions are associated with stronger neural similarity. Second, although the overall sample included 120 children, the number of sibling dyads (39) was relatively small, limiting statistical power for dyad-level analyses, particularly when examining behavioral associations and differences between sibling subgroups. Future studies with larger sibling samples would allow for a more detailed investigation of variability across sibling dyads. Third, the cross-sectional design limits conclusions about how neural and behavioral similarity develops over time. It remains unclear whether similarities in neural processing contribute to socioemotional behavior, whether shared experiences and behaviors shape neural responses over time, or whether these processes mutually influence one another. Longitudinal studies following siblings across development will be important for examining how neural similarity emerges and changes throughout childhood and adolescence. Finally, the present design does not allow genetic and environmental contributions to sibling similarity to be disentangled. Biological siblings share both genetic variation and aspects of their family environment, making it difficult to determine the extent to which the observed neural similarities and associations with behavioral functioning reflect genetic relatedness, shared experiences, or their interplay. Moreover, we did not assess specific shared and non-shared environmental experiences, such as family, school, and broader social contexts, which may contribute differently to similarity across sibling dyads. Future studies incorporating more detailed measures of these environmental experiences, as well as designs including twins, adopted siblings, or other family members, could help clarify the relative contributions of genetic and environmental factors to sibling similarity.

Conclusion

The present study provides evidence that siblings show greater similarity in neural responses during socioemotional processing than unrelated children, and that greater neural similarity is associated with higher average levels of socially competent behavior within child dyads. Specifically, siblings showed greater functional neural similarity during socioemotional processing than unrelated children across the whole brain and in specific regions implicated in socioemotional processing. Furthermore, greater similarity in left vmPFC responses was associated with higher average social competence within sibling dyads, an association also observed across the full sample of child dyads.

Together, these findings suggest that socioemotional functioning and the neural systems involved in socioemotional processing are embedded within familial and broader social contexts, with siblings representing an important context for investigations as some of the earliest and most enduring social partners across the lifespan. Considering siblings alongside other family relationships may provide important insights into the processes involved in brain and socioemotional development, resilience, and adaptation across childhood and adolescence.

Method Participants

Children from 96 families were recruited through letters, word-of-mouth, schools, and educational events. After enrolment, families completed an initial screening by email or telephone and were invited to an in-person session at the Children's Hospital in Zurich. Participants included in the study had sufficient German proficiency to understand the study instructions and to complete the neuroimaging procedures and were reimbursed with a voucher of their choice. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Canton of Zurich (BASEC-Nr. 2021-02096).

In total, 160 children (69 girls and 89 boys) enrolled. All participants included in the analyses (see Procedure) had average or above average IQ scores (see Table 1). Regarding parental educational attainment, 75.6% had a high level (short-cycle tertiary to doctoral level, ISCED 5-8), 13.4% had a medium level (upper secondary to post-secondary non-tertiary education, ISCED 3-4), 9.8% had a low level (lower secondary education, ISCED 0-2) according to ISCED 2011 standards, and 1.2% did not report this information. Although all families resided in Switzerland, their reported countries of origin varied: 31.7% identified Switzerland, 20.7% another European country, 19.5% Switzerland and another European country, 14.6% Switzerland and a non-European country, 4.9% a European and a non-European country, 1.2% a non-European country, and 7.3% did not report their country of origin.

Procedure

Behavioral Assessments

Participants completed behavioral assessments administered in person by trained research staff.

Behavioral data were processed in R (version 4.5.1) (R Core Team, 2025). Children completed verbal and non-verbal IQ assessments using the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC-IV) (Petermann & Petermann, 2008). Verbal IQ data were unavailable for four participants: two lacked sufficient language proficiency to complete this assessment, one was unable to continue due to fatigue, and one did not comprehend the task instructions. Children were also assessed using two subscales from the Intelligence and Development Scales (IDS) (Meyer et al., 2009): Emotion Regulation, which assessed children's knowledge of adaptive strategies for regulating negative emotional states across standardized vignettes; and Socially Competent Behavior, which measured children's ability to generate contextually appropriate behavioral responses to depicted social situations. Raw scores on each subscale were converted to age-and sex-normed T-scores according to the IDS normative tables. IDS data were missing for one participant, for whom the administered version did not correspond to the appropriate age range.

Neuroimaging

MRI data were acquired at the Children's Hospital in Zurich using a 3T General Electric Signa MR scanner and a 48-channel head coil. Families 1 to 71 (94 children) were scanned using one scanner gradient configuration and families 72 to 96 (26 children) using another; no family was split across gradient configurations. T1-weighted structural images were acquired using a sagittal 3D inversionrecovery prepared fast spoiled gradient-echo sequence (BRAVO) with the following parameters: field of view = 256 mm, matrix = 256 x 256, 176 sagittal slices, voxel size = 1.0 mm isotropic, inversion time = 450 ms, flip angle = 12°. Functional images were acquired using an echo-planar imaging (EPI) pulse sequence for T2* contrast with the following parameters: field of view = 240 mm, TR = 2203 ms, TE = 20.3 ms, 41 slices, slice thickness = 2 mm, voxel size = 2.5 x 2.5 x 2.0 mm, and 342 volumes. Slices were acquired in interleaved order. Dummy volumes were acquired to allow for signal equilibration but were discarded prior to analyses.

Participants underwent fMRI while watching a movie featuring socioemotional content.

Prior to image acquisition, participants were instructed to remain as still as possible throughout the movie-watching paradigm. The paradigm was presented on an MR-compatible LCD screen, viewed via a mirror mounted on the head coil, and audio was delivered through noise-attenuating headphones. Audio volume was standardized across participants, unless small adjustments were requested. Stimulus onset was synchronized with scanner trigger (TTL) pulses to ensure precise temporal alignment with image acquisition.

Functional neuroimaging data were not acquired for 19 children due to claustrophobia or task interruption. Data of 21 additional children were excluded due to artifacts caused by dental braces (n = 19) or excessive head motion (n = 2; more than 15% censored scans, see Neuroimaging -Preprocessing). This yielded a sample of 120 children, of whom 69 were siblings, corresponding to 39 sibling dyads, for functional neuroimaging analyses.

Analyses

Analyses fMRI preprocessing was conducted using SPM12 (The Wellcome Centre for Human Neuroimaging, 2020) in MATLAB (MathWorks, 2023) and followed a standardized preprocessing pipeline, including realignment and unwarping, co-registration to each participant's structural image, segmentation and normalization to the ICBM152 template in standard space.

Neuroimaging -Preprocessing.

Spatial smoothing was applied using a 6-mm full-width at half-maximum isotropic Gaussian kernel.

Motion-related artifacts and global signal intensity fluctuations were identified using the Children with more than 15% censored scans were excluded from analyses (n = 2).

Functional neural similarity.

To assess functional neural similarity, mean regional response time series were extracted across the entire movie-watching paradigm from 126 distinct anatomical brain regions for each participant using Nilearn in Python. The extraction included all eligible regions from the third edition of the Automatic Anatomical Labeling (AAL3) atlas (Rolls, Huang, et al., 2020), excluding cerebellar and brainstem regions. Censored scans were coded as missing values (NA) in each participant's regional time series. Functional neural similarity was calculated for 4696 eligible child dyads whose members were scanned with same scanner gradient configuration, of which 39 corresponded to sibling dyads.

Pearson's correlations between regional time series were computed for each dyad (Parkinson et al., 2018). Correlation coefficients were z-scored (i.e., z-standardization, in which M = 0 and SD = 1) within each region to ensure cross-regional comparability, and whole-brain functional neural similarity for each dyad was derived as a voxel-weighted average of these z-scored regional similarity scores. Similarity was not computed for dyads scanned in different scanner gradient configurations to prevent potential scanner-related bias.

Behavioral similarity and social competence.

To assess sibling resemblance in Emotion Regulation and Socially Competent Behavior (IDS subscales), we computed double-entry Spearman rank correlations, which are suited to indistinguishable dyads (Kenny et al., 2006). Spearman's rank correlation was used because the distributions of both subscales deviated from normality (Shapiro-Wilk tests across all children with IDS data, n = 119; Emotion Regulation W = 0.84, p < .001; Socially Competent Behavior W = 0.97, p = .023). Within each sibling dyad, the two members were assigned to Participant 1 and Participant 2 labels at random, so these labels carried no substantive meaning. Each dyad was entered twice, once as (Participant 1, Participant 2) and once as (Participant 2, Participant 1), and ranks were computed across the pooled scores of all siblings. Significance was tested by multiplying the correlation by the square root of the number of dyads (N = 39) rather than the number of rows in the doubled data, and treating the result as a Z-test with two-sided p-values (Kenny, 2015).

To examine associations between neural similarity and social competence, we applied two complementary metrics from the brain-behavior similarity framework (Finn et al., 2020). Children's scores on the Socially Competent Behavior scale from the IDS were ranked and transformed into percentile scores (range: 0 to 1). For the Nearest Neighbors (NN) model, dyadic behavioral similarity was computed as one minus the absolute difference between the two children's percentile ranks, such that higher values indicate greater similarity regardless of where the pair fell on the scale (bothhigh and both-low pairs yield equally high similarity). For the Anna Karenina (AK) model, dyadaverage social competence was computed as the mean of the two children's percentile ranks, such that higher values indicate higher average social competence within the dyad, rather than greater similarity between the two children.

Statistical Analyses.

All statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.1) (R Core Team, 2025). Because children contributed to multiple dyads, observations were not independent. All regression models therefore used cluster-robust standard errors with two-way clustering on the identifiers of both dyad members using the R package multiwayvcov (version 1.2.3) (Hagströmer, 2016). Neural similarity and all continuous predictors were z-scored before model fitting.

Sibling specificity of neural similarity.

To test whether sibling dyads showed greater neural similarity than unrelated dyads, we fitted binary logistic regressions with dyad relatedness (sibling = 1, unrelated = 0) as the outcome and z-scored neural similarity as the predictor, with no covariates.

Models were fitted separately for whole-brain similarity and for each of the 58 socioemotional regions of interest. For the regional analyses, p-values were corrected across the 58 regions using the false discovery rate (FDR) at p < .05. Sensitivity analyses including mean dyadic age and age difference within dyads as covariates were conducted.

Age effects. Age effects on neural similarity were assessed with linear regression, with whole-brain neural similarity as the outcome and the age difference between the two children and mean dyadic age entered as predictors. Models were fitted within sibling dyads and across all related and unrelated dyads.

Sex composition.

To explore whether the association between sibling relatedness and neural similarity varied by sex composition, we fitted a linear regression across all dyads with whole-brain neural similarity as the outcome and relatedness (sibling vs. unrelated), sex composition (same-sex vs. mixed-sex), and their interaction as predictors. This analysis was exploratory.

Neural-behavioral similarity.

Associations between neural similarity and social competence were tested using linear regressions, with left vmPFC similarity as the outcome, either NN behavioral similarity or AK dyad-average social competence as the predictor (entered in separate models), and age difference and mean dyadic age as covariates within the sibling dyads. Within siblings, the NN and AK models were compared using Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC) and the proportion of explained variance (R²). To assess whether the AK association was also present across the broader sample, the AK model was fitted across all related and unrelated dyads.
