To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 4 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

A priori power analyses (G*Power 3.1; Faul et al., 2009), indicated that detecting medium effects (d = .50, α = .05, two-tailed mean comparisons) with 80% power required 64 participants per group. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Consent to Participate. All participants provided digital active informed consent before participation in the study. For adolescents under 16 years of age, all legal guardians provided consent for their child's participation.

Short-Term Effects on Parenting and Adolescent Well-Being of a Personalized Digital Parenting Intervention. A Randomized Controlled Trial

Adolescent mental health problems, such as depressive symptoms and anxiety, are rising globally (e.g., McGorry et al., 2024; Inchley et al. 2020). Strengthening parenting might be an underexplored target for preventing adolescent mental health problems (Morris et al., 2021; Restifo & Bögels, 2009). Theoretical work (e.g., Soenens et al., 2017; 2019) proposes that parenting strategies such as nurturing a positive relationship (hereafter: support), considering adolescents' perspective and providing choice (i.e. autonomy support), and establishing clear expectations and consequences (i.e., structure) are vital to adolescents' mental well-being. However, few parenting interventions translate these insights into accessible, evidence-based programs (David et al., 2023; Spencer et al., 2020). Therefore, this study developed and tested a new "PARADOx" digital parenting intervention that aims to strengthen need-supportive parenting practices, improving adolescent mental well-being.

The Need for a Novel Digital Parenting Intervention

Many parenting resources offer advice on parenting adolescents, ranging from books, to professional advice, to smartphone applications. Digital interventions are highly scalable and cost-effective compared to offline interventions (Gentili et al., 2022), making them particularly suited to prevention. However, evidence-based preventive parenting programs in the context of promoting adolescent mental health remain rare (Yap et al., 2016). We are aware of few such programs. First, recent meta-analyses of online parenting interventions (David et al., 2023; Spencer et al., 2020) identified a web-based parenting intervention specifically for adolescent mental health: Partners in Parenting (PiP; Yap et al., 2017). PiP showed promising short-and medium-term effects on parenting practices such as establishing rules and consequences (Yap et al., 2018; 2019). However, adolescents' mental health problems did not significantly decrease compared to controls at 12-month follow-up, and parent-reports did not translate to adolescent perceptions of parenting (Yap et al., 2019).

Second, one recently developed digital intervention successfully improved autonomysupportive practices in mothers whose children were transitioning to secondary school (Duchesne et al., 2025). Yet, child mental well-being was not assessed. Both interventions highlight the potential of accessible digital tools to strengthen parenting during adolescence, yet either did not assess or find an impact on adolescent mental well-being.

The PARADOx Intervention

Inspired by developments (Ramchandani et al., 2023) in domains where digital interventions are upcoming and widely used in treating and preventing adolescent mental health problems (Dietvorst et al., 2024) and personalizing medicine and therapy (Ng & Weisz, 2015), we developed a digital parenting intervention embedded within a 100-day diary study. The intervention includes two key-components beyond daily self-monitoring of parenting and well-being. First, parents gained self-insight into parenting practices through a data-driven feedback report delivered on day 47. Subsequently, in a 6-week advice phase between days 50 and 89, parents received brief parenting tips to enhance need-supportive parenting (structure, autonomy support and support) in daily life. Our intervention was developed through an iterative process described in the https://osf.io/zfcmd/?view_only=d33029036c154278910d030b76c30281 Initial tips were constructed based on theory and focus groups with adolescents and parents, further curated through expert and parent ratings. Moreover, we developed two versions of the intervention: a universal version containing a feedback report with insights into daily parenting practices in an average family and expert-selected tips , and a personalized version consisting of a data-driven feedback report on families' own functioning and a choice between multiple tips. Personalization of content is hypothesized to enhance relevance and effectiveness (August & Gewirtz, 2019; Ng & Weisz, 2015). Theory (e.g., Smith & Thelen, 2003) and empirical research suggest that parenting effects vary greatly across families (Boele et al., 2023; Bülow et al., 2022a). However, we have encountered few parenting interventions tailored to individual families' dynamics and needs.

The Current Study

This randomized controlled trial (RCT) examined whether the PARADOx digital parenting intervention improved parenting practices (proximal outcome) and adolescent wellbeing (distal outcome). Moreover, we explored if the intervention might unintentionally increase overprotection. Overprotection refers to overly controlling and involved parenting (Ryan et al., 2024), which may frustrate adolescents' basis psychological needs and is associated with poorer mental health (e.g., anxiety; de Roo et al., 2020). Because overprotection often stems from good intentions (Segrin et al., 2012) we test whether our intervention risked parents overcorrecting and adopting "too much of a good thing." We considered the intervention successful if need-supportive parenting increased without turning into overprotection, and adolescent's well-being improved. We followed 547 adolescents and 541 parents with 100 daily diaries to test four preregistered hypotheses: • H1: Parents in intervention groups (universal or personalized) show greater increases in need-supportive parenting (structure, autonomy support, and support) than controls.

• H2: Personalized group parents show stronger increases in these parenting practices than parents in the universal group.

• H3: Adolescents in intervention groups show greater improvements in affective wellbeing (increased positive affect, decreased negative affect) than controls.

• H4: Adolescents whose parents received the personalized intervention show stronger gains in well-being than those in the universal group.

Design

This https://tinyurl.com/y9dc2nef single-blinded, three-arm parallel group superiority RCT allocated parents of adolescents to one of three conditions in an approximate 3:1:1 ratio: (1) a control group, a ( 2 The PARADOx intervention was embedded within https://tinyurl.com/y9dc2nef research project (see https://osf.io/zfcmd/?view_only=d33029036c154278910d030b76c30281). A baseline survey (ca. 45 minutes) was distributed via e-mail on October 13 th 2024 to obtain demographic characteristics. From October 21 st , participants received 111 Experience Sampling (ESM; Keijsers et al., 2022) surveys (5-6 times daily, ca. 2 minutes) and 100 daily surveys (ca. 3-5 minutes) on parenting practices and well-being. ESM and daily surveys, including intervention parenting tips, were distributed via Avicenna. Daily diaries were distributed at 20:00, with up to four in-app reminders sent every 30 minutes. Incentives aimed to promote participants' engagement (https://osf.io/zfcmd/?view_only=d33029036c154278910d030b76c30281), through enjoyment, self-insight and awareness of scientific impact (Bülow, Janssen et al., 2025). Financial incentives were up to €105 for adolescents and €55 for parents, conditional upon engagement. Adolescents could also win €25 gift cards.

Randomization and Blinding

Randomization was conducted in two phases. First, via schools participants were recruited exclusively to the control or intervention group to minimize cross-group knowledge.

Recruitment per school was based on practical considerations (e.g., progress of participant enrollment). Subsequently, all parents recruited to the intervention group were assigned to a universal or personalized intervention using an unstratified, unblocked simple randomization procedure via R-script. Participants were not informed of different study conditions and were thus presumed blinded. Recruitment procedures were identical across conditions but researchers were aware for which group they were recruiting.

The PARADOx Parenting Intervention

While the control group engaged only in 100 days of self-monitoring via daily diaries, intervention groups received a universal or personalized version of two additional components (Figure 1): (1) a one-time parenting feedback report via E-mail based on selfmonitoring data and (2) brief parenting tips via the daily diaries three times per week for six weeks. For an extensive description of the intervention, see https://tinyurl.com/y9dc2nef.

Parenting report

Feedback reports were delivered on diary day 47 and were designed to enhance parents' insight into daily parenting practices over the first 21 study days. The universal group received a report summarizing levels of structure, autonomy support and support in an "average" participating family. Parents in the personalized group received a report on their own daily parenting data. The control group received no report.

Measures

Proximal Outcome -Daily Parental Structure

Parental structure was reported by parents and adolescents using two items on a visual analogue scale (VAS) ranging from 0 ("not at all") to 100 ("very much"): one item ("I made it clear what I expected from my child.") from the Parents as Social Context Questionnaire (PASCQ) -short version (Lang et al. 2021) and one item newly developed with international experts on parenting and self-determination theory ("I made it clear that I had confidence in how my child handled things"). Adolescent items were identical but rephrased. Parent items correlated poorly at the between-person level (rpre-intervention = .21, rpost-intervention = .31) but moderately for adolescent-reports (rpre-intervention = .64, rpost-intervention = .63).

Proximal Outcome -Daily Parental Autonomy Support

Autonomy supportive parenting was assessed with two items by parents ("I let my child make their own plans.", "I considered my child's opinion.") and adolescents on a visual analogue scale ranging from 0 ("not at all") to 100 ("very much"). Items were based on a previously constructed scale (Van der Kaap-Deeder et al., 2017) and have been validated in prior research (Bülow et al., 2022a). Items correlated highly for parent-reports (rpre-intervention = .76, rpost-intervention = .79) and adolescent-reports (rpre-intervention = .82, rpost-intervention = .80).

Proximal Outcome -Parental Support

Daily parental support was reported by parents and adolescents with eight items adapted and translated from two forms of the Network of Relationships Inventory: the Social Provisions Version -Short Form (NRI-SRV; Furman & Buhrmester, 1985) and the Behavioral Systems Version -Short Form (NRI-BSV; Furman & Buhrmester, 2009), each item relating to a specific type of parental support (providing a safe haven and secure base, companionship, emotional support, affection, reassurance of worth, instrumental aid or nurturance). An example item is "I helped my child." Adolescent items were similar but rephrased. Answers ranged from 0 ("not at all") to 100 ("very much"). The scale demonstrated good internal consistency for parent-reports (ωpre-intervention = .82; ωpost-intervention = .89) and adolescent-reports (ωpre-intervention = .82; ωpost-intervention = .84).

Proximal Outcome -Overprotection

Daily overprotection was measured with three items of the Momentary Overparenting Scale (MOP; Luijk et al., 2023), adjusted to match a daily diary design ( "I interfered with my child's life", "I (unnecessarily) worried about my child", "I tried to help my child even though it wasn't necessary").

Adolescents and parents rated items between 0 ("not at all") and 100 ("very much"). The child-report version has previously demonstrated good psychometric properties for daily studies (Luijk et al., 2023). This study found excellent internal consistency for parent-reports (ωpre-intervention = .87; ωpost-intervention = .84) and adolescent-reports (ωpre-intervention = .87; ωpost-intervention = .88).

Distal Outcome -Adolescent Affective Well-being

Adolescents reported daily on their positive and negative affect using a previously translated, shortened, and validated (Bülow et al., 2022a; 2022b) version of the Dutch Positive and Negative Affect Schedule for Children (PANAS-C NL; Ebesutani et al., 2012).

Scales ranged from 0 ("Not at all") to 100 ("Very much"). Positive affect was assessed using two items ("Joyful", "Happy") which correlated highly (rpre-intervention = .94, rpost-intervention = .92). Negative affect was measured using four items ("Mad", "Afraid", "Sad", "Irritated") and demonstrated excellent internal consistency (ωpre-intervention = .97; ωpost-intervention = .97).

A seventh single-item measure "Stressed" was added.

Statistical Methods

Sample Size

A priori power analyses (G*Power 3.1; Faul et al., 2009), indicated that detecting medium effects (d = .50, α = .05, two-tailed mean comparisons) with 80% power required 64 participants per group. Previous research evaluating a digital parenting intervention identified such a medium effect on parenting for the interaction term between an intervention and control group (d = .51, Yap et al., 2019). A meta-analysis on online parenting programs found large effects on positive parenting (e.g., d = 1.00; Spencer et al., 2020). Hence, we expected medium effects on need-supportive parenting.

Anticipating 30% dropout (Van Roekel et al., 2019), we aimed to include 100 parentadolescent dyads per intervention group, and 300 dyads for the control group. Achieved sample sizes (Figure 2) exceeded requirements, enabling identification of smaller effects (d = .34) comparing personalized (N = 99 parents) and universal (N = 95) interventions, and any intervention (pooled) with controls (d = .27, N = 242, see Online Resource 1, Table S1).

Analyses

To test our four hypotheses, we compared mean level changes in parenting and adolescent well-being between intervention groups, using a preregistered multilevel structural equation model per outcome in Mplus 8.11 (Muthen & Muthen, 2017). A binary predictor (time) assessed linear change from pre-intervention (day 34-44 of diary data aggregated) to post-intervention (day 90-100 aggregated). Two binary between-person predictors captured group differences in changes in parenting practices (H1, H2) and adolescent affective wellbeing (H3, H4). The first predictor indicated being part of the control (0) or any intervention group (1; pooled), the second predictor indicated the intervention (universal (0) versus personalized (1) group. Hypotheses were confirmed when group membership predicted the average within-group slope (p < .05) in the hypothesized direction (see Online Resource 1, Figure S1). Although such multilevel models are robust against potential baseline differences (Múthen & Múthen, 2017), we explore baseline mean differences between intervention groups and controls on parenting and adolescent affect.

Change During Intervention Period.

During the intervention phase (day 50 onwards; Figure 1), parent-reported autonomy support and support declined significantly for the control group, but not intervention groups. Adolescents in the universal intervention group reported increased parental structure. No changes in overprotection were observed during the intervention phase.

Preregistered Models on Intervention Effects (H1 and H2).

To identify intervention effects, we assessed whether parenting in the intervention groups (pooled) improved more strongly than controls (H1), and whether parents in the personalized intervention improved more strongly than the universal intervention group (H2). Group comparisons (Table 3) identified no significant differences between intervention groups (pooled) and controls (H1) (-.04 < d < .13; p-values >.063). Furthermore, no group differences were found between the personalized and universal intervention groups (H2) (-.01 < d < .02; p-values >.807) in parent-reported parenting. Hence, parents who received an intervention did not improve more strongly in need-supportive parenting practices than controls and a personalized intervention (versus universal) did not change the trajectory.

Sensitivity.

As sensitivity analyses, we excluded parents who did not view any tip (5.5% -13.9% of parents; Online Resource 1, Tables S5.1, S5.2) and compensated for ceiling and floor effects (n = 9 parents removed; Online Resource 1, Tables S6.1, S6.2). Similar findings emerged as the main analyses.

While structure increased according to adolescents in the universal intervention group, comparing group trajectories yielded no significant group differences.

From baseline to post-intervention, adolescents reported a decrease in positive affect and increase in negative affect, but no notable changes in stress (Table 2, Figure 4).

Examining changes during the intervention showed that only adolescents in the universal intervention group demonstrated a decrease in positive affect and increase in negative affect, but not stress.

We assessed whether adolescents in both intervention groups (pooled) would report greater increases in affective well-being than controls (H3) and whether the personalized intervention group would improve more strongly than the universal intervention group (H4). Comparing controls with the pooled intervention groups yielded no group differences for changes in positive affect, negative affect or stress (-.11 < d < .09; p-values > .087, Table 3). Discussion

Parents are vital to adolescent well-being (e.g. Soenens et al., 2017; 2019) but digital evidence-based parenting programs for supporting adolescent well-being remain scarce (David et al., 2023; Spencer et al., 2020). This preregistered RCT investigated short-term effects of our novel PARADOx digital parenting intervention, encompassing a data-driven feedback report based on daily self-monitoring, and parenting tips. On average across the sample, parents became more critical of their parenting, but adolescents appraised the needsupportiveness of their parents increasingly positive. The harmful practice of overprotection declined according to parents and adolescents. However, the main hypotheses that datadriven feedback reports and parenting tips (personalized or universal) would have benefits above and beyond self-monitoring were not supported. These findings and their implications are discussed below.

The Feasibility of Online Parenting Interventions

Online parenting programs to promote adolescent well-being are generally more accessible and scalable than traditional, offline interventions (Gentili et al., 2022) and can facilitate personalization (August & Gewirtz, 2019), but remain rare (e.g. David et al., 2023).

Previous research demonstrated the feasibility of self-monitoring daily family processes among 15 families (Swendeman et al., 2020). Following more than 500 families for 100 days, we demonstrate its applicability at a larger scale. Moreover, parents in intervention groups displayed higher engagement than controls, suggesting a motivational element of learning about yourself, and your family system (Bülow, et al, 2025). Hence, mass prevention programs for parents can be tailored via data-driven approaches with theory guided advice.

No Added Benefit Beyond Self-Monitoring

We hypothesized that families receiving the intervention would display stronger improvements in need-supportive parenting (H1) and adolescent well-being (H3) than controls who only monitored their (family) functioning. However, no group differences were detected. Effects of our feedback report and parenting tips beyond self-monitoring may have been too small to detect for several reasons. First, given the complex, dynamic nature of parenting, interventions may require time to compound into genuine change in parenting, and especially to affect adolescent well-being. Indeed, effects on parenting may appear months after a parenting intervention ended (e.g., Yap et al., 2019). Second, because tips were embedded in the daily diaries, their availability was limited and their application depended on parents' engagement. Third, mean levels of parenting practices, particularly autonomy support, were high. Some participants may have felt little need to engage in a parenting intervention, which is supported by the finding that mainly structure improved (the lowest scoring practice). Future research may help determine for which families this intervention may be (most) effective, for instance those with lower levels of need-supportive parenting.

No Added Effect of Personalization

Contrary to our hypotheses, the personalized intervention did not outperform the universal intervention in strengthening need-supportive parenting (H2) or adolescent affect (H4). However, adolescents in the universal intervention group, but not the personalized intervention, decreased in positive affect and increased in negative affect, reflecting a potential detrimental side-effect of the universal intervention. Exploring potential harms and their mechanisms could be of importance to intervention research (O'Donohue, 2026), especially given the scalability of digital parenting interventions. Personalization may explain why adolescent affective well-being decreased specifically for the universal intervention.

Across interventions, parents were asked to apply tips they felt matched their family's needs.

Misaligned advice may frustrate adolescents' psychological needs, reducing affective wellbeing (Rodríguez-Meirinhos et al., 2020). Personalization potentially promoted parents' sense of autonomy and competence to apply tips they felt matched their family, and decline tips which did not. Parents in the personalized intervention could choose between multiple tips.

Choice can promote intrinsic motivation, effort, performance, and sense of competence (Patall et al., 2008). The personalized feedback report may have further aided parents' sense of agency and competence in selecting tips by providing insight into their own family dynamics. Conversely, in the universal intervention the report on average parenting practices and no choice between tips may have felt more normative and limited parents' sense of autonomy in applying or declining tips. Hence, parents in the universal intervention may have felt pressured to implement tips they thought did not (optimally) match them or their child, potentially frustrating their child's needs and harming affective well-being. Therefore, we tentatively suggest that personalization may still be an important intervention component for averting harmful side-effects. Future research might determine if, and which aspects of personalization are most salient for averting harms, for whom, why, and when.Parents

Become More Critical, Adolescents More Positive

An unexpected finding of the study is that across the sample, parents generally became more critical of their parenting (i.e., decreased autonomy support and support) whereas adolescents appraised their parents more positively (i.e., increased parental support and structure). Adolescents reporting more parental support than parents themselves is not unexpected (Mastrotheodoros et al., 2019). Potentially, these findings reflect increased parental awareness rather than deteriorations in need-supportive parenting. Earlier work suggests that daily reflection makes parents more aware of their parenting, including perceived shortcomings, but could make adolescents more appreciative of their parents (Swendeman et al., 2020).

In essence, adolescents' perceptions of their parents as need-supportive may be more essential to their well-being than parents' actual behavior (Soenens et al., 2017). Accordingly, item-specific analyses suggest that adolescents increasingly approached their parents as confidants and sources of support, suggesting children's agency in shaping parental support in general (Šutić et al., 2025 (Šutić et al., , Boele et al., 2023) ) and in intervention settings (Novick et al., 2023). This may reflect changes in parenting, or adolescents' increased awareness of their needs or their parents' availability due to their own reflections. Simultaneously, only parents in the control group reported further decreases in autonomy support and support in the intervention phase. This tentatively suggests that the intervention halted an overly critical appraisal of parenting. Therefore, we suggest that feedback might still be important in addition to self-monitoring to increase parental confidence.

Decreases in Overprotection

Overprotection is characterized by overly controlling and involved parenting (e.g., Ryan et al., 2024) and associated with adolescent mental health problems such as symptoms of depression and anxiety (e.g. de Roo et al., 2020). One potential risk of parenting advice we had anticipated, is parents becoming overly involved. Observational data suggest the opposite: parents and adolescents reported a significant decrease in overprotection from baseline to post-intervention, suggesting that daily reflection and parenting advice might reduce this harmful practice.

Limitations and Strengths

This three-arm RCT, incorporating parent and adolescent reports, allowed us to assess a new digital parenting intervention, and compare universal and lightly personalized intervention formats. However, some limitations warrant mention. First, allocation of schools to recruitment for the control or intervention condition was not fully random but influenced by success of recruitment, potentially introducing some influence of school-level characteristics on recruitment between study conditions. However, we found no baseline differences between groups where we found significant change. Additionally, our measure of structure correlated poorly for parent-reports. Future research might investigate differences in how parents apply the expression of expectations and confidence.

Second, personalization was light. Parents received insight into their own family dynamics, but advice was not tailored to family dynamics, instead relying on parents' expertise for choosing tips. Future studies might explore the effect of true personalization of content, for instance based on personal learning objectives. Third, compared to programs which found medium-sized effects (e.g., Yap et al., 2019), dosage of our advice was small.

From a dynamic systems perspective, such light advice may be insufficient to change stable, rooted habits (Verplanken & Orbell, 2022). However, dosage was in line with light digital lifestyle interventions in other domains which demonstrated potential for behavior change (Dao et al., 2021). Future research might investigate the optimal dosage of advice, specifically within the parenting domain.

Despite the large sample size (Spencer et al., 2020), this study may have been underpowered to find intervention effects smaller than in related studies (e.g., Yap et al., 2019). This is supported by slightly positive but non-significant trends in for example parentreported structure and autonomy support in intervention groups compared to controls.

Potentially, our control group, which monitored parenting and well-being daily, may have been too active to find the expected effects. A recent meta-analysis on digital parenting interventions highlighted that RCT's with an active control group demonstrated notably smaller effects on parents and children compared to wait-list controls (David et al., 2023).

Moreover, due to the quantity of analyses run, which risks Type I errors, exploratory findings should be carefully interpreted and replicated in future studies. Finally, despite recruitment targeting a diverse sample, the majority of our parents were highly educated, limiting generalizability to other families.

Conclusion

This study tested a novel digital parenting intervention, "PARADOx" to promote adolescent well-being among 500 parent-adolescent dyads, combining self-monitoring through 100 daily diaries, data-driven feedback, and tips on need-supportive parenting.
