Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Background: Existing evidence on co-occurring binge eating and cannabis use is preliminary, and it is unclear how common the combination is or how much burden it carries. We examined both questions among college students, who are high risk for both behaviors.

Method: We pooled ten annual cohorts (2015-16 to 2024-25) of the Healthy Minds Study (577,962 students, 807 campus-year clusters). Eating pathology was measured with the SCOFF loss-of-control item. A subsample of 17,114 students also completed the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE-Q). Estimates were weighted for non-response and clustered by campus.

Results: Across cohorts, loss of control over eating was reported by 39.0% of past-30-day cannabis users and 30.7% of non-users (adjusted OR = 1.43, p < .001). Severe SCOFF screening (score of 4 or higher) rose from 3.5% in non-users to 7.1% in near-daily users (adjusted OR per step = 1.22, p < .001). Weekly binge eating (EDE-Q) was more common among users (23.4% vs. 15.3%; adjusted OR = 1.68, p < .001). Overall, 8.3% of all students reported both behaviors. Students reporting both had higher odds of 10 out of 11 correlates.

Relative excess risk due to the interaction between cannabis use and loss of control over eating was positive for depression, self-injury, suicidal ideation, and academic harm (odds ratio based; p < .001).

Conclusions: Binge eating is common among students who use cannabis and is more severe at higher frequencies of cannabis use. Importantly, co-occurrence carried higher odds of depression, suicidality, self-injury, and academic harm. Thus, these cross-sectional findings underscore the need for dedicated longitudinal research.

Introduction

Binge eating is characterized by recurrent episodes in which an individual eats an unusually large amount of food and experiences a subjective sense of loss of control over eating. It is the defining feature of binge-eating disorder (BED) and a core feature of bulimia nervosa (BN) in the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5; Association, 2013), wherein binge episodes in BN are followed by compensatory behaviors (e.g., self-induced vomiting). By early adulthood, 5.5% to 17.9% of young women and 0.6% to 2.4% of young men have experienced a DSM-5 eating disorder (Silén & Keski-Rahkonen, 2022). Regular binge eating is more common still, and only a minority of individuals who binge eat meet criteria for BED (Keski-Rahkonen, 2021).

Binge eating also tends to occur alongside substance use (Bahji et al., 2019). Notably, among 414,299 college students, eating disorder indications were associated with every substance use outcome examined, including cannabis misuse (Qeadan et al., 2023). Because these findings do not establish direction, two pathways are plausible. First, cannabis use may promote binge eating, as cannabis directly alters appetite and the reward value of food. In a randomized placebo-controlled study, for example, oral delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) increased liking and wanting of high-calorie foods and the amount of milkshake consumed (Weltens et al., 2019). Further, animal models of binge eating show reduced endocannabinoid and CB1 receptor signaling in the nucleus accumbens and prefrontal cortex (Bourdy & Befort, 2023). As such, cannabis may amplify the rewarding pull of palatable food in individuals who are already prone to loss of control. Second, binge eating may promote cannabis use. In bingeeating disorder, negative affect and guilt rise before binge episodes and fall afterward (Schaefer et al., 2020). Distress of this kind may motivate the use of other coping strategies, and many young adults use cannabis to reduce psychological distress (Wallis et al., 2022).

Although cannabis may plausibly intensify binge eating and vice versa, direct evidence in young people is limited. For example, in a community sample of 1,568 emerging adults, past-year cannabis use was associated with binge eating among women but not men (Elran-Barak et al., 2025). Importantly, the association among women persisted after adjustment for depressive symptoms. Further, among 165 treatment-seeking adults with weekly binge eating, about 23% reported cannabis use in the past three months, and cannabis users did not differ from nonusers in eating disorder or depression severity but reported more alcohol use and problems (Wilkinson et al., 2024). Thus, three questions remain unresolved: 1) how common binge eating is among cannabis-using young adults, 2) whether the association strengthens with frequency of cannabis use, and 3) whether the combination carries more burden than either behavior alone.

College students are an especially informative population for these questions for three reasons. First, they are concentrated in the window in which both conditions emerge, as emerging adulthood centers on ages 18 to 25 (Arnett, 2000). Specifically, cannabis use is highest in this age group and continues to rise (Hinckley et al., 2024). Further, the median age of onset for BED falls in the late teens to early twenties (Kessler et al., 2013). Second, both exposures are changing on campuses: eating disorder symptoms increased among US college students from 2013 to 2020 (Romano et al., 2022). Notably, the share at risk on the SCOFF eating disorder screen (Morgan et al., 1999) rose from 15% to 28% between 2013 and 2020 to 2021 (Daly & Costigan, 2022). Cannabis use also increased more in states that legalized recreational use (Bae & Kerr, 2020). Third, the consequences affect campus life: even subthreshold eating disorder symptoms predicted suicidal ideation and attempts in college students (Lipson & Sonneville, 2020). Additionally, cannabis use has been linked to lower grades (Mullin & Cservenka, 2024). Finally, campus counseling centers are the most common source of student mental health care (Lipson et al., 2019).

The Healthy Minds Study (HMS) is well suited to testing these questions in college students, as it is an annual web-based survey of undergraduate and graduate students that assesses mental health and service use (Lipson et al., 2019). Because it includes both the SCOFF and a substance use battery, binge eating and cannabis use can be examined together in the same students. The second SCOFF item asks about worry over lost control of eating, which captures loss of control regardless of diagnosis and thus allows binge eating to be studied across BED and BN. In addition, a subset of participants completed the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE-Q; Fairburn & Beglin, 1994), which provides a behavioral count of binge days that can be compared with the SCOFF item. Finally, pooling ten annual cohorts yields a larger sample than earlier HMS reports on related topics.

To address these gaps, the current study had four aims. First, we estimated the prevalence of binge-eating psychopathology among cannabis-using and non-using students.

Second, we tested whether this association strengthens with frequency of cannabis use. Third, we tested whether the association holds when the binge eating frequency threshold is aligned with the DSM-5 criteria of once weekly (i.e., 4x in the past month). Fourth, we estimated the burden of co-occurring binge eating and cannabis use on mental health, suicidality, academic functioning, and help-seeking behaviors.

Method Participants

Data comes from ten cohorts (2015-16 to 2024-25) of the Healthy Minds Study (HMS), an annual, web-based survey fielded at participating US colleges and universities (Lipson et al., 2019). All respondents completed three core modules, Demographics, Mental Health Status, and Mental Health Survey Utilization/Help Seeking, and up to 10 elective modules chosen by their institution that focus on separate areas of well-being. We leveraged both the mandatory and elective modules and analyzed variables related to eating behaviors, cannabis and other drug use, mental health, treatment, academic impairment attributed to specific behaviors, and demographic information.

Measures

Cannabis Use

If students indicated that they have recreationally used cannabis products over the past 30 days, they would then be asked the frequency, which is classified into less than once per week, 1-2 days per week, 3-4 days per week, nearly every day, and (nearly) daily.

Eating Pathology

SCOFF eating disorder screener. Developed by Morgan et al. in 1999, the SCOFF questionnaire is a widely adopted screening instrument for eating disorders (Morgan et al., 1999). Scoring at least a 2 out of 5 on the SCOFF's yes/no questions suggests possible anorexia nervosa or bulimia nervosa. Since Bulimia is a diagnostic rule-out for Binge Eating Disorder, adopting the diagnosis-based definition of BED would exclude students who both binge and purge. Hence, we used loss of control over eating (a "yes" on SCOFF question 2), a subclinical behavioral variable, as the primary exposure, rather than a BN or BED diagnosis. We also used SCOFF total scores to define the following thresholds: probable eating disorder (aligning with the original SCOFF cutoff; ≥2), HMS positive screen (HMS defines eating disorder as positive at this level; ≥3), and severe (≥4).

Bingeing and Purging.

The Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE-Q) is a 28question self-report questionnaire that measures the range, frequency, and severity of eating disorder symptoms (Fairburn & Beglin, 1994). The HMS includes open-ended behavioral frequency questions from the EDE-Q. It asks how many days over the past 28-day period a student experienced an objective binge episode, characterized by an unusually large amount of food and a loss of control over eating. We defined three levels of severity: ≥1, ≥4 (meeting DSM-5's weekly frequency criterion), and ≥8 (matching DSM-5's criteria for severe BED and/or BN). Additionally, students were asked the number of times they engaged in each type of compensatory behavior over the past 28 days, including vomiting, laxatives, diuretics, exercise, and fasting.

Mental Health Correlates

Depression. The Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) is a self-report screening questionnaire for depression, developed by Kroenke, Spitzer, and Williams in 1999. Patients are asked to rate how often they are bothered by 9 core depression symptoms over the past 2 weeks, from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). The scores are summed up to 0 to 27, with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms. We analyzed the continuous scores as well as two binary variables provided in the HMS codebook: a positive depression screen (PHQ-9 ≥ 10) and major depression (PHQ-9≥15). However, it is important to note that a positive screen is not a diagnosis.

Anxiety. The Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7 (GAD-7) is a seven-question screen for generalized anxiety disorder and asks patients how much they are bothered by seven core anxiety problems, rated 0-3. We analyzed continuous scores and a binary variable for a positive anxiety screen (GAD-7 ≥ 10).

Suicidality and self-injury.

The binary variables suicide ideation, planning, and attempt evaluates whether a student has seriously considered, planned, or attempted taking their own life over the past 12 months. Non-suicidal self-injury refers to instances where a student hurts themselves on purpose without the intention of killing themselves, which includes but is not limited to cutting, burning, punching or banging themselves, scratching, hair-pulling, biting, interfering with wound-healing, skin-carving, rubbing sharp objects into skin, punching or banging any objects.

Academic Harm from Eating Concerns.

In the HMS, students reported how eating and body image concerns affected their academic performance in the past year. They could select all that apply: did not experience this, experienced this but did not affect academic performance, lower grades on exams and projects, lower grades on courses, incomplete or dropped courses, significant disruption in research, practicum, thesis, or dissertation work, or other. We coded these into a binary variable: 1 if a student endorsed any of the last five boxes, and 0 if they endorsed only the first two. Students who were not shown these items are coded as missing.

Help-seeking and care. We coded 1 if a student reported receiving any clinical mental health treatment (therapy or medication) over the past 12 months, and 0 otherwise. Untreated distress. We calculated this as the proportion of students who screened positive for depression or anxiety but did not receive mental health treatment of any kind.

Data Handling

Out-of-range variables were set to NA, which mostly concerned self-report questionnaires and numerical variables. For example, Binge days was restricted to the 0-28-day range, whereas the purge/fasting variables were not subject to the same restriction, as they asked for the number of occurrences, not days. Age was restricted to 18-64 years. Most students were young adults (76.7% weighted; 95% CI [75.7, 77.8]) aged 18-25. Flourishing scale, PHQ-9, and GAD-7 were restricted to valid scores of 8-56, 0-2, and 0-21 respectively. Race and gender checkboxes were kept at 0/1, with text versions reconciled later.

Differentiating between non-users and non-respondents.

The HMS exports "select all that apply" items as 1 or blank. Thus, for the personal substance use question, a blank cannabis checkbox could mean either that a student saw the list but did not endorse cannabis, or that they were never shown the list. Treating blanks as "no" would understate the prevalence of cannabis use. To distinguish these, we counted a response only if at least one item on the list, including the "none of these" option, was checked. Cannabis use was coded 1 if endorsed and 0 if not endorsed among administered students and set to missing otherwise. This ensured that students who were not administered the battery were not counted as non-users. The same rule applied to SCOFF and academic impairment batteries.

Statistical Analysis

All estimates were weighted with the HMS non-response weight and clustered on campus. Variances were estimated with Taylor series linearization. Campus identifiers were absent from the 2017-18 file and from part of two other files. Records without an identifier were assigned singleton clusters. A sensitivity analysis restricted to students with a known campus identifier is reported. All models adjusted for age, sex at birth, gender-diverse identity, race and ethnicity, and cohort.

Joint models contrasted four groups (neither condition, binge eating only, cannabis use only, and both) with the group reporting neither as the reference. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure controlled the false discovery rate across the 11-correlate panel (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). Two common exposures with large main effects will generally appear submultiplicative. Interaction was therefore assessed on the additive scale as the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) with delta-method confidence intervals (Knol & VanderWeele, 2012). Multiplicative interaction was also tested.

Discussion

Summary of Findings

In this pooled sample of more than half a million US emerging adults, binge eating and cannabis use were common and strongly linked. Specifically, students who used cannabis were more likely to report loss of control over eating than students who did not, and a notable subset of students (8.3%) reported both behaviors. Importantly, students reporting both also had markedly higher rates of depression, anxiety, suicidal thoughts and behavior, self-injury, and academic harm than students with neither. Because both behaviors were common and the co-occurring group fared clearly worse, the comorbidity is common enough to matter for campus populations and burdensome enough to warrant its own attention. Key findings are described below.

Cannabis Use Frequency and Eating Pathology

The patterns observed across levels of cannabis use clarify what this association reflects. Loss of control over eating was about equally common at every frequency of cannabis use and was lower only among students who did not use cannabis, whereas severe screening results became more common as cannabis use became more frequent. Taken together, these patterns suggest that any cannabis use is associated with the experience of losing control over one's eating, and that more frequent cannabis use is then associated with more severe eating pathology. However, given that these analyses are cross-sectional, the direction of these associations cannot be determined. On the one hand, experimental evidence suggests that cannabis use may increase the reward value of food and thereby the likelihood of losing control while eating (Weltens et al., 2019). On the other hand, students who lose control over eating may turn to cannabis, and those with more severe eating pathology may use it more often if it helps them manage the distress that follows binge episodes (Schaefer et al., 2020; Wallis et al., 2022). These two directions are not mutually exclusive and may reinforce one another over time. Additionally, because the gradient was not perfectly regular (i.e., severe screening results were similar at three to four and one to two days per week), a precise dose-response relationship cannot be inferred.

Relation to Earlier Studies

These findings also help to reconcile earlier studies that reached different conclusions.

Specifically, among treatment-seeking adults who all reported binge eating at least weekly, cannabis use was unrelated to eating disorder severity (Wilkinson et al., 2024). Because everyone in that sample binged frequently, the range of severity was restricted, and associations are harder to detect when severity varies so little. In contrast, our student sample spans the full range of severity (i.e., from no loss of control to severe screening results). Elran-Barak et al. (2025) found an association in women but not men in a community sample. While we adjusted for sex at birth, we did not formally test sex differences in this sample. Thus, sexstratified analyses are a clear next step because the association may differ for women and men.

Convergence Across Measures

The EDE-Q subsample strengthens confidence that the association involves behavioral binge eating and not only worry about loss of control over eating as assessed by the SCOFF.

Specifically, weekly binge eating was more common among cannabis users than among nonusers in this subsample, and this difference was statistically significant. This agreement across measures matters because the SCOFF item captures a cognition and not behavior (Morgan et al., 1999). As such, it supports the use of this item as a screen for binge eating in the context of cannabis use. However, the EDE-Q only approximates the diagnostic threshold because it covers 28 days and the DSM-5 requires three months, and self-report questionnaires can also yield higher binge frequencies than interviews (Fairburn & Beglin, 1994). Interview-based assessment is a logical next step for confirming diagnoses.

Possible Explanations

Several explanations could account for the association between cannabis use and binge eating. One possibility is that cannabis increases appetite and the reward value of palatable food (Weltens et al., 2019). However, this explanation alone cannot account for why severe scores on the SCOFF and loss-of-control eating episodes were also elevated, given that greater appetite does not by itself explain a sense of lost control. A second possibility involves negative affect, as binge eating is typically preceded by rising negative affect and guilt and followed by improvements in affect (Schaefer et al., 2020). The escape model similarly proposes that binge eating reduces distress by narrowing attention away from aversive self-awareness (Heatherton & Baumeister, 1991). Cannabis use may serve a similar purpose because depressive symptoms did not fully explain the association among women in earlier work (Elran-Barak et al., 2025). As such, cannabis use and binge eating may be two ways of coping with the same distress. A third possibility is that the two behaviors influence one another over time: adolescent cannabis use has preceded later depression and suicidality (Gobbi et al., 2019), and the resulting distress may in turn maintain binge eating. Because our design is cross-sectional, we cannot determine which explanation is correct. Importantly, each explanation implies a different treatment target (i.e., appetite and reward responses, negative affect and emotion regulation, or both).

Burden of Co-Occurrence

The burden associated with co-occurrence was large, which is important because it shows that the combination is more than a statistical overlap. Notably, students who reported both behaviors were substantially more likely to have attempted suicide in the past year than students who reported neither, and this difference was statistically significant. This finding is consistent with earlier work in which subthreshold eating disorder symptoms (Lipson & Sonneville, 2020) and adolescent cannabis use (Gobbi et al., 2019) each predicted suicidal ideation and attempts. Thus, students who carry both risk factors may be especially vulnerable because each risk factor adds its own pathway to suicidal thoughts and behavior. We examined interaction on the additive scale because it reflects how many students are affected (Knol & VanderWeele, 2012). On this scale, the excess risk was greater than expected from the two behaviors separately for depression, self-injury, suicidal ideation, and academic harm, and these interactions were statistically significant. However, because these estimates came from odds ratios and odds ratios overstate risk ratios when outcomes are common, they should be confirmed with models that estimate risk ratios or risk differences.

Use of Mental Health Care

Students who reported both behaviors were also more likely to be receiving mental health care (i.e., both psychotherapy and psychotropic medication), and these differences were statistically significant. Further, among students who screened positive for depression or anxiety, those reporting both behaviors were less likely to be untreated. This pattern likely reflects greater symptom severity in the co-occurring group, given that more severe symptoms tend to prompt help seeking, and it also fits the broader rise in service use among college students (Lipson et al., 2019). Because these students are already in contact with campus services, those services are well positioned to identify the comorbidity.

Implications for Practice and Research

These findings suggest several practical steps. Clinicians who treat binge eating can routinely ask about cannabis use, and clinicians who address cannabis use can ask about loss of control over eating. Both questions are warranted because each behavior was associated with the other and either may go unnoticed when only one is assessed. Further, because loss of control was about equally common at every frequency of cannabis use, even occasional users merit a brief question about binge eating. Subsequently, trials should test whether integrated treatment outperforms treating either behavior alone, as this is the most direct way to learn whether addressing both behaviors together improves outcomes.

Interpretive Considerations

Findings from the current study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, the HMS draws on self-selected campuses and the weights do not adjust for which campuses take part. As such, prevalence estimates describe students at participating campuses and should not be read as national rates. Second, all participants were enrolled students and, given that the developmental and campus context may influence both behaviors, generalization to peers outside college requires replication. Third, no cohort assessed cannabis use disorder.

Because frequency of cannabis use includes many students without impairment, our estimates may understate associations for problematic cannabis use. Fourth, we did not adjust for tobacco and alcohol use because both are co-used with cannabis and may lie on the causal pathway, and adjusting for them could remove part of the association of interest. Thus, our estimates are best read as total associations.

Conclusions and Next Steps

Next steps include 1) longitudinal and momentary assessment designs, 2) direct assessment of cannabis use disorder, and 3) tests of whether treating one behavior changes the other. Longitudinal and momentary designs are particularly critical because they can establish temporal order, which cross-sectional data cannot. In summary, binge eating among cannabis-using college students is common and linked to a heavy burden of distress. As such, this comorbidity warrants dedicated attention from researchers and clinicians.
