Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p=<.001 A main effect of Gender emerged (bMale-Female= .39, p=<.001): men (M=2.67, SE=.05, 95%CI[2.57, 2.77]) gave higher scores than women overall (M=2.28, SE=.06, 95%CI[2.16, 2.39]).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 5 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

We recruited Italian-speaking participants through convenience sampling.

After removing one duplicate entry, 112 unique participants were identified.

Thus, five more participants were excluded, as they either identified as homosexual (N = 2), non-binary (N = 2), or indicated a low level of attraction to the opposite gender (N = 1).

Due to a technical issue during data collection, two of the 104 eligible participants provided no valid ratings for the stimuli and were therefore excluded from the analyses.

In this introductory phase, participants were informed that they would be asked to evaluate photos and bios from Tinder.

Each stimulus was presented individually, and participants provided ratings for the following dimensions: • For photos: Based on the photo, participants rated the person’s physical attractiveness, likability, trustworthiness, and self-confidence on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all,5 = very much).

After answering these items, participants were asked how interested they would be in having a real date with the person shown in the photo (“How interested would you be in a date with this person?”).

• For bios: Participants were asked to evaluate the perceived interest level, likability, trustworthiness, and self-confidence of the person described in the bio on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all,5 = very much).

Following these assessments, participants were asked about their willingness to meet the person described, using the same question and response scale used for the photos.

All participants provided written informed consent before taking part in the study.

Stimulus evaluations were excluded from the models if the dependent variable or any of the four ratings was missing, while other valid evaluations from the same participant were retained.

Each photo was assessed against a set of criteria, including: • Contextual consistency (subjects depicted in everyday, non-extreme environments); • Target Age perceived to be between 18 and 35 years old, matching the age range of our participants; • Posture and expression standardization (direct gaze, frontal orientation, neutral to mildly positive expressions); • Clarity and visibility (absence of occlusions such as sunglasses, hats, or objects partially covering the face); • Lighting balance (naturalistic illumination, avoiding both overexposure and strong shadowing); and • Single-subject framing, excluding images containing other individuals or salient objects that could introduce unintended semantic cues (e.g., alcohol, sports equipment, luxury items).

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Online dating platforms require users to evaluate potential partners based on sparse, highly curated information, typically profile pictures and short self-descriptions. The present study examined how characteristics of visual and textual cues in dating profiles shape overall desirability (i.e., dateability), focusing on physical attractiveness (for photos) or interest (for bios), alongside likability, trustworthiness, and self-confidence. Participants evaluated photos and bios from simulated Tinder profiles, rating these dimensions together with their willingness to date the target. Linear mixed-effects models revealed that, although physical attractiveness and perceived interest were the strongest predictors of willingness to date the target for photos and bios, respectively, other dimensions also played an important role. Crucially, observed associations varied with participants' gender and prior dating app experience. Gender differences in photo-based evaluations were largely concentrated among experienced users, suggesting that evaluative strategies may differ more with familiarity with dating platforms' dynamics than by gender alone. Experience was also related to differences in the evaluation of bios, with inexperienced users placing more importance on perceived trustworthiness and selfconfidence. Beyond these substantive findings, the study provides an openly available set of ecologically grounded dating-profile stimuli, both visual and textual, empirically characterized across multiple dimensions relevant to social and romantic evaluation. These materials may provide a useful resource for future research on online dating and the integration of visual and textual social information in digital profiles.

1. Introduction

Over the past two decades, the rise of the Internet and smartphones has transformed, among other aspects of our social life, how people seek romantic partners, expanding the possibility of finding love beyond physical proximity (Finkel et al., 2012). Dating platforms, particularly mobile apps like Tinder, use algorithms to facilitate rapid connections, reshaping partner selection (Rosenfeld & Thomas, 2012; Sumter & Vandenbosch, 2019; Castro & Barrada, 2020). Given their widespread adoption, these platforms have drawn increasing interest from researchers, who are trying to investigate "swiping" behavior on dating apps by considering both individual variables that might shape evaluative strategies (such as Big Five Personality Traits or attachment styles; Chopik & Johnson, 2021) and potentially relevant profiles' characteristics (e.g., ethnicity or height; Chopik & Johnson, 2021; Witmer et al., 2025). The present study aims to contribute to this growing body of research by investigating how users respond to both visual (profile pictures) and textual (short textual descriptions -bios) cues in dating profiles, to identify which evaluative dimensions are most strongly associated with dating interest. Using a controlled yet ecological design, we assess the influence of perceived attractiveness (for pictures) or interest (for bios), trustworthiness, likability, and selfconfidence on dating interest, i.e., those dimensions which are taken to play a key role in social perception (Bar et al., 2006; Oosterhof & Todorov, 2008; Todorov et al., 2009) or that have been previously identified as determinant factors in online and offline dating research (Fiore et al., 2008; Brand et al., 2012; Valentine et al. 2020; Appel et al., 2023). In particular, our study explores how these dimensions interact to shape overall desirability and whether people prioritize them differently when judging profiles based on photos versus bios. Additionally, we examine key individual differences that may influence profile evaluation, particularly gender and prior experience with dating apps. Prior research suggests that men and women may use different criteria when selecting partners (Buss & Schmitt, 1993; Fletcher et al., 2004; Eaton & Rose, 2011; Morlat et al., 2026), with women generally being more selective and prioritizing positive personality traits (Fletcher et al., 2004; Todd et al., 2007; Abramova et al., 2016; Tyson et al., 2016). At the same time, experience with dating apps may shape evaluation strategies as users become more familiar with the norms and affordances of these platforms (Pronk & Denissen, 2020; Barrada et al., 2021).

By analyzing photo-based and bio-based judgments in isolation, the present study seeks to establish a baseline characterization of how different profile elements are perceived in online dating scenarios. It also provides an available pool of ecologically grounded profile photographs and bios that are empirically characterized at the individual level across multiple dimensions relevant to social and romantic evaluation. Beyond examining how visual and textual cues relate to dating interest, the present study provides a reusable stimulus resource for future empirical research on online dating dynamics and the integration of visual and textual profile information across modalities.

2. Method

>Method

2.1 Participants

We recruited Italian-speaking participants through convenience sampling. The initial dataset comprised 113 survey responses. After removing one duplicate entry, 112 unique participants were identified. Eight participants were excluded for not meeting the study's eligibility criteria.

Three participants were excluded because they were older than 37; this age group was selected because it represents the core user base of mobile dating applications (young adulthood ; Sumter & Vandenbosch, 2019). Moreover, only participants who reported a predominant attraction to the opposite gender were included (i.e., heterosexual participants). Thus, five more participants were excluded, as they either identified as homosexual (N = 2), non-binary (N = 2), or indicated a low level of attraction to the opposite gender (N = 1). This choice was motivated by the need to ensure sample consistency, since sexual orientation can be relevant in shaping users' motives and strategies on dating apps (McPherson et al., 2025). Due to a technical issue during data collection, two of the 104 eligible participants provided no valid ratings for the stimuli and were therefore excluded from the analyses.

The final sample consisted of 102 participants (54 women, 52.9%), aged 18-37 years (M = 26.5, SD = 4.57). Regarding prior experience with dating apps, 70 participants (68.6%) reported never having used such platforms, whereas 31 (30.4 %) reported some degree of previous experience. Experience data were missing for one participant.

2.2. Materials

To ensure ecological validity, we selected profile photos that closely resemble personal images typically found on Tinder. As a matter of fact, unlike standardized materials, which often feature neutral backgrounds and controlled settings (e.g., Chicago Face Database, Ma et al., 2015), real dating app photos exhibit variability in context, posture, and expression. We thus sourced 120 photos (60 male, 60 female) from stock-image platforms (Adobe Stock, Pexels, iStock, Unsplash, Pxhere, Shutterstock). These images underwent a rigorous selection process to guarantee homogeneity: photos were required to show subjects facing the camera and in everyday environments; moreover, we excluded pictures featuring more than one subject or context-specific objects (e.g., surfboards, wine glasses, …), to avoid external cues that could bias the perception of the subject. Following the same logic, we generated 120 bios, written in Italian, standardized in length (15-27 words), and modeled after real Tinder bios, based on common themes and self-presentation strategies (e.g., humor), to ensure a realistic tone and content. Both pictures and bios were designed to closely replicate Tinder profiles, including graphical elements, to further enhance ecological validity (Fig. 1). Some participants did not complete all scheduled stimulus evaluations, and individual item responses were occasionally missing. Across the 102 participants, 2,759 photo evaluations and 2,760 bio evaluations were recorded, corresponding to 90.2% of the 3,060 possible evaluations per modality under full questionnaire completion. Across participants, each male photo received an average of 24 ratings (range: [21] [22] [23] [24] [25] [26] [27] [28]), while each female photograph received 22 ratings (range: [18] [19] [20] [21] [22] [23] [24]). The corresponding figures for male and female bios were 24 ([21-28]) and 22 ([18-24]), respectively. More information on stimulus selection is available in the Supplementary Materials, together with tables reporting stimulus-level normative ratings (means and standard deviations), separately for male and female profiles (Tables S1-S4).

Additionally, the complete set of bios, stimulus-level normative ratings, and supporting documentation are available on OSF (https://osf.io/vcp3e/overview?view_only=1334e9fdd3f645ca9fd3c9e30c095a53). Because the photographs were obtained from third-party stock-image platforms and remain subject to their respective licensing terms, we do not have permission to redistribute the original image files. Instead, the repository provides a stimulus index containing the corresponding source link and identifying information for each photograph, allowing researchers to locate and obtain the images directly from the original providers under the applicable licenses. At the time of manuscript preparation, the original online sources of 18 photographs could not be located or verified; these stimuli are explicitly flagged in the index.

2.3 Procedure and measures

Participants accessed the experiment through an anonymous online survey hosted on Qualtrics.

After providing informed consent, they completed a demographic questionnaire assessing age, gender, education level, sexual orientation, and previous experience with dating apps on a 1 ("Never used") to 5 ("Very often used") scale. In this introductory phase, participants were informed that they would be asked to evaluate photos and bios from Tinder. They were then randomly assigned to evaluate 30 photos and 30 bios, choosing whether to rate male or female profiles. Each stimulus was presented individually, and participants provided ratings for the following dimensions: • For photos: Based on the photo, participants rated the person's physical attractiveness, likability, trustworthiness, and self-confidence on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all,5 = very much).

After answering these items, participants were asked how interested they would be in having a real date with the person shown in the photo ("How interested would you be in a date with this person?"). Again, they answered using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all interested,5 = very interested).

• For bios: Participants were asked to evaluate the perceived interest level, likability, trustworthiness, and self-confidence of the person described in the bio on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all,5 = very much). Following these assessments, participants were asked about their willingness to meet the person described, using the same question and response scale used for the photos.

The last question in both sections served as the key dependent variable, capturing the level of appeal and interest in a potential meeting elicited by a profile element (photo or bio). We identified this variable as the content's «dateability». To control for order effects, the presentation order of stimuli was randomized. Half of the participants evaluated photos first, then bios; the other half evaluated bios first, then photos. Moreover, the order of rating questions within each stimulus type was also randomized across participants.

The experimental protocol was fully approved by the Institution's Ethics Review Committee on June 24, 2024. All participants provided written informed consent before taking part in the study.

2.4 Statistical analysis

Linear Mixed-Effects Models (LMMs) were used to examine the associations between perceived profile characteristics and Dateability, measured on a 5-point scale, separately for photos and bios. Participants and stimuli were initially included as random intercepts to account for the non-independence of repeated evaluations (Judd et al., 2012). For photo evaluations, the stimulus-level random intercept was subsequently removed because its estimated variance was zero, resulting in a singular fit. Models were fitted using maximum likelihood estimation, and statistical significance was assessed at α = .05 using Type III tests with Satterthwaiteadjusted degrees of freedom. Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike Information Criterion Missing data were handled at the observation level. Stimulus evaluations were excluded from the models if the dependent variable or any of the four ratings was missing, while other valid evaluations from the same participant were retained. Observations were also excluded when participant-level information required by the models, including prior dating app experience, was unavailable. The final models included 2,704 photo evaluations and 2,713 bio evaluations.

3.1 Photo Evaluations

An LMM examined factors influencing dating interest from profile pictures. A main effect of Gender emerged (bMale-Female= .39, p=<.001): men (M=2.67, SE=.05, 95%CI[2.57, 2.77]) gave higher scores than women overall (M=2.28, SE=.06, 95%CI[2.16, 2.39]). For photos, Attractiveness was the strongest predictor (b=.67, SE=.03, p<.001), but Likability (b=.28, SE=.02, p<.001) and Trustworthiness (b=.19, SE=.03, p<.001), also increased Dateability, while Self-Confidence was non-significant (p =.24). A significant two-way interaction between Gender and Experience (F1,99.86 = 11.96, p = .001) indicated that women and men displayed different attitudes depending on their level of experience with the platform (Fig. 2). Follow-up comparisons revealed that gender differences in dating interest varied substantially depending on prior experience with dating apps. Among new users, no significant difference emerged between male and female participants (bMale-Female = .13, SE = .08, p = .11). In contrast, among experienced users, men reported significantly higher dating interest than women (bMale-Female = .65, SE = 0.1, p < .001). Thus, the overall gender difference in dating interest was primarily evident among participants with prior dating app experience. Moreover, Experience also shaped the effect of physical Attractiveness. The two-way interaction between Attractiveness and Experience (F1,2691.06=7.46, p=.006) revealed that physical attractiveness was especially relevant for those participants who reported never having used dating app platforms before (bNew-Experienced=.08, SE=.03, p=.006).

Finally, Gender further modulated the impact of both Likability (F1,2675.79=8.82, p = .003) and Trustworthiness (F1,2691.03=5.22, p = .02; Fig. 2) on Dateability. Follow-up analyses revealed that Likability was positively associated with dating interest for both men (b = .29, SE = .02, 95%CI [.24, .33]) and women (b =.19, SE =.03, 95% CI [.14, .24]), with a significantly stronger association among male participants (p=.003). A similar pattern emerged for Trustworthiness, which was positively associated with dating interest for both men (b =.21, SE=.02, 95% CI [.17,.26]) and women (b=.13, SE= .03, 95% CI [.08, .18]), again showing a stronger association among male participants (p = .02).

3.2 Bio Evaluations

A second LMM examined Dateability scores obtained by bios (Fig. 3). Again, men (M=2.49, SE=.05, 95%CI[2.4, 2.58]) assigned higher Dateability ratings than women (M=2.23, SE=.06, 95%CI[2.12,2.35], p=.001), though scores were overall lower than in the photo condition.

Interest level was the strongest predictor (b=.52, SE=.02, p<.001), similar to physical attractiveness in photos. Again, Likability (b=.24, SE=.02, p<.001) and Trustworthiness significantly predicted Dateability ratings (b=.15, SE=.02, p<.001), while no main effect of Self-Confidence emerged (p=.13). However, a significant Gender × Self-Confidence interaction (F1,2252.42=10.55, p=.001) showed that the relationship between Self-Confidence and Dateability varied by gender. As a matter of fact, Self-Confidence was relevant only to male participants (b=.05, SE=.02, p=.001), while its effect was non-significant in females (p =.23).

The importance of perceived Self-Confidence was also moderated by participants' Experience (F1,2700.88=5.83, p=.02): Self-Confidence had a significant effect on Dateability only for those participants who reported never having used a dating app (b= .04, SE=.01, p<.001), while it was non-significant for most experienced ones (p = .57). Experience also moderated the impact of Trustworthiness (F1,2685.62=5.66, p=.02): even if it was important across levels of Experience, Trustworthiness was more important for those participants who were new to dating apps dynamics (bNew-Experienced=.08, SE=.03 p=.02). Conversely, a two-way interaction between Likability and Experience revealed a reversed pattern (F1,2627.23 = 8.35, p = .004): while being important for everybody, perceived Likability was more relevant for experienced users (bNew-Experienced= -.09, SE=.03, p=.004), suggesting that evaluative strategies differ based on participants' experience. Finally, a marginal two-way interaction between Interest and Gender (F1,2616.4=3.9, p=.048) suggested that, for men, the interesting content of a bio might be more relevant than for women when judging overall Dateability (bMale-Female=.06, SE=.03, p=.049).

All other tested interactions did not yield significant effects, indicating that no further moderating influences shaped the relationship between bio or photo characteristics and dating interest.

4. Discussion

This study examined which dimensions of visual and textual information are associated with dating interest when evaluating dating app profiles, and whether these associations vary by gender and prior dating app experience. Beyond characterizing how users weigh visual and textual cues in dating profiles, the study also provides an available set of ecologically grounded dating-profile materials, systematically normed across multiple dimensions relevant to social and romantic evaluation. These materials may therefore serve as a useful resource for future experimental research on online dating dynamics.

Photo Evaluations

When participants evaluated profile pictures, physical attractiveness was the strongest predictor of dating interest. However, this effect was not exclusive: perceived likability and trustworthiness also contributed independently to dateability, indicating that visual stimuli can elicit rich inferences about personality traits relevant to dating decisions, beyond mere aesthetic evaluations.

Our findings on the dominant role of attractiveness align with previous research highlighting physical appearance as a central factor in romantic attraction and partner selection (Asendorpf et al., 2011; Eastwick & Finkel, 2008; Eastwick et al., 2014; Luo & Zhang, 2009). Importantly, however, attractiveness was strongly associated with dateability among both male and female participants, with no significant gender differences. Instead, gender differences emerged in the relevance of other social dimensions. Specifically, although likability and trustworthiness were positively associated with dateability in both genders, these associations were significantly stronger among men. This finding is noteworthy given traditional accounts that emphasize women's greater attention to personality traits, such as trustworthiness, in mate selection (Buss & Schmitt, 1993; Fletcher et al., 2004; Whyte et al., 2021). Our results suggest that positive social impressions inferred from photos may be more strongly associated with men's romantic evaluations, even after accounting for perceived physical attractiveness. Thus, evaluating visual information appears to involve integrating multiple socially relevant dimensions rather than relying exclusively on aesthetic characteristics, and this integrative process may work differently for men and women.

An especially interesting finding concerns the interplay between gender and prior experience with dating apps. Although men generally gave profiles higher ratings than women, this difference did not hold across experience levels. Among participants who had never used dating apps, no statistically significant gender difference emerged. In contrast, among experienced users, male participants reported substantially higher dating interest in presented photos than female participants. This finding suggests that gender differences in evaluative selectivity may reflect prior exposure to online dating environments rather than stable, gender-related preferences. One possible interpretation is that repeated engagement with dating platforms is associated with the development of different evaluative strategies (Pronk & Denissen, 2020; Barrada et al., 2021). For instance, previous research has documented asymmetries in matching opportunities and user behavior on dating platforms (Tyson et al., 2016; Roca-Cuberes et al., 2023). In this context, men with more online dating experience might express interest in a broader range of potential partners, potentially reflecting adaptation to the competitive dynamics of these environments (Berkowitz et al., 2021). However, this interpretation remains speculative. Given the study's cross-sectional design, we cannot determine whether experience shapes evaluative strategies or whether individuals with different pre-existing preferences are more likely to use dating apps. Nevertheless, the observed interaction challenges an exclusively dispositional interpretation of gender differences in dating preferences and highlights the potential relevance of individuals' previous experiences within digital dating environments.

Experience with dating apps also moderated the association between physical attractiveness and dating interest elicited by the photo. Specifically, attractiveness was more strongly associated with dateability among participants who had never used dating apps. This pattern suggests that prior experience may relate not only to overall evaluative selectivity but also to how relevant particular profile characteristics are. One possibility is that inexperienced users rely more strongly on immediately accessible visual cues, whereas experienced users may incorporate a broader range of impressions into their overall evaluations. Although this interpretation fits the observed results, longitudinal or experimental evidence would be needed to establish whether familiarity with dating platforms actually produces such changes.

Bio evaluations

A somewhat different pattern emerged when participants evaluated textual self-descriptions.

Overall, ratings were lower than in the photo condition, suggesting that textual information alone may invite more cautious or selective judgments. Here, the interest conveyed by the bio played a similar role to attractiveness in the photo condition, showing the strongest correlation with general dateability ratings. Likability and trustworthiness again contributed positively, suggesting these dimensions serve as stable evaluative criteria across modalities in online dating scenarios. The role of perceived self-confidence was more complex. Its association with dateability varied by gender: self-confidence was positively associated with overall judgments among male participants, but not for female participants. One possibility is that self-confidence is not uniformly interpreted as a desirable characteristic in short dating profiles. For some individuals, confident self-presentation may signal interpersonal appeal, whereas for others it may contribute little to judging desirability. Moreover, while projected confidence can signal underlying mate value, intelligence, and social abilities (Murphy et al., 2015; Alexopoulos & Timmermans, 2020), strong displays of confidence or overconfidence can be interpreted as arrogance, and they can backfire (Murphy et al., 2015) Together, these findings suggest that gender-related differences in romantic evaluations may concern different dimensions depending on the information available. In photo evaluations, men showed stronger associations with likability and trustworthiness, whereas in bio evaluations, gender differences concerned self-confidence and interest. This suggests that integrating judgments about different social dimensions into a general impression may depend partly on whether information is inferred from visual appearance or communicated through textual self-presentation.

Prior experience with dating apps was associated with different patterns of social evaluation (Fig. 3): trustworthiness was more strongly associated with dating interest among inexperienced users, likability among experienced users, while self-confidence showed a small positive association only in the former group. One possible explanation concerns the uncertainty inherent in online dating settings, where individuals must evaluate unfamiliar people based on limited and potentially curated information. In this context, inexperienced users may rely more heavily on cues suggesting reliability and confident self-presentation when evaluating potential partners (Toma & Hancock, 2010; Gibbs et al., 2011; Chiu & Pan, 2020; Bandinelli & Gandini, 2022). By contrast, experienced users may develop different expectations about what profile information can reliably convey, placing greater emphasis on perceived interpersonal appeal.

This interpretation is consistent with research suggesting that engagement with dating platforms is associated with differences in users' expectations and approaches (Pronk & Denissen, 2020; Barrada et al., 2021).

General discussion

Overall, these findings provide a baseline for understanding how individuals evaluate visual and textual information in dating app profiles. Across modalities, attractiveness and perceived interest emerged as the strongest predictors of dateability for photos and bios, respectively, while likability and trustworthiness showed consistent positive associations with the person's appeal as a potential date. These results suggest that romantic evaluations based on limited profile information may reflect multiple perceptual dimensions, including both a profile's most immediately salient characteristics and broader social impressions. Importantly, the findings also point toward a more context-dependent understanding of gender differences in romantic evaluation. Rather than supporting a single, uniform pattern of male and female selectivity, the results suggest that gender differences in the evaluation of dating profiles may vary by prior experience with online dating and the specific characteristics being evaluated. The pronounced gender difference in photo-based dating interest among experienced users, together with the different associations between social impressions and dateability, suggests that the evaluation of potential partners may be related to both individual characteristics and previous engagement with digital dating environments.

Beyond these theoretical findings, the present study makes a methodological contribution.

Research on online dating frequently relies on ad hoc profile stimuli developed for individual studies (e.g., Pronk & Denissen, 2020; Appel et al., 2023), making cross-experiment comparisons difficult and requiring researchers to repeatedly construct and pretest new materials. Here, we empirically characterized a pool of 120 profile photographs and 120 short bios designed to resemble the information typically encountered on dating platforms. The resulting stimulus-level normative information covers several dimensions relevant to impression formation in online dating contexts, including attractiveness or interest, likability, trustworthiness, self-confidence, and overall dateability of the profile. These ratings provide researchers with an empirical basis for identifying stimuli with different evaluative profiles according to their experimental aims. The bios and associated normative information are openly available, while the photographic materials are documented through a stimulus index containing source information and retrieval links, subject to the original providers' licensing conditions. This resource may be particularly useful for studies investigating sequential impression formation in digital environments and the effects of profile construction strategies.

Because photos and bios were evaluated separately, their independently characterized properties can inform the construction of new experimental profiles in which visual and textual cues are systematically combined. For instance, future research could manipulate the congruence between visual and textual information to investigate whether conflicting impressions produce additive, compensatory, or updating effects on romantic evaluations.

Similarly, experimental and longitudinal designs could examine whether repeated exposure to dating platforms changes the relative relevance of appearance, interpersonal appeal, and perceived trustworthiness in partner evaluation.

In this sense, the present study provides not only evidence that judgments on dating apps involve multiple considerations, but also a reusable empirical foundation for investigating how such impressions are formed and potentially revised in digital dating contexts.

Limitations & Future Directions

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, photos and bios were evaluated separately rather than as part of complete profiles. This choice reduces the ecological correspondence with ordinary dating-app use, where visual and textual information typically co-occur, but it also enabled us to characterize the contribution of each type of information independently and to obtain stimulus-level normative ratings. An important next step will therefore be to use the present materials to examine how independently characterized photos and bios interact when combined. Second, the correlational nature of the cue ratings prevents strong causal claims, as higher dateability may both result from and reinforce more favorable trait evaluations. Future studies could use the normative information provided here to select stimuli that differ systematically on specific dimensions and manipulate these characteristics experimentally.

The study also focuses on self-reported dating interest rather than actual behavioral choices, and potentially relevant individual differences, such as short-versus long-term mating orientation, were not assessed. Finally, because the sample consisted of young Italian heterosexual participants evaluating opposite-gender profiles, the stimulus norms should not be assumed to generalize unchanged across cultural contexts, age groups, sexual orientations, or gender identities. Further norming in more diverse samples would therefore both extend the usefulness of the stimulus set and provide an opportunity to examine how stable these profile evaluations are across populations.

1.1. PHOTO SELECTION

To ensure ecological validity, we selected profile photos that closely resemble the types of personal images typically found on Tinder. Unlike standardized materials, which often feature neutral backgrounds and controlled settings, real dating app photos exhibit substantial variability in context, posture, and expression. We initially sourced a larger pool of images from stock image platforms (Adobe Stock, Pexels, iStock, Unsplash, Pxhere, Shutterstock), ensuring a diverse yet realistic pool of stimuli, from which we selected a final set of 120 photos (60 male, 60 female) after a multi-step screening procedure.

First, an initial screening was conducted by the research team to exclude images that were clearly atypical of dating app profiles (e.g., professional photoshoots, highly stylized portraits, or extreme contextual cues). Second, images were independently evaluated by three raters, and only those meeting predefined inclusion criteria were retained. In cases of disagreement, images were discussed and excluded if consensus could not be achieved. Each photo was assessed against a set of criteria, including: • Contextual consistency (subjects depicted in everyday, non-extreme environments); • Target Age perceived to be between 18 and 35 years old, matching the age range of our participants; • Posture and expression standardization (direct gaze, frontal orientation, neutral to mildly positive expressions); • Clarity and visibility (absence of occlusions such as sunglasses, hats, or objects partially covering the face); • Lighting balance (naturalistic illumination, avoiding both overexposure and strong shadowing); and • Single-subject framing, excluding images containing other individuals or salient objects that could introduce unintended semantic cues (e.g., alcohol, sports equipment, luxury items).

Importantly, while these criteria ensured comparability across stimuli, we intentionally preserved a degree of natural variability (e.g., slight differences in background or microexpressions) to maintain ecological plausibility and avoid the artificial uniformity typical of standardized face databases.

1.2 BIOS SELECTION

The bios, written in Italian, were standardized in length (15-27 words) and constrained to a comparable level of linguistic complexity (e.g., avoiding rare vocabulary or highly elaborated sentence structures). The bios were written based on real profiles by Italian Tinder users, which were deeply reviewed and analyzed by the research team before stimulus construction. This preliminary exploration allowed us to identify recurring themes, linguistic styles, and selfpresentation strategies, which informed the creation of ecologically valid yet controlled stimuli.

In addition, we reviewed all bios to ensure grammatical correctness and eliminate orthographic errors, which can independently affect impression formation (van der Zanden, 2022).

Content-wise, bios were designed to reflect common themes observed on dating apps -such as hobbies, personality traits, humor, and relational intentions -while avoiding highly idiosyncratic details (e.g., niche interests, strong ideological statements, …). We introduced this constraint to minimize variance driven by individual preferences and ensure evaluations reflected generalizable impression processes rather than personal compatibility. Three independent raters then evaluated each bio for overall valence, clarity, and naturalness, as well as its consistency with typical self-presentational styles observed on dating apps. We discussed and resolved discrepancies through iterative revision, and we modified or excluded bios judged ambiguous or unnatural.
