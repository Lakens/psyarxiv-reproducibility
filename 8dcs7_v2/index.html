Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Abstract

Background: Borderline personality disorder (BPD) is a prevalent and highly impairing condition whose origins cannot be explained by a single causal pathway. Contemporary research and theory inform a developmental view in which genetic, neurobiological, epigenetic, environmental, and psychological factors may interact recursively across time.

Summary: This narrative review synthesizes evidence and theoretical perspectives on the origins of BPD across biological, epigenetic, environmental, social, psychological, and psychodynamic domains. Genetic and neurobiological findings point to polygenic vulnerability, heterogeneous frontolimbic alterations, altered stress-response systems, and possible inflammatory and oxidative mechanisms. Epigenetic research examines possible biological embedding of adversity in stressregulation, neuroplasticity, and neurotransmission pathways. Environmental evidence highlights associations with childhood adversity, invalidating caregiving, parental psychopathology, peer victimization, socioeconomic disadvantage, and cultural context. Psychological and psychodynamic models propose how temperament, maladaptive schemas, attachment disruption, emotion dysregulation, impaired mentalization, and fragmented self-other representations contribute to borderline pathology. Evidence is stronger for associations than for the causal sequences connecting these domains, and BPD specificity remains incompletely established. The review brings these accounts into a developmental synthesis, examining their convergence and differences and tracing proposed reciprocal relationships among biological vulnerability, caregiving and broader environments, psychological capacities, and subjective experience from infancy through adolescence.

Messages: BPD can be understood within a framework of multiple interacting developmental pathways rather than a single cause. Equifinality, multifinality, and differential susceptibility provide perspectives on why different individuals may arrive at similar borderline presentations through distinct routes, while similar risk factors may lead to divergent outcomes. The synthesis translates these relationships into testable propositions about developmental sequences, reciprocal influences, protective processes, and what distinguishes borderline trajectories from other outcomes. It also informs individualized clinical formulation by connecting developmental vulnerabilities with current difficulties and available supports.

We consider these principles alongside transactional [19] and bioecological [20] perspectives, distinguishing theoretical propositions from empirical findings, evaluating the strength and specificity of the evidence, and formulating testable developmental propositions. This article is a clinically oriented narrative synthesis informed by searches of PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Google Scholar, and Web of Science through October 2026, supplemented by backward and forward citation searching. Searches and source selection were iterative and guided by the review's clinical and theoretical aims, rather than by a prespecified search strategy or formal eligibility criteria. Sources were selected for their relevance to BPD etiology and the developmental and theoretical perspectives considered. Selection and interpretation involved author judgement, with evidence weighted according to methodological quality, temporal ordering, replication, consistency, and comparison groups. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses were considered alongside primary studies and their limitations; theoretical works informed proposed mechanisms rather than providing empirical confirmation.

This section integrates existing perspectives on BPD's etiology: we examine how biological dispositions, environmental experiences, and developing psychological capacities may influence one another, identifying convergence, complementarity, and disagreement among theories. This integration considers proposed relationships concerning how patterns of functioning form, become activated, persist, and change, while distinguishing theoretical propositions from empirical findings.

The accounts concern different constructs and outcomes (borderline features, categorical BPD, and broader personality dysfunction or organization), which require separate consideration. Our aim is to connect these theoretical perspectives across developmental periods and levels of functioning and identify their implications for the subsequent critical examination of available evidence.

1. Introduction

Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD) has an estimated point prevalence of about 1.6% and a lifetime prevalence of up to 5.9%, and is diagnosed similarly in men and in women in the general population (in the United States of America) but approximately three times more often in women than in men in clinical settings [1] [2] [3]. Prevalence reaches 9-10% among psychiatric outpatients and 15-25% among inpatients, with some studies reporting rates as high as 50% [1] [2] [3]. The International Classification of Diseases (ICD), 11th Revision [4] includes a borderline pattern qualifier for personality disorder.

Although diagnostically distinct, BPD and complex posttraumatic stress disorder (cPTSD) share clinical features and may co-occur [5, 6].

BPD entails a high economic burden, with reported annual costs per patient of €16,852 (2000 prices) and estimates reaching US$69,231 (2010 prices, purchasing-power-parity adjusted), depending on study design, country, and whether indirect costs are included [7] [8] [9] [10]. In a nationwide matched-control study, direct healthcare and lost productivity costs were more than 16 times those of matched controls [9]. Comparisons across studies also indicate higher societal costs than those reported for anxiety disorders, mood disorders, and epilepsy [10]. Evidence-based psychotherapies were associated with average cost savings of approximately US$3,000 per patient annually (2015 prices) [11]. Quality of life is markedly impaired, with scores around 0.50 on the five-level EQ-5D (EQ-5D-5L), a standardized measure of health-related quality of life, comparable to Parkinson's disease or stroke [10].

BPD is increasingly understood not simply through a diathesis-stress lens, but through developmental frameworks proposing reciprocal influences among biological, epigenetic, psychological, and environmental factors. In this view, outcomes may reflect not only vulnerability under adversity but also plasticity, whereby the same susceptibilities may contribute to pathology or resilience depending on context [12] [13] [14] [15]. How these factors work together over time, and why shared vulnerabilities contribute to BPD rather than another disorder or sustained adaptation, remain incompletely understood. In this paper, we explore the biological, epigenetic, environmental, and psychological dimensions of BPD, and critically integrate existing perspectives across psychiatry, neuroscience, and psychotherapy to examine its possible developmental pathways. The principles of through bidirectional exchanges, both individuals and their environments are modified, thereby changing subsequent development. His unified theory of development integrates four complementary perspectives: personal change, concerning the development and reorganization of individual capacities; context, encompassing interpersonal relationships and broader ecological environments; regulation, emphasizing the interdependence of self-and other-regulation; and representation, addressing how experiences become organized into meanings and expectations that influence subsequent functioning [21, 22]. Together, Sameroff's four perspectives offer a framework for examining how different BPD theories emphasize individual development, environmental contexts, regulatory processes, and psychological representations, without assuming that their proposed mechanisms are equivalent [19].

Building on earlier transactional and developmental perspectives, Judd and McGlashan [23] proposed that adverse environments and neurobehavioral vulnerabilities may contribute to disturbances in attachment and information processing, potentially impairing the integration of cognitive, emotional, and behavioral capacities and contributing to identity disturbance and relational instability. Drawing on longitudinal and observational evidence, Lyons-Ruth and Brumariu [24] proposed a tentative developmental cascade model for BPD in which disruptions in the development of age specific competencies may contribute to further difficulties at subsequent developmental stages. The models emphasize different aspects: Judd and McGlashan foreground the integration of multiple developmental capacities, whereas Lyons-Ruth and Brumariu focus on how difficulties may extend across successive developmental periods. Of particular relevance to diagnostic specificity, Lyons-Ruth and Brumariu contrast proposed trajectories toward BPD characterized by continued relationship-seeking and sensitivity to caregiver withdrawal with pathways toward antisocial behavior characterized by more coercive interactions and aggression. However, the proposed distinctions remain provisional.

Bronfenbrenner and Morris's [20] bioecological model complements Sameroff's transactional perspective by specifying the conditions under which recurring interactions contribute to development. The Process-Person-Context-Time (PPCT) model defines proximal processes as progressively more complex, reciprocal interactions of the individual with people, objects, and symbols that take place regularly over extended periods. The effects depend on the person, immediate and broader contexts, and time. Navarro et al. [25] elaborated the concept of inverse proximal processes through which recurring interactions may contribute to maladaptive development. Sameroff [21, 22] situates changing self-and other-regulation within a biopsychosocial system encompassing neurophysiological, neuroendocrine, genomic, and epigenomic processes. McLaughlin and Gabard-Durnam [26] further distinguish experience-expectant learning -during sensitive periods -from experience-dependent learning throughout life, although plasticity varies with age and experience.

Taken together, these accounts suggest how the nature, timing, and duration of experiences may shape psychological capacities and biological functioning, which may, in turn, further influence transactions. They provide a framework for biological embedding, rather than evidence of particular biological causal pathways or sensitive periods specific to BPD. Psychoanalytic theorists have addressed early relational development through the proposed formation of psychological capacities within caregiving relationships. Winnicott [27] proposed that a reliable and developmentally responsive holding environment supports ego integration and continuity of being as the infant progresses from absolute dependence through relative dependence toward greater independence. Bion [28] developed the concepts of maternal reverie, containment, and alphafunction to describe how emotional experiences may be processed within the caregiver-infant relationship, with functions initially provided by the caregiver potentially becoming part of the child's developing capacity for thinking. Drawing on quantitative observational research on mother-infant interactions and psychoanalytic theory, Beebe and Lachmann [29] distinguish self-contingency (the predictability of an individual's own behavioral rhythms) from interactive contingency (the coordination of behavior with a partner), describing their interdependence and contribution to procedural expectancies. Self-contingency is a narrower construct than self-regulation and should not be equated with it. Considered together, these approaches raise the question of how patterned exchanges relate to developing capacities to organize emotional experience. In our integration, capacities shaped through earlier relationships may subsequently influence participation in later ones. Considered through Sameroff's general transactional framework, Linehan's [30] biosocial theory can be understood as an account of how individual emotional vulnerability and invalidating environmental responses may become interconnected across development and potentially contribute to BPD. Heightened emotional sensitivity and reactivity, together with slower recovery from arousal, may interact with invalidating environmental responses, potentially hindering the development of effective emotion regulation. Crowell et al. [13] further propose that in a substantial subset of individuals who later develop BPD, early trait impulsivity may precede heightened emotional sensitivity and emerge independently of emotion dysregulation, potentially influencing caregiver responses and subsequent emotional and behavioral development. While Linehan emphasizes emotion dysregulation as central to BPD, Judd and McGlashan situate it within a broader disturbance involving attachment organization and the integration of cognitive, emotional, and behavioral capacities. A three-wave longitudinal observational study found partial support for Linehan's biosocial propositions, including reciprocal prospective associations between emotion dysregulation and borderline features and an association between parental invalidation and subsequent emotion dysregulation. However, within-person reciprocal associations between emotional vulnerability and parental invalidation were not consistent across assessment intervals, and parental invalidation did not independently predict borderline features six months later after adjustment for baseline features [31].

Crowell et al. [13] also identify overlapping vulnerabilities implicated in ADHD, conduct disorder, substance use disorders, and antisocial pathology, leaving unresolved which developmental conditions differentiate borderline outcomes from other forms of psychopathology.

Fruzzetti et al. [32] further propose that recurring family transactions may shape not only emotion regulation but also how children evaluate the validity of their own emotional experience.

Repeated dismissal or misinterpretation of distress may interfere with learning to identify, label, tolerate, and communicate emotions, while inhibition or escalation of distress may elicit further invalidation or responses that inadvertently reinforce these patterns. Over time, these exchanges may contribute to self-invalidation, diminishing trust in one's own emotional experience, while also shaping expectations concerning others' responsiveness. On the other hand, responses that validate the child's emotional experience may facilitate more adaptive communication and emotional understanding, potentially contributing to changes in subsequent family interactions. Thus, a proposed developmental process may be connected to a possible maintenance process: responses to emotions may shape self-evaluation, which may subsequently influence emotional communication and others' responses. However, the progression from invalidation to self-invalidation remains insufficiently tested prospectively. Importantly, Fruzzetti et al.'s transactional model does not assign causal responsibility or blame exclusively to caregivers.

Apart from their influence on emotion regulation, recurring caregiver-child interactions may also shape expectations concerning the availability and responsiveness of others. Bowlby's [33] attachment theory conceptualizes these expectations as internal working models that influence subsequent interpersonal experiences and behavior. Gunderson [34] proposed intolerance of aloneness as a core difficulty in BPD, linking it to alternating patterns of anxious proximity-seeking and defensive detachment, as well as impaired object constancy -the difficulty maintaining reassuring internal representations of others in their absence. In a narrative review of 13 empirical studies, Agrawal et al. [35] identified associations between BPD and unresolved, preoccupied, and fearful adult attachment patterns, while noting methodological heterogeneity and partial diagnostic circularity arising from BPD's interpersonal diagnostic criteria. In a theoretical review, Gunderson et al. [36] discussed interpersonal hypersensitivity, emotional dysregulation, failed mentalization, and excess aggression as competing explanations of BPD's core psychopathology, highlighting differences in their proposed primary mechanisms and explanatory scope. Gunderson's interpersonal hypersensitivity formulation does not treat emotion dysregulation, failed mentalization, or excess aggression as the primary explanatory mechanisms, instead emphasizing relational sensitivity.

However, the relative explanatory primacy of these accounts remains unresolved.

Attachment relationships may shape not only expectations of others but also the capacity to understand behavior in terms of underlying mental states. Fonagy and Luyten [37] propose that caregivers' recognition and contingent, marked mirroring of a child's emotional experiences contribute to the development of mentalization and affect regulation, while disruptions in these processes may compromise the differentiation of mental states of self and other. Applied to BPD, a relatively low threshold for attachment-system activation may lead to heightened arousal and deactivation of explicit (controlled) mentalization, increasing reliance on implicit (automatic) processing and potentially distorting interpretations of others' intentions. This formulation emphasizes context-dependent difficulties in mentalization. In a meta-analysis comparing individuals with BPD with healthy controls, Németh et al. [38] found evidence of difficulties in mental-state reasoning and cognitive theory of mind (ToM), but not consistently in mental-state decoding, while affective findings varied by task. These largely cross-sectional comparisons do not directly test Fonagy and Luyten's proposed developmental mechanisms. Considered alongside biosocial and attachment perspectives, the mentalization-based formulation suggests a possible developmental pathway in which relationships sought for reassurance and emotional regulation may also become contexts in which heightened arousal interferes with the capacity to understand and navigate interpersonal experiences. This remains an integrative hypothesis concerning some developmental trajectories toward BPD.

Whereas mentalization-based theories underline issues in understanding mental states, Kernberg's [39] formulation of borderline personality organization, based on object-relations theory and ego psychology, addresses the structural organization and integration of affectively charged selfand object representations. In his formulation, splitting and related primitive defensive operations, such as primitive idealization and devaluation, support the separation of contradictory positive and negative self-and object representations, contributing to identity diffusion and unstable interpersonal relationships. Importantly, Kernberg's borderline personality organization involves generally preserved self-other boundaries and reality testing despite identity diffusion, although reality testing may become transiently impaired under severe stress. Kernberg's concept of borderline personality organization refers to a broader psychodynamic level of personality functioning rather than a specific diagnostic category. However, Kernberg also explicitly recognized and discussed BPD as a clinical syndrome. Alongside Fonagy and Luyten's mentalization-based model, Kernberg's theory places explanatory emphasis on the integration of representations, rather than primarily on the capacity to interpret mental states under attachment-related arousal. A possible connection is that difficulties holding conflicting experiences of a person together may constrain interpretations during conflict, while recurrent disruptions in understanding may hinder the revision of representations. These directions require separate examination; their reciprocity remains hypothetical.

Cognitive and schema models propose that enduring beliefs shape the interpretation of interpersonal events and coping responses [40, 41] describe early maladaptive schemas and the coping responses associated with their activation. For example, in BPD, schemas involving mistrust, abandonment, or defectiveness may shape the interpretation of ambiguous situations, such as a delayed response from a significant other, as rejection, particularly during heightened emotional arousal. Reassurance seeking, withdrawal, or anger may, in turn, influence others' responses, potentially reinforcing the initial expectations and maintaining these patterns. This illustrates both activation and possible maintenance; however, the initial formation of these patterns requires a separate developmental explanation. Young et al. [41] additionally describe shifts between schema modes, conceptualized as temporarily activated configurations of schemas, emotional states, and coping responses. Within our synthesis, persistence may reside partly in recurring patterns of change in emotion, interpretation, and behavior, despite substantial variation in functioning across situations. Whether these patterns generalize to new contexts or reflect continuing adverse conditions is an empirical question.

These theories help clarify how experiences acquire meaning. In our integration, subjective experience denotes the evolving organization of bodily and affective states, expectations, and interpretations, rather than an additional etiological factor alongside biology and environment. Selfinvalidation concerns whether one's emotional response is considered legitimate [32]; mentalization concerns how mental states are understood [37]; and cognitive and schema formulations concern how prior beliefs guide appraisal [40, 41]. Thus, similar interpersonal events may be understood and responded to differently. Baptista et al. [42] add a related consideration through the sense of agencythe experience of control over one's actions and their consequences -distinguishing interrelated implicit (pre-reflective) and explicit (reflective) aspects. In this synthesis, attention is given to how individuals experience their own actions and their effects, alongside how they interpret events and emotions. Attention to subjective meaning complements consideration of objective adversity.

Cicchetti and Rogosch's [16] principles of equifinality and multifinality situate these proposed processes within developmental heterogeneity. Equifinality describes different pathways leading to similar outcomes; multifinality describes similar initial conditions leading to different outcomes within differently organized developmental systems. Applied to BPD, these principles allow for different configurations and sequences of dispositions, relational experiences, and psychological capacities, while recognizing that shared vulnerabilities may also accompany other psychopathology or adaptive functioning. Beauchaine et al. [43], for example, proposed a model in which biology, sex, and environment interact, and in which partially shared vulnerabilities, such as trait impulsivity, may contribute to divergent antisocial and borderline outcomes, with sex-related biological and environmental influences hypothesized to differentiate these trajectories. Whereas Lyons-Ruth and Brumariu [24] emphasize the possible sequence of developmental difficulties, Beauchaine et al. focus on how partly shared liabilities might lead to different outcomes. Equifinality and multifinality describe these possibilities without identifying their mechanisms. Our integration therefore locates a question of specificity in the relationships among shared processes: whether particular configurations and sequences help explain borderline outcomes beyond the separate contributions of their components.

While equifinality and multifinality describe the possibility of convergent and divergent developmental outcomes, differential susceptibility offers a complementary perspective on why environmental influences may have different consequences across individuals. Going beyond the diathesis-stress model, Belsky and Pluess [12] proposed that some individuals may be more responsive both to adverse and supportive environmental conditions. Heightened sensitivity to adversity or a statistical individual-environment interaction alone does not establish differential susceptibility; the model specifically requires greater responsiveness to both unfavorable and favorable environments [44]. Applying this general framework to BPD, Rioux et al. [15] proposed that temperamental characteristics implicated in theories of BPD, including emotional reactivity and impulsivity, might indicate differences in environmental plasticity rather than acting exclusively as fixed risk factors. Importantly, temperamental sensitivity should not be used interchangeably with emotion dysregulation. Unlike transactional models, differential susceptibility concerns variation in responsiveness to environmental conditions rather than specifying the reciprocal processes through which individuals and contexts change. Within our integration, it suggests a possible moderating role for individual dispositions in the influence of relational environments on developing regulatory and representational capacities. Direct evidence that BPD-related characteristics predict greater responsiveness to both adverse and supportive environments remains limited.

Whereas differential susceptibility concerns individual differences in environmental responsiveness, resilience concerns the maintenance or recovery of mental health despite adversity. Kalisch et al.'s [45] positive appraisal style theory of resilience (PASTOR) distinguishes this outcome from its proposed mechanisms, including positive situation classification, reappraisal, and interference inhibition. Social support may reduce adversity, buffer its effects, or influence appraisal; these are distinct possibilities. Fonagy et al. [46, 47] connect resilience with mentalization and epistemic trust: understanding behavior in terms of mental states may support appraisal, while openness to personally relevant, generalizable social communication may allow established interpretations to be revised. These capacities are related in their conceptual account but are not interchangeable. Epistemic mistrust may initially be adaptive in adverse childhood environments, but its persistence may restrict learning and adaptation when those circumstances change. Seen through Sameroff's regulation and representation perspectives, these accounts distinguish opportunities for support from the capacities through which support may be used. In our synthesis, changes in either of them may have far reaching consequences for better or worse, potentially reinforcing or modifying established patterns. Such opportunities extend beyond caregiving to other, wider social settings [20]. Nevertheless, difficulties may not originate as useful adaptations, and their persistence may reflect continuing adversity as well as established expectations or responses. These accounts therefore connect opportunities for adaptive change with both individual functioning and actual environmental conditions.

The timing, duration, and order of these relationships are also significant. Whereas transactions refer to reciprocal person-environment changes, developmental cascades concern how functioning in one period may influence competencies and traits in later periods [24]. Early caregiving may be particularly relevant to emerging co-regulation and relational expectations; peer relationships, increasingly complex mentalization, and identity formation play a significant role later on. Such shifts do not establish BPD-specific sensitive periods, nor should timing lead us to ignore exposure duration or cumulative adversity. In our integration, earlier patterns of functioning may also shape how subsequent challenges are interpreted and negotiated, apart from contributing through accumulated risk. A process may therefore change its developmental function: a response that facilitates adaptation under one set of demands may restrict functioning under another. However, the complete cascade proposed for BPD remains unvalidated, and whether more than one such cascade exists is a topic for empirical study. This distinguishes the formation of a pattern from its situational activation, persistence, and modification. These are separate explanatory questions, not universal developmental stages; a process contributing to initial formation need not be the process that later sustains it.

The developmental outcome under consideration also depends on how borderline pathology is defined. In the categorical Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR) classification, BPD is diagnosed through a specified combination of clinical criteria [48]. The Alternative Model for Personality Disorders (AMPD), presented in Section III of DSM-5-TR, incorporates dimensional assessments of self and interpersonal functioning and maladaptive personality traits, while retaining BPD as a diagnostic type. Similarly, the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11) [4] emphasizes personality disorder severity and trait domains, while allowing an additional borderline pattern qualifier [49]. Kernberg's borderline personality organization and the borderline level of personality organization described in the Psychodynamic Diagnostic Manual, Second Edition (PDM-2) emphasize structural aspects of functioning, particularly identity integration, defensive functioning, and reality testing. The PDM-2 additionally describes borderline personality as a style or type, distinct from the borderline level of organization [39, 50]. Within this psychoanalytic distinction, level of organization concerns the severity and structural integration of personality functioning, whereas style or type concerns its characteristic pattern. Borderline organization may therefore underlie different personality styles and does not itself denote categorical BPD. Although these frameworks describe overlapping clinical phenomena, they are not interchangeable. Developmental explanations should therefore clearly specify whether they refer to a clinical pattern, dimensional impairment, or the underlying organization of personality.

Clarifying the outcomes to be explained also requires distinguishing the developmental roles assigned to different processes. Within the theories reviewed above, antecedent vulnerabilities (characteristics that may increase subsequent risk without determining outcomes) may include emotional sensitivity and impulsivity; developmental conditions may include caregiving and other interpersonal experiences that may increase or reduce risk; and difficulties in emotion regulation, mentalization, or self-other representations may function as intervening processes, emerging manifestations, or factors contributing to the persistence of borderline pathology. However, the same characteristic or process may be included in different categories across development. In particular, moderators (factors that alter the relationship between developmental conditions and later outcomes) should be distinguished from mediators (hypothesized intervening processes that may explain such relationships). Moreover, bidirectional causation requires specific testing, as observed associations may also reflect shared vulnerabilities, individual influences on the environment, or consequences of emerging psychopathology [19]. Thus, the developmental accounts discussed here identify plausible relationships and competing interpretations, but their etiological significance depends on establishing temporal relationships and evaluating alternative explanations. These theories and models provide a basis for examining how biology, epigenetics, environmental, social, and psychological factors may influence one another, while retaining distinctions among their proposed mechanisms and outcomes. The following sections critically evaluate the corresponding evidence, distinguishing findings concerning BPD from broader developmental associations. Section 7 then draws these findings together into possible developmental pathways and identifies testable propositions and directions for future research.

3.1. Genetic Influences

Genetic contributions to BPD have been increasingly substantiated by both twin studies and largescale genomic research. Heritability estimates suggest that approximately 0.35-0.65 of the variance in BPD traits can be attributed to genetic influences with a recent, large Swedish study estimating heritability of liability to diagnosed BPD at 0.46 (95% Confidence Interval [CI] = 0.39 -0.53) [51] and Amad et al. [52] reporting an estimated heritability of about 0.40. Skoglund et al. [51] found that full siblings of individuals diagnosed with BPD had a 4.7-fold higher hazard (95% CI = 3.9 -5.6) of receiving the same diagnosis compared to individuals without an affected sibling. For cousins whose parents were full siblings, the hazard was 1.7 fold higher (Hazard Ratio [HR] = 1.7; 95% CI = 1.4 -2.0).

Witt et al. [53] conducted the first case-control genome-wide association study (GWAS) of BPD. Gene-based analyses identified significant associations with DPYD, involved in uracil and thymidine metabolism and previously associated with schizophrenia (SCZ) and bipolar disorder (BP), and PKP4 (Plakophilin-4), involved in cell adhesion and cytoskeletal organization. The previously reported association of serine incorporator 5 (SERINC5) with borderline personality features reached only nominal significance.

Streit et al. [54] published the largest GWAS meta-analysis to date of BPD. They identified six genome-wide significant loci in the discovery analysis and five additional loci in the combined analysis, alongside nine genes in gene-based analyses; single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-based heritability was estimated at 17.3% on the liability scale (in line with other psychiatric disorders where the SNP-based heritability is approximately one third of heritability estimates of twin studies).

Recent GWAS indicate that BPD shares significant genetic overlap with several major psychiatric disorders, including post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD; rg = 0.77), depression (rg = 0.74), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD; rg = 0.67), SCZ (rg = 0.48), and BP (rg = 0.47).

Significant genetic correlations were also observed with loneliness, externalizing traits, neuroticism, chronic pain, antisocial behavior, and measures of suicide and self-harm. Together, these findings support substantial transdiagnostic genetic liability and a polygenic, pleiotropic architecture, but do not establish mechanisms specific to BPD [54]. Complementing molecular genetic findings, a large Swedish multigenerational study reported familial co-aggregation between BPD and multiple psychiatric conditions, including PTSD, depressive disorders, ADHD, other personality disorders, and drug use disorder, with contributions from both genetic and unique environmental factors [55].

Reflecting this complexity, research has advanced toward polygenic risk models and geneenvironment interaction frameworks, which may help clarify how biological predisposition interplays with developmental experiences [54, 56].

Overall, genetic evidence supports substantial heritable liability to BPD, although the mechanisms linking genetic predisposition, developmental experiences, and divergent psychiatric outcomes remain insufficiently understood.

3.2. Neurobiological Findings

Neuroimaging studies of BPD report heterogeneous, task-dependent alterations in fronto-limbic activation, possible reductions in amygdala and hippocampal volumes, and reduced amygdala habituation associated with childhood adversity, without establishing a BPD-specific neural signature [57] [58] [59] [60] [61] [62] [63] [64] [65] [66]. In a longitudinal neuroimaging study, Constantino-Pettit et al. [67] found that lower estimated amygdala volume at age 13 was associated with greater borderline symptoms during adolescence, although whether this difference preceded symptom onset remains unclear. 3.3. Neuroendocrine and Neurochemical Factors

Psychoneuroendocrine research shows that BPD is often accompanied by dysregulation of stressresponse systems. Findings on hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis functioning are heterogeneous and depend partly on measurement methods, with meta-analytic evidence of increased continuous cortisol output but blunted responses to psychosocial stress. Although early adversity may contribute to altered stress regulation, the underlying causal and epigenetic mechanisms remain uncertain [68] [69] [70] [71]. A randomized placebo-controlled study found blunted cortisol responses to psychosocial stress despite greater subjective distress in women with BPD, while mineralocorticoid receptor stimulation reduced cortisol concentrations in both patients and healthy controls without supporting enhanced mineralocorticoid receptor-mediated feedback as the primary explanation for blunted stress responses in BPD [72].

Oxytocin-related findings in BPD suggest possible links with interpersonal functioning, but do not indicate a uniform biological alteration. Lower plasma oxytocin concentrations and reduced oxytocin receptor expression in peripheral blood mononuclear cells have been reported [73, 74], whereas a study of salivary oxytocin found no significant BPD-control difference [75]. Lower plasma oxytocin was associated with greater attachment-related preoccupation [74], while lower receptor expression, but not plasma oxytocin, was associated with overmentalization errors in a small BPD sample [76]. Plasma oxytocin increased during psychotherapy in one study [74], but this observational change does not establish a treatment mechanism; intranasal oxytocin has also produced mixed interpersonal effects [17, 77]. Overall, differences in sampling, measurement, and interpersonal context limit mechanistic interpretation and BPD specificity; see Uzar et al. [77] for a recent review. Serotonergic candidate-gene studies have investigated associations between BPD and related clinical features, including impulsivity, suicidality, and affective instability, focusing on variants involving tryptophan hydroxylase 1 and 2 (TPH1/2), serotonin receptors 2A and 2C (HTR2A/C), and the serotonin-transporter-linked polymorphic region (5-HTTLPR) of SLC6A4, with some studies reporting positive findings [78] [79] [80] [81]. However, findings are inconsistent, and meta-analytic evidence has not established significant associations for several commonly investigated serotonergic variants [52, 82]. Exploratory genotype-stratified analyses suggested a possible gene-environment interaction involving 5-HTTLPR and serious life events in relation to impulsivity among patients with BPD [80].

Nevertheless, evidence that this variant confers heightened sensitivity to both adverse and supportive environments, as predicted by differential susceptibility theory, remains insufficient [15, 56].

3.4. Inflammatory and Oxidative Stress Pathways

Findings on inflammation and oxidative stress in BPD are heterogeneous, with several studies reporting increased immune activation and oxidative imbalance, while one study identified a subgroup within a BPD sample with relatively elevated antioxidant and anti-inflammatory markers associated with longer illness duration and lower anxiety severity, potentially reflecting compensatory regulation. Overall, these results suggest that immune and redox dysregulation may be associated with clinical features of BPD, but their causal contributions remain unestablished [94] [95] [96]. These alterations, though heterogeneous, may converge with neuroendocrine and monoaminergic dysregulation to sustain chronic stress sensitivity and affective instability characteristic of BPD, potentially interacting with epigenetic processes through which environmental adversity may become biologically embedded [94, 95, 97, 98].

4. Epigenetic Factors

BPD is strongly associated with early-life adversity, making the study of epigenetic regulation relevant to understanding how environmental experiences may become biologically embedded.

Epigenetic processes can influence gene regulation without altering DNA sequence. In BPD, differential methylation has been reported at selected loci involved in stress response, neurotransmission and neuroplasticity; however, these associations do not establish that adversityinduced epigenetic changes cause the disorder.

Epigenetic regulation includes DNA methylation, histone modifications that influence chromatin accessibility, and processes involving non-coding RNAs, such as microRNAs (miRNAs).

Direct research in BPD has focused predominantly on DNA methylation; evidence concerning histone modifications and non-coding RNAs is more limited.

Reported DNA methylation differences in BPD vary across loci, tissues, and studies, including findings involving genes implicated in stress response, neuroplasticity, and monoaminergic signaling, suggesting potential molecular pathways through which early stress exposure may shape emotional reactivity and behavioural regulation [97, [99] [100] [101] [102]. However, these pathways have not been causally established in BPD.

Treatment-associated changes involving brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and, in a small exploratory study, FK506-binding protein 5 (FKBP5) have been observed, but they do not demonstrate reversal of an etiological alteration [101] [102] [103]. For example, BDNF methylation decreased among psychotherapy responders in one study although average methylation increased across the treated sample [102]. Another study observed decreased salivary but not blood BDNF methylation during treatment, without a correlation between methylation change and symptom improvement [103].

These findings are compatible with biological embedding but do not yet explain why similar adversity is followed by BPD in some individuals and by other disorders or no disorder in others.

Psychiatric-comparator studies improve assessment of specificity, but differences in adversity, comorbidity and other sample characteristics complicate interpretation; follow-up studies have also produced null findings and unsuccessful replications [98, [104] [105] [106].

Animal studies suggest that early-life stress can alter histone acetylation, with effects depending on genetic background and developmental context. In mice, changes in histone H4 acetylation have been interpreted as potentially compensatory in relation to anxiety-and depressionlike behavior and antidepressant responsiveness [107]. Research on depression and experimental models links histone-modifying enzymes, including histone deacetylases (HDACs) and histone acetyltransferases (HATs), to BDNF regulation and neuronal plasticity [108]. These findings provide general mechanistic context and suggest pathways that may also be relevant to BPD, although direct BPD-specific evidence remains limited.

Non-coding RNAs, particularly miRNAs, regulate post-transcriptional gene expression and have been implicated in stress-related processes in broader psychiatric and experimental research [109] [110] [111]. In BPD, a genome-wide study reported methylation associations near MIR124-3 and MIR137 with diagnostic status and/or childhood maltreatment [98], while a separate exploratory study examined circulating miR-124-3p, globus pallidus (GP) volume, and spatial co-expression of target genes using reference brain-expression data [112]. Research outside BPD has implicated miRNAs in stress-related processes and psychiatric vulnerability [111, 113], while genetic findings have implicated the MIR137 locus and predicted miR-137 target genes in schizophrenia susceptibility [114]. These findings provide a rationale for investigating the potential relevance of miR-137 to BPD, particularly its possible involvement in pathways linking environmental adversity, gene regulation, and psychiatric vulnerability.

Boström et al. [115] reported significant epigenetic age acceleration as measured by GrimAge in women with a history of suicide attempts and BPD compared with healthy controls. Other epigenetic age measures did not show comparable group differences. Notably, past alcohol or substance abuse, psychotropic medication use, self-reported exposure to violent behavior in youth or adulthood, later completed suicide, and age at first suicide attempt were not significantly associated with GrimAge acceleration within the BPD group in the reported analyses, whereas tobacco use showed a strong association. These findings raise the possibility of altered biological aging in some individuals with BPD and motivate further research into how early adversity and other exposures relate to methylation-based aging measures.

Epigenetic research in BPD remains limited by small, often selected samples, reliance on peripheral tissues, variable adjustment for smoking, medication, comorbidity and cell composition, and inconsistent replication. Predominantly cross-sectional designs leave uncertain whether observed methylation differences precede BPD or reflect environmental exposures, illness-related processes or treatment. Although reported associations involve stress-response, neurotransmission and neuroplasticity pathways, candidate-gene selection partly shapes the evidence, and clinical validity and BPD specificity remain to be established. Longitudinal studies beginning before symptom onset, with adversity-exposed and psychiatric comparison groups, independent replication and functional multi-omics analyses, could clarify the potential contribution of epigenetic processes to divergent developmental outcomes.

5. Environmental and Social Factors

Environmental and social conditions are associated with BPD and borderline features across development, but the strength and interpretation of these associations depend on study design, clinical comparison groups, and whether the outcome is dimensional symptoms or diagnosed disorder. This section evaluates childhood adversity, caregiving, peer environments, and broader social contexts as empirical antecedents or correlates rather than assuming that proposed developmental mechanisms have been causally established (Supplementary Table 4).

5.1 Childhood Adversity

Childhood adversity is frequently reported among individuals with BPD, although association magnitudes differ markedly across samples and designs. In a meta-analysis of 97 studies, Porter et al. [116] estimated that 71.1% of participants with BPD in contributing samples reported at least one adverse childhood experience (ACE), based on prevalence data from 42 studies. Case-control comparisons found substantially higher odds of reported adversity among individuals with BPD than among nonclinical controls (odds ratio [OR] = 13.91, 95% CI = 11.11 -17.43) or other psychiatric groups (OR = 3.15, 95% CI = 2.62 -3.79). These are exposure odds, not the risk of subsequently developing BPD [117]. Epidemiological analyses yielded a smaller estimate (OR = 2.56, 95% CI [1.24, 5.30]); the pooled cohort-study estimate was not statistically significant (OR = 2.59, 95% CI [0.93, 7.30]) [116]. Prospective etiological evidence remains considerably more limited than retrospective contrasts [116].

Adversity type, co-occurrence, and measurement further complicate interpretation. Emotional abuse and neglect produced large contrasts with nonclinical controls in Porter et al.'s [116] casecontrol analyses (OR = 38.11 and 17.73, respectively), but these estimates cannot be interpreted as independent causal contributions or as a stable ranking of risk factors. In youth aged 19 years or younger, Winsper et al. [118] reported associations for sexual abuse (OR = 4.88), physical abuse (OR = 2.79), maternal hostility/verbal abuse (OR = 3.28), and neglect (OR = 3.40). Because adversity types are highly correlated and comparison groups and exposure instruments vary, meta-analytic subgroup differences should not be interpreted as causal rankings. A multilevel meta-analysis found associations between childhood traumatic experiences and borderline features (pooled r=0.30) and between maladaptive parenting and such features (r=0.23), with generally smaller prospective than retrospective estimates [119].

Associations involving emotional abuse and neglect, together with the findings of Schulze et al. [120], highlight chronic relational adversity as a relevant area of investigation in borderline pathology. Emerging evidence points to potential psychological pathways linking ACEs to BPD features. Emotion dysregulation has been identified as a statistical mediator of associations between childhood abuse and BPD features, as well as broader personality disorder traits [121], although the corresponding indirect associations were not found for a broader ACE composite in the study's transdiagnostic analysis. Schulze et al. [120] further reported that emotional abuse may serve as a key link between ACEs and BPD features, while identity disturbance was associated with attachment anxiety; attachment and perceived social support partly accounted for the adversity-BPD association.

Yuan et al. [122] also support the importance of emotional abuse as an independently associated maltreatment subtype, with emotion dysregulation statistically mediating part of the maltreatment-BPD feature association. Schmitz et al. [123] suggest that body dissociation might be a potential link between ACEs and emotion regulation. They found lower levels of body awareness and higher levels of body dissociation in BPD patients in comparison to healthy controls. Body dissociation was positively associated with childhood trauma and emotion dysregulation and statistically mediated their association within the BPD group. Taken together, these findings highlight emotional abuse and neglect potentially as important forms of relational adversity associated with borderline pathology, with emotion dysregulation emerging as a candidate intervening process. Additional associated processes, such as attachment anxiety and body dissociation, further suggest possible pathways through which early adversity may relate to later psychological functioning. However, these predominantly cross-sectional findings do not establish causal developmental mediation or BPDspecific protective effects of social support.

More recently, Vonderlin et al. [124] assessed co-occurring adverse experiences in BPD, psychiatric-control, and nonclinical groups. Emotional abuse, bullying, parental unpredictability, and lack of safety showed conditional associations with current BPD symptom severity, but the crosssectional network cannot establish temporal order or BPD-specific causes.

These environmental influences intersect with genetic, neurobiological, and epigenetic pathways relevant to personality development and vulnerability to BPD [125]. Clinically, BPD and cPTSD show partial symptom overlap but distinct underlying configurations [5]. Nevertheless, it is possible that cPTSD overlaps with some presentations characterized by persistent subsyndromal BPD features [126], particularly when interpersonal avoidance rather than pronounced interpersonal instability predominates. However, this possibility remains a diagnostic hypothesis rather than an established equivalence between the two conditions [127].

Importantly, trauma is neither necessary nor sufficient for the development of BPD. In a qualitative study of 12 pairs of sisters reporting comparable childhood maltreatment, sisters without current psychopathology described greater emotion-regulation capacities, future orientation, and access to supportive relationships than their sisters with BPD. These retrospective accounts identify possible protective processes but do not establish their temporal or causal roles [128]. A scoping review likewise identified associations between lower resilience scores and borderline pathology, but heterogeneous measures and limited longitudinal evidence leave unresolved whether reduced resilience precedes or follows BPD [129]. Genetic, temperamental, and environmental factors may interact to shape development, although the specific developmental processes linking adversity to borderline pathology remain incompletely understood. Prospective multi-informant research is needed to distinguish antecedent vulnerabilities, environmental exposures, intervening processes, and alternative psychiatric or adaptive outcomes [2, 130].

5.2 Parental Psychopathology and Caregiving

The early caregiver-child relationship is central to personality development, and attachment difficulties are frequently associated with BPD. Individuals with BPD commonly exhibit insecure or disorganized attachment patterns, which may be associated with histories of inconsistent, frightening, or emotionally unsupportive caregiving, although these associations do not establish developmental causation [35, 131, 132]. A meta-analysis of adult romantic attachment found that attachment anxiety is strongly correlated with BPD traits (r = 0.48), while attachment avoidance also shows a significant, albeit smaller, association (r = 0.30), without establishing whether these patterns originated in early caregiving experiences [133].

Prospective community-based evidence suggests that prenatal family adversity, maladaptive parenting practices, and high parental conflict in childhood are associated with a greater likelihood of borderline symptoms by early adolescence, supporting the potential developmental relevance of family environments without establishing a causal pathway [134].

Parental psychopathology is also associated with offspring borderline features, including findings involving parental substance dependence, maternal depression, antisocial pathology, and borderline traits [135, 136], although parental depression and antisocial behavior did not predict adolescent borderline symptom trajectories in another longitudinal study [137]. Familial transmission may involve both genetic and environmental contributions, including adverse caregiving, with their relative importance varying across parental characteristics and family contexts [136].

Parental emotional unavailability and neglect, maternal and paternal invalidation, maternal inconsistency, hostility and emotional overinvolvement, low warmth, boundary dissolution, family life stress, maternal and paternal conflict, and harsh punishment have been investigated as potential antecedents or correlates of borderline features, although prospective evidence varies across these factors [134, [137] [138] [139]. Patients frequently retrospectively describe their parents as overinvolved, indifferent, neglectful, or invalidating, and often recall a home atmosphere experienced as conflictual or inconsistent [137] [138] [139]. However, retrospective reports cannot independently establish the temporal or causal role of these experiences.

Adoption and twin-family comparisons found associations between maternal BPD traits, adverse caregiving, and offspring borderline features in both biological and adoptive families, consistent with environmental contributions. In contrast, associations involving paternal BPD traits and parental externalizing psychopathology were more consistent with genetic transmission [136].

However, in a complementary observational study, Bunz et al. [140] found no significant differences in observed adaptive emotion-regulation strategies among 318 young children of mothers with BPD, mothers with anxiety or depressive disorders, and healthy controls, while maladaptive strategies were too infrequent for meaningful group comparisons; later developmental differences remain possible.

Maternal interpersonal emotion regulation strategies were associated with adolescent BPD symptom severity: the association between adolescent negative affect and BPD severity was stronger when maternal problem-solving was high but support/validation was low, and attenuated when support/validation was higher. However, these measures did not predict subsequent changes in BPD symptoms [141]. Parent-child interactions are bidirectional, as adolescent behavior can also predict subsequent changes in parental responses [142], with reciprocal associations also reported between parenting and adolescent borderline features [137].

Exposure to interparental intimate partner violence has been prospectively associated with elevated adolescent borderline features and slower subsequent declines [143], while adult studies link borderline traits with both intimate partner violence perpetration and victimization [144], suggesting that interpersonal violence may represent both a developmental antecedent and a perpetuating interpersonal context. Relationship discord and lack of closeness with parents are also associated with impaired adolescent personality functioning [145]. Conversely, perceived parental closeness is associated with lower BPD severity, with lower identity diffusion potentially accounting for part of this association [146].

Taken together, genetic or temperamental vulnerabilities may interact with caregiving environments marked by psychopathology, invalidation, or violence in the development of borderline features. A large meta-analysis reported findings broadly consistent with transactional accounts, including associations involving early psychopathology, invalidating experiences, and emotionregulation difficulties [119]. Longitudinal evidence associates specific family experiences, including exposure to interparental violence and poorer father-adolescent relationship quality, with less favorable trajectories of adolescent borderline features; evidence that other assessed parenting practices predict symptom persistence or that maternal support/validation mitigates progression remains limited [147]. Broader social contexts -peer, school, and community environments -further shape whether these vulnerabilities are reinforced or attenuated.

5.3. Peer, Structural, and Cultural Contexts

Peer experiences may contribute to the developmental context of borderline pathology, but findings concerning environmental antecedents must be distinguished from interpersonal difficulties accompanying existing borderline features. A scoping review of 39 studies identified associations between borderline features and difficulties involving friendships, peer victimization, and aggression; only eight studies examined more than one peer domain, limiting conclusions about their interrelationships and developmental direction [148].

Prospective research suggests that individual characteristics may precede both later peer difficulties and borderline features. In a longitudinal cohort of 153 children, lower preschool conscientiousness showed nominal associations with poorer school-age peer relationships and higher adolescent borderline features, but these associations did not survive correction for multiple testing, and mediation through peer relationships was not supported [149].

Chronic school-behavior stress in adolescence has been prospectively associated with BPD symptoms in young adulthood, whereas peer-relationship stress did not remain independently associated after adjustment for other risk factors [150]. Individuals with BPD features often struggle to form and maintain friendships, reporting lower perceived support and greater conflict [148]. In one adolescent study, borderline characteristics were associated with less friendship support among boys and greater friendship conflict among both boys and girls [148]. Peer functioning may improve following treatment; a small uncontrolled study reported improved peer attachment after a one-year program of group-based mentalization-based treatment (MBT) [148, 151].

Bullying victimization is associated with borderline features [148]. Longitudinal data indicate that victimization -but not perpetration -statistically mediates part of the continuity of borderline traits from childhood to adolescence [152]. Findings from the Avon Longitudinal Study of Parents and Children (ALSPAC) cohort (n = 6050; 51.4% female) show that chronic self-reported bullying victimization was associated with more than fivefold higher odds of exhibiting five or more BPD symptoms at approximately age 12 (OR = 5.44; 95% CI, 3.86-7.66), after adjustment for measured confounders [153]. In another analysis of overlapping ALSPAC participants, children with greater behavioral dysregulation were more likely to experience bullying, which was subsequently associated with borderline symptoms. This indirect statistical association was significantly stronger for borderline symptoms than for depressive or psychotic symptoms, but was not unique to BPD [154].

Interpersonal violence and relational adversity may also be relevant to BPD development. Adolescent dating violence victimization has been prospectively associated with later internalizing and externalizing symptoms, substance use, and revictimization in general adolescent samples, although findings vary across studies [155]. In a five-year longitudinal study of 818 adolescent daters, borderline features prospectively predicted relational, psychological, and physical dating-violence victimization, primarily among girls, whereas psychological victimization predicted later borderline features more consistently among boys, and sexual victimization predicted later borderline features among girls [156]. In a cross-sectional study, adults with BPD reported greater childhood adversity and earlier sexual debut than those without BPD. A modeled trade-off favoring short-term reproduction over somatic maintenance was associated with an additional 56.5% relative risk of BPD diagnosis at the highest adversity level, although this statistical association does not establish a causal developmental pathway [157].

Prospective studies suggest associations in both temporal directions between borderline features and peer victimization. Children with borderline features at age 12 were more likely to experience peer and cyber-victimization by age 18, even after controlling for emotional and behavioral problems [158], although genetically informative analyses implicated shared genetic liability in many associations with adverse outcomes. Physical victimization predicted relative increases in borderline features over six months among Japanese preadolescents [159]. Findings concerning aggression vary across samples: borderline features predicted later relational aggression among girls with heightened physiological reactivity in one longitudinal study [160], whereas physical rather than relational aggression predicted increases in borderline features in the Japanese sample [159]. In another analysis of the Preschool Depression Study cohort, Naeem et al. [161] found that higher early-childhood relational aggression was prospectively associated with later borderline features after correction for multiple testing, whereas bullying-victimization trajectories were not significant predictors. Because early borderline features were not assessed, the direction of this association remains uncertain. This analysis and Huntington et al. [149] drew on the same parent cohort and do not constitute independent replications. In a cross-sectional adolescent study, jealousy statistically mediated the association between borderline features and cyberbullying perpetration [162].

Beyond interpersonal dynamics, socioeconomic disadvantage adds another layer of vulnerability. Low socioeconomic status (SES), including receipt of social assistance, predicts later borderline features across multiple studies [2]. Low childhood executive functioning also predicts later BPD diagnosis, while an exploratory interaction with low SES was associated with higher dimensional borderline features [163]. In one longitudinal cohort, African American girls and those living in poverty had higher average BPD symptom levels during adolescence [137]. Communitylevel data reveal that the treated incidence of borderline personality pathology was higher in more socially fragmented neighborhoods and certain categories of socioeconomic deprivation, although these findings do not establish effects on disorder onset [164].

Although its role in linking socioeconomic disadvantage to BPD development remains unestablished, shame is associated with borderline pathology: adolescents with BPD features report higher explicit shame than psychiatric peers [165], and daily fluctuations in shame were associated with concurrent hostile irritability among adolescent girls with elevated BPD symptoms, with this within-person association observed only among those of average SES [166]. In adult women with BPD, greater shame and a relatively more shame-prone implicit self-concept have been reported, alongside associations between shame-proneness and lower self-esteem [167].

Cultural contexts may influence how borderline features are expressed and interpreted, although comparative evidence on BPD-specific developmental risk factors remains limited [168, 169]. National data from South Korea show variation in recorded BPD diagnoses across income groups and regions, without establishing whether this reflects underlying prevalence, diagnostic recognition, or access to services [170]. Cross-cultural research also suggests differences in self-harm methods, with self-poisoning more frequently reported in the included Eastern than Western samples, although study heterogeneity and limited BPD-specific evidence restrict generalization [171].

Conceptually, the relational findings reviewed above are compatible with transactional developmental models, in which individual vulnerabilities and environmental experiences may influence one another over time [13, 137]. Over time, these reciprocal interactions may compound psychological burden and contribute to a more persistent course of borderline pathology in some individuals.

In sum, environmental and social contexts may contribute to developmental vulnerability to BPD. Evidence from peer victimization and socioeconomic adversity, alongside findings concerning cultural influences on symptom expression and recognition, highlights the relevance of broader social environments, although their cumulative interactions and causal contributions remain uncertain [2, 148]. Etiological models should consider social contexts alongside familial and individual factors, including potential protective influences that require further investigation. Such environments may inform prevention research and psychotherapeutic approaches, although whether modifying them reduces BPD incidence remains to be established.

6. Psychological Factors

Although biological, epigenetic, and environmental factors have been implicated in BPD, their potential influences may converge within psychological functioning. This dimension reflects how temperament, early relationships, and defensive adaptations shape enduring patterns of thought, emotion, and behavior. Together, these perspectives suggest how psychological mechanisms may mediate biological and social influences, contributing to the chronic instability characteristic of BPD.

6.1 Temperament, Traits, and Schemas

As discussed in Section 2, attachment and schema theories propose that insecure and disorganized attachment associated with inconsistent or frightening caregiving may contribute to lasting psychological vulnerability. Children may develop internal working models of self as unworthy of care and of others as unreliable or rejecting [132]. Over time, these models may contribute to maladaptive cognitive schemas -abandonment, mistrust, or defectiveness -that shape perception and emotion in later relationships [41]. Attachment ruptures may therefore form part of the developmental ground for enduring schema-level vulnerabilities linking early adversity to BPD vulnerability.

However, the cited evidence does not establish this complete developmental sequence.

Temperament adds another layer of vulnerability. It reflects biologically based tendencies in emotional and behavioral response. Longitudinal studies link impulsivity, aggression, and dysregulation -as well as ADHD and oppositional symptoms -to later BPD features [2, 172, 173].

These findings suggest an early diathesis paralleling the biological risks discussed earlier.

In adolescence, emerging borderline features often co-occur with substance use, eating disorders, and self-injury [13, 174]. Prospective associations with internalizing and externalizing psychopathology vary across studies [2]. In a prospective study of girls, poor behavioral control and interpersonal difficulties helped distinguish predictor profiles for later borderline versus depressive symptoms, while mood/anxiety difficulties and parenting patterns differentiated borderline from conduct-symptom profiles [175]. These findings concern prediction of later symptom levels, rather than new onset or established causal pathways. A large longitudinal twin study found that common genetic influences substantially accounted for associations between retrospectively reported childhood abuse and adult borderline traits, although small nonshared environmental associations remained [172]. These findings provided little support for a causal effect in this sample, but retrospective abuse reporting and assessment of borderline traits rather than diagnosed BPD limit broader conclusions about maltreatment's etiological role. Other genetically informed studies have yielded mixed findings: Skaug et al. [176] similarly found little support for a causal effect of childhood trauma on borderline traits, whereas Belsky et al. [177] and Arneberg et al. [178] found associations that persisted after accounting for genetic and environmental factors shared by twins, although they examined childhood borderline-related characteristics and broader stressful life events, respectively. Taken together, these studies suggest that shared genetic and environmental influences explain part -and, in some studies, much -of the association between adversity and borderline features, while findings concerning an additional causal contribution of adversity remain mixed. Adversity therefore remains a plausible developmental contributor, but its causal role cannot be inferred from the strength of its association with BPD alone.

These developmental observations are complemented by trait-based personality research.

BPD is associated with a Big Five personality profile of high Neuroticism and low Agreeableness and Conscientiousness. Extraversion also tends to be lower, while Openness shows mixed results [179] [180] [181] [182]. GWAS data show a significant genetic correlation between BPD and Neuroticism (rg = 0.34) and a nominally significant correlation with Openness (rg = 0.24), but no statistically significant correlations were detected with the other traits [183]. Twin-family data similarly report strong positive associations of borderline personality features with Neuroticism (r = 0.68) and moderate negative ones with Agreeableness and Conscientiousness. Much of this overlap reflects shared genetic influences, especially Neuroticism [184]. Personality traits show lower rank-order stability in BPD than in other personality disorders, particularly Neuroticism and Conscientiousness [185].

Improvement in symptoms parallels decreases in Neuroticism and increases in Agreeableness and Conscientiousness [186].

Alternative models offer complementary descriptions. Research using Cloninger's Temperament and Character Inventory (TCI) associates borderline pathology with higher Novelty Seeking [187]. The HEXACO framework likewise links borderline traits to higher Emotionality and lower Conscientiousness and Extraversion in a nonclinical sample [188].

Temperament operates within reciprocal dynamics. As discussed in Section 2, children's emotional responses and caregiving may influence one another, although evidence for greater responsiveness to both adverse and supportive environments specifically in BPD development remains limited.

Cognitive models propose that early vulnerabilities may contribute to maladaptive cognitive schemas -enduring beliefs about self, others, and the world [41]. Individuals with BPD often hold schemas of unlovability, abandonment, or defectiveness that shape perception and emotion.

Maladaptive schemas are elevated relative to healthy controls but also occur across other disorders [189]. An experimental study found negative interpersonal evaluations without consistent evidence of dichotomous thinking in the task used [190]. An e-diary study found greater self-esteem instability in BPD than in anxiety disorders and healthy controls, whereas affective instability did not distinguish the two clinical groups [191]. This finding does not establish a developmental pathway or equate selfesteem instability with identity disturbance.

In sum, temperament, trait dispositions, and maladaptive schemas may contribute to BPD vulnerability and persistence. Whether their combinations or developmental sequences distinguish BPD from other outcomes remains unresolved.

6.2 Psychodynamic Perspective on Borderline Personality Disorder

Psychodynamic theories have long linked borderline pathology to early relational development. Kernberg [39] emphasized splitting, identity diffusion, and difficulties integrating self-other representations, as discussed in Section 2. Other psychodynamic accounts emphasized limited internal soothing, fear of aloneness, failure of evocative memory, and conflicts between dependence and autonomy [34, [192] [193] [194]. Mentalization-based formulations propose that disruptions in early attachment relationships may impair reflective functioning and affect regulation [195, 196]. Supplementary Table 5 compares these models, their proposed mechanisms, and relevant evidence.

Studies associate borderline features with hypermentalizing -the overinterpretation of others' mental states -in adolescent psychiatric samples [197, 198]. However, a meta-analysis found no significantly stronger association between hypermentalizing and BPD than between hypermentalizing and other psychopathology [199]. This pattern is consistent with aspects of mentalization-based formulations but does not establish the proposed developmental pathway from early attachment disturbances to impaired reflective functioning and BPD.

In sum, psychodynamic theories emphasize disturbances in self-experience and relationships alongside emotional dysregulation, while differing in their proposed mechanisms. These perspectives complement the examination of emotional and behavioral regulation that follows.

6.3 Emotional and Behavioral Dysregulation

Central to BPD's psychology is difficulty regulating intense emotions. Individuals with BPD experience rapid and extreme mood shifts -e.g. plunging from calm to despair or rage within minutes in response to a perceived slight [13]. An experience-sampling study also found that arousal predicted subsequent perceived rejection, without significant differences in this temporal association between BPD, depressive disorders, and healthy controls [200].

Compared with healthy controls, individuals with BPD report relying less on adaptive emotion regulation strategies (reappraisal, problem solving, acceptance) and more on maladaptive ones (suppression, rumination, avoidance); compared with other psychiatric groups, they report greater rumination and avoidance and lower problem solving and acceptance [201]. A systematic review of 55 studies found that individuals with BPD can use adaptive strategies when instructed, although differences vary by strategy and comparison group [202]. Selby et al. [203] proposed the emotional cascade model, in which rumination amplifies distress, prompting dysregulated relief behaviors such as self-injury, binge eating, or aggression -linking affective instability and impulsivity.

Research has also examined emotion recognition and shame. Findings on facial emotion recognition in BPD are mixed [204]. A meta-analysis of 10 studies found greater reported shame in individuals with BPD than in healthy controls [205]. In a prospective study of women with BPD receiving treatment, shame predicted subsequent self-inflicted injury, although results varied by shame measure and adjustment for fear [206].

BPD is also associated with cognitive impairments, notably in inhibition and long-term spatial memory [207]. A meta-analysis found steeper discounting of delayed rewards and poorer Iowa Gambling Task performance, while no significant difference in reversal learning was detected [208].

Impulsivity is a prominent feature: self-reports show high impulsive traits, though task-based deficits are inconsistent. Impulsive behavior may occur in response to strong affect [209]. A metaanalysis [210] reported small-to-moderate motor inhibitory control deficits in BPD compared with healthy controls, with no significant moderation by emotional factors. Self-reported impulsivity was not significantly associated with task performance in the meta-regression analyses, suggesting that these measures may capture different facets of impulsivity.

Impulsivity in BPD is associated with both self-injurious behavior and aggression toward others [211]. An earlier systematic review found no clear independent association between BPD and violence in the general population; reported predictors of violence include childhood maltreatment, prior violence, and comorbid antisocial traits [212]. Using the DSM-5 Alternative Model for Personality Disorders (AMPD), Leucci et al. [213] found verbal and physical aggression associated with antagonism, and physical aggression additionally associated with callousness. Neuroimaging evidence supports possible abnormalities within fronto-limbic networks underlying aggressive behavior [214].

In summary, BPD is associated with difficulties in emotional regulation, impulse control, and interpersonal functioning. Their possible developmental interactions are considered in Section 7.

7. Integrative Synthesis of Developmental Pathways and Future Directions

These accounts suggest one or more possible developmental courses toward BPD. Early in development, genetic, epigenetic, and prenatal influences may contribute to differences in emotional reactivity, recovery from arousal, activity, and emerging behavioral regulation. However, these characteristics do not necessarily lead to later BPD. Environmental factors may also influence biological development. The relevance of these early differences to borderline development may depend on reciprocal exchanges with caregivers and broader social environments, alongside the development of psychological capacities and ways of experiencing oneself. Within this framework, adversity-related pathways are proposed rather than established: genetically informed findings are mixed, and shared liabilities may account for part of the associations attributed to environmental experience (see Section 6.1).

Infants' characteristics and caregivers' capacities and circumstances jointly shape recurring exchanges. Greater emotional sensitivity or difficulty being soothed may increase demands for external regulation, while caregiver responses may ease distress, increase it, or inhibit its expression.

When the child is consistently invalidated or cannot rely on the caregivers' capacity to soothe them, difficulties regulating emotion and expectations that support will be unavailable might emerge. The infant's responses may, in turn, influence subsequent caregiving, improving or reinforcing these patterns. As previously proposed, early impulsivity may be a partly distinct vulnerability in a substantial subset of individuals who later develop BPD. These exchanges occur within broader family and ecological conditions that may support or constrain caregivers' capacity to respond. Also, plasticity factors, as conceptualized in differential susceptibility theory, could interact with these varied nurturing or adverse environmental conditions and, alongside risk and protective factors, further increase the complexity of the possible developmental outcomes.

During early childhood, emotion regulation may become increasingly connected with expectations about others' availability, the ability to identify and communicate emotional states, and increasingly differentiated representations of self and others. Under adverse circumstances, difficulties in these processes may play a role in the development of BPD or borderline personality organization.

Earlier relational patterns may increasingly shape self-appraisal. In the absence of responsive support, children may come to regard their emotional needs as illegitimate or difficulties obtaining comfort as evidence that they are bad or unacceptable. Conversely, with responsive support, emotional understanding and confidence in seeking help might develop. The development of the sense of agency might also be influenced by such experiences. The capacity to integrate contrasting experiences may help children maintain a sense of self and of being valued during conflict and accept contrasting impressions of another person as both caring and capable of causing disappointment. Otherwise, their experience of themselves and others may be strongly influenced by the emotional quality of an exchange.

As peer and school relationships become increasingly important, these developing patterns may acquire new implications. Anticipation of rejection may heighten emotional reactions to ambiguous social situations; intense distress may, in turn, impair interpretation of others' intentions, encourage withdrawal or urgent reassurance-seeking, and influence subsequent interpersonal responses. Attempts to secure closeness or protect against anticipated rejection may inadvertently strain relationships, making the support sought less available and reinforcing expectations of exclusion. Such exchanges may form vicious cycles in which interpersonal insecurity and emotional instability amplify one another. Repeated activation of biological systems involved in stress responses during these exchanges may also shape subsequent stress reactivity and recovery, potentially contributing to the persistence of these cycles. Experiences of acceptance and successful relational repair after conflict may interrupt these cycles and create opportunities to revise expectations of rejection.

Later on, during adolescence, increasing autonomy, closer relationships, and identity development may further alter the significance of these interconnected difficulties. Pubertal changes and the continuing maturation of systems involved in stress responses, reward processing, and behavioral control may interact with these earlier vulnerabilities in the context of new developmental demands. In some individuals, perceived separation or rejection may increasingly destabilize both their sense of self and their emotional state. In some circumstances, close relationships may come to feel essential for maintaining self-worth, while difficulties sustaining a coherent sense of oneself and a sense of meaning and connection in one's life may lead to chronic feelings of emptiness. Shame and rumination may also play a part, further amplifying distress and contributing to behaviors that only provide short-term relief but may maintain difficulties over time. Impulsive responses may bring additional conflict or loss, reinforcing interpersonal insecurity and negative self-appraisals.

Borderline-related features may become recognizable during childhood and, in some individuals, develop into clinically significant borderline pathology during adolescence. Their convergence may consolidate a pattern of affective, interpersonal, and identity instability, although its expression and severity vary across individuals. Responses that may initially have been adaptive under negative circumstances might persist after those circumstances change, eventually losing their adaptive value and becoming detrimental. This possible progression is neither necessary nor uniform. The proposed course links early vulnerabilities with the emergence of mutually reinforcing difficulties in emotion regulation, relationships, and self-experience that may come to resemble a borderline pattern. Similar vulnerabilities may be associated with different outcomes, while distinct combinations of experiences and developing capacities may contribute to borderline pathology. Supportive relationships and changing contexts may facilitate new regulatory capacities, interpretations, and adaptations; however, an individual's ability to benefit from these opportunities might vary. Findings consistent with differential susceptibility in borderline symptom development require further replication. Whether particular combinations, sequences, or reciprocal effects distinguish borderline trajectories from other forms of psychopathology remains an empirical question. Building on these possible developmental pathways, the priority is not simply to expand the list of potential etiological factors, but to clarify how previously identified vulnerabilities, experiences, and protective processes work together over time. The framework organizes candidate developmental relationships rather than specifying a single causal model. Its empirical value depends on whether these relationships improve explanation or prediction beyond simpler accounts.

A central question is why shared vulnerabilities lead to BPD rather than another disorder or sustained adaptation. For example, prospective research has identified partly distinct predictors of borderline, depressive, and conduct symptoms [175], without establishing causal pathways or distinguishing new onset from persistence of earlier borderline features. Building on this work, longitudinal studies following children into adulthood could examine how factors such as emotional vulnerability, poor behavioral control, and recurrent interpersonal difficulties combine and influence one another over time and whether these combinations are more strongly associated with later borderline pathology than with other outcomes. These studies should account for prior symptoms and comorbidity and include comparisons with other psychiatric and personality disorders and sustained adaptation in relationships and everyday life.

A second proposition concerns the temporal relations among perceived interpersonal rejection, self-evaluation, and affect, and whether they differ between BPD and other disorders. In experience sampling, arousal predicted subsequent perceived rejection without significant group differences [200]. Self-esteem instability was greater in BPD than in anxiety disorders, whereas no significant difference in affective instability was detected [191]. Related research found that higher negative affective arousal preceded lower self-esteem in BPD [215]. Although these findings do not establish a single sequence or a developmental pathway, studies combining intensive repeated assessments with longer-term follow-up could test competing temporal directions among these processes and whether the resulting patterns predict later borderline features more strongly than other psychiatric outcomes, after accounting for earlier symptoms. Assessments should distinguish interpersonal events from perceived rejection, changes in self-esteem from broader identity disturbance, and short-term fluctuations from developmental change.

Developmental transactions provide a third focus. Longitudinal findings on reciprocal parenting-child associations are mixed, with some prospective associations supported but not consistently across successive assessments [31, 216]. Observational research also links less mutual reinforcement of positive emotion during caregiver-child conflict with later borderline symptoms [217]. Repeated, multi-informant observations of conflict and repair could test whether failures to restore supportive interaction following distress predict poorer self-regulation and lower expectations of support and whether children's difficulties regulating emotion and behavior, in turn, predict changes in caregivers' responses. Models should compare reciprocal pathways with child-driven, caregiver-driven, and shared-liability explanations, using family and genetic information where available. Repeated biological measurements could examine whether changes in stress regulation precede or follow these transactions, without assuming mediation.

Protective pathways should be examined within the same developmental framework. Existing findings identify candidate protective processes but leave their causal roles unresolved [128, 129].

Prospective studies could test whether reliable support predicts better functioning and fewer borderline features following adversity, and whether this association depends on the developing capacity to seek and use support. Access, perceived availability, and actual use should be distinguished. Differential susceptibility requires evidence that the same measured characteristic predicts greater responsiveness to both adverse and supportive conditions, rather than vulnerability to adversity alone. Epigenetic research could examine whether DNA methylation changes help explain how biological vulnerability and experience jointly shape developmental outcomes. Building on studies tracking DNA methylation during treatment [101] [102] [103], repeated assessments of methylation, adversity, support, stress regulation, and borderline features, beginning before disorder onset, could test whether methylation changes precede or follow emerging difficulties. Comparisons among similarly exposed individuals developing BPD, other disorders, or sustained adaptation would address specificity. Analyses should account for genetic influences, blood-cell composition, smoking, medication, and age, with adjustment guided by their timing and role in the proposed pathway, without assuming that peripheral methylation reflects brain processes. Studies should compare competing explanations -adversity preceding methylation changes that predict later symptoms, symptoms preceding methylation changes, or shared influences contributing to both methylation changes and symptoms -while accounting for prior methylation and symptom levels. Candidate mechanisms would require independent replication and experiments modifying implicated methylation in relevant cellular models to test effects on gene expression or stress-related cellular responses.

Across these research questions and proposed hypotheses, studies should distinguish categorical BPD, dimensional borderline features, general personality dysfunction, and borderline personality organization. Repeated assessments should separate onset, persistence, and recovery, and within-person change from stable individual differences. Long-term follow-up indicates that sustained symptomatic remission is more common than recovery encompassing good social and vocational functioning, highlighting the importance of assessing symptoms and functioning separately [218].

Confirmatory tests should prespecify the population, developmental period, measures, temporal sequence, competing models, and findings that would count against the hypothesis. Replicated evidence favoring unidirectional or shared-liability explanations would challenge the corresponding reciprocal account. Failure to predict borderline outcomes better than comparison outcomes would weaken claims of preferential prediction of BPD. Responsiveness to adversity without greater responsiveness to support would not support differential susceptibility. Such findings should narrow or revise the relevant hypotheses. Interpretation requires sufficiently precise estimates and appropriate measurement timescales; nonsignificance alone is not decisive. Building on existing treatment-process research (e.g., [219]), intervention studies could use targeted interventions and randomized comparisons where feasible to test whether changes in candidate processes precede improvements in downstream processes, symptoms, and functioning, although improvement alone would not establish developmental etiology.

8. Conclusion

BPD can be understood within a developmental framework as arising from the convergence of biological, psychological, and social processes. Genetic, neurobiological, neuroendocrine, neurochemical, inflammatory, oxidative, and epigenetic processes may interact with adverse environments and psychological vulnerabilities. This review integrates existing perspectives to clarify how these relationships may contribute to divergent outcomes, including borderline pathology, other disorders, and sustained adaptation.
