Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Abstract

Because data-collection time in vision research and clinical assessment is limited, investigators must select stimuli that maximize diagnostic information with minimum redundancy. Analyzing individual differences, through factor analysis (FA), principal component analysis (PCA), and multidimensional scaling (MDS), offers a principled approach. The covariance structure of individual variation reveals the minimum number of independent factors underlying a visual function, and therefore the minimum number of stimuli needed for efficient, non-redundant characterization. I term this the One-Stimulus-Per-Factor Principle: one well-chosen stimulus per factor is necessary and, when validated, sufficient. A complementary rule governs discovery: at least two to three stimuli per expected factor are needed to establish the factor structure.

Converging evidence from luminance and chromatic contrast sensitivity, stereoscopic disparity, motion, hue scaling, face recognition, perceptual organization, and other domains shows that the dimensionality of individual variation is often lower than dense sampling suggests, and sometimes higher than sparse clinical testing captures. The approach also tests construct validity, revealing whether behavioral measures index the mechanisms investigators assume. These findings carry implications for clinical instruments across contrast sensitivity, color vision, stereopsis, and visual field assessment, many of which oversample a single factor or undersample the full space. The same framework clarifies psychiatric applications (including contrast-sensitivity changes in depression, schizophrenia, and ADHD) by separating retinalneural deficits from optical contributions such as higher-order aberrations and from attentional lapses, and it extends to oculomotor biomarkers. The article develops the Principle as a tutorial, with a worked example and a loading-structure significance test.

1. Introduction

The call for the Journal of Vision special issue, "Choose Your Stimuli Wisely: Advances in Stimulus Synthesis and Selection," highlights a central challenge in vision science: because datacollection time is limited, investigators must maximize diagnostic information while minimizing redundancy. This article proposes that analyses of individual differences, using factor analysis (FA), principal component analysis (PCA), and multidimensional scaling (MDS), provide a principled solution. Covariance among individuals reveals the number of independent sources of variation underlying a visual function and thus the minimum number of stimuli needed to characterize it. The emphasis on covariance structure has precedent. Brunswik's (1949) representative design held that stimuli should be sampled to preserve natural covariance among environmental variables rather than isolate one at a time, a logic extended here to individual differences.

The use of individual differences to study perceptual processes has a long history. Ruediger (1907), for example, provided an early correlational study of individual differences in the field of distinct vision. Beginning with Galton (1883) and continuing through El Koussy, Thurstone, Pickford, Jones, Vernon, Zachert, and Pirenne, researchers used correlational and factor-analytic methods to study the structure of visual abilities (Burt, 1940 (Burt, , 1946;; El Koussy, 1935; Jones, 1948; Jones & Jones, 1950; Pickford, 1946 Pickford, , 1951;; Pirenne, Marriott, & O'Doherty, 1957; Thurstone, 1944 Thurstone, , 1950;; Vernon, 1947; Zachert, 1951), what Cronbach (1957) called one of psychology's "two disciplines." Thurstone's (1944) Factorial Study of Perception identified factors including closure, illusion susceptibility, perceptual switching, and freedom from Gestalt binding; Peterzell (2020) later recovered most of these factors, focusing on three: illusion susceptibility, perceptual switching, and holistic closure.

The present approach rests on three assumptions that have received substantial empirical support in vision research (Mollon, Bosten, Peterzell, & Webster, 2017; Peterzell, Werner, & Kaplan, 1995; Peterzell, 2016). First, individual differences in visual performance often reflect variation in underlying visual mechanisms. Second, this variation produces systematic covariance rather than random noise. Third, multivariate methods such as FA, PCA, and MDS can recover the number and nature of those mechanisms from the covariance structure (Bosten, 2022; Peterzell & Teller, 2000; Wilmer, 2008). These methods reach beyond counting dimensions: Monte Carlo simulation of explicit generative models, used to confirm the number and form of the underlying mechanisms, has been part of this program from the start (Peterzell, 1991; Peterzell, Werner, & Kaplan, 1993 , 1995; Emery, Volbrecht, Peterzell, & Webster, 2017). Other individualdifferences traditions instead sort observers into types, for example by cluster analysis, rather than assuming one shared factor structure; these are complementary to the present approach and lie beyond its scope.

These assumptions lead to a simple principle of stimulus selection: the minimum number of stimuli needed to characterize a visual function equals the number of independent factors underlying individual differences in that function. I term this the One-Stimulus-Per-Factor Principle. If a visual function sampled at n stimulus values yields k independent factors (k < n), then k strategically chosen stimuli, one near each factor peak, are sufficient to characterize the function. The remainder of this article develops the Principle through a step-by-step tutorial with a worked example and visualizations (Section 3), reviews the converging evidence for lowdimensional structure (Section 4), and extends it to clinical and psychiatric assessment and to the mechanisms, phenotypes, and biomarkers that efficient measurement makes reachable (Sections 8-10).

2. The One-Stimulus-Per-Factor Principle

Choosing stimuli wisely has a principled solution. When a visual function is measured across many values of a stimulus dimension (spatial frequencies, orientations, hue angles, disparities, wavelengths), the measurements are rarely independent: observers who are sensitive at one value tend to be sensitive at neighboring ones. The covariance of these individual differences reveals how many independent sources of variation, or factors, actually underlie the function. The One-Stimulus-Per-Factor Principle follows directly: one well-chosen stimulus per factor is necessary to sample every independent source and, once that stimulus is validated as a selective and reliable index of its factor, sufficient to represent it without redundancy. Additional stimuli within a factor provide diminishing independent information, though they may improve reliability; too few leave independent dimensions unmeasured. This is more than dimensionality reduction. Factor analysis, principal component analysis, and multidimensional scaling have long been used to summarize data after they are collected. The Principle runs the other way, turning the recovered dimensionality into a rule for designing measurement (which stimuli to present, and how few will do) before the next experiment or clinical test is built. The factor count describes the independent sources of individual variation a function contains, which need not correspond one-to-one with physiological mechanisms. It is therefore not a new statistic but a conceptual and practical framework that puts established methods to work for stimulus selection.

Moving from "the covariance has k factors" to "k stimuli suffice" requires three things beyond the factor count, and the tutorial in Section 3 makes each explicit: that the factor structure is established by replication rather than read off a single sample ; that each chosen stimulus is a selective marker of its factor, ideally placed where competing factors contribute nothing (Step 8); and that one marker per factor fixes the number of independent stimulus dimensions, not the number of trials needed to measure each reliably, nor the extra markers prudence may require when a factor must be separated from a neighbor or carried to a new population (Steps 9-10). These conditions are where a factor count becomes a measurement design.

The payoff is broad and practical. The same logic tells a vision scientist how many conditions an experiment needs, a clinician which tests are redundant and which dimensions go unmeasured, and a human-factors or cognitive-neuroscience researcher how to characterize a visual ability without wasted trials. What follows is organized to make the Principle usable: a step-by-step tutorial for applying it (Section 3), the converging evidence that its low-dimensional structure recurs across visual domains (Section 4 and Table 1), and its implications for clinical, psychiatric, and mechanistic measurement (Sections 8-10).

3. Applying the Principle: A Step-by-Step Tutorial

Section 2 argued that many visual functions are governed by a small number of factors. This section turns that observation into a procedure. The principle is applied in two phases (a discovery phase that recovers the factor structure, and an efficient-assessment phase that selects one marker stimulus per factor), and each phase raises practical choices, taken here in turn. Two modes run throughout: a confirmatory mode, when a prior model of the underlying processes exists and the analysis tests whether the covariance matches it, and an exploratory mode, when no model exists and the covariance is used to discover the processes (Peterzell, 2016).

Throughout, I separate the number of independent stimulus dimensions a battery must span, which the factor count fixes, from the number of trials or replications needed to measure each dimension reliably, which classical test theory fixes; conflating the two is a common error, and the principle speaks only to the former. This tutorial assumes no prior expertise in factor analysis. In plain terms, factor analysis looks at how a set of measurements varies together across individuals and infers the few underlying dimensions, called factors, that generate the shared variation; how strongly each measurement reflects a given factor is its loading. It is written for vision scientists; for optometrists, ophthalmologists, and clinical psychologists and psychiatrists; and for human-factors and cognitive-neuroscience researchers who wish to apply the Principle to their own data. Every analytic step uses standard, widely available software. Principal component and common factor analysis, varimax and oblique rotation, parallel analysis, multidimensional scaling, and structural equation modeling (used here only for the confirmatory and measurement-invariance checks) are all implemented in general-purpose statistical packages such as SPSS, R, JASP, jamovi, SAS, and Stata, so no custom code is required. The one exception is the loading-structure significance test used in Step 6, for which a short reference implementation is provided in the Supplement.

Tutorial-level and foundational treatments of this individual-differences approach have appeared before: partial tutorials for contrast sensitivity in infants and adults (Peterzell, Werner, & Kaplan, 1993 , 1995; Peterzell & Teller, 1996; Peterzell & Kelly, 1997; Peterzell, 2016) and for chromatic steady-state and sweep VEP measures (Kaneko, Kuriki, Andersen, & Peterzell, 2025). None is as extensive as the present one, though Peterzell (2016), written for vision scientists, remains an unusually complete treatment. This section consolidates and generalizes these efforts into a single step-by-step procedure that includes, at the retention step, a formal test of loading structure (Step 6).

Step 1. Frame the function and the stimulus dimension.

Identify the physical dimension along which stimuli vary (spatial frequency, orientation, disparity, hue angle, wavelength) and the function to be characterized. The aim of discovery is to learn how many independent sources of individual variation operate along that dimension.

Step 2. Design the discovery battery: sampling density.

Sample the dimension densely enough that at least two to three stimuli fall within the expected bandwidth of each anticipated factor; otherwise neighboring factors cannot be resolved. When the bandwidth is known from prior work (for contrast sensitivity, spatial-frequency channel bandwidths of roughly one to 1.5 octaves), set spacing well below it, for example half-octave steps. When it is unknown, oversample and thin afterward: dropping redundant stimuli after discovery is cheap, whereas discovering that a factor was missed is not.

Step 3. Determine sample size, empirically, not by rule of thumb.

The familiar "subjects-per-variable" rules of thumb, widely repeated but seldom tested against real recovery, are unreliable; what governs recovery is how well-determined the factors are: the size of the loadings, or communalities (how much of each stimulus's variance the factors capture), the number of markers per factor, and the number of factors (MacCallum, Widaman, Zhang, & Hong, 1999; Costello & Osborne, 2005). The size that suffices therefore depends on the signal-to-noise ratio of the data. A direct demonstration comes from resampling the covariance itself (Figure 2): in spectral-sensitivity data with very high signal-to-noise and strong, mechanism-driven individual variation, random subsamples of roughly ten observers reproduce the full-sample correlation structure (Pearson r ≈ 0.96) and recover the same factors, with instability appearing only below about eight, in short, ten mice suffice (Peterzell, 2026c (Peterzell, , 2026d)). This bounds the optimistic end (when a structure is strong and the measurements clean, ten to twelve observers can suffice), but it does not license small samples for noisy or weakly determined structure, where the conventional requirement of many dozens to hundreds stands.

The practical rule follows directly: resample your own data. Draw random subsamples at several sizes, correlate each subsample's covariance matrix with the full one, and inspect the sampling distribution; if subsamples reproduce the structure, the sample is large enough to estimate it stably; if they do not, a larger sample is needed. Because each subsample is drawn from the same observers, this checks the internal stability of the structure, not its replication in an independent sample; where a second sample exists, comparing against it rather than the parent is the stronger test. Step 4. Look before you compute: intuitive factor analysis.

Plot the data before fitting anything. The whole of factor analysis has an intuitive core that the eye can grasp directly: a factor often appears as a band of the stimulus dimension over which the individuals keep approximately the same rank order. Where the same observers stay in nearly the same order from one stimulus to the next, that is, where their sensitivities at the two stimuli are highly correlated, those stimuli are redundant, they measure one underlying thing, and that band is one factor; where the rank order reshuffles, a second source of variation is contributing. Much of what the formal analysis later quantifies in eigenvalues (how much variance each factor captures) and loadings can already be anticipated from retained or broken rank. Two views make this concrete. First, overlay the individual curves and read the approximate rank order straight off them: where the curves hold roughly their vertical order from one wavelength to the next the stimuli are redundant, and where they cross a second source has taken over (Figure 1). In a single-pigment eye the order holds across every tested wavelength: one band, one factor; in a two-pigment eye it holds within a short-wavelength band and a longwavelength band but reshuffles across the middle, where the curves cross: two bands, two factors. Second, examine a scatterplot matrix, which pairs every two stimuli with the data points in the lower triangle and the correlation in the upper (Figures 3 and 4): pairs that tap the same source form tight, positively correlated clouds; pairs that tap different sources scatter or reverse; and the blocks of mutually correlated stimuli are the factors, visible before any statistic is computed (Peterzell, 2016). Wild-type mice form one positive block; two-pigment mice form two. Retained rank, a positive correlation, and a tight scatter cloud are three faces of one fact.

Step 5. Choose the analysis: factor model, scaling, or simulation.

Principal component analysis summarizes total variance and suits pure data reduction; common factor analysis (principal-axis or maximum-likelihood) models the shared variance attributable to latent sources and is the right choice when the goal is to infer the mechanisms that generate individual differences, which is the aim here. The two often agree when communalities are high, but common factor analysis is preferred for the inferential claim at the heart of the principle (Costello & Osborne, 2005).

Factor analysis is not the only, nor always the right, way to recover this structure: there is no single definitive exploratory method, and the choice should be deliberate. Factor analysis and PCA assume that variables are metric and combine linearly (multivariate normality is required only for maximum-likelihood estimation and the significance tests built on it, not for extraction itself), and their rotated solutions are not unique, so an orientation must be chosen by imposing simple structure (rotating the factors so that each variable loads strongly on just one of them).

When those assumptions are strained (ordinal or non-metric data, nonlinear combination, or structure better expressed as distances than correlations), multidimensional scaling is often preferable: it is nonparametric; nonmetric MDS in particular works on ranked dissimilarities, assuming neither linear combination of the measured variables nor metric dissimilarity intervals, does not force simple structure, and often recovers the pattern with fewer components. Kaneko, Kuriki, Andersen, and Peterzell (2025) chose nonmetric multidimensional scaling over factor analysis for exactly these reasons when modeling individual differences in cortical hue tuning.

One caveat applies when the goal is marker selection: an MDS solution locates stimuli in a dimensional space but does not by itself attach a loading pattern to each dimension, so choosing one stimulus per recovered dimension requires the further step of showing that the chosen stimuli give independent and adequate coverage of that space. And when the generative mechanisms can be stated explicitly, Monte Carlo simulation is more powerful still: by generating data from a specified process model and testing whether the analysis recovers it, one tests the mechanisms directly rather than inferring them from covariance alone (the simulation check in Step 7 is a small instance). When the question is instead confirmatory (whether a hypothesized factor structure holds, and holds equally across groups), structural equation modeling provides explicit tests. Independent components analysis (ICA) is a further member of this family. Rather than rotating to simple structure, it uses higher-order statistics to separate components that are statistically independent, not merely uncorrelated, which fixes a unique orientation where rotation otherwise leaves one free. It depends on non-Gaussian sources and larger samples, however, so it suits signal-rich domains such as EEG and fMRI better than the small-sample individual-differences data emphasized here. Treat factor analysis as one instrument in a kit that also holds multidimensional scaling, independent components analysis, structural equation modeling, and simulation, and match the instrument to the data and the question.

Step 6. Decide how many factors to retain.

Deciding how many factors to keep is usually treated as a question about variance: how much each component explains, and whether that exceeds chance. Set aside the eigenvalue-greaterthan-one (Kaiser) rule, whose threshold is arbitrary and which over-or under-extracts depending on the data, surviving as a software default rather than a defensible criterion; its disciplined successor is parallel analysis (Horn, 1965; Costello & Osborne, 2005), which keeps a component only when its eigenvalue exceeds the eigenvalue from random data of the same dimensions. The scree plot, read at the elbow where successive eigenvalues flatten, and Revelle and Rocklin's (1979) very-simple-structure criterion are useful complements. Whichever is used, the factor count it returns is a hypothesis to be confirmed in a second sample, not a number read off one solution.

These criteria share a blind spot. They all ask whether a component accounts for more variance than chance; none asks whether its loadings are ordered with respect to the stimulus dimension, the property that actually identifies a sensory mechanism. A factor whose loadings trace a smooth, interpretable function of wavelength, spatial frequency, or disparity carries information no eigenvalue can; and such a factor can be small, because a general factor has absorbed most of the variance, while remaining unmistakably real. The criterion, which I call the MacLeod-Webster (MW) criterion, was first stated by MacLeod and Webster (1983, p. 82) and carried through their analyses of individual differences in color matches (Webster & MacLeod, 1988; MacLeod & Webster, 1988): the factors were identified not from the variance each explained but from the shape of the loadings across wavelength, read against the spectral signatures of lens density, macular pigment, and photopigment optical density. It has since been applied throughout this individual-differences program, from the earliest analyses of infant contrast sensitivity (Peterzell, 1991; Peterzell, Werner, & Kaplan, 1993 , 1995), through spatial, temporal, chromatic, and electrophysiological channel studies (Peterzell & Teller, 1996 , 2000; Peterzell, Dougherty, & Mayer, 1997; Peterzell & Kelly, 1997; Peterzell, 2016), to current work on contrast sensitivity, orientation, stereopsis, cortical color, and chromatic numerosity (Peterzell & Elliott, 2026; Peterzell, Bachtoula, Llamas-Cornejo, & Serrano-Pedraza, 2025; Kaneko, Kuriki, Andersen, & Peterzell, 2025; Peterzell, Enongue, Read, & Serrano-Pedraza, submitted; Peterzell, Arrighi, Gurioli, Farini, & Grasso, in preparation). Applying the criterion is not new; testing it formally is. The loading-structure significance test (LSS test), developed in the course of a study of stereoscopic depth (Peterzell, Enongue, Read, & Serrano-Pedraza, submitted), does so: it refers the systematic organization of the loadings to an explicit null distribution, exactly as parallel analysis tests eigenvalue magnitude.

Judging a factor by its variance alone invites error in both directions. A small but systematically tuned factor is discarded when a general factor has absorbed most of the variance (a false negative), while a high-variance component bearing no orderly relation to the stimulus is retained (a false positive). The LSS test cuts both ways: systematic tuning is enough to keep a component, however little variance it carries, and high variance alone is not enough to keep one whose loadings bear no orderly relation to the stimulus. The test speaks to stimulus-tuned mechanisms; a general factor with near-uniform loadings, such as the single factor of the wildtype mice, is retained on separate grounds, not by this criterion.

The procedure is simple. Generate many datasets of the same dimensions from uncorrelated normal variables; extract and rotate the same number of components by the identical procedure; assign the real design's stimulus values to the simulated conditions; and, for each simulated solution, measure how systematically the loadings are organized by the stimulus parameter: the proportion of loading variance accounted for by stimulus level (η²) and the correlation between loading and the (log) stimulus value. Both statistics are read against their simulated null distributions, not in absolute terms. Where each stimulus level carries a single loading, η² by level is uninformative (it reaches its maximum for random and real data alike), and the loadingversus-stimulus correlation carries the test. Where several conditions share each stimulus level, as in the stereopsis example below, η² reveals systematic organization in its own right. The observed component is significant when its organization exceeds what random data of the same size produce. Permuting the stimulus labels across the observed loadings gives the same conclusion by an independent route. The two nulls answer complementary questions: the simulated data test whether the loadings are more organized than components from data with no covariance at all, while the label permutation, holding the observed loadings fixed, tests specifically whether they are ordered with respect to the true stimulus values rather than to a random assignment. The loading-stimulus correlation targets monotonic tuning; where a factor is expected to be bandpass or otherwise non-monotonic, as spatial-frequency and orientation mechanisms often are, a nonlinear trend or tuning-model fit (or η², where the design places several loadings at each stimulus level) should be used in its place. The test establishes that an extracted component's loadings are organized by the stimulus; it does not by itself fix the number of latent sources in the covariance, which remains the task of extraction and parallel analysis. Because several components, rotations, and tuning statistics may be examined, significance should rest on a prespecified statistic or a correction for multiple comparisons; a maximum-statistic permutation test that repeats the full extraction, rotation, and selection is the strongest safeguard.

The photopigment data illustrate the LSS test directly. Return to the transgenic-mouse loadings of Figure 5, from the five animals engineered to express a human L pigment alongside the native M pigment. The two varimax factors trace smooth functions of wavelength (the M and L photopigment spectra), and the test confirms it: the share of each factor's loading variance captured by a smooth trend across wavelength is .96 for the M factor and .89 for the L factor, each more systematically organized than every one of 10,000 datasets simulated from uncorrelated variables of the same dimensions (p < .0001), with a permutation of the wavelength labels returning the same verdict. Both photopigment factors are therefore significant by loading structure, not by variance alone: the covariance reproduces the genetics in only five animals. The wild-type animals make the complementary point: their single factor loads almost uniformly across wavelength (one undifferentiated M pigment, every wavelength redundant), so there it is the dominant first eigenvalue, not a tuned loading shape, that marks the factor as real. The LSS test is decisive precisely where factors are expected to be tuned, as the two-pigment case is. The same test has since been applied beyond spectral sensitivity, to the chromatic channels underlying color-numerosity adaptation (Peterzell, Arrighi, Gurioli, Farini, & Grasso, in preparation), and to stereoscopic disparity, next.

A second worked example: a small factor that is unmistakably real (stereopsis).

A study of stereoscopic depth shows the payoff. Disparity thresholds were measured for sinusoidal depth corrugations with retinal spatial frequency (cycles per degree, CPD) and relative spatial frequency (cycles per object, CPO) varied independently, and components were extracted to test whether disparity-sensitive mechanisms are organized by retinal or by relative frequency (Peterzell, Enongue, Read, & Serrano-Pedraza, submitted). The second component is diagnostic.

In 10,000 simulated datasets of the same dimensions, the proportion of its loading variance accounted for by CPD level (η² = .71 under varimax, .74 under oblimin) was matched in only 0.3% and 0.2% of simulations, and the correlation between loading and log CPD (|r| = .83 and .85) in 0.02%; permuting the condition labels gives the same result. The corresponding tests against CPO are nonsignificant: the component is organized by retinal spatial frequency specifically. Yet this same component has an eigenvalue of 1.25, below the simulated distribution of second-component eigenvalues, so conventional parallel analysis would reject it.

It is smaller than the second component of nearly every random dataset while being more systematically organized by retinal frequency than 99.7% of them, because a general factor absorbed most of the variance, not because it is noise. The loadings, not the eigenvalue, are the evidence that a second component, tuned in cycles per degree, is present.

The LSS test applies wherever factors are expected to order along a known stimulus dimension: wavelength, spatial frequency, disparity, chromatic distance. It turns the intuitive reading of Step 4 (a factor is a band over which individuals keep approximately their rank order) into a formal, testable criterion for stimulus-related loading structure: a factor earns its place when its loadings are systematically tuned to the stimulus dimension, however much or little variance it carries.

Step 7. Rotate, interpret, and test stability.

Rotate to simple structure. Thurstone's (1947) aim in rotating was sparseness: to drive as many loadings as possible toward zero, so that each factor is marked by only a few variables and each variable loads on only a few factors, because that sparse pattern is the one that can be read and interpreted. Use varimax (which holds the factors uncorrelated) if the factors are assumed independent, but an oblique rotation (oblimin, promax), which lets them correlate, is usually more realistic for visual mechanisms; report the factor correlations rather than forcing orthogonality. Interpret each factor by its highest-loading stimuli, its marker variables. Then establish that the solution is stable: resample the loadings and report their variability, split the sample and compare solutions by factor congruence (how closely the two sets of factors match), and, wherever possible, replicate in an independent dataset. A further check is to simulate: generate data from a known model, with independently varying channels of specified tuning, and confirm that the whole pipeline recovers the known number and form of the factors before trusting it on real data (Peterzell, Werner, & Kaplan, 1993; Emery, Volbrecht, Peterzell, & Webster, 2017; Peterzell, 2016). A factor structure is "established" when it replicates across samples with high congruence, not when it merely emerges once.

Step 8. Select marker stimuli: one selective dimension per factor.

For efficient assessment, retain for each factor the stimulus that reads it most selectively. The natural first choice is the stimulus nearest the factor's peak loading, but selectivity is sharper when the marker is chosen by the opposing factor: place each factor's marker at the stimulus value where every other factor is at a zero crossing, where the others load zero, so that the marker reflects variation in its own factor, minimizing contributions from the other modeled factors. With three or more factors no stimulus may null every competing factor at once; there, choose each marker to minimize its cross-loadings while keeping a strong target loading. When the factors are roughly mirror-symmetric the two criteria nearly coincide, because the opposing factor's zero crossing falls near the target factor's own peak; but the zero-crossing rule is the principled one, since it preserves selectivity even when the factors are asymmetric or partly overlapping, provided the target factor still loads strongly enough there to measure it reliably (Step 9). In the two-pigment example the L factor loads zero at about 515 nm, which places the M marker there, and the M factor loads zero at about 594 nm, which places the L marker there (Figure 6). This is the sense in which one stimulus per factor is necessary and sufficient: it is the minimum set of selective stimulus dimensions that spans the factor space without redundancy. It is not a claim that one measurement suffices. Sufficiency is also conditional on validation: a stimulus that isolates a factor in the discovery sample cannot be assumed to do so in individuals or populations whose factor structure differs, so each marker's selectivity must be confirmed in the target population (Step 10).

Step 9. Secure reliability: trials, and markers, per factor.

Estimating an individual's standing on a factor to usable precision requires averaging over enough trials, exactly as any threshold estimate does; and where a stimulus loads only moderately, or where one wishes to guard against a stimulus that loads differently in some individuals, two or more markers per factor provide insurance, the same logic by which the psychometric tradition recommends at least two markers per factor (Thurstone, 1947; Cattell & Tsujioka, 1964). The factor count fixes the number of independent stimulus dimensions; reliability fixes the number of trials; and prudence may call for more than one marker per dimension. The efficiency the principle delivers is a reduction in dimensions sampled, not a license to under-measure.

Step 10. Validate the reduced battery.

Confirm that the reduced set reproduces the information in the full one: correlate each marker with an independently estimated factor score, preferably one computed with that marker excluded, and confirm with a confirmatory factor analysis that the reduced set recovers the intended structure. Because the reduced battery has one indicator per factor, a confirmatory model of it is not identified without added constraints: fix each marker's measurement-error variance from its observed variance together with an independently established reliability estimate, such as its test-retest reliability. Even then the model may be only just-identified rather than genuinely tested, so validation against the full battery in independent data remains the stronger demonstration. A reduced battery earns its efficiency only once this preservation is shown.

Applying the principle in a new clinical population.

Factor structures do not automatically transfer. To deploy the principle clinically: first, either establish the structure within the target population or test whether a known structure holds there, using measurement-invariance tests (multi-group confirmatory factor analysis, a structuralequation-modeling technique that checks whether the same factors, with the same loadings, hold across groups); second, confirm that each marker loads strongly on its intended factor in that population; third, where the structure differs (for instance, a deficit that fractionates a factor seen in controls), re-derive the markers; and fourth, set the number of markers per factor by the clinical goal, since a single marker may suffice for screening while diagnosis that must separate nearby mechanisms, or meet a target sensitivity and specificity, may warrant more. The principle identifies which stimuli are informative; it does not remove the obligation to validate them against outcomes.

Applied this way, the principle is not a substitute for psychometric care but a way to direct it, spending measurement effort on the dimensions that carry independent information, and not on the many stimuli that merely echo them.

Three regularities stand out. First, in spatially and temporally tuned functions (luminance and chromatic contrast sensitivity, stereopsis, and motion), individual differences resolve into only a few broad factors, typically two or three, far fewer than dense sampling suggests (e.g., Peterzell & Teller, 2000; Peterzell & Elliott, 2026; Peterzell, Serrano-Pedraza, Widdall, & Read, 2017; Luna & Serrano-Pedraza, 2020); under adaptive-optics correction one apparent channel resolves into a distinct optical factor from higher-order aberrations rather than a neural one (Peterzell & Elliott, 2026; Figure 7). Second, in chromatic functions the dimensionality is often higher than simple opponent models predict (three to four factors for color matching, at least nine for hue scaling, up to ten cortical hue mechanisms), so here efficient testing means adding chromatic directions, not cutting them (MacLeod & Webster, 1988; Emery et al., 2017 Emery et al., , 2023;; Kaneko et al., 2025). Third, the same covariance logic reaches beyond thresholds, to face recognition, visual attention, perceptual organization, numerosity, synesthesia, and even bioengineered-mouse spectral sensitivity, each decomposing into a small set of independent dimensions. Across domains the recovered dimensionality runs from about two factors to ten or more, so the Principle prescribes fewer stimuli in some functions and more in others.

5. Reinterpreting Wilson's Spatial Channels in Light of New Evidence

Across more than 30 years of factor-analytic work on luminance contrast sensitivity, a stable two-factor structure has emerged below approximately 2.5 cpd: one factor near the lowest tested frequencies and a second around 1-2 cpd, positionally consistent with what Wilson and colleagues termed the A and B mechanisms (Wilson, McFarlane, & Phillips, 1983; Wilson & Gelb, 1984; Wilson, 1988). Most restricted spatial frequencies to below 2.5 cpd to minimize optical contamination.

Studies that did include higher spatial frequencies frequently reported additional factors (Sekuler, Wilson, & Owsley, 1984; Billock & Harding, 1996; Simpson & McFadden, 2005; Bosten et al., 2017; Min & Reynaud, 2024), sometimes read as evidence for cortical channels. However, Peterzell and Elliott (2026) showed this is almost certainly incorrect: their optical-quality factor (F3) disappeared entirely under AO correction, indicating that at least some of what had been read as higher-frequency retinal-neural channels likely reflects higher-order aberrations.

This reinterpretation extends to the retinal-neural factors themselves. Under AO correction, just two emerged, both more broadly tuned than classical channel models predict; they may reflect magnocellular and parvocellular pathways rather than multiple narrow cortical channels, as speculated by Baker (2013) and Baldwin, Baker, and Hess (2016). Orientation-tuning evidence (Peterzell, Bachtoula, et al., 2025) likewise supports fewer, broader mechanisms.

Peterzell (2026) showed that Min and Reynaud's (2026) finding of two spatial frequency channels within static luminance contrast sensitivity can be reinterpreted as one retinal-neural factor (Wilson B / F2) and one optical factor (F3), rather than two distinct retinal-neural channels. The age-related upward shift in the crossover between these channels may follow from worsening higher-order aberrations with age rather than neural dedifferentiation (see Figure 7).

6. The "Common Factor" Question: Within-Function Versus Across-Function Analysis

A related literature asks whether a general factor underlies visual performance across diverse tasks. Studies of visual illusions, contrast sensitivity, contour integration, motion processing, local-global perception, magnocellular tasks, and resting-state EEG features generally find high reliability within individual measures but weak correlations across different measures (Cappe et al., 2014; Cretenoud et al., 2019; Garobbio et al., 2024; Goodbourn et al., 2012; Gordillo et al., 2023; Grzeczkowski et al., 2017; Herzog et al., 2025; Tulver, 2019). This dissociation persists even with objective steady-state visually evoked potentials (SSVEPs) rather than subjective judgments, making a response-criterion or motivational explanation less plausible (Morea et al., 2026). Together, these findings suggest that many visual tasks tap distinct sources of individual variation rather than a common visual factor.

These results do not conflict with the present framework. Those studies correlate qualitatively different functions; the One-Stimulus-Per-Factor Principle concerns covariance within a single function sampled across a parametric stimulus dimension. Within-function analyses recover robust factors because their stimuli tap shared mechanisms, whereas weak across-function correlations suggest the tasks may depend on partly distinct ones, a separation that, as Garobbio et al. (2024) showed, is stable across years rather than measurement noise. The distinction bears directly on construct validity. When measures presumed to index a common process load on shared factors, as when Sekuler, Wilson, and Owsley (1984) recovered two spatial-frequency bands matching Wilson's A and B mechanisms, the assumption gains support; when reliable measures fail to covary, it is weakened.

Contrast sensitivity provides a useful example. Long interpreted as the envelope of numerous narrow spatial channels, it instead appears to reflect a much smaller set of broad factors, including at least one optical factor that is not retinal-neural (Peterzell & Elliott, 2026). The practical lesson is that a task's label does not guarantee what it measures: contrast sensitivity at 0.5 cpd under normal viewing indexes retinal-neural variance, whereas at 8 cpd it indexes optical quality, even though both stimuli look "neural." Factor analysis thus serves not only to identify efficient stimulus sets but also to test whether behavioral measures index the mechanisms they are intended to measure.

7. Implications for Stimulus Selection

The special issue's theme of optimal stimulus selection aligns closely with the framework described here. A visual function's factor structure defines the minimum stimulus ensemble needed to characterize it: stimuli within a single factor's span are redundant, while stimuli sampling different factors capture distinct and maximally informative sources of variation.

A two-stage strategy applies across domains: first, measure a visual function across a dense stimulus set and extract its factor structure; then reduce testing to one representative stimulus per factor (Figure 8). The resulting set may be smaller (e.g., luminance contrast sensitivity, orientation tuning, stereopsis) or larger (e.g., hue appearance), depending on the dimensionality the data reveal; for temporal contrast sensitivity, the factor structure of de Lange and counterphase-modulation functions sets the number of frequencies needed (Mayer et al., 1995; Peterzell et al., 1997; Dobkins et al., 2000). This approach also provides a principled basis for model comparison. Discrepancies between predicted and observed factor structures point to model inadequacy, as demonstrated for coloropponent models (Emery et al., 2017 (Emery et al., , 2023)), spatial channel models (Peterzell & Elliott, 2026), and narrowly tuned orientation channel models (Peterzell, Bachtoula, et al., 2025).

7.1 The Discovery Phase: When More Than One Stimulus Per Factor Is Needed

The One-Stimulus-Per-Factor Principle applies only after a factor structure has been established.

During discovery, denser sampling is required.

Factor analysis identifies latent dimensions from covariance among measured variables. If only one stimulus samples a factor, the factor's tuning cannot be estimated because there are no neighboring stimuli with which it can covary. At least two to three stimuli should fall within the expected bandwidth of each factor so that correlated clusters emerge and tuning profiles can be recovered. For example, sampling 10 orientations in 10° steps recovers two orientation factors crossing near 50°, roughly five stimuli per factor, whereas 45° spacing would leave only one or two per factor, too few to resolve the structure (Peterzell, Bachtoula, et al., 2025).

The distinction is straightforward. Discovery requires multiple stimuli per factor; efficient assessment requires only one. Once the factor structure has been established, testing can be reduced to a single representative stimulus near the peak of each factor.

Recent applied work illustrates both phases. Seemiller et al. (2023) factor-analyzed an operational vision-screening battery in 192 observers, recovering five factors (low and mediumto-high spatial-frequency vision, stereoacuity and fusional range, cone contrast sensitivity, and motion perception) that together explained 74% of the variance. The five-factor solution suggests that a substantially reduced battery might preserve much of the original information, and the recovered factors predicted operational aptitude better than any single test, illustrating how the discovery phase can guide the selection of a reduced assessment battery. In amblyopia, McKee, Levi, and Movshon (2003) likewise factor-analyzed a large battery of visual measures and separated an acuity-limited factor from a contrast-sensitivity factor, showing that the pattern of deficit across tests, not any single measure, characterizes the condition. Seemiller and colleagues have since extended the same dimension-reduction logic to oculomotor biomarkers (Seemiller, Knapp, & Winterbottom, 2024). Factor-analyzing smooth-pursuit, saccade, and fixation metrics in 87 observers, Seemiller, Taylor, and Winterbottom (2025) recovered five factors accounting for approximately 60% of the variance, with smooth-pursuit and saccade biomarkers loading on separate factors, evidence that the two systems carry largely independent information despite their shared neural architecture. Here the discovery phase points directly to the efficient set: because pursuit and saccade metrics do not collapse onto a common factor, an efficient battery would retain one representative biomarker per factor rather than treating eye movements as a single dimension.

8. Implications for Optometric and Clinical Assessment

Clinical tests embody assumptions about dimensionality, whether explicit or implicit. Contrast sensitivity, color vision, stereopsis, and visual field assessments are widely used to evaluate visual function (contrast sensitivity alone informs the assessment of conditions ranging from cataract and glaucoma to optic neuritis and LASIK outcomes; Wierzbowska et al., 2025), yet the stimulus selections underlying many instruments were developed without reference to the factor structure of individual differences. As a result, some tests appear to oversample a single factor, whereas others fail to sample the full factor space. The studies reviewed above suggest that clinical instruments could often be redesigned more efficiently by aligning stimulus selection with empirically derived factor structures. Zachert (1949 Zachert ( , 1951) ) applied factor analysis to the Bausch & Lomb Ortho-Rater and asked whether the battery measured as many distinct visual traits as it contained tests. Although the instrument included numerous acuity and phoria measures, factor analysis recovered far fewer underlying dimensions. Six acuity tests loaded on a common factor rather than measuring distinct abilities. Zachert's conclusion anticipated the present argument: a test battery may contain many stimuli while sampling only a few independent dimensions of visual function.

The CSV-1000 measures contrast sensitivity at 3, 6, 12, and 18 cpd. In light of the factor structure reported by Peterzell and Elliott (2026), these frequencies likely sample primarily the optical-quality factor that dominates above approximately 2.25 cpd. Consequently, the instrument may measure the same dimension repeatedly while failing to sample the two retinalneural factors evident at lower spatial frequencies. A more efficient design might test approximately 0.5 cpd, 1.5 cpd, and 4-6 cpd, thereby sampling the low-frequency retinal-neural factor, the higher-frequency retinal-neural factor, and the optical factor directly. In short, by this account the CSV-1000 risks sampling optical-quality variation repeatedly while undersampling the retinal-neural dimensions identified at lower frequencies.

8.1 Other Contrast Sensitivity Instruments

The CSV-1000 is not the only clinical contrast sensitivity test that may be suboptimally designed. The Pelli-Robson chart and the Mars Letter Contrast Sensitivity Test measure letter contrast sensitivity rather than a spatial-frequency-specific threshold (their letters span roughly 0.5 to 5 cpd; Pelli, Robson, & Wilkins, 1988), which limits how cleanly they map onto the factors recovered with sinusoidal gratings. The FACT chart adds coverage by including 1.5 cpd, which dips into the retinal-neural range, but its 3-18 cpd frequencies likely load on the same optical-quality factor, making four of five potentially redundant. Standard Snellen and ETDRS acuity primarily assess high-resolution vision, which is strongly influenced by optics but also depends on retinal-neural processing.

8.2 Color Vision Tests

The factor structure recovered from hue scaling (Emery et al., 2017 (Emery et al., , 2023) ) and cortical color coding (Kaneko et al., 2025) suggests that current clinical color tests may undersample the chromatic domain. The Ishihara plates primarily assess a single red-green axis, while HRR and Cambridge Colour Test (Trivector) procedures sample only two or three confusion axes. These instruments were designed to detect common forms of color deficiency rather than characterize the full dimensionality of color appearance. The number of chromatic directions needed therefore depends on the goal: screening for the common deficiencies these tests target calls for different coverage from characterizing individual differences in hue appearance. The Farnsworth-Munsell 100-Hue Test comes closest to sampling the full chromatic space, but its clinical use is limited by administration time. The present framework suggests that efficient assessment may require approximately one stimulus per recovered hue factor. For hue scaling, this would correspond to roughly eight to nine strategically chosen chromatic directions distributed around the hue circle, potentially capturing much of the information provided by far denser sampling.

8.3 Stereoscopic Tests

Clinical stereotests such as the Randot, Titmus-Wirt, TNO, Frisby, and Lang tests typically assess disparities at a limited range of magnitudes, orientations, spatial frequencies, and temporal conditions. Individual-differences analyses suggest a substantially richer structure, including multiple spatial-frequency-tuned mechanisms (Peterzell et al., 2017; Reynaud & Hess, 2017), orientation-sensitive mechanisms (Llamas-Cornejo et al., 2025), and temporally tuned stereomotion mechanisms (Llamas-Cornejo et al., 2024). Consequently, many clinical stereotests sample only a small portion of the known disparity space. A more comprehensive assessment would include representative stimuli from each major spatial-frequency, orientation, and temporal factor.

8.4 Visual Field and Perimetric Tests

Standard automated perimetry measures sensitivity across retinal locations but typically uses a single stimulus size and temporal condition. In effect, it samples one spatiotemporal region of visual processing at each location. This approach may overlook spatial-frequency-tuned and temporally tuned mechanisms that are differentially affected by disease. Frequency Doubling Technology (FDT) perimetry illustrates the opposite strategy. By design, it targets a specific low-spatial-frequency, high-temporal-frequency mechanism and therefore provides selective information about a particular visual pathway. Its strength is specificity; its limitation is restricted coverage of the broader factor space. A factor-based approach to perimetry would extend testing across a small number of strategically selected spatial and temporal conditions, allowing multiple independent mechanisms to be assessed without requiring exhaustive sampling.

9. Implications for Psychiatric and Neurological Research

The retinal-neural versus optical distinction identified by factor analysis of the CSF extends beyond optometric assessment into psychiatric and neurological research, where contrast sensitivity has been widely used as a biomarker, with notably inconsistent results. Attributing such deficits to neural mechanisms without accounting for optics can mislead above approximately 2.5 cpd; moreover, because the optical and retinal-neural factors are correlated, long-term optical degradation may itself affect neural processing, consistent with evidence from amblyopia (Leguire, Rogers, & Bremer, 1990) and myopia (Steinfeld & Murray, 2025; Tian et al., 2024).

9.1 Depression and Retinal Contrast Gain

The strongest psychiatric CS finding comes from Bubl and colleagues, who used the pattern electroretinogram (PERG), a retinal-ganglion-cell measure driven by contrast-reversing checkerboards, to show a roughly 50% reduction in retinal contrast gain in major depressive disorder relative to controls (Bubl et al., 2010); the deficit correlated with severity and normalized with remission (Bubl, Kern, Ebert, Riedel, Tebartz van Elst, & Bach, 2015). The PERG uses large checks at low effective spatial frequencies, placing it within the retinal-neural factor range identified by Peterzell and Elliott (2026), and the finding has been replicated by Friedel and colleagues (2021, 2024) with improved recording. However, Fam, Rush, Bhakat, and Chia (2013), using a different PERG method (transient rather than steady-state, 0.8° checks), found normal retinal responses despite reduced behavioral contrast sensitivity, a discrepancy that may reflect differences in PERG methodology, though the conflicting results remain unresolved.

9.2 Schizophrenia and the Magnocellular Debate

The schizophrenia contrast-sensitivity literature remains divided. Some investigators have interpreted greater deficits at low spatial frequencies as evidence for magnocellular dysfunction (Butler et al., 2005; Revheim et al., 2006), whereas others argue that deficits are broadly distributed across spatial frequencies and do not selectively implicate magnocellular pathways (Skottun & Skoyles, 2007; Cadenhead et al., 2013). A recent meta-analysis confirmed a robust contrast-sensitivity deficit overall but highlighted potential confounds, including medication effects, inadequate optical correction, and limited assessment of attentional lapses (Linares et al., 2025). More recently, Kadivar, Ilapakurti, Dobkins, and Cadenhead (2024) examined contrast sensitivity in clinical high-risk and first-episode psychosis using moving (temporally modulated) 1.22-cpd luminance and chromatic gratings, a spatial and temporal frequency well placed to sample the low-frequency retinal-neural factor.

The factor structure described earlier helps interpret these discrepancies: differences at higher spatial frequencies may reflect the optical factor, whereas deficits persisting at lower frequencies after optical correction implicate retinal-neural involvement. Because the optical factor at higher frequencies reflects higher-order aberrations that survive standard refractive correction, matching patients and controls on refraction and acuity removes lower-order optical differences but not this higher-order contribution, which only adaptive optics fully isolates. Distinguishing them requires sampling both regions of the contrast-sensitivity function rather than a narrow frequency range.

9.3 ADHD: An Informative Negative Finding

Using the same PERG paradigm that revealed reduced retinal contrast gain in depression, Bubl et al. (2013) found no abnormalities in adults with ADHD. Within the PERG paradigm's sensitivity, this null result suggests the retinal-neural mechanisms indexed by PERG are relatively preserved in the ADHD group studied, thereby constraining theories of ADHD-related visual dysfunction.

9.4 Applying the Principle

The One-Stimulus-Per-Factor Principle suggests psychiatric CS research has been hampered by testing at spatial frequencies that do not cleanly index the factor of interest. A study measuring CS only at 6 or 12 cpd under normal viewing may be measuring optical quality (Factor 3) rather than retinal-neural function (Factors 1 and 2); if the condition affects retinal-neural processing but not the eye's optics, the study finds a null not because there is no deficit but because the wrong factor was sampled. Testing contrast sensitivity at 12 cpd to measure retinal-neural dysfunction is like measuring blood pressure to diagnose a broken bone: the instrument is reliable, but it indexes the wrong system.

The decisive experiment, not yet conducted, would test psychiatric patients and matched controls across spatial frequencies spanning both sides of approximately 2.25 cpd, with acuity carefully corrected, attentional lapse rates estimated via easy catch trials, and medication status recorded.

Factor analysis of the resulting CSFs would reveal directly whether the condition loads on the retinal-neural factors, the optical factor, or both. The retinal biomarker literature, including OCT ganglion-cell-layer thinning and ERG amplitude reductions in schizophrenia and depression (Komatsu et al., 2024; Silverstein & Rosen, 2015), converges with this approach, since these measures may index related retinal-neural processes, a possibility that joint factor analysis could test.

A note on limitations is warranted. The factor-analytic studies reviewed here typically employ modest sample sizes (N = 16 to 61 for most psychophysical studies, with Bosten et al.'s N = 1,060 as a notable exception). These solutions should be treated as provisional and confirmed through replication, converging methods, and larger samples. The spatial-frequency boundaries cited (e.g., the ~2.25 cpd retinal-neural/optical crossover) are approximate and may shift with stimulus parameters, population, or factor-retention criteria. Factor analysis also recovers the covariance structure of individual differences without revealing the physiological identity of the underlying mechanisms, so mapping statistical factors onto specific neural pathways warrants caution.

10. One Stimulus per Factor, Several Payoffs

The factors the Principle recovers are the raw material for several scientific constructs, not one.

Depending on the evidence a given dimension earns, the same recovered factor can be read as a statistical summary of shared variance (a latent factor), as an indicator of a posited informationprocessing operation (a latent mechanism), as a stable, interpretable individual profile at Marr's (1982) algorithmic level (a computational phenotype, a term borrowed from computational psychiatry; Montague, Dolan, Friston, & Dayan, 2012; Patzelt, Hartley, & Gershman, 2018), as a heritable, disease-linked trait (an endophenotype), or as an objectively assayed indicator of a biological process (a biomarker). These roles connect behavioral measurement to algorithmic description and biological implementation, each earned by a progressively stricter standard of evidence (Peterzell, 2026b): reliability, replication, and mechanistic relevance for a computational phenotype; Gottesman and Gould's (2003) five criteria, including heritability and co-segregation within families, for an endophenotype; and objective biological measurement for a biomarker (FDA- NIH Biomarker Working Group, 2016). A single dimension can occupy several of these roles at once, but only those its evidence supports (Table 3).

The One-Stimulus-Per-Factor Principle optimizes the pursuit of every one of these roles, and does so efficiently. Each role, from latent mechanism to biomarker, rests on two prior achievements: that the dimension was discovered at all, and that it can be measured well. The discovery phase, by sampling densely enough to resolve every factor within its bandwidth (Steps 2-3), maximizes the chance of uncovering all the latent dimensions: a mechanism, phenotype, endophenotype, or biomarker that is never discovered can never be characterized or used. The What makes the Principle distinctive is that it secures these preconditions with minimum redundancy. A compact battery of one selective marker per established factor estimates a person's standing on each established dimension without the overlap of redundant tests, and can be made adaptive, measuring only the dimensions a given question requires (cf. Robinson, 2012, on deep phenotyping in precision medicine). Efficiency and scientific reach are thus two aspects of one discipline: to measure all the factors, and to measure each one well, is at once to make assessment efficient and to lay the groundwork for every role those factors may earn.

The payoff spans audiences. To a clinician it turns a test from a single number into a profile, locating a patient on every dimension rather than against one cutoff, so that disease, which rarely lowers every mechanism equally, shows as a change in the pattern. To a basic scientist it offers a non-invasive way to count and test visual mechanisms. To applied and human-factors work it yields performance-predicting traits. In each case the same efficient measurement serves: one stimulus per factor, no more, no fewer.

11. Conclusion

Choosing stimuli wisely requires recognizing the dimensionality of variation across observers, not only designing naturalistic stimuli or constraining a model in a single observer. The One-Stimulus-Per-Factor Principle gives a concrete rule: determine the number of independent factors underlying a visual function, then test with exactly that many stimuli, one per factor. When the factor structure is not yet known, the complementary discovery-phase rule applies: sample densely enough to place at least two to three stimuli within each factor's expected bandwidth.

The analysis of individual differences, through FA, PCA, MDS, and related techniques, provides the information to apply both rules. The step-by-step tutorial in Section 3 is intended to make these rules directly usable, from discovery through selective marker selection and validation, and Section 10 shows how the same efficient measurement underwrites the latent mechanisms, computational phenotypes, endophenotypes, and biomarkers that a precision science of vision will require.

Across sensory, perceptual, cognitive, clinical, and electrophysiological domains, the dimensionality of individual differences provides a practical guide to stimulus selection. Its purpose is not reduction per se but matching stimulus selection to the dimensionality of the function being measured. The resulting framework offers immediate applications to both research protocols and clinical instruments, from contrast sensitivity and color vision to stereopsis and perimetry.

Recent adaptive-optics findings (Peterzell & Elliott, 2026) and orientation tuning (Peterzell, Bachtoula, et al., 2025) suggest that individual differences previously attributed to neural spatial channels may instead reflect optical quality variation (Peterzell, 2026a). This simplifies contrast sensitivity from multiple narrowly tuned channels to two broadly tuned retinal-neural mechanisms plus an optical factor.

I encourage the vision science and optometric communities to incorporate individual differences more routinely into the design, analysis, and interpretation of experiments and clinical assessments. Whether or not the framework proves as durable as the factorial tradition from which it descends, the core claim is simple enough to test: one stimulus per factor, no more, no fewer.
