Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

Abstract

Introduction: Recent research has increasingly adopted complex dynamical systems theory to understand psychopathology. A key proposition is that psychopathological symptoms may operate at different time scales, with different dynamic speeds shaping the evolution of the symptom system. This study examined the extent to which variability in symptom dynamic speed reflects symptom differences, person differences, and person-by-symptom interactions.

Methods: We analyzed five intensive longitudinal experience sampling datasets comprising 265 participants and 33,059 retained observations. Dynamic speed was quantified for each person-symptom time series using lag-1 autocorrelation (AC) and mean period (MP) derived from Lomb-Scargle spectral analysis. Multilevel variance decomposition was used to partition variability into person, symptom, and person-by-symptom components.

Results: Across the two dynamic speed indices, symptom variance was generally nonsignificant. Person variance and person-by-symptom interaction variance were significant in several datasets and generally exceeded symptom variance, although pairwise differences between variance components were not consistently significant.

The findings provide limited evidence for consistent symptom differences in dynamic speed; person differences and person-by-symptom interactions may play a more prominent role. These results highlight the importance of idiographic approaches to understanding symptom dynamics and underscore the need for further research into the clinical implications of dynamic speed.

1. Introduction

In recent years, clinical psychology has increasingly adopted a complex dynamical systems perspective to study psychopathology, conceptualizing psychopathological symptom systems as composed of multiple dynamic processes-i.e., different psychopathological symptoms-that continuously evolve over time (Borsboom, 2017; Olthof et al., 2023; Robinaugh et al., 2026; Scheffer et al., 2024a Scheffer et al., , 2024b;; Zhang et al., 2026). An important characteristic of complex dynamical systems is that different components or dynamic processes may operate on different time scales, which manifests as different dynamic speeds (Carpenter & Turner, 2000; DiFrisco, 2017; Walker et al., 2012). Some processes can change substantially over relatively short periods, whereas others change more slowly and gradually evolve over longer time scales. The former can be considered fast variables, whereas the latter can be considered slow variables (see Figure 1; Carpenter & Turner, 2000; Walker et al., 2012). This distinction between fast and slow variables helps characterize the dynamic organization of complex dynamical systems: faster processes may respond more rapidly to external perturbations or changes in the internal state of the system, whereas slower processes may shape or constrain changes in fast variables and their responses to external perturbations over longer time scales, thereby influencing system stability and state transitions (Carpenter & Turner, 2000; Hasselman, 2023; Walker et al., 2012). For example, in a lake ecosystem, phosphorus concentration can be considered a slow variable, whereas algae can be considered a fast variable (Carpenter & Turner, 2000; Rinaldi & Scheffer, 2000). When phosphorus concentration has not yet crossed a critical threshold, algae may fluctuate rapidly, but their fluctuations remain constrained within a certain range, and the system remains in a clearwater state. As phosphorus concentration gradually increases and crosses the critical threshold, however, algae may change abruptly, and the system may suddenly shift from a clear-water state to a turbid-water state.

[Figure 1 near here] This perspective on differences in time scales has also begun to enter clinical psychology. For example, Lunansky et al. (2020) proposed that personality-related processes can be understood as relatively slow dynamic processes, whereas psychopathological symptoms primarily operate on relatively fast time scales. Moreover, psychopathological symptoms span multiple domains and dimensions (Kotov et al., 2017), suggesting that different symptoms may operate at different time scales and motivating the examination of their relative dynamic speed. If some symptoms function as slow variables, their relatively slow dynamics may reflect more persistent vulnerability or maintenance processes within an individual and may therefore constitute more fundamental elements of a psychopathological symptom system (Carpenter & Turner, 2000; Hasselman, 2023; Lunansky et al., 2020; Walker et al., 2012). Their development may take longer, and interventions targeting them may require greater effort. In contrast, if some symptoms function as fast variables, they may reflect state-like fluctuations and may respond more rapidly to external stimuli or interventions. Thus, understanding the relative dynamic speed of symptoms may help distinguish different elements within a psychopathological symptom system and provide insights into psychopathological mechanisms, prevention, and intervention. Taking depression as an example, it comprises symptoms spanning different domains, including affective symptoms (e.g., depressed mood), self-evaluative symptoms (e.g., guilt and feelings of worthlessness), and physiological symptoms (e.g., appetite and sleep) (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). Intensive longitudinal studies have shown that different depressive symptoms may exhibit different dynamic patterns, such as differences in persistence, which may indicate differences in the dynamic speed of depressive symptoms (Crowe et al., 2019; Ebrahimi et al., 2021).

At the same time, whether the dynamic speed of psychopathological symptoms has a general and consistent pattern across individuals remains unclear. In recent years, clinical psychology has increasingly emphasized individual heterogeneity in psychological processes and the importance of idiographic research (Molenaar, 2004; Piccirillo & Rodebaugh, 2019; Wright & Woods, 2020). Intensive longitudinal studies have also shown substantial individual differences in the dynamics of psychological symptoms, suggesting that the same symptom may exhibit different dynamic characteristics across individuals (Ebrahimi et al., 2021; Fisher et al., 2017; Nemesure et al., 2024; Reeves & Fisher, 2020).

However, to our knowledge, no study has systematically examined whether differences in the dynamic speed of psychopathological symptoms arise primarily from individual differences, symptom differences, or person-by-symptom interactions. The present study therefore addresses this question. As an exploratory study, we did not formulate specific hypotheses.

2.1. Data sources

To capture the dynamic speed of psychopathological symptoms, the present study used data from a longitudinal experience sampling design, in which individuals were asked to report their psychopathological symptoms repeatedly at a predetermined frequency over a specified period. We used four publicly available datasets from the OpenESM platform (https://openesmdata.org/; Siepe et al., 2026), namely Bailon et al. (2020 ), Fisher et al. (2017), Reeves & Fisher (2020), and Wang et al. (2016). In the following, these datasets are identified by their first authors.

In addition, we collected a dataset ourselves, which we refer to as Dataset E. All procedures were reviewed and approved by the ethics committee of the first author's institution. We recruited middle school students from southern China. In early March 2026, before a mental health class began, we announced our study. The study was described as recording daily mental health states, and recruitment was announced; interested students could participate. Participants were included based on active participation and informed consent from both themselves and their guardians. They completed a paper-and-pencil questionnaire for approximately 15 minutes each evening before going to bed for 90 consecutive days, continuing until mid-June 2026. Participants could withdraw at any time and received a small snack as a reward after each completed assessment. Seventy-five participants took part in the study, of whom 70 reported their gender and age (37 girls, 33 boys; mean age = 12.943 years, SD = 0.778, range = 12-15 years).

The Reeves dataset focused on PTSD symptoms; the Fisher dataset and Dataset E focused on depressive and anxiety symptoms and related symptoms; and the Bailon dataset focused on current affect (unidimensional, negative to positive) and subjective physical activity, two relatively broad indicators. Although these two do not directly belong to a specific disorder, they are often included as indicators in psychopathology research (e.g., long-term negative affect and long-term psychomotor retardation in depressed patients; American Psychiatric Association, 2022). Because the present study focuses on the dynamic speed of psychopathological symptoms, these two indicators may reflect the distinction between affect and subjective bodily sensations as two broad categories of psychopathological symptoms. Therefore, in this study, we also roughly treated them as psychopathological symptoms, which is to some extent consistent with current scholarly discussions of psychopathological symptoms as multi-level and broad categories (Kotov et al., 2017).

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.6.1 (R Core Team, 2026). The primary analysis code is available at https://figshare.com/s/5bc834209336e0d2f806.

2.2.1. Preprocessing

For the five datasets, some individual time series were particularly short (e.g., most participants in the Bailon dataset completed only one assessment). Because the primary analyses were conducted at the level of each individual and each symptom, we screened individual person-symptom time series based on the requirements of previous time-series studies (Jamalabadi et al., 2025) and excluded time series that could not support the subsequent analyses. Specifically, because the subsequent analyses involved lag-1 autocorrelation (AC), treating two adjacent observations as a lag-1 pair would be inappropriate when the interval between them is large (e.g., when a participant in a daily sampling study missed five days and then returned). Therefore, the criteria for time-series length and number of observations were primarily determined by this consideration.

Following previous research (Jamalabadi et al., 2025), we set the maximum allowed interval between two adjacent observations to 3 days. For the Wang dataset, which involved measurements every 2 days, this threshold was adjusted to 7 days. In addition, we required at least 30 usable lag-1 pairs to compute AC. Specifically, the number of usable lag-1 pairs had to satisfy the following criterion: total number of observations -number of long gaps -1 ≥ 30. This requirement was intended to ensure a sufficient number of observations for estimating each individual-symptom time series separately. 2.2.2. Calculation of Dynamic Speed Indices

In this study, the speed of symptom fluctuations was conceptualized as a continuous and relative property. To quantify this property, we used two statistical indices. The first was AC, defined as the lag-1 autocorrelation. A lower AC indicates weaker temporal autocorrelation of symptom states, reflecting a relatively higher dynamic speed and potentially faster state-to-state changes (i.e., lower inertia; Kuppens et al., 2010), and thus a tendency toward a relatively faster variable. In contrast, a higher AC indicates greater state persistence and a relatively lower dynamic speed, and thus a tendency toward a relatively slower variable.

The second index was the mean period (MP) derived from spectral power analysis (Warner, 1998), which characterizes the periodic components of fluctuations. In simple terms, MP estimates the average length of a symptom's fluctuation cycle: a shorter MP means the symptom tends to rise and fall more quickly over time. A shorter mean period indicates a relatively higher symptom dynamic speed and a tendency toward a relatively faster variable, whereas a longer mean period indicates a relatively lower dynamic speed and a tendency toward a relatively slower variable. Spectral power analysis is based on the principle of the Fourier transform, which decomposes a time series into periodic components at different frequencies and estimates the contribution of each frequency in terms of power (Jamalabadi et al., 2025; Ruf, 1999; Zechmeister & Kürster, 2009). Because our datasets were not necessarily sampled at equal intervals (e.g., some datasets included multiple assessments within a day), we used the Lomb-Scargle method (Ruf, 1999; Zechmeister & Kürster, 2009), which is suitable for unevenly sampled time series and does not require equally spaced observations.

For datasets with multiple assessments per day (Bailon, Fisher, and Reeves), observation times were converted to hours relative to the first observation. For datasets with one assessment per day (Wang and Dataset E), days were used directly as the time unit. Specifically, for each participant-symptom time series, observation times were converted to relative times with respect to the date (or hour) of the first observation, with the first observation set to time zero. The Lomb-Scargle power spectrum was then calculated directly from the resulting time series. We subsequently calculated the power-weighted mean frequency, took its reciprocal, and converted it to days to obtain MP as an index of the average fluctuation cycle of the symptom time series.

2.2.3. Sources of Variability in Dynamic Speed Indices

To distinguish the sources of variability in the two dynamic speed indices (AC and MP)-namely, person differences, symptom differences, and their interaction-it was necessary to account for the estimation uncertainty of the extracted features. Although conventional variance decomposition can quantify these sources of variability, it does not explicitly account for the uncertainty associated with estimating each index from a finite time series. We therefore estimated the standard error (SE) of each index for every personsymptom combination.

For AC, the SE could be directly calculated using the asymptotic approximation for a correlation coefficient. Because the SE of MP could not be directly obtained from its estimation procedure, we used a block bootstrap procedure (Künsch, 1989) to estimate it. The block length was set at the dataset level as median ( N ) 1 / 3 /median( f ) (Hall et al., 1995), where median N is the median number of observations per person-symptom cell and median f is the median sampling frequency (observations per day) per cell. All cells within a dataset shared the same block length. This approach allowed the blocks to retain sufficient autocorrelation structure while avoiding overly long blocks that would leave too few resampling blocks for each individual. For cells involving multiple assessments within a single day, the relative temporal order and actual time intervals between observations within each block were retained when constructing the blocks, thereby preserving the temporal dependence structure as much as possible. For each person-symptom combination, 2,000 bootstrap resamples were generated. The SE of MP was then estimated as the standard deviation of the resulting bootstrap distribution.

We then fitted a multilevel meta-analytic model using the metafor package in R (Viechtbauer, 2010). Although this framework is commonly used for meta-analysis, our aim here was not to synthesize effect sizes across independent studies, but to decompose variability in dynamic speed across persons, symptoms, and their combinations while accounting for the estimation uncertainty of each index. For each dynamic speed index, the estimated index value was treated as the observed effect size, and its squared SE was specified as the sampling variance. Person, symptom, and person-by-symptom interaction were included as crossed random effects. This specification allowed us to quantify the overall contributions of these three sources of variability, rather than estimate differences among specific predefined symptoms. The variance components were estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML), and the proportion of the total modeled variance attributable to each component was calculated.

Finally, we used the estimated variance-covariance matrix of the variance components to conduct pairwise linear contrasts. Wald-based standard errors, 95% confidence intervals, and significance tests were calculated to assess differences between variance components and to determine whether person differences, symptom differences, or their interaction accounted for relatively more variability in dynamic speed.

As a robustness check, we repeated the variance decomposition analyses after applying a Fisher's z transformation to AC (z =tanh -1 AC) and a log transformation to MP ( log MP), to examine whether the results were sensitive to the distributional shapes of the dynamic speed indices.

4. Discussion

The present study drew on five longitudinal experience sampling datasets and used time series of multiple psychopathological symptoms reported repeatedly by individuals over time. Using two indices, AC and MP, we examined the variability in and sources of the dynamic speed of psychopathological symptoms, with a focus on symptom differences, person differences, and person-by-symptom interactions.

The most robust finding was that symptom differences did not explain variability in the dynamic speed of psychopathological symptoms; that is, there was little evidence that certain symptoms are consistently faster (shorter cycles; fast variables) or slower (longer cycles; slow variables) than others. This is consistent with critiques of traditional approaches to classifying psychopathological symptoms (e.g., the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders [5th ed., text rev. ; DSM-5-TR; American Psychiatric Association, 2022]) that assume symptom attributes are relatively consistent across individuals (i.e., groupconsistent; Fried, 2020; Kotov et al., 2017). Although such critiques have often focused on symptom-level patterns-for example, some individuals show co-occurring depression and anxiety whereas others do not-suggesting that symptom attributes may differ across individuals, our results provide evidence from a different dynamic property: dynamic speed.

By contrast, person differences may explain variability in the dynamic speed of psychopathological symptoms. This aligns with the growing emphasis on idiographic approaches in clinical psychology (Fisher et al., 2017; Molenaar, 2004; Nemesure et al., 2024; Piccirillo & Rodebaugh, 2019; Reeves & Fisher, 2020; Wright & Woods, 2020).

However, this should not be interpreted simply as stable person-level traits determining whether all symptoms are fast or slow-that is, one person being fast across all symptoms and another being slow across all symptoms. Results from some datasets suggested possible person-by-symptom interactions. In summary, these findings suggest that, when examining the dynamic speed of psychopathological symptoms, it may be necessary to adopt an idiographic perspective and attend to individual-specific symptom differences.

Although longitudinal experience sampling allows the dynamic speed of psychopathological symptoms to be quantified and compared using AC and MP, the clinical implications of these differences remain to be explored. From a dynamical systems perspective, slow variables may be particularly important because they can shape or constrain changes in fast variables and their responses to external perturbations over longer time scales, thereby influencing system stability and state transitions (Carpenter & Turner, 2000; DiFrisco, 2017; Hasselman, 2023; Walker et al., 2012). Taking the PTSD system in the Reeves dataset as an example, if "avoidance" is identified as the relatively slowest symptom for an individual in clinical settings, an important question is whether this indicates that the symptom plays a more critical role in the system and should therefore be prioritized or given greater attention in PTSD intervention or relapse prevention. This question may be considered in relation to the control parameters that influence the stability and overall dynamics of the system (Cui et al., 2025; Olthof et al., 2023). In psychopathology, control parameters can be conceptualized through a stress-vulnerability framework, in which external stressors and internal vulnerabilities jointly constrain the dynamics of the system (Hankin & Abela, 2005). Given that these constraints are deeply intertwined within a personenvironment system (Olthof et al., 2023), a key empirical question is whether relatively slowdynamic PTSD symptoms are more strongly constrained by global control parameters, and whether they could serve as observable markers of this stress-vulnerability interplay. Further empirical research is therefore needed to establish the clinical significance of the dynamic speed of psychopathological symptoms.

The present study has several limitations, which also suggest potential directions for future research. First, although many researchers have emphasized that psychopathological systems should be conceptualized as open systems (Robinaugh et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026), the boundaries of the psychopathological systems represented in the five datasets were not consistent across datasets. For example, the Reeves dataset focused primarily on PTSD symptoms, whereas other datasets included symptoms that may span multiple disorders, such as depression and anxiety in the Fisher dataset and Dataset E. In addition, some symptoms in certain datasets may be relatively broad and may be relevant to multiple disorders. For example, in the Bailon dataset, "current affect" (i.e., overall good versus bad, rather than specific emotions as assessed in some disorder-specific measures) and "subjective physical activity" may both be relevant to depression, anxiety, and bipolar disorder (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). Nevertheless, despite differences in system boundaries and symptom composition across datasets, some broadly similar patterns emerged in the sources of variance in dynamic speed, which may offer tentative support for the robustness and generalizability of our findings. Future studies should more explicitly define the boundaries and constituent components of the psychopathological systems under investigation.

Second, the five datasets differed substantially in their measurement intervals. In particular, Dataset E and the Wang dataset did not include multiple assessments per day, which may have obscured short-term fluctuations and limited our ability to detect differences in dynamic speed occurring over relatively short time scales. This may partly explain why MP tended to be larger in these datasets than in datasets with more frequent measurements.

Nevertheless, despite this difference in sampling frequency, the variance decomposition results revealed some broadly similar patterns across datasets, which may provide tentative support for the robustness of our main findings. Importantly, AC and MP are not the only possible indices of dynamic speed. Future research should develop and evaluate additional measures of dynamic speed and examine whether the present findings can be replicated using alternative operationalizations.

Third, the present study relied exclusively on self-reported measures of psychopathological symptoms. Self-report measures are inherently subjective and, in intensive longitudinal designs, cannot provide continuous coverage of psychopathological symptoms because frequent assessments may increase participant burden and affect compliance and data quality (Wrzus & Neubauer, 2023). Moreover, active self-report is generally limited to periods when participants are awake and able to respond, making it difficult to capture psychopathological symptom dynamics during sleep. In contrast, wearable devices can continuously collect physiological and behavioral signals in daily life, including heart rate, heart rate variability, electrodermal activity, skin temperature, and physical activity, which may provide complementary information about psychopathological symptoms and their dynamics (Elgendi et al., 2026). Future research could therefore integrate wearable sensing with intensive longitudinal self-report to further examine the physiological basis of individual and symptom differences in the dynamic speed of psychopathological symptoms.
