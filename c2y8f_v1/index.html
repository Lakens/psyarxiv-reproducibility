Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Gaming disorder (GD) is an emerging addictive disorder, comparable to gambling disorder, that has been included in the International Classification of Diseases, Eleventh Revision (ICD-11). Globally, the study of GD has grown rapidly but the research base has been criticized for its inconsistent assessment. This comprehensive systematic review aimed to update two previous reviews (2013, 2020) to evaluate all English-language GD tools and their research evidence. A database search identified 49 tools employed across 1,296 peer-reviewed studies involving more than 2.7 million participants. Tools were evaluated according to: conceptual alignment and practical utility; compatibility with DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 criteria; sample and study characteristics; and psychometric properties. The evaluation showed that the GD field has greatly proliferated across its health subdisciplines and quadrupled in size since 2020.

Based on review data from 2013 and 2020, and assuming similar growth and sustainability, the GD field may increase to more than 2,000 studies by 2033. Key findings included: (1) tool coverage of DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 criteria has improved, particularly for assessment of impaired control; (2) instrument choice differs considerably across epidemiological, neurobiological, and treatment research streams; and (3) many tools demonstrate basic reliability and validity indicators, but more rigorous psychometric validation is needed. The GD field would benefit from an international 'gold standard' tool to harmonize measurement and generate a more cohesive evidence base. To advance, the field should capitalize on opportunities for innovation in screening to more effectively assess the unique needs, challenges, and characteristics of gaming populations.

1. Introduction

Video gaming is a massively popular form of digital entertainment, with more than 3.8 billion active players worldwide (Newzoo, 2024). For most individuals, gaming is a recreational activity that confers personal and social benefits (Bediou et al., 2018; Halbrook et al., 2019). However, for some, persistent patterns of gaming can have serious negative mental health and social consequences. In its most severe form, problem gaming is conceptualized as 'gaming disorder' (GD), an addictive disorder comparable to gambling disorder. The condition is estimated to affect between 1 and 3% of individuals worldwide (Stevens et al., 2021; 2023), with higher rates among those who are younger, male, and from East Asia (Kim et al., 2022).

Globally, the task of systematic monitoring of GD, particularly in young and vulnerable subpopulations, has gained support, but has been complicated by the field's diverse and inconsistent approaches to measurement (King et al., 2020a; 2026).

Although the concept of GD has received criticism (Aarseth et al., 2020; van Rooij et al., 2018), the condition has received support from within psychiatry, clinical psychology, and public health (Castro-Calvo et al., 2021; González-Bueso et al., 2018; Paulus, Ohmann, Von Gontard, & Popow, 2018). International research on problematic gaming has supported its inclusion in major health classification systems: first, as a provisional (i.e., research rather than clinical) entity termed internet gaming disorder (IGD) in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) in 2013, and retained with no changes in the DSM-5-TR in 2022, and then officially included as a clinical disorder in the Eleventh Revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) in 2019. These taxonomies have somewhat different diagnostic criteria, but share the conceptualization of GD as an addictive disorder.

The two categories also explicate issues related to its boundary with normality, i.e., the need to distinguish problematic gaming from high but healthy or adaptive involvement in the activity (Billieux et al., 2019; Bonnaire & Lambert, 2026; Brandtner & Brand, 2026; King et al., 2026; Nielsen & Rigter, 2026; Schimmenti, Scalone, & Casale, 2026).

Taxonomic developments have catalyzed research into GD assessments and have coincided with greater public health interest in potential harms associated with excessive screen time, particularly in young people (Gillespie et al., 2026). However, the GD field faces challenges in achieving wider adoption of its theoretical models and assessment practices.

These challenges have been complicated by the phenomenon of research teams working in research 'silos' whereby similar research agendas have been pursued using slightly different methodological and conceptual foundations (King, Billieux, & Delfabbro, 2025; Radunz et al. 2025). As a result, different research teams have produced many new but similar tools based on the DSM and ICD systems, but this has led to uncertainty about best practice (King et al., 2020b King, Billieux, & Delfabbro, 2025). Given the massive growth and momentum of GD research, it is important to critically re-evaluate GD tools and their empirical evidence.

Previous reviews of GD assessments have reported various inconsistencies and psychometric weaknesses (King et al., 2013; Griffiths, King, & Demetrovics, 2014; Lortie & Guitton, 2013; Petry et al., 2014; Starcevic, 2013). The most recent systematic review of GD assessment was conducted by King et al. (2020a). Their comprehensive review identified 32 unique assessment tools across 320 empirical studies, which represented almost the totality of GD quantitative research (i.e., only studies with named tools were included). The authors observed that the field had grown four times larger since the previous review (i.e., King et al., 2013) and that new tools had been produced at a mean rate of 2.5 per year. Several limitations of GD tools were highlighted: inconsistent coverage of DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 criteria; scoring and cut-off variations; strong reliance on convenience sampling underpinning psychometric data; limited cross-cultural validation; limited replication and clinical validation against a robust gold standard; and predominance of tools with similar content and structure. The review concluded that no single tool was the conventional choice or had greater evidential support for its psychometric properties.

Inconsistent and inadequate measurement of GD has major implications for considering the quality and coherence of its evidence base and its future potential, as well as wider impacts such as allocation of resources to address health problems related to the condition. Population studies rely on screening tools to guide policy decisions and therefore these tools must provide valid data on technology-related health and social problems (Xiao et al., 2026). Systematic reviews of GD prevalence have raised concerns that variability in estimates is largely attributable to differences in assessment tools and their scoring (Stevens et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2022). Less attention has been given to the psychometric properties of these tools, including the characteristics of samples in available data. Similar issues of consistency and sample representativeness arise in other research streams that directly concern GD, including studies of the neurobiological foundations of addictive behaviors and health intervention research, which demand precise evaluations for determining eligibility and tracking clinical change.

Another important area of GD research requiring precise measurement is the study of the biological bases of problematic gaming. Neurobiological studies of GD have attempted to identify structural or functional brain differences associated with the condition, and self-report or clinician-administered tools are used to guide case-control group classifications (Schnack & Kahn, 2016). A review of neurobiological studies of GD reported that studies employed an average of 46 participants for two or more group comparisons (Kuss, Pontes & Griffiths, 2018).

At that scale, potential misclassification is highly consequential. Neurobiological studies therefore require assessment tools with high diagnostic precision and strong construct validity, which are requirements not always met by tools used in epidemiological screening. Relatively few neurobiological studies employ assessment tools with well-established psychometric properties or report the diagnostic thresholds used to define case groups (Chang et al., 2023).

1.2 The present review

The GD field is characterized by strong research growth and broad public interest. Since the publication of the previous comprehensive review of GD assessment (King et al., 2020a), the field has embraced the ICD-11 classification for GD (and, to a lesser extent, the subclinical diagnostic category for hazardous gaming). This has led to renewed growth in measurement tool development and greater potential for diversity in assessment, which has its advantages and disadvantages. With these developments, it is timely to evaluate the field's conventions and general quality and coherence of evidence through the lens of its assessment practices.

The present review aimed primarily to evaluate all available GD measurement tools and their associated empirical evidence. Following the approach by King et al. (2020a), this review refers to 'GD' but recognizes that this term is not universal and its criteria differ slightly across the DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 systems. The field also involves studies of subclinical problematic gaming, intensive but non-problematic gaming (high involvement), and studies with a focus on 'internet use' encompassing gaming activities. A secondary aim of this review was to evaluate the nature and quality of all quantitative GD research, to update the observations provided in previous major reviews (King et al., 2013; 2020a). The present review was conducted more than five years after the publication of the ICD-11, which stimulated the development of new screening approaches and steered focus away from certain GD symptomatology (e.g., tolerance and withdrawal) (Castro-Calvo et al., 2021). The previous review's publication date also greatly exceeds the Cochrane Library's recommended period of two-year update cycles; therefore, it was considered urgently necessary to evaluate change in this fast-moving field.

The previous reviews (2013, 2020) were guided by a standard framework for evaluating psychological assessment (Cicchetti, 1994; Groth-Marnat, 2009). For the present review, this framework was retained but updated with some minor refinements related to the revised description of GD in the ICD-11. This review sought to update the 2020 review by evaluating all new GD assessment tools (i.e., those published from 2019 to 2025) and included all associated research literature. Additionally, this review enables an updated perspective on the evidence and research metrics associated with tools published prior to 2019. In addition to describing the main characteristics of all GD tools, this review sought to address the following questions: (1) are current tools consistent with the DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 diagnostic criteria?

(2) which GD tools are more conventionally used in each research area (e.g., epidemiological, neurobiological, interventions)? (3) which tools have received more rigorous evaluation and evidential support? (4) has GD assessment improved since the previous comprehensive review in 2020 and since the World Health Organization's (WHO's) recognition of GD in the ICD-11?

2. Methods

This systematic review was conducted in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) statement (Page et al., 2021). The review was preregistered, and data and analyses are available on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO ID: CRD420251036437). This review's methodology was based on our previous comprehensive systematic review (King et al., 2020) and included comparative analysis of 2020 review data (N=320 studies) with present review data.

2.1 Phase 1: Identification and selection of tools

This review aimed to identify and then examine the strengths and weaknesses of all available tools for assessing problematic gaming and/or GD, as defined in the DSM-5-TR and/or ICD-11. To achieve this, the review was designed to identify all quantitative research which employed a cited GD tool using (i) systematic review methods (database searches) and (ii) all citation data (Google Scholar) associated with each GD tool.

In accordance with the PRISMA guidelines (Page et al., 2021), the following inclusion criteria were applied: (1) peerreviewed and published in the English language; (2) all test items and response categories needed to be accessible, either listed in an appendix or provided in tabular format (e.g., a table presenting factor loadings); (3) the tool had to be specifically designed to measure problematic gaming or GD, and not broader constructs like internet addiction or other online excessive behaviors, although exceptions were made for widely used internet addiction measures that explicitly referred to the test sample's gaming activities, such as the Young Internet Addiction Test (YIAT; Young, 1998), the Compulsive Internet Use Scale (CIUS; Meerkerk, Van Den Eijnden, Vermulst, & Garretsen, 2009), and the Screener for Substance and Behavioral Addictions (SSBA; Schluter, Hodgins, Wolfe, & Wild, 2018); (4) a self-report measure completed by the individual and not by an external rater (e.g., a parent or clinician); and (5) the tool was original, not a composite or adaptation of diagnostic criteria or other measures.

The first phase involved identifying tools published since the previous comprehensive review (King et al., 2020). In October 2025, the first author, MH, performed a systematic literature search of the relevant literature published between January 2019 and October 2025.

The search was broadened by consulting various electronic databases, including Google Scholar, PsycINFO, PsycARTICLES, PubMed, ScienceDirect, Scopus, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science, to obtain comprehensive data. The search strategy involved identifying papers associated with the development, validation, or testing of scales that measure IGD and GD. The following Boolean search string was employed to guide the search process: (measure OR tool OR test OR validation OR psychometric OR screening OR diagnostic OR item OR instrument) AND ("internet gaming disorder" OR "gaming disorder" OR gaming OR "video game addiction" OR "problematic gaming" OR "problematic video game use" OR "game use disorder" OR "GD" OR "IGD" OR "VGA") AND ("DSM-5" OR "ICD-11"). The search results were documented and presented in Figure 1, using the PRISMA framework to illustrate the yield of 11,271 studies. The selection and screening of these articles were performed on the Covidence system for managing systematic review data. To ensure accuracy and prevent redundancy when selecting studies for data extraction, duplicates were automatically identified and removed after importing all search results.

The first two screenings, based on title and abstract, were conducted by MH and MS.

Studies determined to be relevant were retained for further review. At the next step, the full texts of remaining studies were independently evaluated by the two reviewers (MH and MS).

All studies were analyzed according to predetermined eligibility criteria. Disagreements on study eligibility were discussed to reach consensus. Where necessary, a third reviewer (DK) was consulted. After collating results, the titles and abstracts were screened, which identified 17 new tools which were combined with the original list of 32 tools (King et al., 2020). 2.3 Tools Evaluation Framework

This review aimed to provide a comprehensive and critical overview of the psychometric properties and practical qualities of all screening tools for problem gaming and GD, encompassing tools based on DSM-5-TR IGD and ICD-11 GD. The present review was designed to extend the previous systematic review by King et al. (2020a). As indicated by the search results, the empirical literature that involves GD tools has quadrupled in size since 2019 (i.e., from 320 to 1296 studies), and the field's total citation count has more than doubled, thus necessitating a major review update.

To guide tool evaluation, the previous review involved a multidisciplinary team comprised of 14 experts and leaders in the GD field, including psychiatrists, clinical psychologists, psychometricians, and professors of research. This team formed an expert group who developed the tool evaluation framework based on fundamental psychometric information (Cicchetti, 1994; Groth-Marnat, 2009). The 2020 review involved evaluation of 32 tools based on 320 empirical studies published up to 2019. As noted earlier, the GD field has grown significantly since its recognition in the DSM and ICD health nomenclatures, and many research teams have independently developed their own screening tool or tools. In this review, we evaluated all GD screening tools, inclusive of those identified in the previous review, using the framework reported in King et al. (2020). Specifically, this review set out the following research priorities: (1) to evaluate each tool's compatibility with the DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 systems, and (2) to critically evaluate each tool's practical features and psychometric properties. Some minor amendments to the 2019 framework were made to reflect the revised clinical description of GD in the ICD-11.

2.3.1 Overview of existing tools

First, all 49 tools were summarized according to the following basic characteristics: (1) tool abbreviation (i.e., tool acronym or short-hand name; some tools were disambiguated by adding author names, e.g., 'Petry IGD' and 'Lemmens IGD' to minimize potential confusion with other scales with IGD as their namesake); (2) author and date (derived from original publication source); (3) tool components (i.e., constructs reportedly measured by the tool); (4) number of items (i.e., all tool items); some tools, e.g., AICA-Sgaming and C-VAT2.0, include additional items for clinical judgement but are not scored; (5) response format (i.e., response options for tool items, e.g., yes/no); (6) cut-off score (i.e., cut-off for 'problematic' or 'addicted' status on the test, based on original source reference, if reported); (7) age (i.e., participant with the lowest age who provided a valid response in the original study); (8) country of origin (i.e., country of research team's institution, with priority given to first author); and, (9) language versions (i.e., known language versions of the tool, based on the published evidence base only).

2.3.2 DSM-5 TR/ICD-11 symptom coverage

The second stage of the review involved comparison of the representativeness of each screening tool against the GD criteria described in the DSM-5-TR and ICD-11. The aim was to determine the extent to which each tool captured the primary symptoms and areas of functional impairment in each system. These variations are important to document because they can affect prevalence estimates in population research and may affect classification accuracy in clinical practice. However, it is acknowledged that screening does not necessarily require full coverage of DSM-5-TR or ICD-11 criteria to be effective. The intent was to identify the common priority areas for screening and to identify potential conceptual gaps in brief screening approaches. For this assessment, the symptom descriptions and elements of tools were cross-referenced with each of the diagnostic criteria. IGD in the DSM-5-TR has nine criteria (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). The ICD-11 criteria refer to three essential diagnostic features: Impaired control over gaming, increasing priority given to gaming over other life activities, and continuation or escalation of gaming despite the occurrence of negative consequences. The ICD-11 also recognizes additional clinical features not required for diagnosis, including tolerance, withdrawal symptoms, and craving. Functional impairment is delineated into five domains: (Personal: Psychological and physical well-being, Social: Interpersonal relationships and social functioning, Educational: Academic performance and engagement, Occupational: Work performance and job-related responsibilities, Financial: Economic burden and spending patterns related to gaming). A detailed breakdown of the impairment types evaluated is provided in the legend of Table 2.

2.3.3 Quantitative evidence summary

The third step of the evaluation involved synthesis of the overall evidence base for all 49 assessment tools. This evidence was systematically summarized according to the following process. First, the total citation count for each tool was extracted from Google Scholar, regardless of publication type, and categorized by period (i.e., citations prior to 2019 and citations from 2019 onwards). Second, the number of empirical studies that employed each tool was categorized into pre-2019 and 2019-present periods. Third, studies that employed nationally representative and/or clinical samples were identified. Studies were categorized as intervention studies (i.e., involving psychotherapy, prevention, or other treatment protocols), studies using clinical samples (i.e., participants seeking or entered into treatment), longitudinal studies (i.e., studies involving repeated observations of a cohort or sample), prevalence studies (i.e., those reporting rates of GD using representative samples), and neurobiological studies (i.e., studies using neuroimaging techniques such as fMRI or neurocognitive measures). Each tool was summarized with reference to its sample characteristics, including sample size, age composition, and recruitment method. This included the total number of participants across all studies for each tool, participant age group (adolescents vs. adults), and recruitment strategy (convenience vs. non-convenience sampling).

2.3.4 Psychometric properties

The fourth review step involved synthesis of psychometric evidence for each tool. The purpose was to evaluate the nature, scope, and consistency of empirical evidence on each tool's reliability and validity. This analysis was guided by the framework used in King et al. (2020a), with extensions to incorporate more recent developments in test refinement and clinical utility. Six domains were examined: (1) Dimensionality, including the type of statistical analysis (e.g., exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis) and empirically-derived factor structures (e.g., unidimensional or multidimensional solutions); (2) Reliability, including internal consistency (e.g., Cronbach's alpha) and test-retest reliability coefficients; (3) Refinement, including evidence derived from Rasch analysis, item response theory (IRT), and tests of measurement invariance; (4) Validity, encompassing convergent and criterion-related validity; (5) Functional impairment, identified by standardized indicators of functional impairment or health and quality of life measures; and (6) Clinical use, referring to implementation in structured clinical interviews with patients. Data for these domains were extracted from all 1,296 articles. To supplement manual search, the advanced search function in Adobe Acrobat was used to search for psychometric terms and values. Variables that required direct reporting, such as Cronbach's alpha or correlations between two variables, were extracted. For complex analyses involving interpretation (e.g., Rasch modelling, IRT, and measurement invariance), the review followed the procedure used in King et al. (2020a), in which a senior psychometrician reviewed and confirmed the consistency and accuracy of reported findings. This included verification of statistical interpretations, such as the correct application and reporting of root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) values. All extracted values and interpretations were compiled into a master spreadsheet for further analysis.

2.3.5 Synthesis of review findings

Given the substantial volume of review material (N=1,296 articles), a summary 'quick reference' table (Table 7) was created to provide a concise overview. The table was developed by a process of expert roundtable discussion, as described in King et al. (2020a). The table legend provides detailed explanation of each component and its scoring rubric. The scoring system was designed to reflect the quantity and consistency of the empirical evidence. A score of 0 indicated absence of evidence; a score of 1 indicated a single relevant study; and a score of 2 denoted two or more studies. For reliability, internal consistency values of 0.70 or higher were considered acceptable thresholds for inclusion, in line with psychometric standards (Cronbach, 1951; Schmitt, 1996; Lance, Butts, & Michels, 2006), while recognizing limitations such as scale length inflating alpha coefficients (Cicchetti, 1994; Groth-Marnat, 2009). For convergent and concurrent validity, associations with a correlation coefficient (r) of 0.30 or higher were used as the benchmark, representing a moderate effect size (Cohen, 1992).

It is important to note that the scoring system was not weighted and should not be interpreted as a measure of overall quality or clinical utility. Some tools may appear to have greater empirical support due to the quantity of evidence but may still have limitations. Tools with fewer studies may simply be newer and thus less studied, rather than being less valid.

Most tools (n=40) were presented as suitable for use in adolescents, but minimum age thresholds varied; the most common (n=29) lower threshold was 12 or 13 years. All tools had Englishlanguage versions, and most tools (n=44) were available in at least one non-English language; 36 languages were represented across all tools.

3.2 DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 symptom coverage

Table 2 provides a summary of the symptom coverage of tools in relation to DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 criteria for GD. The DSM-5-TR refers to nine GD criteria: preoccupation, withdrawal, tolerance, unsuccessful attempts to stop, loss of interest in other activities, continued use despite harm, deception, escape, and significant life interference. The ICD-11 specifies essential criteria including impaired control over gaming, increased priority given to gaming, and continued use despite negative consequences. The ICD-11 also lists additional clinical features and diagnostic considerations. Overall, 11 tools fully covered both the DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 core (but not all additional) diagnostic criteria: Petry IGD, IGDS9-SF, PIE-9, IGDT-10, SCI-IGD, CVAT2.0, IGUESS, DIA, ICMH-IGD, GOSQ, and T-GDS.

Individual symptom coverage varied considerably across tools. The most consistently represented DSM-5-TR criterion was criterion 9 ('harm') which was covered by 45 tools (97%). Other commonly assessed criteria were preoccupation (n=40), withdrawal (n=38), unsuccessful attempts to control use (n=43), and escape (n=31). Deception (n=24) and continued use despite harm (n=25) were the two least assessed criteria. Among tools published since 2020, deception (4/17; 24%) and escape (5/17; 29%) have been the least covered criteria.

A total of 11 tools (22%) refer to all nine DSM-5-TR criteria: Petry IGD, IGDS9-SF, PIE-9, IGDT-10, SCI-IGD, CVAT2.0, IGUESS, DIA, ICMH-IGD, GOSQ and T-GDS. This coverage compares favorably to the 2020 review in which 8 tools fully covered the DSM criteria.

Considering each tool against the ICD-11 GD criteria, all 49 tools included at least one item referring to impaired control. Most (n=48) referred to increased priority given to gaming, and all post-2019 tools (n=17) referred to continued use despite harm (compared to only 9/32 pre-2019 tools). There were 24 tools that covered all three ICD-11 core criteria and at least one area of functional impairment. Regarding additional clinical features, most tools (n=32) referred to tolerance, although tolerance in relation to problem gaming and GD has been critiqued for imprecision and lack of validity (see Castro-Calvo et al., 2021; King et al., 2016; Razum et al., 2023), and some (n=12) referred to craving. Measurement of functional impairment referred to social consequences (n=46), followed by personal (n=37) and occupational (n=29) harms. Financial consequences (e.g., gaming-related debt or overspending) were specified in only 8 tools, none of which were published post-2019.

3.3 GD tool evidence base and bibliometrics

Table 3 presents a summary of the empirical evidence types and citation metrics associated with each GD tool. The GD literature is composed of 1,296 studies (i.e., studies that employ and cite a GD tool) associated with 19,350 Google Scholar citations. This observation compares to 320 studies identified in the 2019 review (King et al., 2020a), indicating that the GD research article base has expanded to four times the size in the proceeding five-year period.

The most used tools since 2019 have been the GAS-7, Petry IGD, IGDS9-SF, IGDT-10 and YIAT, which have each been used in at least 100 empirical studies.

The GD evidence base was categorized into studies with a primary focus on: psychometric validation (n=139), neuroimaging (n=107), repeated measures or longitudinal assessments (n=93), prevalence (n=63), and interventions (n=39). Overall, the most frequently employed tools were the IGDS9-SF (n=418, 32.3% of the evidence base), the YIAT (n=137), and the GAS-7 (n=118). The IGDT-10 (n=108) and Petry IGD (n=101) have been the most frequently used in neurobiological studies; the YIAT was developed in 1998 to assess general 'internet addiction' and precedes DSM and ICD conceptualizations. The IGDS9-SF was the most used instrument in validation studies (n=29), but most studies (n=28) have been conducted by independent research teams (i.e., teams not involving the tool's lead author). The IGDS9-SF has been used in the most prevalence studies (n=15), and longitudinal studies (n=39). In terms of citations, the YIAT and YDQ are the two most cited tools, reflecting their prescience and earlier publication (late 1990s) and broader application across internet and gaming addiction fields. The GDT has been the most cited ICD-based tool to date.

3.4 Sample characteristics and recruitment

Table 4 presents a summary of the samples involved across the 1,296 GD studies. The total number of participants across all GD research was N=2,704,327; however, some participants (e.g., panel cohorts) may have been involved in multiple independent studies. This result indicates that the GD field has approximately quadrupled in size within the past 7 years.

A total of 392,191 participants has been administered post-2019 tools developed based on ICD-11 criteria (NB: 67% of this data involves the GDT). Overall, there were 28 tools with at least 75% of its pooled sample recruited using convenience sampling, which included 16 tools with research exclusively based on convenience samples. About half (52.5%) of the published research has involved adolescents (i.e., <18 years). The IGDS9-SF has the largest total participant pool (n=1,421,515), including the most participants recruited via non-convenience sampling (n=890,335). The GAS-7, Lemmens IGD-9, IGDT-10, and GDT each have total participant pools exceeding 190,000. All other tools have less than 90,000 participants, and most (n=31) have fewer than 20,000 participants.

As a supplementary analysis, all sample data were examined according to the DSM-5-TR criteria covered by the tools they had been administered in their respective studies. The objective of this analysis was to determine the extent to which participants had been able to respond (e.g., affirmatively, negatively) to specific items referring to GD symptoms. The total N in each study corresponding to each tool was assigned to each of the relevant DSM-5-TR symptoms covered by each tool. This process yielded an estimated total number of participants with the possibility of responding to any given GD symptom (e.g., assuming the item on the survey was completed). This evaluation showed that, overall, the DSM constructs with the greatest representation in participant responses were those referring to unsuccessful attempts (97% of the total participants), loss of interests (91%), and jeopardized life opportunities (90%). The lowest was continued use despite harm (70% of participants), but this compared favorably to the value of only 12% reported in King et al.'s (2020a) review; this large increase may be explained by the strong uptake of IGDSF-9, IGDT-10, and GADIT measures. This analysis showed that the research base includes 1,734,638 participants (57% of all samples) who have participated in a study using a tool that has complete coverage of DSM-5-TR criteria for GD. Only 5 out of 17 (29%) post-2019 tools assessed deception and secrecy related to gaming activities, which likely reflects that the ICD-11 does not refer to these features. There has been critical debate regarding, and Delphi analysis (Castro-Calvo et al., 2021) of, the utility of these criteria, e.g., arguments that gaming is less likely to involve deception due to parental or partner surveillance of gaming and gaming-adjacent activities.

3.5 Psychometric properties of tools

Table 5 presents the first of two tabular summaries of the 49 tools' psychometric properties. The table summarizes available evidence on test dimensionality, reliability, and test refinement. Most tools (n=45) have been evaluated using factor analytic (FA) techniques (or principal component analysis), with variability in FA solutions across tools. Only 14 tools (A-EQ, POGU, IGD-20, Petry IGD, Lemmens IGD-27, PIE-9, DIA, YDQ, PMVGS, GHAS, LMGDQ, GDSQ, GDSS, PGS-A) have been evaluated by exploratory FA followed by confirmatory FA within the same study. Among the most used tools (i.e., GAS-7, Lemmens IGD-9, IGDT-10, GDT), the FA studies have consistently tested 1-factor solutions using confirmatory testing only, reflecting a priori reasoning that GD is a unidimensional construct.

Among newer tools published in the ICD-11 era, there are varied factor structures, with most tools having between 2 and 4 factors, based on EFA and CFA approaches, indicating that most ICD-11-based tools may not be unidimensional. The ICD-11-based GADIS-A has the largest FA research base for a non-unidimensional tool, which reports that cognitive-behavioral symptoms and negative consequences of gaming represent distinct aspects of GD. The GADIS-A has notably more items (n=5) that refer to harm/conflict (i.e., school, hygiene, social contacts, interests) compared to most other GD tools.

Internal consistency, usually indicated by Cronbach's alpha or McDonald's omega, was reported in validation studies for 47 tools. Reliability values were generally high, with most values exceeding .80 in studies involving test validation. The SCI-IGD and C VAT 2.0 were the only tools without reported internal consistency values. The IGDS9-SF has the largest body of research reporting internal consistency, with 21 validation studies, followed by GDT (n=19), GADIS-A (n=13), and IGDT-10 (n=10). Test-retest reliability was reported for 25 tools, with the most common interval period of two weeks. No tool had more than 3 studies that had evaluated test-retest reliability. Eight tools (i.e., GAS-7, IGDS9-SF, IGDT-10, CIUS-14, CIUS-8, GDT, and GADIT) have been examined using item response theory (IRT) or Rasch modeling. Generally, these studies provided support for the model fit and measurement invariance of each respective test.

Table 7 provides a synthesis of all tool evaluation areas to provide a 'quick guide' for researchers and clinicians. The purpose of this table was to summarize specific tool characteristics and supporting evidence, with each tool rated and assigned a total score. Higher total scores reflect broader coverage of evaluation domains but do not necessarily indicate superior overall quality or suitability for all contexts. Further, as noted in King et al. (2020a), the table provides a brief overview of strengths and weaknesses, but is limited by the following considerations: (1) tool scores are based on an unweighted data representation underlying each indicator, meaning that tools with the same criterion score may differ in rigor or quantity of evidence, e.g., the analysis did not differentiate between tools that meet certain score thresholds (e.g., 2 studies) and those that greatly exceed this threshold; (2) total scores should not necessarily be considered an overall indicator of tool quality; and, (3) higher scores in some areas may be undermined by weaknesses in other areas (e.g., tools with poorer symptom coverage may affect utility of prevalence data).

Notwithstanding limitations of this framework, the GD tools that fulfilled the most test indicators were the IGDS9-SF and IGDT-10, consistent with King et al.'s (2020a) findings.

These tools are noteworthy for total DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 symptom coverage, evidence base reporting strong psychometric properties, and use in multiple research types. Notably, tools that were more highly rated in King et al. (2020a) improved on their total ratings in the present review (e.g., the IGDS9-SF increased from 16 to 21). Other relatively highly rated tools included the GAS-7, IGD-20, and Petry IGD, reflecting their larger and more diverse evidence, and continued use in the ICD-11 era of GD research. The older YIAT scored relatively highly across criteria, despite its weak representation of GD criteria and poorer concurrent validity, due partly to its prescience and frequent use in research in Asian countries, particularly neurobiological research. Among tests developed post-ICD-11, the GADIS-A, ACSID-11 and GDT have the most evidential support. Overall, GD tool evidence has developed positively in several areas but remains mixed. There are multiple tools with relatively greater evidential support in distinct areas (e.g., GAS-7, Lemmens IGD-9, IGDS9-SF, IGDT-10), but there was no markedly superior tool accounting for all practical and/or psychometric considerations.

4. Discussion

The gaming disorder field is burgeoning, set against an international context of growing social, academic, and political concerns about negative human-technology interactions. This review aimed to evaluate all available quantitative evidence involving at least one GD assessment tool and to consider these research contributions with respect to the field in general.

The scope of this review was very substantial in its own right and relative to all previous reviews on GD topics. Overall, this review identified 49 tools employed across 1,296 peerreviewed studies involving more than 2.7 million participants. At the current rate of growth, and assuming its sustainability, the GD field may expand to more than 2,000 studies by the year 2033. The main findings were: (1) tool coverage of DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 diagnostic criteria has improved since the previous 2020 review, particularly for assessment of 'continued use despite harm'; (2) although some tools have gained considerable research momentum, instrument choice differs greatly across epidemiological, neurobiological, and treatment research streams and this variability does not appear to have any guiding rationale; (3) the psychometric properties of tools are generally acceptable on basic reliability indicators but most have not been subject to rigorous validity checks or clinical validation; and (4) as noted above, the GD evidence base has quadrupled in size since 2020 but these research efforts have not produced consensus on which tool is ideal for any given population or research purpose.

This review reinforces the view that problematic gaming tools are based predominantly on the clinical descriptions of GD in the DSM and ICD systems. It bears noting that only the ICD-11 refers to GD as a formal clinical diagnosis and that its status in the DSM classification will likely not change until the next edition. Tools tend to be either: (i) a direct transposition of DSM/ICD criteria into questions or self-referential statements, or (ii) less commonly, a mix of DSM/ICD-based items and items referring to other gaming-related experiences (e.g., shame and regret about playing, coping with stress by playing, daydreaming about games). This finding seems to reflect that the study of problematic gaming has predominantly locked into a confirmatory approach that imposes a general view of problem gaming as an addiction rather than exploring other potential explanations (Billieux et al., 2015; Kardefelt-Winther, 2017). As a related aside, there was a lack of explicit reference to procrastination and habit-related concepts in the identified tools (Nordby, Løkken, & Pfuhl, 2019).

The tendency of researchers to conceptualize problematic gaming as an addictive disorder consistent with DSM and ICD systems means that tools may be less sensitive or purpose-built to assess forms of sub-clinical gaming (e.g., hazardous gaming) and emerging issues. This seems particularly relevant to research involving adolescents which has a broad focus on 'problematic gaming' (and not necessarily GD), where it is conventional for tools to refer exclusively to addiction concepts (e.g., IGDS9-SF, IGDT-10, YDQ). The field appears to lack alternative surveys for adolescents that may be more amenable to their views about gaming and its developmental functions in personal (e.g., identity) and social (e.g., peer group belonging) domains. The 'one size fits all' approach -whereby GD tools attempt to evaluate the functional impact of gaming in relation to GD symptoms only -may limit the capability of studies to test alternative hypotheses about the role of gaming in mental health and wellbeing.

A related symptom coverage issue involves the general differences in DSM and ICD tools and the extent to which this may contribute to divergence of data streams. This review found that almost 400,000 participants out of 2.7 million participants have been administered ICDbased tools; however, the use of DSM tools seems to be standard practice. DSM-based tools have a broader scope of content and refer to symptomatology adapted from substance use disorder criteria that have attracted critical attention, particularly regarding criteria related to preoccupation, tolerance, and withdrawal (Castro-Calvo et al., 2021; Kardefelt-Winther, 2014; King & Delfabbro, 2014). Although these features are considered relevant to understanding the experiences of many individuals with gaming problems, they also tend to be reported by highly engaged gamers and therefore can inflate prevalence rates (Zhou et al., 2024).

This review provides insights into the conventions in assessment at the level of subdisciplines in the GD field. Significantly, most neurobiological studies of GD (n=67) have used the YIAT. This instrument was published in 1998 by Kimberly Young, one of the first psychologists with an interest in understanding the mechanisms of problematic usage of the internet. The YIAT was developed and tested in Young's clinical practice and its content reflects the types of issues affecting individuals in that time and context. Although the tool is highly cited and has been used in more than 100 studies since the finalization of the ICD-11, the tool has some face validity issues related to applying outdated types and patterns of internet use to gaming behavior (see King et al., 2020a). These issues not only raise validity-related doubts, but also concerns regarding specificity, which is particularly relevant to neurobiological studies (as well as other similarly sized studies, e.g., experimental and case-control studies) where sample sizes are typically small (e.g., 10 to 30 individuals) and classification errors may attenuate or obscure meaningful between-group differences (Vaccaro & Potenza, 2019).

Further complicating this evidence base, few studies report the diagnostic thresholds used to define case groups (Chang et al., 2023). Together, these issues may contribute to inconsistent observations in GD genetics research (Park et al. 2018) and would recommend the need for innovation and improved procedures in this subfield. This review examined differences in research evidence at the criterion level of GD tools.

It is acknowledged that screening tools do not necessarily need to assess all features of a condition to be a useful screen. However, as outlined in the ICD-11, certain GD criteria are particularly salient and central to defining the condition, particularly indicators related to impaired control, and these should be prioritized in brief measures. The most significant improvement since the 2020 review has been the greater representation of the item that assesses 'continued use despite harm', which increased from 12% to 70% of the pooled sample of participants. This item is valuable because it identifies a key indicator of impaired control and supports differentiation of individuals with gaming problems from those who report high but non-problematic gaming (Billieux et al., 2017; 2019). The improved focus on impaired control/continuation despite negative consequences in GD tools seems to indicate the field's strong uptake of the streamlined ICD-11 criteria that emphasize its importance.

Another finding was that none of the new tools included items about deception and financial harm due to gaming. The omission of items on lies, secrecy, and concealing behavior, which are common in problem gambling experiences and assessment (Fulton, 2022; Stinchfield, 2013), may reflect views that these experiences are not always relevant to all problem gaming, particularly when conducted under social surveillance in the home (Castro-Calvo et al., 2021; King & Delfabbro, 2019). These experiences are also personally sensitive and may be less freely disclosed, especially in sessions with a partner and family members. However, not measuring deception may overlook its value in understanding harms, which is particularly central to public health approaches to non-substance-related addictive behaviors (Price, Hilbrecht, & Billi, 2021). Similarly, the lack of GD items on the act of spending money on gaming and the financial consequences of game-related spending may be a 'blind spot' in GD screening. In recent years, there has been significant expansion of the so-called 'free to play' games market that generally involves making regular small purchases (microtransactions) to sustain gaming activity (Carey et al., 2022; King & Delfabbro, 2018). These products pose greater risks for unregulated and excessive spending due to gambling-like elements (Raneri et al., 2022), and these issues may be identified among individuals referred for treatment.

This review provides a comprehensive summary of the psychometric properties of GD tools. Consistent with the 2020 review, most tools demonstrated acceptable to good internal consistency based on standard alpha indicators. Similarly, test-retest reliability has been evaluated for 25 instruments, most commonly over two-week intervals, establishing adequate short-term temporal stability. The present review found that most tools' (45/49) dimensionality had been evaluated by at least exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis. In contrast to the 2020 review which reported that tools consistently showed a unidimensional factor structure, this review presents a more complex profile. Notably, analysis of ICD-11-based instruments have yielded two-, three-, and four-factor solutions.

It is also important, when considering ICD-11-based tools, to distinguish whether they assess all three main criteria (impaired control, increasing priority, and continuation) plus functional impairment, or whether they measure only two of the three main criteria but include additional items that assess the clinical features of GD as defined in the ICD-11 (e.g., craving).

While these are interesting in individual cases, they are not mandatory for diagnosis. The ACSID-11 (Müller et al., 2022), for example, has a four-factor structure, with three items measuring each of the three main criteria (factors 1-3) and two items operationalizing functional impairment (factor 4). The GADIS-A, as another example, has demonstrated consistent delineation of its cognitive-behavioral symptoms and functional consequences in two factor solutions (Paschke et al., 2021). These findings suggest that it may be necessary for some tools to carefully check item content and re-evaluate their scoring, particularly those with item-agnostic cut-offs. This undertaking could also evaluate whether response format architectures should distinguish active from remitted symptoms. Established WHO screening instruments for other addictive disorders (e.g., ASSIST/AUDIT), for example, separate lifetime from recent symptom endorsement (Humeniuk et al., 2008; Saunders et al., 1993). This review found that there is a small but emerging literature that is attempting to validate tools in studies using structured psychiatric interviews and against standard measures of quality of life. The GD field is currently limited by its lack of a standard interview schedule for GD and gaming-related problems, and therefore most studies have used interview protocols, such as the MINI, to assess comorbidity in GD trials. The next advance for the field would be to incorporate GD in standard international interview schedules or to develop a new schedule that assesses GD alongside consideration of adjacent conditions (e.g., other addictive disorders), relevant comorbidities (e.g., anxiety), and issues for differential diagnosis (e.g., autism spectrum). The WHO Flexible Interview for ICD-11 (FLII-11) developed by the WHO and the Columbia-WHO Center for Global Mental Health is positioned to potentially meet these requirements (Reed et al., 2024). Regarding GD tools and quality of life indicators, this review found that only 12 of 49 instruments had been assessed in this way. In studies using the WHOQOL-BREF, some studies reported multiple domain impairments (e.g., Larrieu et al. 2022; Sharma et al. 2022), but data on the negative health and social effects of GD in young and non-clinical populations is lacking.

4.1 Limitations of the review

This review acknowledges several limitations, including many of the issues reported in King et al.'s (2020) review. First, this review was restricted to English-language publications, and tools without English versions or translations were excluded. This approach may have excluded a significant proportion of GD literature in the East Asian context, although major teams in these countries often publish in English journals. Second, the study was limited by the use of Google Scholar citation data as of December 2025. Citation and study count for each tool should be viewed as estimates only rather than precise figures, due to limitations inherent to aggregating citation-indexing across multiple databases. Third, the scoring system in Table 7 was not weighted and therefore not sensitive to large differences in evidential support in specific areas. Total scores reflect certain lines of evidence rather than evidence quality and should not be interpreted as instrument rankings or endorsement. Fourth, this review was exceptionally large and involved three database reviewers who extracted and compiled data over several months. There were integrity checks across review stages, but it should be noted that this was ultimately a human process and therefore subject to human error.

Future GD tool evaluations could be improved in several ways. Diagnostic accuracy evaluation could be refined by including indicators of sensitivity, specificity, and related classification metrics (e.g., area under the curve [AUC] derived from receiver operating characteristic [ROC] curve using a structured clinical interview as the criterion). To innovate on citation count approach, and given the large size of the GD literature, future work could conduct a formal bibliometric analysis (e.g., including measures of performance and networks) to evaluate the relative contributions of research beyond mere quantity of articles. Another area of analysis is differentiating the specific settings in which tools are used, such as primary care, addiction services, youth health, schools, and public health programmes, to then guide selection of the most appropriate measure for a given context. The process of cultural adaptation could also be examined; for example, examining whether the original meaning of items is preserved across contexts. Finally, broadening the content mapping beyond ICD-11 and DSM-5 TR criteria to capture domains such as gaming behavior and spending, physical health, sleep, and diet, may contribute to a fuller conceptualization of issues associated with the condition.

4.2 Conclusions

The task of assessing GD is complicated by the broad continuum of gaming that includes recreational gaming that has benefits and improves quality of life. Nevertheless, over the past four decades, many tools have been developed to identify problematic gaming to address various public health and clinical objectives. The present review confirms the previous 2020 systematic review's prediction that the GD field would continue to grow following the inclusion of GD in the ICD-11 and that the field may be hindered by overproduction of new tools. Overall, this review identified 49 tools used across 1296 studies, and this literature may be broadly characterized by its rapid but uncertain growth. Some tools have gained more uptake and research momentum than others, but instrument choice seems to be based on researchers' personal preferences rather than evidence or study requirements.

As noted previously, there is a need for a 'gold standard' tool to harmonize research efforts to generate a more cohesive and convincing evidence base. If more tools are to be developed, the field would benefit from synergistic diversity rather than producing more short self-report checklists: for example, researchers could trial tests based on alternative (nonaddiction) conceptualizations of problematic gaming; examine gaming-related harms more extensively; incorporate developmental and family dynamic considerations; and, test alternatives to self-report (e.g., visual tests, performance-based tests; implicit measures). The GD field is still relatively young in comparison to the addictive disorders that it sits alongside in clinical nomenclature. There is certainly value in applying the lessons that have been successful in these areas, but the GD field must also develop and innovate its assessment to cater to the unique qualities of gaming and problematic gaming presentations.
