What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Conversation unfolds between speakers as a series of coordinated updates from prior ideas expressed in earlier utterances to new sets of ideas. This process of speakers and conversation partners jointly updating prior "conversation states" is an important piece of the cognitive scaffolding that enables social coordination, and even collective cognition -the capacity for individuals to pool their cognitive resources to solve problems together. However, a functional data-driven description of how this process works is missing in the extant literature. This report provides a functional description of how conversation states are updated in terms of information-theoretic processes, while analyzing a corpus of 5,448 conversations in nine typologically diverse languages. Our results show that individuals form a strongly connected distributed computational system during conversation, which generates new information according to a power-law distribution. These properties of conversation may be the foundation upon which human interaction encodes and solves a potentially unbounded number of problems and ideas.

Significance statement: While a number of studies take for granted that conversation acts as a kind of complex dynamical system for social coordination, the literature lacks a foundational, function-centered, computational description of how conversation facilitates this. The current study leverages one of the largest collections of conversation data to date, compiled from several massive corpus projects, to root conversational progression in information-theoretic principles. Our results offer a new foundation that connects several research programs in the language sciences and enables new formal theoretical development.

* Fractal scaling facilitates encoding and processing infinite kinds of problems and tasks [28, 29]

1 Introduction

What actually happens when you say something during a conversation? Every utterance we speak is comprised of sets of (related) ideas and concepts [1] [2] [3]. In order for conversation to progress, both ourselves and our conversation partners (CPs) must somehow build on those ideas [4] [5] [6], often only focusing on a subset of them we find relevant or interesting [2, 7].

This behavior is central to human coordination. It's an important piece of scaffolding for coordinating with one another in many diverse domains, including active learning [8], maintaining a pleasant conversation [9], and pooling our cognitive resources together as a group to solve problems [10] [11] [12] [13] [14] [15]. Each of these are examples of extended [16] [17] [18] or, more specifically, collective [19, 20] cognition -the process of coordinating with one another to co-construct some group-level cognitive outcome.

We can observe the outcomes of collective cognition in a number of speaker-partner behaviors including: (1) alignment in speakers and partners' linguistic features [21, 22]; (2) formation of complementary behaviors during interaction [23] [24] [25] [26]; and in (3) periodic structure of sentence-level entailments between adjacent utterances [27]. These diverse patterns of coordination may be undergirded by a set of principles that characterize an underlying system that organizes interaction. Diverse collaborative behaviors would emerge from that system's organizing principles, yet the present literature lacks a quantitative model that may serve as this foundation. This missing piece is an important bridge that would synthesize how interaction builds in a turn-by-turn manner into larger coordinative behaviors.

Because each contributor of new ideas in a conversation is a unique individual, and because the outcomes of their interaction are shared, how people collaborate to build on prior concepts should exhibit principles of distributed computation -when separate, unique components work together to form a singular computational system. Naturally occurring distributed computation has two important features: (1) power-law, or fractal scaling * † in their outputs [30, 31], and (2) demonstrable mutual influence across the components of the system [30].

Thus, we hypothesize that conversation, as a dynamical system that facilitates speakers' and partners' building on prior concepts, exhibits these two traits.

One way of measuring differences from one set of concepts to the next is to calculate the information [32] required to move from the ideas in different utterances. Movement between sets of things (metaphorically, like locations) generates statistical information [32] [33] [34]. Ergo, if we start from some prior utterance (x), we can calculate how much information is required to move from the ideas in it to any subsequent utterance (y). If each turn by speakers or CPs uniquely generates new information [2, 4, 6], but needs to be related to prior turns in order to support computation [30, 31], then the way information accrues across turns should exhibit properties of distributed computation. This forms a fundamental constraint on how information accrues over conversation in order to maintain its utility as a mechanism for joint reasoning processes, broadly and irrespective of specific task structure. We look here for evidence of distributed computation in the current study.

The current study analyzed patterns in how, given any random starting point in a conversation, speakers add new information to build on prior idaes. We applied a novel information-theoretic and language-modeling framework for big data to a massive dataset of 5448 conversations compiled from dozens of naturalistic corpora of people engaging in normal conversations. Our results demonstrate that conversation can provide a vital medium for distributed, universal computation where parties generate information via moving away from prior ideas in ways that allow for infinite -i.e., universal -variability.

5 General discussion

Across conversations, languages, and language families, we find that (1) speakers and their partners accumulate new information according to a fractal power-law order parameter, (2) this information accrual rate arises from speakers engaging in their immediate conversational context, and (3) speakers appear to coordinate their rates of information accrual, with speakers generating new information relative to their own utterances at a slightly faster, more "critical" rate than CPs, but CPs being in sync with them in their own information generation behavior. Though, evidence for coordinated differences in speaker and CP behavior is only weakly powered. Indeed, in the crosslinguistic study, which, while leveraging a smaller number of examples, encompasses greater diversity in human participants and behaviors, complexity matching appears to be more likely than synergy.

An important implication of the fractal power-law exponents is that conversations tend to be more orderly (i.e., less information dense) for turns that are closer to a particular reference point. This tendency points towards a tendency for humans to communicate in a way such that turns in close proximity are more related to one another semantically, and that similarity isn't inherently stable or uniform across time. Pick any turn and speakers will generate, or "pack", the most information density in latter turns.

Power-law scaling, and especially fractal powerlaw scaling, allows for non-normative starting points in the amount of information people generate, even in close proximity to an initial utterance x. What does this mean? There may not be a characteristic rate of information that one contributes to a conversation right after an utterance x is proclaimed. That can vary. But the behavior of adding information -less early on, and rapidly increasing before leveling off -is consistent across speakers. The dynamics are shared, even if the initial amount of information contributed varies.

The presence of power-law scaling, context specificity, and coordinated behavior all hint towards speakers working together as a kind of distributed computational system. Distributed computation occurs when multiple interacting components can work together to "compute" outputs across a universal set of possible problems [30, 31]. Such systems in nature require (1) power-law scaling (which allows outputs to vary in initial values but to scale in predictable ways, supporting computation over an infinite number of possible problems [28, 29, 41, 42]) and (2) demonstrable coordination between the components of the system. Conversation meets both of these criteria. Thus, conversation can provide the requisite scaffolding for collective cognition.

6 Conclusion

Framing conversation as optimized for generating and compounding new information provides a scaffolding linking a number of potentially contradicting prior theories. For example, while Interactive Alignment Theory (IAT: [21, 22]) predicts that speakers will increasingly align on shared linguistic and cognitive structures regardless of all other factors, Communication Accommodation Theory (CAT: [43, 44]) predicts that alignment is modulated according to social and cognitive goals. Interpersonal Synergy (IS: [23] [24] [25] [26]) contradicts IAT as well, positing that people tend to form compensatory behaviors rather than just aligning. Other work appears to indicate that alignment at the level of the semantic entailments for whole, adjacent utterances has a periodic structure, contradicting all three theories above [27, 45]. All of these behaviors, however, can be linked from the current perspective. If conversation is (functionally) a tool for compounding information, then each of these theories is a different outcome of more general information generation processes.

A universal pattern in how information accumulates across languages points to a possible, usage-based "linguistic universal".

Linguistic universals are typically posited to be universal grammatical features shared across languages [46], though they are quite rare [47], and empirically validated universals tend to be either pragmatic [48, 49] or arise from clear biological constraints [50]. The current results are indeed more pragmatic, and tell us nothing about grammatical structures of the languages surveyed. But if language in communication is optimized for coordinating how speakers and CPs generate new information, then various groups can be left to evolve varied linguistic features and norms based on the immediate needs of those communities [51, 52]. This does not necessarily contradict generative approaches either, as observed (versus possible) features likely arise from human cognitive constraints.

While we present evidence that power-law rules discussed here are widespread, their genesis is opaque. Did humans evolve to innately generate power-law distributed outputs during communication? Or is this a learned behavior that develops as children age? Future studies could look to longitudinal development of information generation processes in children as a possible way to answer this question.

There are further limitations to the findings described here. For example, while in aggregate there is a tendency for particular values for powerlaw exponents, there is still variation. What might cause that variance? Perhaps differences in context trigger subtle differences in the rules we uncovered? The current study answers questions about aggregate phenomena, but these finer-grained questions remain unaccounted for.

The current work is focused exclusively on understanding dynamical patterns in conversation -i.e., in multiparty interactions. But contemporary research shows that monologic, intra-personal communication shares many of the same attributes of dialog [53, 54]. While outside of the current paper's scope, future work should look at the similarities (and differences) between dialogic and monologic communication. What is shared (and what differs) between the two will likely tell us important information about the cognitive constraints that structure both.

A.1 Measuring changes in semantic information from one point in time in a conversation to the next

How does one identify power-law changes in information being generated during the course of a conversation? A number of a priori choices about how to represent the temporal dimension of a conversation need to be made in order to effectively measure this. We decided on the following: It is not clear the degree to which speakers experience the passage of time per se during conversation. Nor did we have access to time-stamped data in all cases. We thus opted to represent time in terms of the number of turns having occurred prior to some utterance. Thus, if a speaker produces a turn after 20 turns have already occurred, the measurement t for that turn is 21. Temporal distance, or ∆ t , between any two turns is thus the number of turns between two utterances.

Notably, conversations occur between two or more speakers in our data. This also poses an interesting problem. What is an appropriate measurement of distance when attempting to analyze conversations for what may be powerlaw distributed effects? In the current study, we wanted to know the degree to which information changes from one speakers' first utterance y after some utterance x to their next utterance y after x. Our hypothesis is that changes in information will scale according a power-law the farther apart the first utterance y is from the next utterance y, for each speaker in a conversation. So, for example, if we are testing how easily we can recover the semantic content some prior utterance x at time t = 1, a speaker's (or conversation partner, p) first utterance after x occurs at time t = 4, and their next utterance occurs at time t = 7, then ∆ t for that speaker moving from their first turn to their next turn is ∆ t = 7 -4 = 3. If their next utterance occurs at time t = 10, then ∆ t = 10 -4 = 6. This distinction treats each combination of speakers -the speaker having spoken the first utterance, x, and the speaker having spoken some subsequent utterance(s) y -as unique time-series in the data. Our hypotheses, thus, center on the idea that information scales according to a power-law as ∆ t increases in each time-series. We denote an utterance y spoken by some speaker s as y s . An example conversation demonstrating how we partition time is included in figure 1a.

One last note: prior work has shown that utterances made by the same speaker who spoke the utterance x (self , or formally sp(x) = sp(y)) tend to generate less new information in more temporally proximal turns, but scale much faster in how much information they generate, when compared to utterances spoken by one of their conversation partners in the same conversation (conversation partners, p, or formally sp(x) ̸ = sp(y)). We thus differentiate between the two conditions using the above mentioned annotations.

A.1.1 Measuring information gain in semantic content

Every utterance in a conversation causes that conversation to update to a new state [5, 6]. Those states are comprised of a number of things, but chief among them is a set of prior semantic concepts.

There are a number of ways to measure differences (or their inverse, alignment) in semantic content when comparing between utterances [56]. One recent, and particularly useful, mechanism for measuring changes in semantic content from one utterance to the next is convergence-information (CI) and convergence-entropy (CE) (For CE, see: [35]; for CI, which is derived from CE, see: [36]; and for a related measurement to CI, see also [57]). We focus in this paper on CI. CI uses a large language model (LLM) to convert two utterances (x and y) into a series of embeddings for each token in those utterances (for all tokens i ∈ x and j ∈ y; see equation 5). It then compares every token i ∈ x to tokens j ∈ y to find the embedding in y that is closest to the embedding for the token i in the utterance x in that embedding space (measured using Cosine Error, or CoE). It then converts the distance between the token i ∈ x and its nearest neighbor in y to a probability (equation 6), and then asks how much Shannon information [32] is required to reconstruct the utterance x using those nearest neighbors (equation 7).

Importantly, α is characteristic of how information accumulation behaves. When α ≈ 0, information accumulates in an unpredictable, unorderly way regardless of time. This means that some early parts of the CDF could jump well beyond what we would expect even logarithmically of later changes to the CDF. When ||α|| ≈ 1, information accumulation scales linearly over time. Often, this is due to Gaussian random processes, where each addition of information is "randomly" sampled from a simple Gaussian distribution and thus each new addition of information is approximately equal to the last contribution. Notably, when ||α|| > 1, this indicates that information accumulates slowly at first but then rapidly increases as time progresses. Conversely, ||α|| < 1 indicates that information accumulates quickly at the onset, and then hits an upper limit or ceiling as time progresses. While our analysis is different from some spectral analyses [see : 62], the result of a power-law exponent ||α|| < 1 is similar to that of 1/f scaling: as time progresses, any additional gains in information decrease. This fractional power-law scaling is indeed definitionally fractal in the current case, even if it is not periodic or "pink", as it is directly related to self-similarity in the density of mass as a function of time [37, 38].

It is worth noting that each speaker might contribute a new utterance at varying lengths of time after their first utterance. For example, a speaker might contribute an utterance at time t = 4, and they may not speak again until some 20 turns later at time t = 24. Given that we calculate an integral for each time series, large variations in time between subsequent utterances can potentially bias our results towards fractal scaling if they are not controlled for in some way-there may be huge stretches in time where it seems like information accrual is utterly stagnant. While that would yield exciting results for our study-we are precisely interested in whether or not information accrues according to a fractal pattern as a necessary condition for supporting distributed computation and thus collective cognition-that result would likely be an artifact of our analytical method rather than a genuine result. Instead, we integrate our data according to how many utterances in the series for the speaker-CP pair has passed thus far, and then leverage the difference in time ∆ t as an index for how much of the integral is "available" at that point in time. This works because there is an exactly bijective mapping between the number/index of an utterance in some series and its ∆ t . If a speaker contributes three utterances -one at time t = 4, another at time t = 13, and another at time t = 20 -then the amount of information that that speaker has contributed by their second turn at time ∆ t = (13 -4) = 9 is the total amount of information in the integral after their second turn. While this "squishes" the distribution for the integral significantly in some ways, it biases our results towards a null result rather than fractal scaling outright, either making the integral appear more linear/Gaussian than it actually is over time, or more chaotic if there is no characteristic slope to how the CDF scales.

A.2 Identifying coordination across components of a conversation (complexity matching)

Complexity matching is a tool for identifying the relationship in what are called the "order parameters" [39, 40] for separate components in a complex system. In the current case, we take the power-law exponent for subsequent utterances y spoken by the same speaker of utterance x (sp(x) = sp(y)), and compare that to the power-law exponent for utterance y spoken by the conversation partner(s) of the speaker of x (sp(x) ̸ = sp(y)). We test for statistically significant differences in this output using a simple T-test procedure with equal variances. There are two reasons for this simple procedure: first, the method proposed in [39] is mathematically equivalent to the comparison between probability distributions (see equation 11, p. 12). Second, using the T-Test procedure retains information about possible systematic asymmetries, which themselves may be evidence not of complexity matching per se, but of influence dynamics as described in [39], or possibly interpersonal synchronization in behavior [23, 24]. In the event that speakers are engaged in interpersonal synchrony rather than attempting to achieve complete alignment (as is implied by complexity matching), then the simple comparative method of the T-test used here is preferable as a means of surfacing those possibly asymmetrical relationships.

The CDF for each speaker represents the proportion of total information that they will thus contribute to the conversation after some utterance x.

There is a useful and intuitive way to think about how the integral over total information is operating in this instance, and how that relates to power-law and fractal scaling. If one drew an x and y axes on a piece of paper, where the x-axis is the number of turns between the first turn spoken by a speaker or CP after x and a current turn being measured, and the y axis is the amount of information for the comparison of the utterance x to the utterance y at that point (i.e., ∆ t ), the integral gives the total amount of the paper covered/under the curve up to that value of ∆ t . The proportional integral shrinks the size of that paper to a single cube. The scaling exponent in this measurement regime, thus, tells us the expected total mass of the distribution up until that point, and fractional scaling here indicates that the mass curves in on itself in a way that is fractal or scale-free.
