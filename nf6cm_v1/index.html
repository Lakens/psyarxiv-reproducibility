description Research suggests that the difficulties experienced by people with Williams syndrome (WS) in their daily lives could be largely explained by alterations in Executive Functions (EF) (Greer et al., 2017). EF constitute a set of high-level cognitive processes essential for the planning, organization, initiation, and self-regulation of goal-directed behavior, allowing for adaptation to new and changing environmental contexts (Diamond, 2013).

Within EF, inhibitory control has been one of the most widely studied components in this population. There is a certain consensus in identifying three types of inhibition based on the information processing stages in which they occur: perceptual inhibition, cognitive inhibition, and motor inhibition (Aydmune & Introzzi, 2018; Harnishfeger, 1995). The existing literature shows considerable variability both in the types of inhibition evaluated and in the tasks employed for their measurement in individuals with WS. Studies such as those by Menguini et al. (2010), Greer et al. (2013, 2017), and Carney et al. (2013), which compare the executive functioning of individuals with WS to those with typical development (TD), observe difficulties in inhibition processes within the WS population. Conversely, Costanzo et al. (2013) do not find difficulties in these processes among individuals with WS when compared to individuals with Down syndrome (DS) and those with TD. Therefore, the findings remain inconclusive.

Despite the growing interest in this topic, there are currently no syntheses available that allow for robust conclusions to be drawn regarding the inhibitory profile in individuals with WS. For this reason, the present study aims to conduct a scoping review of the existing literature and synthesize the findings obtained from behavioral, cognitive, motor, and ecological inhibition tasks, as well as to examine the consistency of the results based on the methodological characteristics of the studies.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

How Code Check works

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Inhibitory control is a core component of executive functioning and plays an important role in cognitive, behavioral, and socio-emotional adaptation. It is commonly conceptualized as comprising three components: cognitive, motor, and perceptual inhibition.

People with Williams syndrome (WS) frequently exhibit executive difficulties, including inhibitory impairments. However, findings remain dispersed across studies using different assessment approaches. This scoping review aimed to map the evidence on inhibitory functioning in WS, considering its three components and ecological functioning assessed through informant-based questionnaires.

Methods: Following Joanna Briggs Institute recommendations and PRISMA-ScR guidelines, searches were conducted in ProQuest, Web of Science, Scopus, and EBSCOhost. Empirical studies published between 2000 and 2026 involving genetically confirmed WS cohorts were included. Overall, 46 documents comprising 50 studies were reviewed.

Results: Studies were predominantly cross-sectional and evaluative, relying mainly on performance-based tasks. Samples were generally small and heterogeneous, and intervention studies were scarce. Findings indicate that inhibitory functioning is an area of vulnerability in WS, although difficulties vary across components. Most studies using informant-based questionnaires also reported clinically relevant difficulties in everyday inhibitory functioning.

Associations with cognitive, behavioral, and socio-emotional variables were identified.

Conclusions: Inhibitory functioning represents an area of vulnerability in WS, with potential implications for everyday adaptation. However, evidence remains uneven across components, particularly perceptual inhibition. Further research using specific assessment measures is needed to clarify these difficulties. Longitudinal studies are also required to understand developmental trajectories, while intervention research should establish which inhibitory components may be amenable to improvement.

1. Introduction

Executive functions (EF) constitute a set of high-level cognitive processes essential for the planning, organization, initiation, and self-regulation of goal-directed behavior (Diamond, 2013; Miyake et al., 2000). The psychometric model proposed by Miyake et al. (2000) posits three semi-independent components of EF: inhibition, working memory, and cognitive flexibility. In a subsequent update, Friedman and Miyake (2017) maintain this tripartite architecture, yet suggest that response inhibition may act as a common underlying mechanism shared by the other two components.

Within this framework, inhibitory control constitutes one of the core components of EF.

Inhibitory processes are defined as the cognitive capacity to control behavior, attention, memories, thoughts, language, and emotions, in order to facilitate necessary or prioritized behaviors through the suppression of task-or situation-irrelevant information (Diamond, 2013; Introzzi et al., 2016). There is a certain consensus in differentiating its components based on the stages of information processing in which they take place, thus distinguishing among perceptual inhibition, cognitive inhibition, and motor inhibition (Aydmune & Introzzi, 2018; Borella et al., 2013; Traverso et al., 2018).

Perceptual inhibition refers to the capacity to select relevant stimuli and suppress interference generated by environmental distractor stimuli (Hasher et al., 2007; Introzzi et al., 2016). A classic paradigm for measuring this inhibitory type is the Flanker task (Eriksen & Eriksen, 1974) in which the participant must identify a target stimulus located between two distractor stimuli (Introzzi et al., 2016). Cognitive inhibition enables the overriding of an intrusive automatic mental representation currently activated in working memory or long-term memory, facilitating the suppression of unwanted memories or thoughts (Aydmune & Introzzi, 2018; Baddeley et al., 2009; MacLeod, 2007; Nigg, 2000). This component is decisive for resolving proactive interference situations, in which prior learning hinders the acquisition of new information (Introzzi et al., 2016). Stroop-type tasks (Stroop, 1935) constitute a prototypical paradigm of this situation (Engle, 2002; Fontana et al., 2021; Gerstadt, 1994). Finally, motor inhibition refers to the capacity to suppress prepotent or automatic behavioral responses, thereby allowing for behavioral regulation and socially adaptive actions (Diamond, 2013). This component serves as the foundation for behavioral self-control by enabling impulse regulation and the adaptation of behavior to contextual demands and social norms (Diamond, 2013; Verbruggen & Logan, 2009). Go-NoGo-type tasks, whose objective is to inhibit or halt a current motor response to replace it with a different one, constitute a prototypical paradigm of this situation.

Inhibitory control plays a fundamental role in general cognitive functioning, as it has been linked to the development of skills such as fluid intelligence (Diamond, 2013), reading comprehension (Borella et al., 2010), and mathematical performance (Cragg et al., 2017), as well as learning and information retrieval processes (Aydmune et al., 2019). Likewise, it has been associated with the regulation of impulsivity and adaptive behavior in social and emotional contexts (Carlson & Wang, 2007), memory retrieval (Anderson, 2001), and cognitive aging trajectories (Hasher & Zacks, 1988).

Alterations in inhibitory processes have been linked to various consequences. In the general population, the decline of inhibitory control with age contributes to age-related cognitive decline, because older adults present significant difficulties in ignoring irrelevant information, which affects attentional and memory processes (Hasher & Zacks, 1988; Aydmune & Introzzi, 2018). Furthermore, deficits in inhibition have been related to risk behaviors such as substance use (Dalley & Robbins, 2017), difficulties in emotional regulation (Schmeichel & Tang, 2015), and problems in learning and attention (Diamond, 2013).

In the clinical setting, alterations in inhibitory control have been described in various neuropsychological disorders, such as obsessive-compulsive disorder (Chamberlain et al., 2005) or schizophrenia (Bullen & Hemsley, 1987); and in neurodevelopmental disorders (NDD) such as Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) (Barkley et al., 1997) or autism (Ozonoff & Strayer, 1997). In individuals with NDD who present with intellectual disability (ID) of genetic origin, difficulties in inhibition are also commonly found (Borella et al., 2013; Palomino et al., 2019; Spaniol & Danielson, 2022).

Among the various NDD associated with ID of genetic origin is Williams syndrome (WS). WS is caused by a hemizygous microdeletion of approximately 25-75 genes on chromosome 7q11.23, with an estimated incidence ranging from 1 in 7,500 to 10,000 live births (Mervis & John, 2010; Strømme et al., 2002). From a cognitive standpoint, it is characterized by a heterogeneous profile, featuring relative strengths in linguistic abilities, verbal short-term memory, and face processing, alongside significant difficulties in visuospatial construction and reasoning (Kozel et al., 2021; Mervis & John, 2010).

At the behavioral level, it is characterized by a distinctive social phenotype marked by high sociability, a tendency toward interpersonal approach, and social disinhibition (Riby et al., 2014), as well as behavioral problems and a high prevalence of comorbidities such as ADHD and anxiety disorders (Rhodes et al., 2011). Cognitively, significant difficulties are observed in attention, memory, and language, accompanied by impairments in social cognition processes, including theory of mind (Campos, 2009; Karmiloff-Smith, 1995), and alterations in emotional regulation (Rhodes et al., 2011). Finally, on a neurobiological level, the syndrome presents marked emotional hyperreactivity associated with specific patterns of neurofunctional processing and neuroanatomical correlates (Kozel et al., 2021).

In this context, alterations in EF, and particularly in inhibitory control, could serve as a relevant explanatory mechanism for understanding the difficulties observed in the daily lives of individuals with WS (Greer et al., 2017). Cáceres-Álvarez et al. (2025) conducted a systematic review on executive functioning in individuals with ID and found widespread alterations across different executive components.

However, the results were not analyzed based on specific etiology, which limits the identification of differential executive profiles associated with concrete syndromes.

Complementarily, the meta-analysis by Spaniol and Danielsson (2022) showed that individuals with ID exhibit significantly lower performance in EF tasks, including inhibition, compared to control groups matched by mental age, with particularly marked effects observed in individuals with fragile X syndrome and WS. Nevertheless, they do not distinguish between different inhibitory types. Meanwhile, in a recent scoping review, Baeza et al. (2026) concluded that EFs constitute an area of vulnerability in individuals with WS, observing difficulties across the various components evaluated, including inhibition.

Overall, although available evidence suggests that executive difficulties, and especially those related to inhibition, constitute a relevant characteristic of individuals with WS, findings remain dispersed across studies that differ in, and fail to differentiate between, the types of inhibition evaluated, the tasks employed, and sample characteristics. Furthermore, to our knowledge, to date there is no scoping review focused specifically on inhibitory functioning in this population.

Therefore, the present scoping review aims to: (1) examine and map the available evidence on inhibitory functioning and its types (cognitive, motor, and perceptual inhibition) in individuals with WS through the integration of studies published since the year 2000 and the specific analysis of how each has been evaluated using standardized questionnaires, behavioral scales, and various performance tasks; and (2) synthesize existing findings in order to identify consistent patterns, discrepancies between studies, and gaps in the literature, as well as to guide future lines of research in this field.

2. Method

>Method

2.1. Design

A scoping review was conducted following the methodological recommendations of the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) described by Peters et al. (2020), which is structured into seven fundamental phases: (1) protocol development, (2) protocol registration, (3) identification of evidence through the search strategy, (4) selection of relevant studies, (5) data extraction, (6) synthesis and presentation of results, and (7) interpretation of findings and drawing conclusions.

Likewise, this review complied with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist (Tricco et al., 2018).

To ensure transparency and reproducibility, the study protocol was registered a priori on the Open Science Framework (OSF) platform (see: https://osf.io/73ps9/overview for further methodological details).

Given that scoping reviews aim to map and synthesize the available evidence on a research topic regardless of the quality of the identified studies, a methodological quality appraisal of the included sources was not performed (Tricco et al., 2018).

2.2. Research Question and Objective

The primary research question developed to guide this scoping review was: "What empirical evidence exists on the assessment of inhibitory control in individuals with WS?" Therefore, the main objective was to map the available evidence regarding the assessment of inhibitory processes in this population.

2.3. Search Strategy

The literature search was carried out following the FDC Framework (Faceting, Derivation, Combination) proposed by Codina (2020). The faceting stage consisted of a preliminary search for articles in a single database (ProQuest), using the following query string: "Williams syndrome" AND "inhibition". From the results retrieved, key terms from articles related to inhibitory functioning in individuals with WS were identified. Next, the derivation phase involved identifying synonyms and related terms in both Spanish and English. Finally, in the combination phase, the identified terms were combined using Boolean operators to construct the final search strings.

Two complementary search strategies were designed: a general search on EF and a specific search on inhibition. To identify studies related to WS, the terms "Williams syndrome" OR "Beuren" OR "Williams-Beuren" OR "7q11.23" were used. These terms were combined using the Boolean operator "AND" with the terms corresponding to each search strategy. In the case of EF, the terms "executive function" OR "executive control" OR "cognitive control" were used.

For the specific search on inhibition, the terms "inhibition control" OR "interference control" OR "response inhibition" OR "cognitive inhibition" OR "inhibition processes" were employed. The retrieved documents were imported into Zotero (version 7.0.24). After removing duplicates, a total of 213 publications were obtained.

2.5. Data Extraction

Data extraction from the selected studies was performed manually by the first author, without the use of automation tools. All relevant data were entered into an Excel table, including publication type, publication year, geographical origin, sample characteristics, methodology employed, objectives, and main findings.

To classify geographical origin, the country where the research was conducted was recorded. In studies with multinational samples, classification was based on the predominant country in the sample. When this information was not available, the country of affiliation of the first author was used as the criterion.

Two methodological clarifications were adopted for the analysis of the samples. First, in studies where data came from questionnaires completed by parents, caregivers, or other informants (e.g., BRIEF), individuals with WS about whom the information was reported were counted as participants. Second, when a single article or thesis included several distinct studies with independent samples, objectives, or procedures, each was considered an independent unit of analysis. As a result, the 46 included documents yielded a total of 50 analyzed studies. This decision was adopted because, in numerous cases, it was not possible to determine with certainty whether there was sample overlap. This same criterion was applied to the analysis of methodological characteristics, objectives, types of inhibition evaluated, and results obtained.

In order to analyze the role that inhibition played within the research objectives, the studies were classified according to the centrality assigned to this construct. Primary centrality was considered when the main objective of the study was to examine some aspects of inhibitory functioning in individuals with WS. Conversely, it was classified as secondary centrality when inhibition was evaluated as part of a broader objective, such as the study of global executive functioning, the characterization of the cognitive or behavioral profile, or the analysis of other neuropsychological processes.

3. Results

>Results

3.1. Main Characteristics of the Studies

The majority of the analyzed studies corresponded to articles published in scientific journals (n = 34; 74%), followed by doctoral dissertations or master's theses (n = 10; 22%) and preprints (n = 2; 4%).

The temporal distribution of the publications showed a progressive increase in scientific production on inhibitory functioning in individuals with WS over the years. During the period between 2000 and 2009, 4 studies were identified (9%), while the highest number of publications was concentrated between 2010 and 2019 (n = 30; 65%). Finally, between 2020 and 2026, 12 studies were located (26%).

3.2. Sample Characteristics

The total number of participants with WS across the 50 analyzed studies was 1,628. The sample sizes for each study ranged from 8 to 308 participants with WS, with a median of 20.5 and a mean of 32.56 (SD = 42.7). The distribution showed a marked positive skew, with a high concentration of studies in the range of 15 to 25 participants with WS (n = 27; 54%), while a minority of studies with large sample sizes substantially increased overall variability.

Regarding sex distribution, this information was available in 42 studies, which collectively included 1,442 participants with WS. A virtually balanced proportion between sexes was observed, with 705 males (48.9%) and 737 females (51.1%).

Regarding the age of the samples, 8 studies (16%) exclusively included a child population (<12 years), while 5 studies (10%) focused on an adult population (>18 years). No studies were identified with samples composed solely of adolescents (12-17 years) or older adults (>65 years). In contrast, most studies presented samples with participants across different age groups: 16 studies (32%) included samples spanning all developmental stages, 11 studies (22%) combined child and adolescent populations, and 10 studies (20%) jointly included adolescents and adults.

In terms of intelligence quotient (IQ), 25 studies reported the mean as a measure of central tendency. Of these, the majority of the WS samples fell within the range of 50 to 69, indicating mild ID (n = 22; 88%), while one study (4%) presented values consistent with moderate ID, ranging between 35 and 49 (Ángeles-Rojas et al., 2025). Likewise, two studies (8%) reported means above 75, placing them on the borderline of low intellectual functioning (John & Mervis, 2010; Woodruff-Borden et al., 2010). Within this set, 14 studies also reported IQ ranges. Among them, 1 study presented a range spanning from mild to moderate ID, 6 studies featured broad ranges extending from severe ID to functioning within the normative range, and 7 studies had ranges extending from moderate ID to functioning within the normative range. Additionally, one study reported the median IQ (median = 58) (Condy et al., 2020), while another study described the sample's cognitive level as "mild to moderate" without providing additional quantitative data (Butti et al., 2024).

Regarding control/comparison groups, 26 studies (52%) included some type of control group.

Among them, most studies (n = 14; 28%) compared the performance of individuals with WS to typically developing (TD) groups. Additionally, 4 studies (8%) used comparison groups composed of a clinical population, specifically individuals with Down syndrome (DS), and 8 studies (16%) simultaneously included both TD groups and clinical populations. On the other hand, 24 studies (48%) did not incorporate control groups. Among these, 8 studies (16%) drew interpretations and conclusions based on the performance of the WS sample itself, while 16 studies (32%) relied on normative data/standardized norms as a benchmark to contextualize their results.

Finally, regarding the matching criteria between individuals with WS and comparison groups, most studies (n = 21) used chronological age and/or mental age as matching variables. In some cases, these criteria were supplemented with additional variables, such as gender, and less frequently, hand dominance, IQ, or socioeconomic status. Likewise, a minority of studies (n = 3) used specific measures of verbal or nonverbal ability to establish equivalence between groups. A small number of studies (n = 3) did not perform any explicit matching procedure between the compared groups.

3.3. Objectives and Methodology Employed

Regarding the objective of the research studies, most of the analyzed works were evaluative in nature (n = 48; 96%), focusing on the assessment of inhibitory functioning in individuals with WS. Only two studies (4%) adopted an intervention design aimed at improving aspects related to inhibitory processes in this population.

Regarding the centrality assigned to inhibition within the research objectives, 20 studies (40%) were classified as having primary centrality, whereas 30 studies (60%) addressed it as a secondary focus.

In terms of study design, cross-sectional designs predominated. Specifically, 27 studies (54%) employed comparative cross-sectional designs, comparing the performance of individuals with WS against one or more comparison groups. On the other hand, 21 studies (42%) used singlegroup cross-sectional designs focused on describing and analyzing inhibitory functioning within the WS sample itself. Finally, the two intervention studies (4%) employed pretest-posttest quasi-experimental designs with comparison groups, including one comparing two intervention programs and another with a waitlist control group.

Regarding the assessment instruments used, performance tasks predominated for evaluating inhibitory processes. Specifically, 32 studies (64%) relied exclusively on performance tasks, while 15 studies (30%) utilized third-party informant questionnaires or rating scales.

Additionally, 3 studies (6%) combined performance tasks with questionnaires.

3.4. Main Results and Conclusions

In order to facilitate the synthesis of the findings, the results were organized according to the different types of inhibition described in the literature: cognitive inhibition, motor inhibition, and perceptual inhibition. Additionally, a fourth section dedicated to "ecological inhibition" was included, understood as the manifestation of inhibitory difficulties in everyday contexts and evaluated primarily through questionnaires.

Cognitive inhibition

Of the 16 studies that evaluated cognitive inhibition, 14 provided interpretable results regarding inhibitory functioning in this population. The remaining two presented descriptive statistics without conclusive findings (Hocking et al., 2015) or used inhibition as a correlational variable (Little et al., 2013).

Approximately half of them (n = 6, 43%) reported difficulties in this component. Two of these based their conclusions on comparisons with normative data or on the analysis of the sample itself using a Stroop-type semantic interference task (Braga, 2013) and the NEPSY-II (Butti et al., 2024).

The remaining four studies identified difficulties based on comparisons with other groups. Three of them used TD groups: Carney et al. (2013) found a lower number of correct responses in the VIMI task, while Osorio et al. (2012) and Van Jaarsveldt and Jarrold (2025) observed a greater interference effect in the Stroop task. Finally, one study compared the performance of individuals with WS against both TD groups and a clinical group: López-Frutos et al. (2025) observed poorer performance by individuals with WS in the Day-Night task compared to both a DS group and a TD control group. On the other hand, two studies (14%) found mixed results. Foti (2015a, 2015b) analyzed retrograde inhibition in verbal and visuospatial domains, finding a retrograde inhibition effect in individuals with WS only in the verbal domain.

Finally, six studies (43%) found no difficulties in cognitive inhibition. These included comparisons with TD groups (Costanzo et al., 2013; Menghini et al., 2013; Porter et al., 2012), with DS groups (Porter et al., 2007 (Porter et al., , 2022)), or analyses based on normative data (Atkinson et al., 2003).

Additionally, four studies analyzed the relationship between cognitive inhibition and other variables in this population. Little et al. (2013) found a positive association between inhibition and social approach behavior. Likewise, Braga (2013) reported that poorer performance on the Stroop task was related to higher levels of self-injury and aggressiveness, whereas better performance was associated with better language skills. Osorio et al. (2012) found negative correlations between inhibition and IQ, and positive correlations between inhibition and working memory. Finally, Porter et al. (2007) found no significant associations between inhibition and the social approach and emotional recognition measures used.

Motor inhibition

Of the 23 studies that evaluated motor inhibition, 21 provided interpretable results regarding inhibitory functioning in this population. The remaining two presented only descriptive results or used inhibition as a correlational variable (Camp, 2014b; Purser et al., 2015).

Almost half of the studies (n = 10; 48%) reported difficulties in motor inhibition. Four of them based their conclusions on comparisons with normative data or on the analysis of the sample itself, observing a high number of commission errors on the CPT task (Marini et al., 2010; Willfors et al., 2021), low performance on the Detour box task (Atkinson et al., 2003), and difficulties in delay of gratification tasks (Arnold, 2022). The remaining six studies identified difficulties through comparisons with TD groups. Individuals with WS showed a higher number of commission errors, slower reaction times, or greater inhibitory costs in the CPT (Ashworth et al., 2015), Go/No-Go (Menghini et al., 2013), VIMI (Carney et al., 2013), H&F (Zarchi et al., 2014), inhibition task (Camp et al., 2014a), and Counterpointing task (Atkinson et al., 2003). Camp (2014a) and Zarchi et al. (2014) also included clinical groups for comparison. However, significant differences were observed only when compared to the TD groups.

Five studies (24%) obtained mixed results. In general, individuals with WS showed alterations in some inhibitory indicators -such as reaction times, sensitivity, or neurophysiological markerswhereas on other indices, such as accuracy or commission errors, no differences were observed relative to the comparison groups (Greer et al., 2013 (Greer et al., , 2017;; Hocking et al., 2013; Osorio et al., 2012; Sahlev et al., 2019).

For their part, six studies (29%) found no consistent difficulties in motor inhibition compared to TD groups (Capitão et al., 2010; Mobbs et al., 2007; Van Jaarsveldt & Jarrold, 2025), TD and DS groups (Costanzo et al., 2013; López-Frutos et al., 2025), or TD and fragile X syndrome groups (Cornish et al., 2007).

On the other hand, ten studies analyzed the relationship between motor inhibition and other variables. Findings showed associations with IQ, working memory, chronological age, emotional regulation, symptoms of inattention and hyperactivity, and performance on planning tasks (Arnold, 2022; Camp, 2014a; Hocking et al., 2013; Mobbs et al., 2007; Osorio et al., 2012; Willfors et al., 2021). The remaining studies found no significant correlations with the analyzed variables (Ashworth et al., 2015; Camp, 2014b; Capitão et al., 2010; Cornish et al., 2007).

Two studies complemented these findings using neurophysiological and neuroimaging techniques. Mobbs et al. (2007) observed reduced activation in frontostriatal regions involved in inhibitory control, while Greer et al. (2017) described alterations in the neurophysiological mechanisms of inhibition, reflected in a reduced amplitude of the N2 component, despite comparable behavioral performance on some measures.

Finally, one intervention study was identified that evaluated computerized response inhibition training without finding significant improvements in commission errors on a Go/No-Go task following the intervention or at follow-up (Brei, 2017).

Perceptual inhibition

Only two studies evaluated perceptual inhibition in individuals with WS using Flanker-type tasks. Both found difficulties in this component. Condy et al. (2022) observed that the majority of individuals with WS scored at least two standard deviations below the mean on age-adjusted scores. For their part, Zarchi et al. (2014) found lower performance in individuals with WS compared to participants with TD, DiGeorge syndrome or velocardiofacial syndrome, and ID of non-specific etiology.

Additionally, Condy et al. (2022) analyzed the relationship between task performance and other variables, finding a significant negative correlation with age.

Ecological inhibition

Of the 16 studies that evaluated ecological inhibition using questionnaires, 12 provided interpretable results regarding this component. The remaining four used inhibition as a correlational variable (Arnold, 2022; Camp et al., 2014b; John & Mervis, 2010) or did not report results relative to this dimension (Woodruff-Borden et al., 2010).

The majority of the studies (n = 9; 75%) reported difficulties in ecological inhibition. Six of them based their conclusions on comparisons with BRIEF normative data, finding clinically elevated scores (Boulton, 2018; Greiner de Magalhães, 2021; Kazzi et al., 2021; Ng-Cordell et al., 2018; Reeve, 2024; Schwarz, 2016). The remaining three studies identified difficulties through comparisons with other groups, observing higher scores in individuals with WS than in TD and/or DS groups (Camp et al., 2016; Hocking et al., 2024; John, 2011). The remaining four studies (33%) found no difficulties. All of them based their conclusions on comparisons with BRIEF normative data (Hocking et al., 2015; Nanovic, 2017; Pérez-García, 2015a , 2015b).

On the other hand, 13 studies analyzed the relationship between ecological inhibition and other variables. Findings showed associations with age, IQ, anxiety, social functioning, novel problem solving, aggressive behavior, and externalizing problems, as well as with other executive dimensions and measures of cognitive development (Arnold, 2022; Boulton, 2018; Camp et al., 2016; Greiner de Magalhães et al., 2021; John & Mervis, 2010; Kazzi et al., 2021; Nanovic, 2017; Ng-Cordell et al., 2018; Pérez-García, 2015a; Reeve, 2024; Schwarz, 2016). In contrast, four studies found no significant associations between ecological inhibition and the analyzed variables (Camp et al., 2014b; Hocking et al., 2015 Hocking et al., , 2024;; Pérez-García, 2015b).

Finally, one intervention study was identified; in it, Ángeles-Rojas et al. (2025) found no significant improvements in the BRIEF-2 inhibition scale following the application of two teleintervention programs.

4. Discussion

The present scoping review aimed to examine the available knowledge on inhibitory functioning in individuals with WS and to integrate the evidence published since 2000. In particular, it sought to identify and characterize the different types of inhibition addressed in the literature-including cognitive, motor, perceptual, and ecological inhibition-as well as to analyze the main approaches and tasks used for their assessment in this population. To the best of author's knowledge, this review constitutes the first systematic synthesis focused on inhibition in individuals with WS. A total of 46 documents were included, leading to the independent analysis of 50 studies.

The results indicate that interest in the study of inhibition in individuals with WS has experienced notable growth over the last two decades, especially from 2010 onwards. The majority of the reviewed studies were scientific articles conducted primarily in European countries.

Regarding sample characteristics, the review shows that most studies employ small sample sizes. This limitation, which is foreseeable given the low prevalence of WS (Mervis & John, 2010; Strømme et al., 2002), restricts statistical power to detect group differences.

Another notable aspect is the high heterogeneity of the included samples. The use of samples combining different developmental stages predominated, whereas studies focused on a single developmental stage were barely identified, making it difficult to characterize specific developmental trajectories. Furthermore, no studies focused exclusively on adolescents or older adults were found. Similarly, most studies reported mean IQs compatible with mild ID, which corresponds to the profile described in the literature (John & Mervis, 2010). However, IQ ranges were very wide and, in numerous cases, spanned from moderate or severe ID to levels close to normotypic intellectual functioning.

The methodology employed across studies also showed considerable variability. Approximately half of the studies incorporated comparison groups, generally with TD participants, while the rest relied on normative standards or interpreted results based on the performance of the WS sample itself. Likewise, matching criteria between groups were heterogeneous; chronological age and mental age predominated, although some studies used additional variables such as sex, IQ, or verbal and non-verbal abilities. This lack of sample homogeneity hinders direct comparisons between studies and likely constitutes one of the main explanatory factors for the discrepancies observed in the results.

Regarding the research objectives, most studies focus on the evaluation of inhibitory functioning, with intervention studies being scarce. Likewise, cross-sectional designs predominated, which limits our understanding of the evolution of inhibitory processes throughout development. Added to this is the great diversity of instruments used to assess inhibition. Although performance tasks predominated, questionnaires and a wide range of experimental paradigms were also used, which evaluate different inhibitory components and involve additional demands from other executive processes.

When examining the results according to the different inhibitory types (Aydmune & Introzzi, 2018; Borella et al., 2013; Traverso et al., 2018), it is observed that WS does not present a general and uniform deficit. Regarding cognitive inhibition, approximately half of the studies identified difficulties in inhibiting automatic responses, both through comparisons with TD and clinical groups and based on normative data.

Regarding motor inhibition, almost half of the studies reported difficulties, frequently observing a higher number of commission errors, slower reaction times, or greater inhibitory costs than in comparison groups on inhibitory tasks of this type. Studies incorporating neurophysiological and neuroimaging measures support these findings, describing alterations in frontostriatal circuits and neural mechanisms involved in motor inhibitory control. However, studies with mixed results or no group differences in both types of inhibition were also identified, which could be explained by the notable heterogeneity in the methodologies employed.

On the other hand, perceptual inhibition was the least studied component, as only two investigations evaluated it specifically. Despite the small number of works, both coincided in pointing out an inferior performance of individuals with WS, suggesting that the ability to filter out irrelevant information could constitute another area of vulnerability. Nevertheless, further studies are necessary to confirm this pattern.

Finally, so-called ecological inhibition showed the most consistent pattern of results. Most studies using the BRIEF as a measure of inhibition described clinically relevant difficulties in daily life. This finding is particularly relevant because it complements information obtained through laboratory tasks and suggests that inhibitory difficulties have a functional impact on the daily behavior of individuals with WS.

Another notable aspect is the relationship observed between inhibitory functioning and other cognitive, behavioral, and emotional variables. The different components of inhibition showed associations with IQ, working memory, age, language, emotional regulation, anxiety, behavioral problems, social functioning, and cognitive development. These findings reinforce the idea that inhibitory processes play a central role in the daily adaptation of individuals with WS and that their impairment may explain the frequent everyday problems in this population (Greer et al., 2017; Morris, 1999 Morris, /2023)).

Finally, the review highlights the scarcity of studies aimed at intervening in inhibitory functioning. Only two specific investigations were identified, neither of which found significant improvements following the intervention. Consequently, it is necessary to develop more intervention studies to determine whether inhibitory control is a cognitive process susceptible to improvement.

4.1. Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be noted when interpreting the results. First, as this is a scoping review, a formal evaluation of the methodological quality or risk of bias of the included studies was not performed. Although this procedure is not a requirement in this type of review (Tricco et al., 2018), its inclusion in future systematic reviews would allow for an assessment of the strength of the available evidence.

Second, the review focused on a narrative synthesis of the available evidence without conducting a quantitative meta-analysis. As a future direction, it would be valuable to perform a quantitative review to estimate the effect size of inhibitory difficulties across its different components, which would contribute to obtaining a more precise estimate of the magnitude of the impairments in this population.

Finally, the classification of tasks according to the different types of inhibition entailed a certain degree of interpretation by the authors. Many of the tasks used simultaneously assess multiple executive processes; thus, assigning them to a single category may simplify the complexity of the underlying processes. Future studies could account for this complexity when selecting and classifying inhibition measures by using tasks with higher specificity, in order to improve comparability across studies.

4.2. Conclusions

The results indicate that inhibitory functioning constitutes an area of vulnerability in individuals with WS, with difficulties identified across all inhibitory subtypes (cognitive, motor, perceptual, and ecological). Nevertheless, the most consistent evidence is observed in ecological inhibition, assessed via questionnaires, which suggests that inhibitory difficulties have a significant impact on daily functioning. Likewise, the associations between inhibition and cognitive, behavioral, and socio-emotional variables reinforce the relevance of inhibitory difficulties to daily life problems in this population. However, the considerable sample and methodological heterogeneity across studies hinders the direct comparison of results.

The review has also identified key gaps in the literature. First, the absence of longitudinal studies prevents the characterization of developmental trajectories of inhibitory functioning in this population. Second, no research was found focusing on adolescents or older adults. This latter aspect is particularly relevant, as the lack of studies prevents the examination of premature cognitive aging trajectories typical of individuals with ID, and specifically in older adults with WS (Devenny et al., 2004). Third, articles evaluating perceptual inhibition are scarce; thus, the available evidence is insufficient to draw firm conclusions. Finally, research on intervention is very limited, making it impossible to determine whether inhibitory difficulties can be improved through specific programs.

Overall, this review provides an integrative view of the available knowledge on inhibitory functioning in individuals with WS, identifying both the main findings across different components of inhibition and the limitations of current literature.
