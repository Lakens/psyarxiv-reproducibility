Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Conventional mental health treatments may not translate to carceral settings. A clearer understanding of the experiences and preferences of incarcerated individuals is needed.

Based on literature and expertise from community partners, this project surveyed individuals at a women's prison about their treatment preferences.

Results showed high favorability for mental health treatment across the board, with the highest ratings for self-administered options. Participants also preferred Department of Corrections providers over peers and individual support over group.

Clinical considerations for treatment for incarcerated women are discussed including prioritizing confidentiality, investing in self-administered options, incorporating individualized elements into group programming, and using preference surveys to inform resource allocation.

Mental Health Treatment Preferences of Individuals Incarcerated at a Women's Prison

Rates of most mental illnesses are higher in the incarcerated population than in the general population (Prins, 2014). Rates of mental illness are particularly high in incarcerated women, with three out of four struggling with mental health (James and Glaze, 2006), half meeting criteria for PTSD during their lifetime (Baranyi et al., 2018), and the majority with a history of a substance use disorder (National Institute on Drug Abuse, 2020). Women are the fastest growing incarcerated demographic in the United States (Monazzam and Budd, 2023). Specific care and consideration towards providing feasible, acceptable, and effective mental health interventions for incarcerated women is essential. However, research on mental healthcare for incarcerated women shows small effects for reductions in depressive, anxiety, and PTSD symptomatology, inconclusive long-term impacts, and a lack of high-quality controlled intervention studies (Bartlett et al., 2015; Malik et al., 2021; Yoon et al., 2017).

Importance of Treatment Preferences

The American Psychological Association defines Evidence-Based Practice (EBP) in psychology as "the integration of the best available research with clinical expertise in the context of patient characteristics, culture, and preferences" (APA Presidental Task Force on Evidence-Based Practice, 2006, p. 278). Moreover, patient preference has been known to be a key factor in therapeutic care for almost two decades. Meta-analyses show that clients matched to their treatment preference show larger reductions in symptomatology (Delevry and Le, 2019; Lindhiem et al., 2014; Swift and Callahan, 2009). Patients who have treatment matched to preference and/or are involved in the treatment decision process also endorse a stronger therapeutic alliance, report greater treatment satisfaction, and have lower attrition rates (Lindhiem et al., 2014; Swift and Callahan, 2009; Windle et al., 2020). Hence, the implementation of treatment approaches that are consistent with preferences of incarcerated women could bolster effectiveness.

Treatment Options During Incarceration

Upon review of the literature of types of treatments, treatment providers, and treatment modalities offered in carceral settings, we discussed treatment options with our DOC partners, and the options discussed below were deemed feasible. Two common providers of mental health services during incarceration are clinicians and peers. An incarcerated person may prefer a clinician if they feel safer with someone who has extensive training. However, prison clinicians are often focused on crisis management and may have limited time for building therapeutic relationships (Fazel et al., 2017; Hawton et al., 2014). Another option is peer support. A metaanalysis on the effects of peer support for mental illness in the community found no support for a reduction in psychiatric symptoms, but did find evidence of increased self-reported views of recovery and empowerment (White et al., 2020). Peer support in the prison setting can reduce conduct reports during incarceration and recidivism after release, as well as build self-awareness and purpose in peer providers (Bagnall et al., 2015; Kottke-Weaver, 2023). No research with incarcerated women has investigated preference between facility staff and peer specialists for mental health treatment.

Both individual and group therapy can be beneficial for mental health and there are generally no clinically meaningful differences in outcomes between the two treatment approaches within community (Lockwood et al., 2004; Ryan et al., 2005) or incarcerated samples (Yoon et al., 2017). Individual therapy provides an opportunity to target specific experiences, emotions, and core beliefs. Individual therapy might be preferred by someone who struggles with vulnerability or asserting themselves in group settings. Group therapy can provide community and an opportunity to connect. Group therapy might be preferred by someone who wants to hear about others' experiences and appreciates space to listen.

Psychotropic medications are widely used in the correctional environment, particularly for women (Bentley and Casey, 2017; Hassan et al., 2014). Qualitative work with incarcerated women demonstrates complex views around psychotropic medication; many women endorse its benefits to maintain emotional stability, while others cite limited appointments with providers as a major barrier to managing side effects or lack of efficacy (Casey and Bentley, 2017; Hassan et al., 2013). Given the ubiquity of medications and the mixed findings from qualitative studies, it is important to gain further clarity on preferences for this treatment option.

Self-administered support is one way to expand mental healthcare in prison at low cost.

Two feasible options are self-help workbooks and audio-based interventions. Two studies that utilized self-help workbooks with incarcerated populations found decreases in anxiety (Maunder et al., 2009) and depression (Pardini et al., 2014). While there is no evidence yet on the effectiveness of an audio-based intervention in prison, mindfulness interventions have shown positive impacts for psychological wellbeing and behavioral functioning in this population and translate well to audio (Auty et al., 2017; Per et al., 2020). Self-administered options have multiple benefits for individuals receiving mental healthcare in prison. Stigma surrounding mental illness is high in prison populations (Skogstad et al., 2005). A negative perception of those who experience mental health issues reduces the likelihood of treatment-seeking and treatment adherence (Corrigan, 2000; Farabee, Hall, Zaheer, and Joshi, 2019). The privacy of self-administered interventions could thus decrease hesitation to seek mental healthcare and increase retention. In addition, research has shown that many incarcerated individuals are reluctant to receive mental healthcare from prison staff due to concerns about confidentiality and fear of consequences such as solitary confinement (Skogstad et al., 2005; Way et al., 2013). A self-administered option could provide access to mental health treatment for someone who would be unlikely to engage in treatment otherwise due to concerns around stigma and confidentiality.

Lastly, some individuals may not be ready for continuous treatment but are interested to learn more about mental health. A one-day psychoeducation workshop to better understand the presentation of disorders such as anxiety, depression, and PTSD could begin the process of selfawareness for these individuals and may lead to future engagement with treatment.

Psychoeducation alone without treatment reduces depression and psychological distress (Donker et al., 2009) and increases mental health knowledge (Lincoln et al., 2007). This option could allow for a large group of individuals to receive resources at one time.

Study Questions and Hypotheses

While the need for more effective and acceptable mental healthcare within prison is clear, identifying the treatment preferences of individuals incarcerated at a women's correctional facility remains a missing piece to more impactful and widespread mental healthcare utilization.

There are four main study questions in service of this goal: 1) What are the mental health treatment preferences of incarcerated women? 2) How desired are self-administered treatment options? 3) Do treatment preferences vary by the provider of the service (prison therapist or peer) and the format of the support (individual or group)? Does preference for individual or group options vary by provider? 4) How favorably do individuals rate treatment options overall? Due to the relatively small literature on preferences of mental health treatment during incarceration, there are two hypotheses. First, for question two, we hypothesize that selfadministered treatment options will be perceived favorably by incarcerated women considering potential distrust for prison staff, stigma around mental illness in incarcerated populations, and concerns around confidentiality and vulnerability in the prison setting. Second, for question three, we hypothesize that incarcerated women will rate treatment from a prison therapist less favorably than treatment from a peer given prior evidence of distrust for prison staff.

Methods

Participants

Participants are 156 people surveyed from one mixed-security women's correctional facility in the Midwest. Participants were recruited through a larger parent research project related to mental health screening or through a trauma treatment research study.

Survey Development

After considering past literature, we solicited input on the treatment options in our survey from our research partners at the state Department of Corrections (DOC). Due to feedback from the DOC about the feasibility of virtual individual or group therapy, these two items were removed. Community partners with lived experience of incarceration also reviewed the survey to provide feedback and were consulted to assist with interpreting the results of the study. Above all, the survey was built to include choices that could be realistically implemented.

Final Survey

The final eight-question survey included the following options: 1) individual therapy with a DOC therapist, 2) group therapy with a DOC therapist, 3) individual support from a peer, 4) group support from a peer, 5) medication, 6) self-help workbook, 7) self-paced audio intervention, and 8) one-day psychoeducation workshop. Participants rated how much they would like each treatment option on a Likert scale from "-2" (strongly dislike) to "2" (strongly like). A score of "0" indicated neither like nor dislike. All treatment options were elaborated on with a one sentence description, as well as a visual depiction.

Analysis Strategy

All major research questions, hypotheses, and analytic decisions were preregistered at https://osf.io/dhuxt/. We calculated means and standard deviations for each treatment option, as well as percentages of participants who "like" or "strongly like" each option as a metric of overall desire for a treatment option in a sample. We ran one sample t-tests for each treatment option to calculate if the mean scores were significantly different from zero (neither like nor dislike). We used a Holm-Bonferroni correction for the eight tests (Holm, 1979).

We ran a linear mixed-effects model using the lme4 package in R that accounted for the by-subject random effects to assess the impact of treatment format (individual or group) and provider (DOC or peer) on preference for the four relevant options. To account for the nonindependence in the data we included a by-subject random intercept in the model, so parameter estimates are unbiased leading to a reduction in type I and type II error rates (Brauer and Curtin, 2018). In the preregistration, we indicated that age, education level, and race would be included as covariates in the model. However, when the model did not converge, it became clear that with a fully within-subjects design, between-subjects covariates are unnecessary. The final model was: lmer(preference_score ~ provider*format (1 + provider*format|subID).

Discussion

Three out of four incarcerated women experience mental health difficulties (James and Glaze, 2006), but research has rarely solicited feedback on their desired treatment options. We surveyed incarcerated women about their mental health treatment preferences, presenting a variety of options that might appeal to different individuals and that could be feasibly implemented in prison. A discussion of key findings and takeaways is presented below.

The desire for mental health treatment across the board is high. At least 40% of individuals selected "like" or "strongly like" for every treatment option with 79% liking the most preferred option (self-help workbook). Even the least preferred option (peer group support) was rated favorably by almost half of individuals. The mean number of options rated favorably by each individual was 4.81, demonstrating discerning but broad interest in mental health treatments. 97% of participants liked or strongly liked at least one treatment option. In a resource poor setting like a carceral facility, a "menu" of options could broaden access to vital mental healthcare.

In line with our hypothesis, self-administered treatment options were rated favorably by participants. The self-help workbook (79%) and MP3 audio program (76%) were the two most preferred options. Of those individuals who rated only one or two treatments favorably, the self-help workbook was the most desired option. Self-administered mental health treatments have been shown to be effective (Cavanagh et al., 2014; K. J. Martin et al., 2018), and can provide a meaningful way to pass time during incarceration (Pederson et al., 2024). Utilizing a self-administered intervention may be attractive for both incarcerated individuals because it affords privacy, continuity, and independence in the treatment process, and for correctional facilities by providing access to care for more individuals at a lower cost to staff time.

Contrary to our hypothesis, participants preferred treatment with a DOC provider to support from a peer provider. Given prior research indicating mistrust for DOC psychiatric staff (Bright et al., 2022; Way et al., 2013), this result is surprising. Without qualitative data to understand the lower preference for peer support, we can use the literature on challenges related to peer support to provide context. Research shows that peer support for mental health can create issues around boundaries, role conflict, and power (Miyamoto and Sono, 2012; Repper and Carter, 2011; Rodrik, 2024). The role as a peer provider allows disclosure of personal experiences to build rapport and trust with a patient. However, this is atypical for a patientprovider relationship and the line between provider and friendship can lead to confusing relationship boundaries. Certain obligations (such as reporting suicidal intent to the DOC) might become difficult to navigate for the peer provider and lead to a perceived breach of trust for the patient (Miyamoto and Sono, 2012). Issues with power are also magnified in the prison environment (Repper and Carter, 2011). Peer providers are paid for their services by the DOC.

The payment, extensive training provided by the DOC, and the position title lead to power differentials. It is possible that individuals who did not rate peer support favorably see the power differential with a peer in the prison environment as too threatening to both their physical and emotional safety. In addition, there may be a need to interact with peers in settings outside of the support role (e.g., in dorm housing) which can cause distress around boundaries and confidentiality (DeHart and Mason, 2022). Ultimately, while peer support is a viable option for those who are not sensitive to issues of boundaries, role conflict, and power differentials, it is not a universally preferred option. That being said, peer support was rated favorably by a substantial portion of our sample and remains an important pathway to mental health support in the prison setting.

Our results also indicate that despite evidence for mistrust of DOC psychiatric staff, the desire for individual treatment from DOC remains high (72%). In one mixed-methods study assessing mental health treatment needs of incarcerated women with history of intimate partner violence, participants expressed a demand and desire for individual therapy with trained clinicians (Lynch, Fritch, et al., 2012). Another study with incarcerated men reported that individuals perceived prison psychologists as able to effectively assist with a range of emotional and mental health needs even with concerns about confidentiality and working within the "the system" (Skogstad et al., 2005). Individuals with previous contact with facility mental health staff had more positive attitudes about seeking help for mental health during incarceration (Skogstad et al., 2005). Our results, along with prior empirical evidence presented here, suggest incarcerated individuals value educational credentials and training in their mental health providers and the exigency for treatment may override fears around confidentiality.

There was a strong preference for individual treatment and support options compared to group options, and this difference was larger for treatment with a DOC provider than with a peer. This finding is importantly contextualized within the prison setting where group treatment and programming are more widely available. While research shows that both group and individual interventions can be equally effective (Yoon et al., 2017), and that group therapy is seen favorably by incarcerated individuals (e.g., Zielenski et al., 2021; Doyle et al., 2022), patients may prioritize confidentiality, privacy, and individual processing over interpersonal connection in mental health treatment. In addition, the one-day workshop (67%) was preferred to multi-session group options (DOC = 51%, peer = 42%). It is possible that individuals are not ready to commit to treatment, but are still interested in learning more about mental health. Given the greater availability of group options in this setting, it is also likely that previous experiences with groups in prison shaped participant responses. Prior work with incarcerated women identified that incarcerated individuals view group treatments within prison as a "lecture" and desire more active discussion and engagement from DOC providers (Rodrik, 2024). Therefore, lower group ratings could reflect either priorities for treatment, reflection on prior experiences, or both.

Lastly, our results mirror previous research that indicates mixed opinions about psychotropic medication use within incarcerated populations with half of the sample rating medication favorably (Casey and Bentley, 2017; Hassan et al., 2013). Medication is viewed by both prison staff and incarcerated individuals as a viable option to reduce symptomatology and avoid mental health crises (Hassan et al., 2013). Medication, when taken voluntarily, is an additional way incarcerated individuals can practice personal agency over their mental healthcare in an environment with strict limits on personal choice (Bentley and Casey, 2017). While medication is a desired approach by half of individuals, there is a sizable percentage who did not rate this option favorably.

Clinical Considerations for Correctional Facilities

Our findings provide guidance for mental health care best practices for individuals in prison settings based on feedback from the users of the services themselves. Facilities can consider the following: 1) Confidentiality is key.

Multiple findings from this study (i.e., the dominance of selfadministered options and the preference for individual options over group options) point to the importance of confidentiality in mental health treatment for this population. Therefore, clarity around the limits to confidentiality (e.g., threat to oneself or others) should be prioritized in all treatment formats and with both types of providers.

2) Invest in self-administered options. While staff prioritize their efforts on crisis care, other lower-risk individuals who are still struggling with mental health can benefit from selfadministered options. Self-administered treatments provide portability and continuity. An individual may not be able to maintain regular appointments with the same group or therapist due to environmental factors outside the individual's control, such as release, transfer, lockdowns, court, restricted housing placement, changes in work schedule, staff shortages or turnover, etc. Self-administered options can be continued despite these barriers. In addition, selfhelp options can be utilized while an individual is on a waitlist for therapy. With high mental health staff turnover and long waitlists to access prison-based programming (Pederson et al., 2024), self-administered options are a worthy investment.

3) Incorporate individualized elements into group programming. Seventy-two percent of individuals rated individual therapy with a DOC provider favorably. While it may be infeasible to increase access to individual therapy with a DOC staff to this extent, correctional facilities can incorporate more individualized elements into group programming. For example, if homework is assigned, allocate time to look over individuals' worksheets and answer clarifying questions.

Together with quick individual check-ins or expressions of care before and after groups, the attractiveness of group options may increase.

4) Incarcerated individuals want to work on self-improvement in prison.

The high treatment ratings across the board as well as the favorability for both self-administered and the one-day workshop point to a desire for self-learning and self-work. This mirrors previous research on correctional programming which indicates that individuals want to take advantage of offerings but limited availability, strict eligibility criteria, and long wait times cause frustration and a sense of alienation (Pederson et al., 2024). An opportunity such as a one-day workshop that appeals to many and can provide psychoeducational resources to a large group (ideally individuals keep resources after the workshop) can fill idle time and instigate the self-reflection process for those who have not yet engaged in mental health treatment.

5) Preference surveys can be used for resource allocation.

At intake, mental health is typically assessed briefly to identify current high severity issues such as active psychosis, self-harm, suicidality, and substance withdrawal (M. S. Martin et al., 2018). These screening tools are short and differentially sensitive at identifying mental health needs (Dietzel et al., 2017).

Best practices include a second mental health screening stage after intake in order to triage those who require more detailed mental health assessment and intervention (Simpson and Forrester, 2018). At this second mental health screening, a preference survey could assist with resource allocation. Our survey was low burden to fill out at only eight questions and surveys can be customized to reflect the offerings of a particular facility. DOC providers are more likely to choose a treatment option that the incarcerated individual will respond to clinically and reduce the risk of misallocating resources with information from a preference survey. For instance, an individual who rates group therapy as "neutral" but self-help workbook as "strongly like" may take up a valuable group therapy spot from someone else who is more likely to benefit given their openness to the group. If used during intake or screening, the purpose of the survey must be clearly articulated and ethically considered. A preference survey should not be adopted to provide only one type of care. For example, if an individual is interested in both medication and therapy or peer support, medication should not be the only strategy implemented. Mental healthcare often requires a multi-pronged approach of both medication and psychotherapy.

Limitations and Future Directions

While the current research is novel in that it is the first quantitative study to assess mental health treatment preferences in an incarcerated population, it is limited in a few notable ways that hinder generalizability and warrant further research. First, we only assessed treatment preferences at a women's facility. Incarcerated men and women present with different mental health profiles and pathways to their disorders. Future research should also assess the specific preferences of incarcerated men.

Second, due to the recruitment procedures for the current research, our sample is not representative of the average population at a women's correctional facility. Fifty-eight percent of the sample was screened for participation in a trauma treatment study before completing the survey, with at least 36% of the sample meeting criteria for PTSD (56 out of 90 recruited for the PTSD study; not assessed for the remaining 66 participants). While the pathway to recruitment for the study was not random, the rates of PTSD are in line with the typical prevalence of PTSD for incarcerated women (Baranyi et al., 2018). The exclusion of individuals with psychosis due to concerns around capacity to consent may have had a larger impact. Up to 15% of individuals incarcerated at state prisons in the US experience psychotic symptoms (James and Glaze, 2006) and this population in particular has high mental health treatment needs. In addition, our sample underrepresented Black individuals and individuals with less than a high school education. These individuals are historically excluded from mental health research (Spates, 2012; Steele et al., 2007) and their perspectives are needed to paint a fuller picture of treatment preferences for this population and to best inform clinical practice.

Finally, the absence of both qualitative data to supplement survey choices, as well as data on participants' prior experiences with mental health treatment while incarcerated, limited our ability to make conclusions about the "whys" behind individuals' ratings. Future research should collect data on prior experience with each treatment option to parse apart true like or dislike for treatments from responses biased by the novelty of an option or prior experience.

Conclusion

In this study, we surveyed 156 individuals at a women's correctional facility about their mental health treatment preferences. The results demonstrate a strong overall desire for a variety of mental health services, with a particular preference for self-administered options. These data provide useful guidance on preferred mental healthcare options for incarcerated individuals.
