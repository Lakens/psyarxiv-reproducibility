What we found

We found 12 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Food insecurity is increasingly recognised as an important social determinant of eating disorder (ED) pathology. Although research in this area has grown substantially, evidence remains fragmented across ED pathology, diagnoses, age groups, and clinical and non-clinical populations. This review aims to update and synthesise the literature on the relationship between food insecurity and eating disorder pathology.

Relevant studies were identified by searching PubMed/Medline, Web of Science, PsycINFO, Scopus, and the grey literature up to September 2026. Random-effects meta-analyses and narrative synthesis were carried out. The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool. Public and patient involvement and engagement informed the process throughout. The protocol for this review was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420261291381).

We included 140 articles in the review (n=628,090 participants) and, of these, 72 in the meta-analysis. Food insecurity was associated with greater overall eating pathology, with pooled eORs ranging from 1.75 (1.18-2.58) to 2.36 (0.15-0.31) across the three effect-size measures. Positive associations were also observed for binge eating, dietary restriction, purging, vomiting, and laxative use, although effect sizes varied across outcomes and analyses. Findings were mixed for excessive exercise and negative body image, with some studies reporting significant associations with food insecurity and others finding no evidence of an association. Overall, the included studies were of moderate to high methodological quality.

To date, the existing literature supports the role of food insecurity in the aetiology of eating disorder pathology and highlights the need for future theoretical frameworks and prevention interventions.

1. Introduction

Food insecurity is defined as limited or uncertain access to sufficient, safe, and nutritionally adequate food (USDA Economic Research Service, 2022a). It is a major global public health concern, affecting approximately 2.3 billion people worldwide in 2024 (28% of the global population) (UNICEF, 2025) and is associated with adverse physical and mental health outcomes, including depression, anxiety, stress, and suicidal ideation (see Moosavian et al., 2025, for a review). More recently, food insecurity has been identified as a potential risk factor for eating disorder (ED) pathology (Hazzard et al., 2020), which encompasses disturbances in eating behaviours and weight-and shape-related cognitions, including dietary restraint, eating, shape and weight concerns, binge eating, avoidant or restrictive eating, and compensatory behaviours (American Psychiatric Association, 2022; Fairburn & Beglin, 2008). An earlier narrative review found cross-sectional associations between food insecurity and a range of ED pathology, including binge eating, compensatory behaviours, and diagnoses of binge-eating disorder and bulimia nervosa (Hazzard et al., 2020). Given the subsequent growth of this literature, an updated synthesis is needed to clarify the breadth of associations between food insecurity and different forms of ED pathology.

Several mechanisms may explain the association between food insecurity and ED pathology. Firstly, food insecurity may disrupt regular eating patterns through a "feast-famine" cycle, whereby periods of restricted intake during times of scarcity are followed by increased consumption when food becomes available (Dinour et al., 2007; Hamrick & Andrews, 2016; Hazzard et al., 2023). Repeated cycles of deprivation and renewed food access may increase vulnerability to loss-of-control and binge eating (Polivy, 1996; Zunker et al., 2011). Indeed, meta-analytic evidence shows that food-insecure adults have 66% higher odds of binge eating and 2.7-fold higher odds of binge-eating disorder than food-secure adults (Abene et al., 2024). The "famine" component of food insecurity may likewise also be relevant to restrictive eating pathology. Although restriction remains comparatively understudied, emerging evidence suggests that limiting intake during food insecurity may serve both resource-conservation and weight-control functions, with these motivations often cooccurring (Karvay et al., 2025). However, the extent to which food insecurity is associated with restrictive ED pathology has yet to be systematically quantified.

Another potential mechanism linking food insecurity to ED pathology may operate through body image. Food insecurity may increase reliance on inexpensive, energy-dense foods and limit access to structured physical activity, potentially contributing to higher body weight and widening the discrepancy between an individual's perceived body and culturally valued appearance ideals (Domingo et al., 2021; Lamont & Speed, 2025). Higher body weight may increase exposure to weight stigma, which, when internalised, can contribute to body dissatisfaction and greater engagement in unhealthy weight-control behaviours such as purging, laxative use and excessive exercise (Harriger & Thompson, 2012; Rossi et al., 2023; Levinson et al., 2024). Indeed, research suggests that adults with severe food insecurity were consistently more likely than those with marginal food security to report compensatory behaviours, including vomiting, laxative/diuretic use, fasting, and excessive exercise (Becker et al., 2017). Unhealthy weight control behaviours also remain comparatively understudied in the food insecurity literature, with many studies combining behaviours (e.g. purging, laxative use, excessive exercise) limiting behaviour-specific estimates. Recently, some research has even suggested that food insecurity may also contribute to avoidant or restrictive eating in young people (Berge et al., 2020). When food budgets are limited, the financial cost of purchasing foods that children may reject can encourage reliance on familiar, reliably accepted foods and reduce opportunities to introduce greater dietary variety (Harris et al., 2019). However, this pathway remains largely theoretical and requires further empirical investigation.

Given the growing evidence and strong theoretical links, it is important to quantify associations between food insecurity and the full spectrum of ED pathology. Since Hazzard et al.'s (2020) review, the evidence base has expanded substantially, partly reflecting greater recognition that EDs affect people across socioeconomic, gender, and racial and ethnic groups, challenging the historical stereotype that they primarily affect "skinny, White, affluent girls" (Halbeisen et al., 2022; Huryk et al., 2021; Sonneville & Lipson, 2018). Pressures on food access have also intensified globally due to rising living costs, food-price volatility, armed conflict, climate-related disruption, and the lasting effects of the COVID-19 pandemic (Laborde et al., 2021; Saccone & Vallino, 2025). Accordingly, the present systematic review and meta-analysis aim to (a) provide an updated synthesis of associations between food insecurity and ED pathology across symptom domains, diagnoses, and diverse populations, and (b) quantify the strength and consistency of these associations through meta-analysis.

2. Method

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines (Page et al., 2021). The completed PRISMA checklist is provided in Table S1. The protocol for this review was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420261291381).

2.1 Search strategy

A systematic and comprehensive search was conducted across nine electronic databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, PsycINFO, Scopus, Web of Science, ProQuest, ET, medRxiv, and PsyArXiv. The final search was conducted in September 2026. No restrictions were applied by publication date or language during the search.

The final search combined food insecurity terms with ED pathology terms including ED diagnoses, binge eating, restrictive eating, dieting, fasting, purging, compensatory behaviours, ARFID, PICA, excessive exercise, body dissatisfaction, and body image concerns. The full reported search strategies for each database is located in Table S2.

2.2 Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies included participants of all age groups from either clinical or general population samples. Studies were required to include measures of both food insecurity and eating pathology (e.g., body image concerns or disordered eating). Quantitative studies examining the association between FI and ED outcomes were included, including crosssectional and longitudinal observational studies, as well as randomized and non-randomized studies as well as randomised and non-randomised intervention studies that reported associations between the two constructs. For intervention studies, associations based on baseline measures (pre-intervention) were extracted where available.

Studies were excluded if they involved non-human subjects, did not assess both food insecurity and ED pathology, or used qualitative designs. Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses were also excluded.

2.3 Study selection

Articles identified through the literature search were uploaded to Rayyan (Ouzzani et al., 2016), where duplicates were identified using the automated duplicate-detection function and manually checked where necessary.

Following deduplication, two reviewers (AMN and GP) independently screened titles and abstracts against the review's eligibility criteria. Both reviewers initially screened 10% of records independently to assess inter-rater agreement. Following two rounds of calibration, Cohen's kappa exceeded 0.80, after which the remaining records were screened independently by both reviewers.

Full-text screening was conducted by four reviewers (FT, GP, AN, and CW) working in pairs, with each paper assessed independently by two reviewers. Each pair initially screened 10% of reports to establish inter-rater agreement (Cohen's κ > 0.80), before independently screening the remaining reports. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus among the reviewers.

2.4 Data extraction and coding

A data extraction form was developed and piloted on 10% of included studies. Two reviewer pairs (GP and FT; AMN and CW) independently extracted data, achieving over 90% agreement during piloting. The remaining studies were extracted independently, with discrepancies resolved through discussion.

The following information was extracted from each included study: author, year of publication, sample size, country, mean age and standard deviation, socioeconomic status, race and ethnicity, sex assigned at birth and gender (n, %), body mass index (mean, SD), sample characteristics (e.g., community or clinical sample), study design, measure of food insecurity, measure of ED pathology, effect size type, overall findings, and covariates included in adjusted analyses.

Where information was unclear or not reported in the published article, study authors were contacted for additional data/clarification.

2.5 Quality appraisal

Methodological quality was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT; 2018), comprising two screening questions and five design-specific criteria across different study designs. Although the review's eligibility criteria permitted a range of methodologies, only quantitative findings contributed to the final synthesis and meta-analysis. Studies were therefore appraised using the quantitative descriptive criteria for non-comparative studies (MMAT; Hong et al., 2018). The criteria assess methodological rigour, including the appropriateness of the sampling strategy, representativeness of the sample, quality of outcome measurement, risk of non-response bias, and appropriateness of the statistical analyses. Where an included report used a mixed-methods design, only its quantitative component was appraised because only quantitative findings were extracted and synthesized.

The same procedure for assessing inter-rater agreement was used for quality assessment. Two reviewer pairs (GP and FT; AMN and CW) independently assessed the included studies (after 90% agreement was reached), with discrepancies resolved through discussion. In accordance with MMAT guidance (Hong et al., 2019), ratings for each individual criterion were reported rather than calculating a summed score.

2.6 Patient and Public Involvement Statement

A lived-experience advisory group comprising two individuals with experience of both food insecurity and EDs and one individual with experience of food insecurity and caring for a child with an ED, contributed to the development of the review protocol. Through group discussions, contributors affirmed the relevance of the research questions and emphasised the importance of examining food insecurity across a broad spectrum of ED pathology. They encouraged the inclusion of less frequently studied ED presentations, particularly ARFID, which informed the review's broad scope and search strategy. Contributors also influenced the eligibility criteria, highlighting the relevance of food insecurity and ED pathology during childhood and adolescence, informing the decision to include populations of all ages. Contributors further highlighted how food insecurity and ED pathology can coexist with other physical and mental health conditions, informing the decision not to exclude studies involving individuals with comorbidities. Contributors also co interpreted the findings and offered possible explanations for the mixed results concerning excessive exercise, body image, and ARFID-related pathology, informing their interpretation in the discussion.

2.7 Data synthesis and analyses

2.7.1. Effect size coding

As studies reported associations between food insecurity and eating pathology using different effect-size metrics (e.g., odds ratios (ORs), prevalence ratio (PR); raw frequency of food insecurity and ED outcome, and correlation coefficients), we conducted separate metaanalysis for each effect size metrics to pool to effect sizes. To facilitate comparison across the different types of pooled effect estimates reported across meta-analyses, we followed the approach recommended by Fusar-Poli and Radua (2018) and converted all pooled effect estimates into equivalent odds ratios (eORs), using the effectsize package in R (Ben-Shachar et al., 2020). PRs could not be converted because outcome prevalence in the food-secure group was unavailable. Both original pooled estimates and corresponding eORs, where calculable, are reported.

Effect sizes were extracted for all relevant outcomes and grouped into domains (e.g., body dissatisfaction, shape concerns, and muscularity concerns were grouped under body image). To ensure effect-size independence (Cheung, 2019), each meta-analysis included only one effect size per unique sample. Where multiple eligible outcomes were reported, the outcome most frequently assessed across studies was selected.

2.7.2. Data analysis

All analyses were conducted in R (version 3.6.0) using the metafor package. Due to the included studies having a variety of measurements, ages, and sample characteristics, a random effects model was selected. Heterogeneity across studies was assessed with the I² statistic and Cochran's Q test (Higgins et al., 2003), with I² values around 25%, 50%, and above 75% taken to indicate low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively (Higgins et al., 2003). Potential small-study effects were assessed using funnel plots alongside Egger's regression test (Egger et al., 1997), run when at least 10 studies were available, as tests for funnel plot asymmetry have low power with fewer studies (Sterne et al., 2011). Leave-oneout sensitivity analyses assessed the robustness of pooled estimates by sequentially excluding individual studies and recalculating the pooled effects (Aung et al., 2026).

Four longitudinal studies had data available for the meta-analysis. Given this small number, estimates from both cross-sectional and longitudinal studies were pooled, and the influence of longitudinal estimates on the results was assessed using the aforementioned sensitivity analyses. Because the number of included studies was small; moderator analyses were limited to three variables: (1) age (2) gender (quantified as % of women in each sample) (3) publication year. Moderator analyses were carried out on variables which were reported in a minimum of six studies (Fu et al, 2011).

2.7.3. Narrative synthesis

When effect estimates were not reported in a form suitable for meta-analysis, findings were synthesised narratively using the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) approach, with results grouped according to the outcome variable (Campbell et al., 2020). This included studies that reported only the direction or significance of an association without sufficient data to calculate an effect size, and studies whose estimates could not be meaningfully pooled due to differences in how food insecurity or eating pathology was measured or categorised. Studies reporting adjusted effect estimates were also synthesised narratively when differences in the covariates included in their models made the estimates unsuitable for pooling.

3.4.5. Food insecurity and restriction

Food insecurity was associated with restriction across all four effect-size metrics. In the meta-analysis of unadjusted ORs (k=9; Fig. S10), the pooled OR was 3.04 (95% CI 2.14-4.33, p < 0.0001). Between-study heterogeneity was substantial.

The association was smaller but remained significant in the meta-analysis of adjusted ORs from the primary studies (k=4; Fig. S11; aOR = 1.65, 95% CI 1.38-1.97, p < 0.0001), equivalent to about 65% higher adjusted odds. Heterogeneity was low to moderate, and the result was stable in leave-one-out analyses (aOR range 1.54-1.76, all p ≤ 0.0005).

Three studies reported PRs, and food insecurity was associated with a 40% higher prevalence of restriction (PR = 1.40, 95% CI 1.27-1.54, p < 0.0001; Fig. S12). There was no observed heterogeneity, although the confidence interval around I² was wide given only three studies. The result was stable in leave-one-out analyses (PR range 1.39-1.43, all p < 0.0001).

Finally, the meta-analysis of correlation coefficients (k=10; Fig. S13) showed a small positive correlation between food insecurity and restriction (r = 0.19, 95% CI 0.09-0.29, p = 0.0002). Heterogeneity was high. There was no evidence of small-study effects (t(8) = 0.09, p = 0.93).

Where studies combined self-induced vomiting and laxative use, or reported purging as a composite outcome, these outcomes were classified within the purging category. In the meta-analysis of adjusted ORs from the primary studies (k=3; Fig. S14), food insecurity was associated with higher adjusted odds of purging (aOR = 1.75, 95% CI 1.34-2.30, p < 0.0001). Heterogeneity was low. The association remained significant in leave-one-out analyses (aOR range 1.61-2.25, all p ≤ 0.008).

In the meta-analysis of PRs (k=3; Fig. S15), food insecurity was associated with a higher prevalence of purging (PR = 4.25, 95% CI 1.34-13.46, p = 0.01). Heterogeneity was very high. The pooled estimate was sensitive to the omission of individual studies. Excluding Klimek 2026 gave a non-significant result with a very wide interval (PR = 4.44, 95% CI 0.62-31.53, p = 0.14). Excluding Hallward et al. 2023 (PR = 7.10, 95% CI 2.32-21.54) or Efstate et al. 2025 (PR = 2.38, 95% CI 1.03-5.51) left the association significant.

In the meta-analysis of unadjusted ORs (k=6; Fig. S16), food insecurity was associated with higher odds of laxative use (OR = 1.95, 95% CI 1.20-3.19, p = 0.01). Between-study heterogeneity was moderate to substantial.

3.4.8. Food insecurity and vomiting

In the meta-analysis of unadjusted ORs (k=6; Fig. S17), food insecurity was associated with higher odds of vomiting (OR = 1.78, 95% CI 1.36-2.33, p < 0.0001). Between-study heterogeneity was low to moderate.

Of the 19 pooled estimates with available eORs, 15 showed statistically significant positive associations between food insecurity and ED pathology. Restrictive eating demonstrated the strongest association (eOR = 3.04), followed by overall ED pathology (eOR = 2.36) and binge eating (eOR = 2.18), with the latter two estimates derived from the correlational metaanalysis. Among adjusted estimates, the strongest associations were observed for overall ED pathology and purging (both eOR = 1.75), followed by restriction (eOR = 1.65) and binge eating (eOR = 1.56). Compensatory exercise showed the smallest statistically significant association (eOR = 1.45). No statistically significant associations were observed for ED diagnosis.

3.5. Moderator analysis

Moderator analyses were largely null (See tables S.4-S.7. for full reported moderator analyses). Neither publication year nor mean age significantly moderated the associations. A higher proportion of female participants was associated with smaller effects for restrictive eating (b = -0.006, p = .031) and lower pooled adjusted ORs for ED diagnosis (b = -0.016). All moderator findings should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of studies, limited statistical power, and multiple testing.

3.6 Narrative synthesis

3.6.1. Food insecurity and binge eating pathology

Among adolescents, cross-sectional studies reported positive associations between food insecurity and binge-eating pathology (Kim et al., 2021; Lebrun-Harris et al., 2026). Similarly, a case-control study (k = 1) found that adolescents from food-insecure households had 5.63 times greater odds of binge-eating disorder than those from food-secure households. (Andani & Adi, 2021). Among treatment-seeking youth, Urban et al. (2023) reported that 5.9% of those experiencing family-level food insecurity (n = 17) had a diagnosis of bingeeating disorder, compared with 2.0% of those without food insecurity. Anorexia nervosa binge/purge subtype was also more prevalent among youth experiencing food insecurity (29.4% vs. 16.3%). Additionally, Bruening et al. (2017) found that caregiver binge eating was associated with greater odds of adolescent binge eating after accounting for food insecurity. Among adults, 12 cross-sectional studies reported associations between food insecurity and binge-eating pathology (O'Connor et al., 2023; Rodrigues et al., 2026; Stinson et al., 2018; Morales et al., 2024), loss-of-control or uncontrolled eating (Bejarano et al., 2025; Richards et al., 2017), and emotional eating (Knippen et al., 2020; Sharpe et al., 2016; Souza et al., 2025). Among older women experiencing severe food insecurity, Wilfred et al. (2021) reported that 20.4% (n = 64) engaged in weekly binge eating. Longitudinal studies (k = 2) further demonstrated that food insecurity predicted subsequent binge eating (Hazzard et al., 2022; Hooper et al., 2022). Additionally, an ecological momentary assessment study (k = 1) found that periods of greater relative food availability predicted subsequent binge-eating symptoms among young adults experiencing food insecurity (Hazzard et al., 2023).

However, in other cross-sectional studies (k = 7), associations between food insecurity and binge eating were either non-significant (Poll et al., 2020; Westrick et al., 2020) or nonsignificant in adjusted models accounting for sociodemographic characteristics or other covariates (Goode et al., 2021; Rezeppa et al., 2025; Simone et al., 2021; Souza et al., 2025; Richards et al., 2017). Similarly, one longitudinal study found that food insecurity did not predict subsequent binge eating after accounting for baseline symptoms, sociodemographic characteristics and BMI (Kosmas et al., 2025). Other cross-sectional studies (k = 2) identified indirect pathways linking food insecurity to binge-eating pathology. Spoor et al. (2023) found no direct association but reported an indirect effect of food insecurity on binge-eating psychopathology through between-day variability in eating patterns (i.e., meal skipping). Additionally, among youth in the US National Survey of Children's Health, food insecurity accounted for 57% of the association between broader economic insecurity and past-year binge eating (Martin et al., 2026). Using the same survey, Sanjeevi and Hooker (2026) found no significant association between housing insecurity and binge eating in either male or female adolescents, which remained non-significant after adjustment for food security status.

3.6.2. Food insecurity and overall ED pathology

Overall, cross-sectional evidence generally supported an association between food insecurity and greater overall ED psychopathology among children (McGowan et al., 2022; Barry et al., 2022). Among adolescents, food insecurity was associated with higher global EDE-Q psychopathology (Andrade et al., 2025). Additionally, Sanjeevi and Hooker (2026) found cross-sectional associations between food insecurity and greater odds of reporting at least one disordered eating behaviour among both male and female adolescents in the 2022 US National Survey of Children's Health.

A similar pattern was observed in adults. Food insecurity was associated with greater overall disordered-eating patterns (Kendall et al., 1996), higher ED risk in Saudi Arabian (Zafar et al., 2025) and Chilean samples (Ugarte-Perez et al., 2025), and higher global EDE-Q psychopathology among women experiencing current food insecurity (O'Connor et al., 2023). In a seven-country study, greater food insecurity was also associated with greater ED psychopathology across both higher-and lower-income settings (Morales et al., 2024). Stinson et al. (2018) similarly found disordered eating behaviour in the food insecure group compared to food secure group. Alternatively, other cross-sectional studies (k = 4) found no significant association between food insecurity and higher disordered eating behaviours (Arikawa et al., 2020; Rodrigues et al., 2026; Bagci et al., 2026; Bryson et al., 2026). In one longitudinal study of postpartum women, marginal food security during pregnancy predicted greater disordered-eating attitudes at both 3 and 12 months postpartum after adjustment for pre-pregnancy BMI, age, race, education, income, marital status, number of children, smoking, and breastfeeding duration; however, food insecurity itself was not significantly associated with disordered-eating attitudes at either 3 or 12 month follow-up (Laraia et al., 2015).

Regarding ED diagnosis, Accurso et al. (2025) reported that 53.3% of lower-income adults with diagnosed EDs were at high risk of food insecurity, while Yang (2025) observed higher rates of recent ED diagnosis or treatment among students with low/very low food security compared to those without. In contrast, Urban et al. (2023) found similar mean ED symptom severity among youth ED patients with and without family-level food insecurity, although the food-insecure subgroup was small (n= 16).

3.6.3 Food insecurity and dietary restraint

Cross-sectional research indicates that food insecurity was associated with greater restricting symptoms (Westrick, 2020), dietary restraint (O' Connor et al., 2023; Karvay & Burke, 2026; Morales et al., 2024; Bryson et al., 2026), adolescent-reported restraint (Andrade et al., 2025; Hazzard et al., 2022), and restrictive eating behaviours (Lebrun-Harris et al., 2026). Hall (2021) similarly found that COVID-related food insecurity was associated with greater dietary restraint, although this association was not observed using a standard measure of food insecurity. Hall et al (2021) also found that of those reporting skipping two consecutive meals (n = 58), 37.9% experienced severe food insecurity compared with 20.7% who were food secure. However, four studies found no cross-sectional relationships between food insecurity and dietary restraint in pregnant (Shriver et al., 2023; Laraia et al., 2013) and adolescent samples (Kim et al., 2021). Additionally, Urban et al (2023) found that restricting-type anorexia nervosa was less prevalent among those experiencing food insecurity (47.1%, n = 8/17) than those without food insecurity (63.4%, n = 436/688) in EDtreatment seeking youth. Two studies suggest that food insecurity may also operate as a pathway linking broader material hardship to restrictive eating. For example, among youth in the US National Survey of Children's Health, Sanjeevi and Hooker (2026) found that food insecurity attenuated the association between housing insecurity and fasting/meal skipping in female adolescents, while Martin et al. (2026) found that food insecurity mediated 27% of the association between economic hardship and fasting/meal skipping. In a 14-day ecological momentary assessment study, Karvay et al. (2025) found that restriction among food-insecure adults occurred predominantly for financial reasons, although weight-control and combined financial and weight-control motivations were also reported. Spoor et al. (2023) additionally identified meal skipping as an indirect pathway linking food insecurity to binge-eating psychopathology. Two studies found longitudinal relationships between food insecurity and restrictive ED pathology (Kosmas et al., 2025; Barry et al., 2026), whilst one study only found weak associations (Naraindas et al., 2026) and one found no associations (Hazzard et al., 2022).

3.6.4 Food insecurity and compensatory behaviours

Compensatory behaviours were grouped to include self-induced vomiting, laxative or diuretic misuse, and excessive exercise. Cross-sectionally studies found that food insecurity was associated with greater unhealthy and extreme weight-control behaviours (encompassing purging/vomiting, diet pills, laxatives or diuretics) in adolescents and young adults (Hazzard et al., 2022; Caouette et al., 2026; Lebrun-Harris et al., 2026) and also in adults (Karvay and Burk., 2026; Morales et al., 2024). Wilfred et al. (2021) also found that among older women experiencing severe food insecurity (n = 64), 12.6% reported driven exercise, 7.9% self-induced vomiting, and 11.0% laxative or diuretic use. Additionally, Hall (2021) found that among participants reporting self-induced vomiting (n = 11), 45.5% experienced severe food insecurity, compared with 18.1% who were food secure. Similarly, among those reporting laxative use (n = 14), 50.0% experienced severe food insecurity compared with 28.6% who were food secure. However, other studies reported no significant association between food insecurity and extreme unhealthy weight-control behaviours (Simone et al., 2021), or purging (Spoor et al., 2023; Westrick et al., 2020). Studies examining associations between food insecurity and excessive exercise generally found no significant association (Karvay & Burke, 2026; Lebrun-Harris et al., 2026; Martin et al., 2026; Kosmas et al., 2025; Morales et al., 2024). However, Bryson et al (2026) found a significant association between recent food insecurity and excessive exercise. Additionally, Martin et al (2026) found that whilst food insecurity accounted for an estimated 39.4% of the association between economic insecurity and compensatory weight-control behaviours, the mediation effect of food insecurity was not statistically significant.

In longitudinal studies, Hooper et al. (2022) found that adolescent food insecurity did not predict later unhealthy weight-control behaviours in young adulthood eight years later, while Hazzard et al. (2022) similarly found that cross-sectional associations between food insecurity and unhealthy and extreme weight-control behaviours in adolescence were not maintained five years later. Kosmas et al. (2025) found that baseline food insecurity predicted greater excessive exercise and purging in college students at a 3-month follow-up, even after adjustment for baseline levels of these behaviours,

3.6.5 Food insecurity and body image

Among adolescents and young adults, cross-sectional studies reported associations between food insecurity and greater body dissatisfaction and drive for thinness (Shankar-Krishnan et al., 2021), greater shape and weight concerns or overvaluation (Andrade et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2021; Lebrun-Harris et al., 2026), and more severe muscle dysmorphia symptoms (Ganson et al., 2023). However, Kim et al. (2021) found no association between food insecurity and body dissatisfaction specifically. Similarly, a prospective UK study found no association between household food insecurity and appearance evaluation among adolescents two years later (Naraindas et al., 2026). Among adults, cross-sectional studies found that food insecurity was associated with lower body-image satisfaction (Jung & Parker, 2008), greater body dissatisfaction and shape/weight overvaluation (O'Connor et al., 2023; Karvay & Burke, 2026), and greater appearance-related distress (Radtke et al., 2025). However, findings were not consistent across studies. Soulliard et al. (2022) found that associations between food insecurity and body image were attenuated after adjustment for sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. Additionally, a 15-day ecological momentary assessment study found no significant association between momentary food insecurity and concurrent body dissatisfaction across repeated assessments (Kapadia et al., 2025).

3.6.6 Other ED-related pathology

Findings for avoidant or selective eating were mixed. Several studies reported greater ARFIDrelated pathology, including fear of aversive consequences and low interest in food, among food-insecure young people (Caouette et al., 2026; Lebrun-Harris et al., 2026) and adults (Karvay & Burke, 2026). Urban et al. (2023) found that 17.6% (n = 3/17) of ED treatmentseeking youth experiencing family-level food insecurity had an ARFID diagnosis, compared with 12.9% (n = 89/688) of those without food insecurity. Consistent with this, Venkatesan et al. (2026) found that individuals with cyclic vomiting syndrome who screened positive for ARFID risk were significantly more likely to be at risk of food insecurity than those without ARFID risk. However, Brown et al. (2018) found no differences in food fussiness by foodsecurity status, while Costa et al. (2025) prospectively found that food insecurity at age 10 was associated with greater food-approach traits and emotional over-and undereating three years later, at age 13, but not food fussiness. Interestingly, Sanjeevi and Hooker (2026) found that housing insecurity was independently associated with low interest in food and extreme picky eating among female adolescents when controlling for food insecurity.

Evidence for pica was similarly inconsistent. Roy et al. (2018) found that greater food insecurity was associated with higher odds of pica during pregnancy in US mothers, whereas Chung et al. (2019) found no significant association between household food insecurity and pica among women in Kenya. Ezzedin et al. (2017) found that pica during pregnancy was significantly more prevalent among food-insecure women (15/103) than food-secure women (10/197). However, this association was no longer statistically significant after adjusting for maternal education, perceived economic status, and iron supplementation during pregnancy.

A separate body of research examined diabetes-specific disordered eating. Among adolescents with type 1 diabetes, Julceus et al. (2024) found that food insecurity was associated with greater disordered-eating symptoms and more than twice the odds of screening positive for disordered eating after adjustment for sociodemographic and diabetes-related factors. Julceus et al. (2025) similarly found progressively higher disordered-eating scores across marginal, low, and very low food-security groups in a large sample of youth and young adults with type 1/type 2 diabetes.

4. Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provided evidence of consistent associations between food insecurity and a broad spectrum of ED pathology, including overall ED psychopathology, binge eating, dietary restriction, and compensatory behaviours such as purging, self-induced vomiting and laxative use. However, findings for excessive exercise, body image concerns and ED diagnoses were more mixed. The follow-up meta-regression analysis suggested that the associations were mostly consistent across gender and age.

Our meta-analyses identified associations between food insecurity and both binge eating and restriction, with a more extensive evidence base for binge eating across adults and young people, extending previous findings (Abene et al., 2023). Our findings support the feast-famine hypothesis, whereby restriction and binge eating may reflect different phases of disrupted food access. However, prospective evidence remains limited and mixed, and some binge-eating associations attenuated after sociodemographic adjustment (Goode et al., 2021) or accounting for financial and psychological factors (Simone et al., 2021). One possible explanation is provided by the stress-process model, which proposes that social circumstances influence psychological distress through chronic strains and access to coping resources (Pearlin et al., 1981). Indeed, binge eating may represent an attempt to manage distress, raising the possibility that psychological factors are not simply confounders but part of the pathway linking food insecurity to binge eating (Haedt-Matt & Keel, 2011). Longitudinal research is needed to clarify temporal relationships and distinguish confounding from mediation.

Food insecurity was associated with purging behaviours, including self-induced vomiting and laxative use. Feast-famine cycles may involve restriction during food scarcity followed by perceived overeating when food becomes available, potentially triggering weight-gain concerns and compensatory purging, consistent with the cognitive behavioural model of bulimia nervosa (Fairburn et al., 2003). In contrast, findings for excessive exercise were mixed, potentially reflecting the lack of a clear theoretical pathway linking it to food insecurity. For example, Chaimano et al. (2026) found an indirect association between food insecurity and exercise addiction through muscle dysmorphia, suggesting that body-image concerns may contribute to the relationship between food insecurity and pathological or compensatory exercise. Findings were also mixed regarding body image concerns which may reflect differences in the constructs assessed, including body dissatisfaction, thinness concerns, muscularity and appearance evaluation. Many measures emphasised thin-ideal pressures, despite body-size preferences varying across cultural and socioeconomic contexts (Ogden & Thomas, 1999; Wardle & Griffith, 2001), potentially overlooking preferences of food-insecure individuals. For example, the insurance hypothesis proposes that uncertain food availability may favour greater energy storage, potentially shaping preferences for larger body sizes (Nettle et al., 2017; Ambroziak et al., 2017). Indeed, Lauten et al. (2026) found that whilst food-insecure adults reported greater body dissatisfaction, they were also more likely to prefer a larger body or maintain their current size. Thus, body dissatisfaction among food-insecure individuals may not reflect a desire for thinness, highlighting the limitations of measures focused on thinness concerns.

Across the meta-analyses, food insecurity was consistently linked to greater overall ED psychopathology, in line with Hazzard et al. (2020). This may reflect shared processes, including shame (Davis et al., 2023), depression and stress (Pourmotabbed et al., 2020), which may increase vulnerability to ED symptoms. In contrast, the pooled association with ED diagnosis was positive but non-significant. This may partly reflect limited statistical power due to the small number of studies, alongside socioeconomic inequalities in ED recognition and access to care, as individuals from lower socioeconomic backgrounds are less likely to receive a diagnosis or specialist treatment despite experiencing symptoms (Sonneville & Lipson, 2018). Evidence for ARFID-related symptoms and diabetes-specific disordered eating was likewise mixed and based on few studies, therefore firm conclusions cannot be drawn and further research is needed.

4.1. Limitations and Future Directions

A key limitation is the geographical concentration of the evidence, with 105 studies conducted in the US. This may limit the generalisability of the findings as the experience of food insecurity is partly shaped by country-specific welfare and food-assistance systems. In the US, SNAP benefits are typically distributed monthly, which may contribute to fluctuations in food availability across the benefit cycle (Hazzard et al., 2023). These mechanisms may vary across welfare systems and payment schedules, with cross-national evidence showing differences in the strength of associations between food insecurity and ED behaviours (Efstate et al., 2025). Future research outside the US is therefore needed to establish generalisability of the findings globally.

Two field-specific methodological limitations warrant consideration. First, the small number of studies contributing to several meta-analyses limited our ability to explore sources of heterogeneity (e.g., individual-versus household-level food insecurity), although moderator analyses did not indicate that age or gender significantly influenced effect estimates. Second, the few longitudinal studies, many of which drew on overlapping cohorts and examined different ED outcomes, further precluded meaningful comparisons of effect estimates across study designs. Additionally, studies contributing to adjusted odds-ratio meta-analyses controlled for different demographic, socioeconomic and psychological factors, reducing comparability of pooled estimates. This is particularly important given evidence that food insecurity is associated with ED pathology beyond broader socioeconomic disadvantage (Hazzard et al., 2020). Inappropriate adjustment may introduce bias, as variables such as weight status may act as confounders (e.g., through weight-related discrimination and financial disadvantage), mediators, or colliders depending on the causal relationship between food insecurity and ED pathology (Hazzard et al., 2020). Future studies should therefore pre-specify causal assumptions and use theory-informed confounder selection.

Future research should also move beyond documenting associations to focus on two key priorities: first, establishing causal relationships between food insecurity and ED pathology; and second, investigating how food insecurity can be identified and addressed within ED care. Indeed, growing evidence suggests that food insecurity can interfere with treatment by worsening mental health (Bek et al., 2023) and limiting access to food and making recommendations such as regular eating and structured meal plans difficult to follow (Bryson et al., 2026). ED clinicians have also reported limited knowledge and guidance for managing food insecurity, highlighting the need for routine screening, formal training, financially realistic treatment planning, and clear referral pathways to food or financial support (Kuehne et al., 2023). Policy implications also warrant investigation as changes could exacerbate food insecurity. For example, in the US, withdrawal of enhanced SNAP benefits in 2023 was associated with increased food insecurity (Richterman et al., 2023) and poorer physical, but not mental, health outcomes (Liu et al., 2025). However, ED outcomes were not assessed, leaving it unclear whether such policy changes also influence ED risk, symptom severity, or recovery. Natural-experiment studies examining ED outcomes before and after changes to food-assistance policies could help identify potential unintended consequences and inform policies that protect both food security and ED-related health outcomes.

Overall, this review provides consistent evidence of associations between food insecurity and a broad spectrum of ED pathology, highlighting its potential importance in the development and maintenance of ED symptoms and its relevance to clinical practice.
