Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Event-related EEG research has traditionally relied on controlled laboratory tasks with limited ecological validity. Video games offer a middle ground between laboratory and real-world settings, combining intuitive interaction with experimental control. This scoping review, reported according to PRISMA-ScR, characterizes video game paradigms used in event-related EEG research and relates their findings to canonical laboratory effects. Database and supplementary searches identified 29 studies using 26 paradigms. Most paradigms used custom-built games and required visuomotor navigation or targeting within continuous gameplay, and most analyses were time-locked to feedback events such as hits or collisions. Findings showed selective convergence with laboratory effects, particularly for P3-like positivities sensitive to event probability, outcome magnitude, and player relevance, and for movement-related activity. Evidence for errorand feedback-related components was more limited, while early sensory responses were rarely examined. We discuss emerging patterns in the use of video games as event-related EEG paradigms, the correspondence between game-elicited and laboratory EEG effects, and the methodological challenges of studying event-related activity during gameplay. In sum, this synthesis aims to guide the selection and design of game paradigms, inform expectations about neural responses during gameplay, and support their interpretation in light of established laboratory findings in future research in this emerging field.

1. Introduction

Cognitive event-related EEG research has traditionally relied on highly controlled, trial-based laboratory tasks. While such paradigms offer strong internal validity and reproducibility, their simplified structure and unintuitive dynamics limit their ability to capture the neural underpinnings of how humans naturally engage with complex, dynamic environments. Moreover, many laboratory tasks are repetitive and abstract, which may reduce participant engagement and compromise data quality. Increasingly, researchers emphasize the need for experimental paradigms that capture cognitive processes under more naturalistic and dynamic conditions (e.g., Shamay-Tsoory and Mendelsohn, 2019; Matusz et al., 2019; Huk et al., 2018; Wise et al., 2023). For EEG studies, however, increasing ecological validity presents a particular challenge. Real-world settings offer little control and can introduce substantial measurement noise that reduces the reliability and interpretability of EEG signals, whereas naturalistic stimuli such as films can be presented under controlled laboratory conditions but lack the interactive demands that characterize many cognitive operations. Video games provide a promising middle ground between these approaches (Allen et al., 2024; Brändle et al., 2022; Naselaris et al., 2018).

In particular, video games offer interactive environments that capture aspects of real-world complexity while being designed primarily for enjoyment. Their intuitive interactions guide player behavior and sustain engagement, while their programmable nature can give researchers control over experimental variables and event timing. By drawing on a broad range of human abilities, video games have become increasingly useful tools for studying perceptual, motor, and cognitive processes (e.g., Green and Bavelier, 2012; Bavelier et al., 2012; Anguera et al., 2013; Boot et al., 2008; Boot, 2015; Mishra et al., 2016; Bediou et al., 2018; Bavelier and Green, 2019; Dale et al., 2020). Importantly for event-related EEG research, gameplay generates continuous, time-resolved data of environmental events, player responses, and action outcomes, which provide opportunities to time-lock neural activity to meaningful events within an ongoing interactive task.

Yet, applying event-related EEG approaches to video gameplay raises methodological and interpretive challenges. In conventional laboratory tasks, discrete stimuli, responses, and outcomes can be separated in time, their physical properties controlled, and their probabilities and magnitudes systematically varied. During gameplay, events may instead emerge gradually within changing scenes, overlap with ongoing actions and other events, and acquire their probability and significance from the game state and the player's behavior. These features complicate precise event marking, the separation of overlapping neural responses, and the distinction between effects of sensory properties and task relevance. They also raise questions about how game-elicited activity relates to established laboratory EEG effects. During continuous visuomotor control, for example, information about an impending error may become available before an outcome occurs, blurring conventional distinctions between response-related errors and external feedback.

Similarities in waveform or frequency band therefore require further functional evidence to establish correspondence with canonical components. Addressing these challenges requires considering both how game events are defined and measured and how their neural responses depend on the demands and context of play.

While previous reviews have examined selected aspects of gaming and virtual reality in neuroscience (Palaus et al., 2017; Burelli and Dixen, 2024; Calvo-Morata et al., 2022; Vasiljevic and de Miranda, 2020; Bohil et al., 2011; Ninaus et al., 2014; Bailenson et al., 2025), cognitive eventrelated EEG research during video gameplay has not yet been the focus of a systematic synthesis.

In this scoping review, we thus characterize the video game paradigms and in-game events used for event-related EEG analyses, summarize the associated neural findings, and relate them to canonical laboratory effects where possible. We also identify recurring methodological challenges and illustrate, with examples from the reviewed studies, how they may be addressed. In sum, this synthesis aims to guide the selection and design of game paradigms, inform expectations about the neural responses elicited during gameplay, and support their interpretation in light of established laboratory findings in future research in this emerging field.

The remainder of this work is structured as follows. In the Methods section, we describe the eligibility criteria, search strategy, study selection, data charting, and synthesis approach. In the Results section, we report the selection of studies, characterize the video game paradigms, and present the EEG findings for stimulus-related, response-related, and feedback-related events.

In the Discussion section, we consider patterns in the use of video game paradigms, examine correspondences with and departures from laboratory EEG effects, and address methodological challenges and recommendations. We conclude with the review's limitations and directions for future research.

2. Methods

This review was carried out in line with the Joanna Briggs Institute methodological guidance for scoping reviews (JBI; Peters et al., 2020; Pollock et al., 2023) and is presented according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR; Tricco et al., 2018). A scoping review approach was considered appropriate because our aim was to map an evolving and heterogeneous literature rather than address a narrowly defined question through quantitative synthesis (Munn et al., 2018; Tricco et al., 2018).

2.1. Eligibility criteria

Articles were deemed eligible if they reported original research conducted with healthy human participants using video game paradigms to examine event-related EEG correlates, with analyses focused on in-game events. Eligible event-related EEG analyses encompassed both event-related potentials (ERPs) and time-frequency approaches assessing event-related changes in oscillatory power, including event-related desynchronization/synchronization (ERD/ERS) and event-related spectral perturbations (ERSP). Only English-language, peer-reviewed journal articles were considered, and no limits were placed on publication date or participants' age, gender, or prior video game experience.

To identify appropriate paradigms, we adopted the video game definition proposed by Allen et al. (2024), which focuses on virtual tasks that offer an interactive setting that is straightforward to engage with. However, given the fundamentally subjective nature of enjoyment, we relaxed Allen et al. (2024)'s criterion that entertainment must be the video game's primary purpose.

We thus included paradigms that were video game-like in the sense that they were intuitive, interactive, and contained at least a potential element of enjoyment that made the activity intrinsically engaging, regardless of whether entertainment was the primary design goal. To maintain comparability with standard laboratory tasks, we excluded studies using exergames, that is, games incorporating physical activity as a core gameplay element, and video games requiring movement beyond button presses, display taps, or joystick control. We excluded studies focused on EEG responses to externally delivered haptic stimuli. Mechanical resistance used to modify gamepad controls was not itself an exclusion criterion when the EEG analyses concerned players' actions or in-game events.

We also excluded studies using brain-computer interfaces (BCI) or other biofeedback systems in which neural or physiological signals, rather than deliberate participant responses, were used to control or modify the video game. Studies that used a BCI system but recorded EEG passively during normal gameplay controlled by deliberate participant actions were included if they reported event-related EEG analyses, irrespective of whether the broader research project involved BCI development. We further excluded studies conducted in neurofeedback settings or involving any form of electrical, magnetic, or ultrasound stimulation of the brain or any other body part, as neither the use of biosignals to control virtual environments nor interventions altering neural activity fall within the scope of this review. To ensure methodological consistency across studies, we included studies using standard EEG systems or dry electrodes integrated into VR headsets, and excluded studies using intracranial EEG or mobile EEG systems. Finally, we excluded studies examining inter-brain connectivity or synchrony, as the complexity of associated social-interaction processes exceeds the scope of this review, which focuses on the cognitive processes of individuals.

2.2. Search strategy

To identify eligible articles, we searched PubMed and Web of Science. The initial search was conducted in February 2025. To keep the review up to date during manuscript preparation and revision, we conducted a second search in June 2026. Both searches were conducted by the first author and combined keywords referring to event-related EEG and variants of video game as follows: ((EEG) OR (electroencephalography) OR (ERP)) AND (video games OR videogame OR video-game OR videogaming OR video gaming OR computer game OR computergame OR computer-game OR computergaming OR computer gaming OR virtual reality game OR VR game OR virtual reality-game OR virtual reality gaming). Results from PubMed and Web of Science were combined into a single table and duplicate records were removed. These database searches were supplemented by Google Scholar searches and manual screening of the reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews.

2.3. Selection of sources of evidence

The screening and selection of eligible studies from all unique records were conducted manually and independently by the first two authors. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, with continuous consultation from the third author. This process comprised three stages, during which the inclusion and exclusion criteria were iteratively refined. We screened titles, abstracts, and full texts sequentially. Full-text screening focused primarily on the Methods and Results to establish whether EEG was recorded during active gameplay, analyses were event-related, and paradigms met our operational definition of a video game.

2.4. Data charting process

Data from selected studies were extracted using a standardized data-charting form, developed collaboratively by the first and third authors, and summarized in a table. Data charting was primarily conducted by the first author, with the continuous involvement of the third author. Any uncertainties regarding study methods, procedures, analyses, or reported findings were discussed among the authors and resolved by consensus. The form was refined iteratively throughout the review process as additional relevant aspects emerged during extraction. The final charting form, along with all charted data, is available in the PsychArchives repository (https://doi.org/10.23668/psycharchives.22650.

Extracted data items covered the characteristics of each study, the video game employed, EEG recording and analysis procedures, and the study's key findings.

2.5. Synthesis of results

Because the included studies employed heterogeneous paradigms and methods, we synthesized their findings qualitatively in a narrative review, following best-practice guidelines (Siddaway et al., 2019) and the JBI methodological guidance for scoping reviews (Peters et al., 2020).

In our narrative review, we first characterize the video game paradigms (Section 3.2) using the classification scheme outlined in Section 2.5.1, which distinguishes a paradigm's development origin, task requirements, gameplay organization, and presentation format. We then organize the corresponding event-related EEG findings by stimulus-related, response-related, and feedbackrelated in-game events (Section 3.3), following the grouping principles and terminology outlined in Section 2.5.2. We relate these findings to canonical event-related EEG effects where possible.

In the Discussion, we synthesize patterns in the use of video game paradigms, examine convergence and divergence between game-elicited and laboratory EEG effects, address methodological challenges and recommendations, and consider the review's limitations and directions for future research.

2.5.1. Synthesis of video game paradigm characteristics

Overall, we use the term video game to refer to the game itself and the term video game paradigm to refer to the game-based task as implemented for research. Distinct task variants of the same game are counted as separate paradigms, whereas repeated use or reanalysis of the same variant is not. Along with its development origin (custom-built vs. commercial), we characterized each video game paradigm by its task requirements, gameplay organization, and presentation format to capture both a game's behavioral demands and its properties with relevance to experimental design in event-related EEG studies. As the range of games could not be anticipated, all categories, particularly the task requirements, were derived inductively during data charting. A game's task requirements are meant to capture the predominant behavioral demands on which successful performance depends: decision-making requires probabilistic or strategic choices among alternatives, go/no-go requires executing or withholding responses contingent on discriminative stimuli, spatial search requires identifying and approaching goal-relevant objects in a virtual environment, spatial puzzle-solving requires arranging visuospatial patterns, and visuomotor navigation and/or targeting requires the spatially accurate and well-timed execution of actions, such as steering, dodging, or aiming. Two further requirements, first-person-shooter (FPS) combat and team combat, capture combat-centered gameplay in complex, richly rendered environments with human-like characters and realistic or narrative settings. These combine demands such as navigation, strategic decisions, and coordination with teammates. We note that games often combine several task requirements. We therefore coded only the demands that primarily determined performance, rather than processes shared by most games or insufficiently described in the original studies. We also report each game's closest commercial genre for orientation, but focus our synthesis on task requirements because games within the same genre can engage markedly different cognitive and motor processes.

We characterized gameplay organization using three features, each coded as present or absent.

Bounded epochs denote a trial-like structure of clearly delimited decision or action epochs, in contrast to continuous gameflow in which events unfold within an ongoing stream of play. We classified responses as self-paced when players chose when to initiate individual actions, without an imperative cue or a prescribed response deadline. Finally, we coded concurrent demands when players pursued two or more independent objectives simultaneously. We distinguished these at a coarse, attentional level: navigating around obstacles while separately shooting at targets counted as two demands, whereas the perceptual, motor, and timing demands jointly required for a single task, such as aiming, did not count separately.

Finally, we classified a game's presentation format by visual format and player perspective. Two-dimensional (2D) games included interface displays (abstract visual elements, such as geometric shapes or symbols, without environmental context), side views (lateral perspectives, including horizontally scrolling environments and static profile views, in which players navigate through a landscape), and displays with simulated depth, which used visual cues to suggest depth within a 2D display. We further differentiated three-dimensional (3D) games, which allow movement along three spatial dimensions without immersive technology, and virtual reality (VR) games that use a head-mounted display. For 3D games, we distinguish between top-down (bird'seye), third-person (positioned behind or above the player character), and first-person (aligned with the character's visual field) perspectives. For VR games, we distinguish between a first-or third-person view.

2.5.2. Event-related EEG findings

To synthesize the event-related EEG findings, we grouped them according to whether the signals were time-locked to stimulus-related, response-related, or feedback-related video game events. This grouping reflects neurocognitive models that describe information processing as a perception-action cycle spanning sensory input, behavioral output, and accompanying performance monitoring (Fuster, 2002 (Fuster, , 2004;; Badre, 2025; Botvinick et al., 2001; Ullsperger et al., 2014b). It also corresponds to the event categories commonly distinguished in event-related EEG research. We define stimulus-related events broadly to include both discrete stimulus presentations, as in conventional laboratory tasks, and unexpected changes within the video game environment. Response-related events comprise participant-initiated actions, such as button presses or joystick movements. Feedback-related events signal the rewarding or punishing consequences of players' actions or performance. Such feedback may take the form of explicit outcome displays or arise directly from gameplay, for example when an action results in a successful hit, a collision, or the loss of a life.

We present these three groups sequentially, while acknowledging that the underlying cognitive processes may overlap and interact rather than follow a strict temporal order. Within each group, we organized the findings around the cognitive functions investigated or the sensitivity of neural responses to psychologically relevant properties of game events. Where possible, we related these findings to canonical event-related EEG components and their established laboratory modulations (cf. Supplementary Material S1). Note that we use the term canonical event-related EEG component to denote well-known and commonly assessed event-related EEG effects that have been studied extensively and are covered in standard textbooks and reviews (e.g., Luck and Kappenman, 2011; Luck, 2014; Polich, 2007; Schomer and Lopes da Silva, 2017). For ease of presentation, we use shortened labels for commonly numbered ERP components (e.g., N1 instead of N100 and P3 instead of P300). We retain N400, subcomponent labels such as P3a and P3b, and the original designation P300-CE. Components reported as FN are labeled FRN. These conventions apply to the main text, tables, and Supplementary Material. Because component latencies, electrode sites, and spectral frequency ranges varied across studies, we report these alongside the corresponding findings in the overview tables wherever the original reports provided this information.

Task requirements

The most common task requirement was visuomotor navigation and/or targeting (n = 20, 77%). Within this group, control demands ranged from comparatively simple, quickly learned inputs, such as single-button tapping or one-dimensional steering (e.g., Pei et al., 2025; Martínez-Tejada et al., 2021), to multi-effector control requiring extended practice (e.g., Maclin et al., 2011; Mariman et al., 2023). The paradigms also differed in the complexity of their gameplay. At one end of this range were games built around repetitive, score-based actions, as in target-shooting tasks (e.g., Figure 3H), a paddle game (Pong; Rebert et al., 1984), or an endless runner (Flappy Bird ; Pei et al., 2025). At the other end were mission-or combat-based environments that additionally involved FPS combat (n = 3, Arake et al., 2022; Salminen and Ravaja, 2008; Shin et al., 2012) and, in two cases, team combat (Shin et al., 2012; Li et al., 2025). The remaining task requirements were rarely the primary demands. Decision-making was the central demand in three paradigms (e.g., Figure 3A) and go/no-go in two, one with a trial-based (Figure 3B) and one with a continuous format (Figure 3C). Spatial search (Figure 3D) and spatial puzzle-solving (Tetris; Bernal et al., 2024) were each represented by a single paradigm. Beyond their predominant requirements, many games involved further demands that did not primarily determine performance and were therefore not coded separately. The spatial search game, for instance, required players to navigate through a virtual town, but its central task goal was finding goal-relevant stores rather than navigating precisely. Conversely, several visuomotor games required players to fire only at certain targets, which implicitly involves target search and response inhibition, but neither constituted the central task goal. Notably, all games centered on decision-making, go/no-go, spatial search, or spatial puzzle-solving were custom-built, whereas all commercial titles involved visuomotor navigation and/or targeting, three of which also involved FPS or team combat.

Gameplay organization

Six paradigms organized gameplay into bounded epochs (e.g., Figure 3A, B and H), comprising two decision-making, one go/no-go, and three navigation and/or targeting paradigms. Only three paradigms, two decision-making and one navigation and/or targeting paradigm, allowed self-paced responses, meaning that players could choose the timing of individual responses. In the others, movement was often self-paced in a broad sense, in that players could typically move through the environment at will, but discrete responses were reactive, their timing constrained by the appearance or approach of targets, tokens, or enemies. Twelve paradigms required players to manage two or more concurrent demands, typically navigation combined with shooting. All of these also involved navigation and/or targeting requirements.

Presentation format

Presentation formats ranged from 2D displays (n = 12), including displays with simulated depth and those depicting abstract geometric forms (e.g., Figure 3E) or minimalist but thematically contextualized scenes (e.g., Figure 3F and G), to 3D environments, presented from a top-down view (n = 2, e.g., Figure 3B), a first-or third-person perspective on a standard screen (n = 6, e.g., Figure 3B and D), or in immersive VR with a first-or third-person perspective (n = 7, e.g., Figure 3C, H and I). Because one study presented the same task in both a top-down and a first-person version (Petras et al., 2016), these counts sum to 27.

3.3. Event-related EEG findings

Of the 29 included studies, 10 reported EEG activity time-locked to stimulus-related events, 6 to response-related events, and 18 to feedback-related events. Twenty studies reported ERPs, 14 reported time-frequency analyses of event-related spectral power changes (including ERD/ERS and ERSP), and 6 reported other EEG analyses, such as hemispheric asymmetry indices, timelocked power estimates, or EEG-based classification. These categories are not mutually exclusive because several studies analyzed more than one event type or measure. Figure 2 summarizes event locking and EEG measures at the paradigm level, counting paradigms used in multiple studies only once. Stimulus-locked analyses predominated among the seven paradigms centered on decision-making, go/no-go, spatial search, or puzzle-solving (5 of 7), whereas feedback-locked analyses predominated among paradigms with visuomotor requirements (15 of 20).

3.3.1. Stimulus-related events

Stimulus-related events varied considerably in modality, response demands, and role within gameplay (Table 1). Notably, early sensory processing was rarely examined, with only isolated reports of P1 or N1 effects (Petras et al., 2016; Wimmer et al., 2024). This scarcity may partly reflect the research questions of the reviewed studies, most of which targeted later components, but also the experimental demands of early sensory analyses. Early visual components are highly sensitive to physical stimulus properties such as luminance and contrast (Luck, 2014), which are difficult to control against the continuous visual change characteristic of video games. Moreover, analyses of early visual components are particularly susceptible to temporal imprecision introduced by manual event marking (e.g., Shin et al., 2012) or low game refresh rates (e.g., Maclin et al., 2011). To date, event-related EEG research using video game paradigms thus appears to have focused predominantly on later, largely endogenous components, even when time-locking EEG activity to basic stimulus onsets. Rather than examining early sensory processing, the studies reviewed primarily addressed the role of stimuli within gameplay. In this regard, we identified three clusters of stimulus-related events of interest: (1) stimuli that convey goal-relevant information, (2) stimuli that prompt response selection, and (3) stimuli that signal changes in the conditions of ongoing play.

(1) Stimulus events conveying goal-relevant information Two studies examined event-related changes in spectral power during the processing of information encountered in gameplay, focusing on its relevance to current goals or its mode of presentation. In a taxi-driving game (Figure 3D), participants searched for passengers and delivered them to designated stores. Frontal theta power decreased more for target stores than for neutral or non-target stores, consistent with a role in goal-directed object recognition and categorization (Weidemann et al., 2009). Stores emerged gradually during navigation, so stimulus onset was operationalized as the moment a store occupied 0.35% of the display area, a threshold at which target stores were being directly approached on 95% of trials. Because stores were distinguished by their relevance to the current destination rather than by their visual features, this contrast points to higher-order rather than perceptual processing.

In a VR decision-making task involving a virtual agent, alpha-band ERD/ERS time-locked to speech onset varied with communication modality (Chang et al., 2022). Embodied agents elicited parietal and occipital alpha ERS, gestural agents elicited ERD at both sites, and voice-only agents produced parietal ERD and occipital ERS. The authors attributed these differences to greater speech-onset predictability when the agent's body was visible and to additional attentional demands imposed by sudden gesturing. Although consistent with the association between alpha ERD and attentional engagement, this interpretation remains qualified by the joint variation of communication modality and predictability, particularly because anticipatory alpha ERD can itself be modality-specific (Pfurtscheller and Lopes da Silva, 2017, cf. Supplementary Material S1).

(2) Stimulus events prompting response selection Four studies examined stimulus events that required players to select or withhold a response according to task rules, including go/no-go cues and memory probes. Three embedded go/nogo contingencies in video games and reported N2 and P3 components associated with response requirements, task performance, and training (Petras et al., 2016; Sanuki et al., 2022; Qiao and Iramina, 2026). In laboratory tasks, no-go stimuli typically elicit a larger N2 than go stimuli, commonly associated with response inhibition or conflict (Folstein and Van Petten, 2008; Donkers and van Boxtel, 2004, cf. Supplementary Material S1). Only Petras et al. (2016)directly compared these conditions, finding a larger N2 to no-go than go targets in a virtual shooting scenario (Figure 3B). The N2 was also larger when human-like targets appeared close in a first-person perspective than in a distant, bird's-eye view. The authors attributed this difference to heightened moral conflict, although perceived distance was confounded with target size and visual complexity.

The other two studies used the same VR multitasking game (Figure 3C), in which participants navigated an environment while monitoring color changes in a monster's jewel and their avatar's clothing, responding when the colors differed (go) and withholding a response when they matched (no-go). Successful go, successful no-go, and failed no-go trials elicited N2 and P3 components, whereas failed go trials showed an attenuated N2 and no P3, suggesting that the cues had not been registered (Sanuki et al., 2022). Training-related changes depended on gaze strategy: participants who primarily fixated the target field and perceived the color cues parafoveally developed enhanced no-go N2 and P3 components, whereas those who directly fixated the cues showed a reduced go P3 (Qiao and Iramina, 2026). The authors interpreted these patterns as increased conflict-monitoring demands for indirectly attended no-go cues and more efficient processing of directly attended go cues, respectively.

Memory-based response selection was examined in the standard variant of Space Fortress, in which players navigated a spaceship, attacked a fortress with correctly timed shots, and avoided damage (Maclin et al., 2011). Alongside these ongoing tasks, players classified appearing mines as friend or foe. Each mine served as a memory probe because its letter code had to be compared with a previously memorized set of codes to determine the appropriate response. The study combined gameplay with an auditory oddball task to test whether practice reduced attentional demands. Reduced demands were expected to yield a smaller P3 to game events and a larger P3 to oddball tones, reflecting a reallocation of attentional resources (Isreal et al., 1980; Kramer et al., 1985; Polich, 2007, cf. Supplementary Material S1). Unlike the P3 to feedback-related events in the same study (Section 3.3.3), the mine-onset P3 did not decrease with training and tended to increase. The authors attributed this persistence to changes across blocks in which letter codes identified foes, requiring players to continue consulting the memorized codes rather than relying on an automatized response.

(3) Stimulus events signaling changes in the conditions of ongoing play Four studies examined events that changed ongoing gameplay by perturbing control, interrupting visual continuity, or reducing task difficulty. Two introduced unexpected perturbations of visuomotor control, either brief deviations of cursor movement in an obstacle-avoidance game (Spüler and Niethammer, 2015) or glider rotations and target displacements in a VR flight simulation (Wimmer et al., 2024, Figure 3I). Both reported a similar frontocentral ERP sequence following perturbation onset: an early positivity, a negativity at 220-300 ms, a positivity at approximately 300-400 ms, and a later negativity at 400-500 ms. The perturbations were designed to elicit system-generated errors, with potential applications in online error correction and system adaptation. Accordingly, both studies interpreted the early negativity and subsequent positivity within an error-processing framework, as ERN/FRN-and Pe-like activity reflecting detection of, and adjustment to, externally generated errors (cf. Supplementary Material S1). A comparable ERP sequence followed brief display freezes in a VR slingshot game, introduced to investigate disruptions of presence (Center et al., 2026). However, comparison with normal trials, in which the released ball continued along its expected trajectory, showed that only the P3b and N400 differed: freezes reduced the P3b and enlarged the N400, whereas N2 amplitude remained unchanged. The authors related the attenuated P3b to interrupted extraction of goal-relevant information and context updating, and the enlarged N400 to a violation of environmental coherence. This interpretation extends the N400's classical association with semantic incongruence (Kutas and Hillyard, 1980; Kutas and Federmeier, 2011) to expectancy violations concerning the physical behavior of the virtual environment (cf. Supplementary Material S1).

A different change in gameplay was examined in a custom-built VR adaptation of Tetris, where an occasional helper piece cleared four rows and slowed the game (Bernal et al., 2024).

Spectral power changes around the clearing of rows were interpreted as reflecting reduced mental and visuospatial workload. Frontal alpha and frontoparietal beta power increased before the event, whereas parietal alpha decreased and parietal beta increased afterward, followed by a later decrease in frontal theta. The introduction of distinct helper pieces among standard tetrominos resembles an oddball manipulation, for which infrequent targets have been associated with alpha suppression (Yordanova et al., 2001, cf. Supplementary Material S1). However, helper-piece frequency was not reported, and appearance depended on stack height. Although this triggering rule was not disclosed, players may have learned it through experience, leaving the rarity and unexpectedness of these events uncertain.

3.3.2. Response-related events

Six studies reported EEG activity time-locked to response-related events, including key or button presses, trigger releases, and thumbstick displacement onsets (Table 2). Compared with the diverse stimulus-related events, these response events and the processes examined had more direct counterparts in traditional laboratory EEG research, particularly studies of movement preparation, where established movement-related signatures include the readiness potential (RP), the lateralized readiness potential (LRP), and sensorimotor alpha/beta power decreases and subsequent increases (ERD/ERS) (Shibasaki and Hallett, 2006; Smulders and Miller, 2012; Pfurtscheller and Lopes da Silva, 2017, cf. Supplementary Material S1). Other studies drew on traditional laboratory EEG research related to action execution and performance monitoring. Nevertheless, responses differed in the extent to which their timing was freely determined by the player or constrained by ongoing game demands. In sum, we identified three clusters of response-related analyses: (1) preparation of self-paced actions, (2) action selection and execution during ongoing visuomotor control, and (3) response-related performance monitoring.

(1) Preparation of self-paced actions

One study specifically designed the game Free Wally (Figure 3A) to test whether embedding actions in a meaningful, reason-based context alters neural movement preparation (Verbaarschot et al., 2019). Players directed water jets to the left or right to displace approaching agents and protect a captive whale, choosing when and in which direction to act. A structurally matched control game used visually similar but abstract stimuli without meaningful goals or consequences.

Both conditions elicited an RP beginning approximately 2 s before the response, an LRP emerging approximately 200-400 ms before the response, and sensorimotor alpha/beta ERD followed by ERS. These patterns did not differ significantly between conditions, suggesting that the meaningful game context did not measurably alter the examined movement-preparation signatures.

An incidental finding in a VR slingshot game extended this evidence (Center et al., 2026).

Participants launched balls at color-coded targets by releasing a controller trigger at a freely chosen time. Ball release was preceded by a slow central negativity beginning approximately 400 ms before the response, consistent with an RP-like buildup. Release also served as the trigger for the display freezes described in Section 3.3.1. However, unlike Free Wally, the VR slingshot game used by Center et al. (2026) was designed to investigate disruptions of presence rather than movement preparation, so the preparatory negativity was an additional observation rather than a planned component of interest.

(2) Action selection and execution during ongoing visuomotor control

Three studies examined response-related activity during ongoing navigation and shooting, where action timing was constrained by approaching obstacles, environment borders, or target availability (Mariman et al., 2023; Anderson et al., 2020; Cavanagh and Castellanos, 2016). In a visuomotor game combining cursor navigation and shooting (Figure 3E), beta-band ERD/ERS around both response types changed with practice, most clearly in participants who trained the components separately before combining them in the final session (Mariman et al., 2023).

The authors linked these changes to shifting demands on motor control and task integration.

However, navigation movements were shorter on the first day than in the final session, so changes in response duration may also have contributed to the EEG differences.

In the Autoturn variant of Space Fortress, the spaceship automatically aimed at the fortress, while players controlled thrust and firing using separate keys. Both responses elicited lateralized frontocentral negativities consistent with an LRP (Anderson et al., 2020, cf. Supplementary Material S1). Although players chose when to press, thrust responses were constrained by navigation demands, while fortress destruction required 11 shots spaced at least 250 ms apart, followed by one further shot within 250 ms. Firing too quickly during the initial sequence reset the fortress's accumulated vulnerability. These movement-preparation signatures thus occurred during actions with substantial spatial and temporal constraints.

Finally, in the space shooter Escape from Asteroid Axon, players navigated a starfighter, collected stars and ammunition, and fired missiles at enemies while avoiding crashes. Missile launches elicited a frontocentral delta-band negativity around the button press, which the authors interpreted as a correlate of action selection (Cavanagh and Castellanos, 2016, Figure 3G).

(3) Response-related performance monitoring

Two studies examined response-locked potentials resembling the ERN, CRN, and Pe, established laboratory signatures of performance monitoring (Gehring et al., 2012; Vidal et al., 2000; Falkenstein et al., 2000, cf. Supplementary Material S1), and related their amplitudes to game performance.

First, Mariman et al. (2023) examined performance monitoring in a visuomotor game combining navigation and shooting. Participants shot at targets by displacing the right gamepad thumbstick against additional elastic resistance and trained navigation and shooting either together or separately. EEG activity time-locked to shooting movements showed a positive deflection approximately 200 ms after the end of thumbstick displacement, corresponding to about 600 ms after displacement onset. The authors interpreted this deflection as Pe-like activity reflecting action monitoring. Its amplitude decreased with training in the whole-practice group, in parallel with decreasing errors, linking the response-locked activity to improvements in visuomotor performance.

Second, Arake et al. (2022) examined a first-person VR military shooting game in which participants navigated through an environment, took cover, and shot at enemies by pressing the gamepad's "R" button while avoiding being hit. Gunshots elicited a frontocentral negativity approximately 100 ms after the response, with larger amplitudes following missed than successful shots, which the authors classified as ERN and CRN, respectively. ERN amplitude also correlated with shooting performance across participants, being larger in those who required fewer shots and less time per kill. Of note, misses outnumbered hits by about four to one, reversing the ratio of errors to correct responses typically targeted in ERN paradigms (cf. Gehring et al., 2012). The dominant ERN source was localized to the rostral rather than the more commonly reported dorsal anterior cingulate cortex. The authors suggested that this localization might reflect negative-affect processing associated with the increased threat of being hit after missing a target, extending the interpretation beyond error detection alone.

3.3.3. Feedback-related events

A majority of the reviewed studies (18 of 29, 62%) reported EEG activity time-locked to feedback-related events, defined as events signaling the rewarding or punishing consequences of players' actions (Table 3). Feedback-related events formed the largest event class and the only one examined using commercial titles. Although these events have apparent counterparts in the outcomes presented in traditional laboratory feedback paradigms, they generally arose from ongoing visuomotor performance and carried consequences for subsequent play. Notably, the prevalence of feedback-locked analyses did not translate into frequent reports of components specifically associated with feedback processing. P3-like positivities were the most commonly reported ERP responses, whereas FRN and RewP findings (cf. Supplementary Material S1) were comparatively rare. Spectral analyses addressed a broader range of processes, including learning, attention, and affect. We identified three clusters of feedback-related EEG activity examined in these studies: (1) P3 responses and their sensitivity to outcome properties and player relevance, (2) feedback-and error-related potentials, including the FRN/RewP and anticipatory errorrelated activity, and (3) spectral modulations associated with game outcomes.

(1) Feedback-related P3

P3-like positivities were reported in eight studies, including one reanalysis of an existing dataset, and varied considerably in latency, scalp distribution, and interpretation (Maclin et al., 2011; Mathewson et al., 2012; Anderson et al., 2020 Anderson et al., , 2024;; Fincham et al., 2026; Yokota et al., 2019; Li et al., 2025; Pei et al., 2025).

Several findings resembled the sensitivity of laboratory P3 responses to outcome probability and magnitude (Glazer et al., 2018; Stewardson and Sambrook, 2020, cf. Supplementary Material S1). In two Space Fortress variants, amplitudes were larger for rare deaths than frequent kills (Anderson et al., 2020) and for deaths than missed shots (Anderson et al., 2024, Figure 3F).

P3 latency was shortest after kills and longest after vulnerability resets caused by firing too quickly. The authors attributed this difference to greater predictability of kills and deaths from preceding gameplay, whereas resets occurred more abruptly (Anderson et al., 2020). P3 responses also varied with an outcome's relevance to the individual player. In a cooperative two-player Space Fortress variant, in which one player drew the fortress's fire while the other attacked it, amplitudes were larger and latencies shorter for a player's own death than for the teammate's, and kill-related amplitudes were larger in the player responsible for the kill (Fincham et al., 2026). The authors interpreted this pattern as tracking goal-relevant event boundaries defined by each player's role. In a two-player baseball game, by contrast, P3 responses did not differ between players, which the authors attributed to similar outcome salience for both, although an earlier frontocentral negativity differed between them (Yokota et al., 2019).

Three studies explicitly examined relationships between game-related responses and those elicited in laboratory tasks. Maclin et al. (2011) combined Space Fortress with an auditory oddball task before and after training. The P3 to fortress hits decreased with training while the P3 to oddball tones increased, and both changes correlated with performance improvements.

The authors interpreted this reciprocal pattern as a reallocation of attentional resources freed by automatization of gameplay, consistent with laboratory work on P3 and resource allocation (Isreal et al., 1980; Kramer et al., 1985, cf. Supplementary Material S1). Both responses peaked at approximately 400 ms with a parietal maximum. By contrast, the P3 to memory-probe mine onsets peaked later, tended to be more frontal, and did not show this reciprocity (Section 3.3.1).

Pei et al. (2025) examined a custom-built reimplementation of Flappy Bird, a side-scrolling game in which players repeatedly tap to keep a bird airborne and guide it through gaps between pipes that act as obstacles. They compared EEG responses to the bird colliding with a pipe with ERPs from five laboratory tasks recorded in a separate cohort. Collisions elicited a broadly distributed central positivity around 300 ms that exceeded the laboratory responses and increased rather than habituated across the initial blocks. Although resembling the classic P3, particularly the novelty P3 (Polich, 2007; Ranganath and Rainer, 2003, cf.

Supplementary Material S1), its amplitude and persistence led the authors to propose a distinct critical-event variant, the P300-CE. They characterized it as endogenous because collisions involved no abrupt visual change.

Finally, Cavanagh and Castellanos (2016) trained classifiers on EEG from oddball and gambling tasks completed by the same participants before gameplay. Rewarding missile hits and punishing crashes in Escape from Asteroid Axon both showed a bias toward oddball-target patterns within the canonical P3 time range, suggesting shared salience-detection processes. Missile hits additionally resembled gambling wins, whereas crashes were not classified as losses and leaned slightly toward wins. Unlike gambling losses, crashes lacked strong frontal midline theta and showed a shorter ERP time course. The authors suggested that crashes may not have elicited the same need for cognitive control, or that gambling losses capture only one way of processing unfavorable outcomes.

(2) Feedback-and error-related potentials Only one of the reviewed studies explicitly distinguished both an FRN and a RewP, reporting these components following misses and hits, respectively, in a VR game in which players shot down approaching cannonballs (Yokota and Naruse, 2021, Figure 3H). Cannonball curvature and size varied task difficulty and thus the assumed probability of a hit, while time pressure and accumulated damage varied the consequences of the outcome. The FRN was larger in easier situations, when misses were unexpected. The RewP was larger in harder situations, when hits were unexpected, and when outcomes were more consequential. These effects broadly corresponded to laboratory findings on outcome probability and magnitude (Sambrook and Goslin, 2015; Paul et al., 2020 Paul et al., , 2025, cf, cf. Supplementary Material S1).

Several studies reported deflections in the same latency range as the FRN and RewP with tentative or no component identification. In cooperative Space Fortress, Anderson et al. (2024) identified a frontocentral negativity within a principal component separating punishing from rewarding outcomes and suggested a correspondence to the FRN. In Escape from Asteroid Axon, Cavanagh and Castellanos (2016) tentatively associated the plateau following the P2 to missile hits with the RewP. Other studies reported positivities after rewarding collections in a spacenavigation game (Martínez-Tejada et al., 2021) or negativities after kills and vulnerability resets in Space Fortress (Anderson et al., 2020), without assigning them to specific components.

Three studies reported negativities shortly before or at the onset of punishing outcomes (Yokota et al., 2019; Martínez-Tejada et al., 2021; Spüler and Niethammer, 2015). These responses fall between conventional laboratory categories: ERN and Pe analyses typically align activity to responses when error information is internally available, whereas FRN analyses align it to externally provided feedback (Gehring et al., 2012; Krigolson, 2018, cf. Supplementary Material S1). Here, activity was aligned to visible outcomes but emerged earlier than a typical FRN and was interpreted as error anticipation or error processing. Specifically, in a game requiring players to collect tokens while avoiding obstacles, a negativity began approximately 500 ms before collisions (Martínez-Tejada et al., 2021). Similarly, in a cursor-control game, collisions with falling blocks elicited a negativity peaking approximately at collision onset, followed by a positivity interpreted as Pe (Spüler and Niethammer, 2015). In a baseball video game, likewise, a negativity emerged before the outcome became visible. Here, two players competed as batter and pitcher while viewing the same screen, but the negativity appeared only in the batter who committed the error (Yokota et al., 2019). Its amplitude was larger when the batter was losing, supporting an association with subjective error experience and motivational relevance. In the baseball and cursor-control games, the negative-positive sequence further resembled performance-monitoring activity (Ullsperger et al., 2014b, cf. Supplementary Material S1). Notably, all three studies required continuous visuomotor control, allowing information about impending outcomes to accumulate before their visible occurrence. The early negativities therefore resemble findings from motor-control paradigms in which predicted errors elicit ERN-like activity before an overt failure (Krigolson and Holroyd, 2007b; Maurer et al., 2022).

(3) Spectral modulations associated with game outcomes

Feedback-related spectral analyses most often examined alpha power or hemispheric alpha asymmetries, followed by theta and delta power, with beta and gamma modulations reported less frequently. Correspondence with laboratory associations between outcome valence and oscillatory activity was mixed. Laboratory studies commonly report increased frontal midline theta after unfavorable outcomes and increased delta or frontal beta after favorable outcomes (Luft, 2014; Glazer et al., 2018, cf. Supplementary Material S1). In the reviewed games, delta and beta increases occurred after both rewarding and punishing events (Cavanagh and Castellanos, 2016; Li et al., 2025; Salminen and Ravaja, 2007). Note, however, that such modulations have also been linked to learning, outcome salience, and motor control (Luft, 2014; Glazer et al., 2018). Theta power decreased after both outcome types in two games (Salminen and Ravaja, 2007; Li et al., 2025), and crashes in Escape from Asteroid Axon lacked the pronounced frontal midline theta enhancement elicited by gambling losses in the same participants (Cavanagh and Castellanos, 2016). Delta increases also coincided with P3 responses (Li et al., 2025; Maclin et al., 2011). In Space Fortress, correlated training-related changes in delta and P3 suggested that they might reflect overlapping processes (Maclin et al., 2011).

Two studies linked spectral activity to learning. In a game requiring players to choose between approaching doors under changing reward and punishment probabilities, theta power correlated positively and alpha power negatively with trial-wise precision-weighted prediction errors, which the authors interpreted in terms of belief updating (Eguinoa et al., 2026). The theta effect parallels findings from laboratory reinforcement-learning tasks (Cavanagh et al., 2010, cf. Supplementary Material S1). In Space Fortress, pre-training delta and alpha power following fortress hits predicted subsequent learning within the game and on Stop, Sternberg, and N-back tasks.

By contrast, the fortress-hit P3 correlated with initial game performance but did not predict learning (Mathewson et al., 2012).

Other studies emphasized attention, arousal, and motor demands. In Super Monkey Ball 2, players tilted a platform to guide a ball-encased monkey toward a goal while collecting tokens and avoiding falls. Token collection, often requiring risky steering near platform edges, was followed by reduced frontal high-alpha power, interpreted as increased attention. Goal completion elicited increased parietal theta and widespread alpha power, interpreted as relaxation after reaching the goal (Salminen and Ravaja, 2007). Beta power increased after token collection, goal completion, and falls. As tokens were often positioned near edges, falls could occur while players pursued rewards, making their valence ambiguous, and steering immediately before or during a fall could also contribute to subsequent beta synchronization (Salminen and Ravaja, 2007; Pfurtscheller et al., 1996, cf. Supplementary Material S1). In the jump-and-run game Super Meat Boy, gamma power was higher during death events than during general gameplay, which the authors attributed to heightened attention and arousal (McMahan et al., 2015).

Affective processing was emphasized in a first-person shooter, where wounding and killing opponents elicited increased occipital theta power (Salminen and Ravaja, 2008). The authors linked this response to the violent content of the events, noting its absence in their earlier study of the non-violent ball-rolling game (Salminen and Ravaja, 2007). Hemispheric alpha asymmetries were also reported in four studies using commercial games. Frontal asymmetry shifted in opposite directions after hitting an opponent and being shot in one first-person shooter (Shin et al., 2012), while kills elicited a central low-alpha asymmetry in another (Salminen and Ravaja, 2008). Falls in Super Monkey Ball 2 elicited a frontal low-alpha asymmetry (Salminen and Ravaja, 2007). 4. Discussion

This scoping review mapped how video game paradigms have been used to examine eventrelated EEG activity during gameplay. The findings indicate that established neural signatures can be observed during ongoing, goal-directed behavior, although their correspondence with laboratory effects varies across components and paradigms. This highlights the potential of video games to extend cognitive EEG research while raising questions about how gameplay shapes the measurement and interpretation of neural responses.

In the following, we first summarize the current use of video games as event-related EEG paradigms in the reviewed literature (Section 4.1). We then discuss converging and diverging findings on event-related EEG effects between the video game studies reviewed here and conventional laboratory paradigms (Section 4.2). In Section 4.3, we address recurring methodological challenges associated with using video game paradigms for event-related EEG research and how these challenges may be addressed. Finally, after acknowledging some limitations of the current scoping review, we close with potential directions for future research at the intersection of video game paradigms and EEG research.

4.1. Video games as event-related EEG paradigms

The reviewed literature used a diverse set of video game paradigms, although most were custom-built and involved navigation and/or targeting during continuous gameplay. Across 29 studies, we identified 26 distinct paradigms, of which 19 were custom-built, 20 involved navigation and/or targeting, and 20 lacked bounded decision or action epochs. Repeated use across studies was concentrated in Space Fortress (Donchin, 1989), represented by three variants across five studies (Maclin et al., 2011; Mathewson et al., 2012; Anderson et al., 2020 Anderson et al., , 2024;; Fincham et al., 2026), and a VR go/no-go multitasking game examined in two studies (Sanuki et al., 2022; Qiao and Iramina, 2026).

The reviewed studies illustrate how tasks and manipulations from traditional laboratory research can be embedded in gameplay. Examples include go/no-go contingencies to probe cognitive control (Petras et al., 2016; Sanuki et al., 2022; Qiao and Iramina, 2026), self-paced voluntary responses to study deliberate action (Verbaarschot et al., 2019), operationalizations of outcome probability and magnitude through task geometry and game progress (Yokota and Naruse, 2021), and volatile reinforcement structures to examine probabilistic decision-making (Eguinoa et al., 2026). Gameplay also provides ways to establish an event's relevance through the player's goals and role. In a taxi-driving game, the same stores served as targets or nontargets depending on the passenger's destination (Weidemann et al., 2009). In two-player games, events on a shared display elicited different neural responses depending on the player's role (Yokota et al., 2019; Fincham et al., 2026). In Flappy Bird, collisions were analyzed as changes in the game situation without an abrupt visual change at the event itself (Pei et al., 2025).

These examples illustrate how video game paradigms can incorporate traditional laboratory tasks while also making event relevance depend on the unfolding context of play. They provide opportunities to examine goal-related significance beyond differences in the physical stimulus, although the extent to which these influences can be separated depends on the individual design.

The events selected for EEG analysis were unevenly distributed across these paradigms.

Feedback-locked analyses were used in 16 of the 26 paradigms, stimulus-locked analyses in nine, and response-locked analyses in only six. Only three studies made movement preparation, motor learning, or response-locked error processing a primary objective (Verbaarschot et al., 2019; Mariman et al., 2023; Arake et al., 2022). This distribution may partly reflect the predominance of continuous gameplay. Objects often approach gradually, and navigation may require sustained control, while hits, collisions, and deaths provide discrete events for time-locking. Access to event timing may also contribute. Studies using commercial titles relied on external event annotation, for instance from screen recordings, and all included feedback-locked analyses. Because most commercial paradigms also involved continuous gameplay, the contributions of game structure and event access cannot be separated from this review. Nevertheless, gradual stimulus changes and extended actions can support event-related analyses when events are defined operationally, as illustrated by the display size of an approached object (Weidemann et al., 2009) and the onset and offset of thumbstick displacement (Mariman et al., 2023). The current distribution of analyses may therefore reflect both researchers' objectives and the events that their selected games make accessible.

4.2. Event-related EEG effects during gameplay

In the following, we discuss points of convergence and divergence between the event-related EEG effects observed using video game paradigms and more traditional laboratory paradigms, broadly ordered by their frequency of observation in the reviewed literature. Throughout, we distinguish similarities in response morphology and functional sensitivity from evidence provided by direct comparisons with laboratory tasks.

P3-like positivities

P3-like positivities provided the most frequently reported evidence of convergence between video game paradigms and traditional laboratory tasks. They occurred across event types, including go/no-go stimuli (Petras et al., 2016; Sanuki et al., 2022; Qiao and Iramina, 2026), memory probe-like mine onsets (Maclin et al., 2011), display freezes (Center et al., 2026), and rewarding and punishing outcomes (Maclin et al., 2011; Anderson et al., 2020 Anderson et al., , 2024;; Fincham et al., 2026; Yokota et al., 2019; Li et al., 2025; Pei et al., 2025). Beyond their resemblance in latency and scalp distribution, several responses varied with factors known to modulate the laboratory P3. Outcome-related amplitudes varied with event probability (Anderson et al., 2020) and outcome magnitude (Anderson et al., 2024), consistent with laboratory findings on expectancy and magnitude (Hajcak et al., 2005; Glazer et al., 2018; Stewardson and Sambrook, 2020; Paul et al., 2025). P3 latency also varied with the predictability of outcomes from preceding gameplay (Anderson et al., 2020), while both amplitude and latency depended on an event's relevance to the individual player (Fincham et al., 2026 , cf. Yokota et al., 2019). These findings suggest that P3-like responses during gameplay retain sensitivity to event probability, magnitude, and relevance, although these similarities alone do not establish that the underlying processes are identical.

Studies that compared game-elicited positivities with laboratory responses more directly revealed both similarities and differences. The P3 to rewarding fortress hits showed a reciprocal relationship with the P3 to tones in a concurrent oddball task, consistent with shared attentional resources (Maclin et al., 2011). Rewarding and punishing outcomes in Escape from Asteroid Axon also resembled oddball targets in cross-paradigm classification (Cavanagh and Castellanos, 2016).

In contrast, pipe collisions in Flappy Bird elicited a positivity that was larger and more persistent across repetitions than responses in traditional laboratory tasks, including a reinforcementlearning task, leading the authors to propose the P300-CE (Pei et al., 2025). These findings suggest convergence in resource allocation and spatiotemporal response patterns alongside differences in amplitude and habituation. Because the studies tested different properties, they do not establish a uniform correspondence or a general divergence between game-elicited and laboratory P3 responses. Several authors interpreted the game-elicited positivities primarily in terms of the salience and goal relevance of critical game events (Anderson et al., 2020 (Anderson et al., , 2024;; Fincham et al., 2026; Cavanagh and Castellanos, 2016; Pei et al., 2025) or the attentional resources they engage (Maclin et al., 2011; Cavanagh and Castellanos, 2016; Fincham et al., 2026; Pei et al., 2025). This emphasis is compatible with traditional laboratory accounts of the P3 that stress behavioral relevance and motivational significance (Walentowska et al., 2016), but leaves less direct evidence about whether these responses track outcome valence, reward prediction errors, or learning during gameplay.

Error-and feedback-related components

Evidence for convergence with traditional laboratory measures of error and feedback processing was more limited. Although feedback-locked analyses were reported in 18 of the 29 studies, only four related their findings to the FRN or RewP. One study specifically delineated both components (Yokota and Naruse, 2021), two proposed these correspondences tentatively (Anderson et al., 2024; Cavanagh and Castellanos, 2016), and one examined perturbations of ongoing control rather than action outcomes (Spüler and Niethammer, 2015). Reports of ERN-, CRN-, or Pe-like activity were also uncommon and extended beyond the response events to which these components are conventionally time-locked. Of the six events associated with such activity, two were responses (Arake et al., 2022; Mariman et al., 2023), two were perturbations of ongoing control (Spüler and Niethammer, 2015; Wimmer et al., 2024), and two were punishing outcomes (Yokota et al., 2019; Spüler and Niethammer, 2015). This pattern limits conclusions about correspondence with laboratory components, but does not establish their absence during gameplay. It also raises the question of whether conventional distinctions between response-related errors and externally presented feedback adequately describe performance monitoring during continuous game control.

The motor-control literature offers a useful framework for interpreting this pattern. Most reviewed paradigms required navigation and/or targeting (20 of 26), making the precision and timing of ongoing movements central to performance. Such demands resemble tracking, aiming, and throwing tasks, whereas traditional laboratory studies of the ERN and FRN have often used discrete choices and outcomes in speeded-response, gambling, time-estimation, and learning tasks (Gehring et al., 2012; Sambrook and Goslin, 2015; Faßbender et al., 2023). During continuous control, errors can arise from action selection, movement execution, or failure to achieve the task goal (Krigolson and Holroyd, 2006, 2007a). Information about performance accumulates through efferent, proprioceptive, and visual signals, so an impending miss or collision may be apparent before the game registers the outcome. Consequently, a single erroneous response may be difficult to identify, and the internal and external information conventionally associated with the ERN and FRN may overlap (Krigolson, 2018; Maurer et al., 2021). One proposed distinction separates movement errors, or deviations from intended movement parameters, from action-effect errors, or failures to achieve the action goal. For action-effect errors, this account distinguishes predictive error valuation before feedback from postdictive valuation after feedback, associating these with the ERN and FRN, respectively (Maurer et al., 2021). This framework offers a possible interpretation of negativities preceding punishing game outcomes (Yokota et al., 2019; Martínez-Tejada et al., 2021; Spüler and Niethammer, 2015), which may resemble responses to predictable errors in tracking and throwing tasks (Krigolson and Holroyd, 2007b; Maurer et al., 2022).

Likewise, activity time-locked to game-induced perturbations can be related to motor-control studies that introduce unexpected deviations during ongoing movement (Krigolson and Holroyd, 2006, 2007a; Krigolson et al., 2008; Anguera et al., 2009). Motor-control findings also identify two sources of variability relevant to gameplay: error-related activity scales with graded error magnitude (Anguera et al., 2009; Frömer et al., 2016; Maurer et al., 2022), and learning can shift error valuation toward prediction, with increasing ERN and decreasing FRN amplitudes (Maurer et al., 2022). Differences in error magnitude and stage of practice may therefore contribute to variation across game studies, even when events share the same label.

The context and consequences of game outcomes offer another possible explanation for differences from traditional laboratory findings. Feedback-locked activity can reflect overlapping contributions from reward prediction errors, surprise, and salience (Ullsperger, 2024), whose relative contributions may differ across tasks. During gameplay, an outcome may be predictable from the preceding action yet remain consequential because it changes the player's situation or prospects for subsequent play. A missed shot, for example, may leave the player exposed to attack. Such consequences could alter attentional and affective responses beyond those elicited by an abstract gain or loss, although the reviewed studies did not isolate this influence from differences in task structure. This possibility is consistent with the larger and more persistent positivity following collisions in Flappy Bird (Pei et al., 2025) and with the finding that crashes in Escape from Asteroid Axon did not resemble gambling losses in cross-paradigm classification (Cavanagh and Castellanos, 2016). The localization of an ERN following missed shots in an immersive shooting game to the rostral anterior cingulate cortex also differed from its more usual dorsal localization (Arake et al., 2022 , cf. Ullsperger et al., 2014a), but does not by itself establish an effect of game context. These findings suggest that describing events as "errors"

or "negative feedback" is insufficient to establish their functional equivalence across game and laboratory paradigms. Whether differences arise from continuous control, the consequences of outcomes, or their combination remains unresolved.

Movement-related activity

Movement-related findings provided more direct evidence of convergence, although only a few studies examined them. In the clearest controlled comparison, the RP, LRP, and sensorimotor ERD/ERS associated with deliberate actions did not differ significantly between a meaningful game and a structurally matched control game with visually similar but abstract stimuli (Verbaarschot et al., 2019). This suggests that adding meaningful goals and consequences did not measurably alter the examined signatures under those task conditions. Both this paradigm and a VR slingshot game permitted self-paced responses and elicited an RP (Center et al., 2026).

The later onset reported in the slingshot game may partly reflect a baseline window overlapping the period of earlier preparatory activity, rather than a difference in preparation itself. Lateralized negativities consistent with an LRP also preceded key presses in Space Fortress, where navigation demands and shot-spacing rules shaped response timing (Anderson et al., 2020). Sensorimotor alpha and beta ERD/ERS were broadly consistent with traditional laboratory patterns (Pfurtscheller and Berghold, 1989; Pfurtscheller et al., 1996; Pfurtscheller and Lopes da Silva, 2017) in both studies that examined them (Verbaarschot et al., 2019; Mariman et al., 2023).

However, practice-related changes in the thumbstick-control study may partly reflect changes in movement duration (Mariman et al., 2023). The available evidence therefore supports the occurrence of established movement-related signatures during gameplay, while highlighting the need to distinguish effects of game context from differences in response timing and movement execution.

Early sensory components

For early sensory components, the evidence was too sparse to assess convergence with traditional laboratory findings. Only isolated studies reported P1 or N1 effects (Petras et al., 2016; Wimmer et al., 2024). Their limited coverage may reflect the reviewed studies' emphasis on later components, as well as difficulties in isolating early sensory responses during gameplay. Early visual components are sensitive to luminance, contrast, and other physical stimulus properties (Luck, 2014), which may vary continuously within a game. Manual event annotation (Shin et al., 2012) and low game refresh rates (Maclin et al., 2011) may further limit the temporal precision needed to resolve these responses. These constraints offer possible explanations for the limited evidence, but do not demonstrate that early sensory components are absent or fundamentally different during gameplay. The review therefore provides a stronger basis for evaluating later, endogenous responses than early sensory processing.

Event-related spectral findings

Spectral findings showed selective correspondences with traditional laboratory findings, but substantial differences in tasks and analyses limited comparisons across studies. Theta modulations, mostly at frontal sites, distinguished successful from failed go responses (Sanuki et al., 2022), increased with training in proportion to performance gains (Qiao and Iramina, 2026), differed between goal-relevant and irrelevant objects (Weidemann et al., 2009), and scaled with trial-wise prediction errors (Eguinoa et al., 2026). These findings are broadly compatible with accounts of frontal midline theta as a signal of performance-relevant information (Cavanagh et al., 2012), although they do not establish a common mechanism across the paradigms. Feedbackrelated spectral activity corresponded less consistently to laboratory associations with outcome valence. Delta and beta power increased after both rewarding and punishing events, while the frontal midline theta enhancement often associated with unfavorable outcomes was not consistently observed (Salminen and Ravaja, 2007; Cavanagh and Castellanos, 2016). Delta increases also coincided or covaried with P3 responses to the same events (Maclin et al., 2011; Li et al., 2025), leaving open whether they provided distinct evidence about outcome processing. Beta modulations are likewise difficult to interpret specifically as reward-related activity because laboratory research also links beta to movement-outcome evaluation and maintenance of the current motor state (Glazer et al., 2018; Luft, 2014). Post-movement beta synchronization (Pfurtscheller et al., 1996) may extend into outcome-locked windows when outcomes closely follow corrective movements (see Salminen and Ravaja, 2007). Other alpha and beta effects were interpreted in terms of attention, arousal, or workload (Salminen and Ravaja, 2007; Chang et al., 2022; Bernal et al., 2024), but sensory-modality differences (Chang et al., 2022), unreported event rarity (Bernal et al., 2024), and concurrent motor activity (Salminen and Ravaja, 2007) limited the specificity of these interpretations. Occipital theta and gamma increases were each reported in single studies (Salminen and Ravaja, 2008; McMahan et al., 2015), while hemispheric alpha asymmetries were examined only in commercial games and interpreted in affective or attentional terms (Salminen and Ravaja, 2007, 2008; Rebert et al., 1984; Shin et al., 2012). Some asymmetry measures covered intervals of several seconds, providing only coarse temporal information about outcome processing (Shin et al., 2012; Rebert et al., 1984). The spectral evidence therefore supports some functional parallels with laboratory findings, but rarely isolates whether a modulation reflects outcome evaluation, sensory processing, or the motor demands accompanying the event.

4.3. Methodological challenges

Four recurring methodological challenges emerged across the reviewed studies: establishing precise event markers, controlling low-level stimulus properties, separating temporally adjacent events, and accounting for event probabilities and outcome magnitude. The following discussion considers how these challenges affect the interpretation of event-related EEG during gameplay and, with examples from the reviewed studies, how they may be addressed.

Event marking and timing precision

Precise event marking requires distinguishing when an event occurs in the game engine, when it becomes perceptually available, and when it is recorded relative to the EEG. Access to these timestamps depends partly on the choice of game. Studies using commercial titles marked events manually during gameplay (Shin et al., 2012) or retrospectively from video frames (Yokota et al., 2019; Salminen and Ravaja, 2008; Li et al., 2025). Frame-based annotation can identify visible changes, but its precision depends on recording frame rate, synchronization with the EEG, and the consistency of event identification. Li et al. (2025) addressed several of these issues by using two independent raters, correcting for a measured screen-mirroring delay, quantifying inter-rater consistency, and releasing gameplay recordings and the annotation protocol. Custom-built games facilitate direct event logging, but engine timestamps still require validation against the actual presentation of stimuli, including display delays and refresh timing. Recording both actions and their outcomes is particularly valuable when interpreting activity that precedes an outcome, because its timing relative to the preceding movement may distinguish preparatory activity from outcome anticipation. Events that emerge gradually require an explicit operational definition in addition to an accurate timestamp. Weidemann et al. (2009), for example, defined onset by the display size at which an object was reliably being approached. This provides a reproducible marker, but it need not coincide with perceptual detection, categorization, or response initiation.

Event-marking procedures should therefore report both their temporal uncertainty and the stage of the unfolding event that the marker represents.

Control of low-level stimulus properties

Precise event timing alone does not establish what an event-related response reflects. Attributing an ERP difference to an event's psychological significance also requires accounting for differences in its physical properties. Luminance, contrast, size, and sensory modality can influence ERP responses independently of the cognitive process under investigation, particularly at early sensory latencies but also at later stages (Luck, 2014). In video games, these properties may vary systematically between event classes because events occur within changing scenes and their appearance is tied to gameplay. Go and no-go stimuli may differ visually (Petras et al., 2016), while player perspective and presentation modality may introduce differences in target size, visual complexity, or cross-modal predictability (Petras et al., 2016; Chang et al., 2022).

An event that interrupts gameplay may also elicit a response to the accompanying visual interruption. Spüler and Niethammer (2015) addressed this problem by delaying the game pause until one second after a collision. These examples illustrate the need to distinguish the event of interest from sensory changes that accompany it. Where possible, stimulus properties should be matched across conditions or varied independently of the psychological contrast. Gameplay can support this separation by assigning different significance to the same stimulus through changes in the player's goals or role, as illustrated in Section 4.1.

Even when physical differences between event classes are controlled, the response elicited by each class may combine sensory and cognitive activity. Two studies argued against a purely visual explanation because the event involved no abrupt change in visual input (Pei et al., 2025) or did not stand out from the visually rich gameplay surrounding it (Salminen and Ravaja, 2008). These observations help characterize the eliciting event, but do not by themselves isolate the processing of its meaning. Conversely, visual effects such as a screen flash when the player is hit can introduce a sensory response that coincides with outcome processing (Shin et al., 2012). Interpreting event-locked activity therefore requires documenting the sensory changes accompanying an event and, where feasible, using matched control conditions to distinguish their contribution from that of the event's task relevance.

Temporal proximity of events

A further challenge is separating the response to an event from activity elicited by neighboring events. Closely spaced game events can produce overlapping EEG responses, making activity attributed to one event partly reflect preceding or subsequent events. This problem is especially relevant when actions and outcomes follow one another rapidly or when collectibles are placed near hazards, allowing rewarding and punishing events to occur in close succession. Increasing event spacing can reduce overlap, but may also alter the gameplay under investigation. Several reviewed studies incorporated temporal separation into the game mechanics: stimuli appeared at controlled intervals during otherwise continuous play (Sanuki et al., 2022; Qiao and Iramina, 2026; Maclin et al., 2011), recharge delays or shot-spacing rules shaped the timing of actions (Arake et al., 2022; Maclin et al., 2011; Anderson et al., 2020), and bounded action epochs introduced a trial-like structure (Verbaarschot et al., 2019; Petras et al., 2016; Center et al., 2026; Eguinoa et al., 2026). Such constraints should be evaluated against the time course of the responses of interest, since a minimum interval between actions does not necessarily prevent their neural responses from overlapping. Other studies excluded overlapping epochs (Martínez-Tejada et al., 2021; Arake et al., 2022; Mariman et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025) or retained only the first event within a specified window (Cavanagh and Castellanos, 2016). These approaches reduce the available data and may preferentially retain periods of less eventful gameplay. Where temporal separation would undermine the intended task, deconvolution methods offer an alternative for estimating overlapping responses, provided that the relevant events are recorded and the design supports their separation (Ehinger and Dimigen, 2019).

Context-determined event probabilities and outcome magnitude

Event probability and outcome magnitude also influence many canonical EEG components.

In video games, these quantities may depend on the unfolding situation and the player's skill rather than on an experimentally fixed schedule (e.g., Arake et al., 2022). Differences attributed to event type or outcome valence may therefore also reflect differences in rarity, predictability, vestigation where the task permits, with interval distributions reported and overlap correction considered when temporal separation is impractical (Ehinger and Dimigen, 2019). For continuously controlled games, design and analysis should additionally account for graded movement errors and outcomes, drawing on the motor-control ERP literature rather than relying exclusively on categories developed for discrete choices. Finally, functional interpretations should be supported by evidence beyond response morphology, for example by relating single-trial activity to model-derived quantities (Eguinoa et al., 2026), component amplitudes to behavioral performance (Arake et al., 2022), or neural measures to performance on independently administered tasks (Mathewson et al., 2012), and by comparing game-elicited responses with those from matched control or laboratory tasks (Verbaarschot et al., 2019; Cavanagh and Castellanos, 2016; Pei et al., 2025).

4.4. Limitations

Several limitations of the present review should be acknowledged. First, our search strategy may have missed eligible studies that did not describe their paradigms using terms such as "video game," "computer game," or "VR game." Our exclusion of studies focusing primarily on emotion or multi-feature classification may have omitted relevant individual findings, and the exclusion of BCI-related titles may have eliminated studies that use BCI terminology to describe their EEG systems or applicability of their findings without having actually used neural input for game control. We attempted to mitigate the latter through careful screening at the abstract and full-text stages, which led to the inclusion of two studies despite BCI-related terminology. The wider BCI literature, which frequently employs game-like environments, may nevertheless contain further findings relevant to cognitive event-related EEG research that our criteria excluded. Second, determining whether a paradigm qualifies as a video game is inherently difficult, given the broad range of possible definitions (Arjoranta, 2019; Stenros, 2017).

Our operational definition, based on that proposed by Allen et al. (2024), emphasized intuitive interaction and engagement as the features most relevant for advancing ecological validity in psychological research, but alternative criteria could lead to different inclusion decisions. Third, as is appropriate for a scoping review, we did not conduct a formal quality assessment of the included studies or systematically evaluate whether their analysis methods aligned with current standards. The synthesis should therefore not be read as implying that the included studies provide equally strong evidence. Moreover, to relate the findings to the extensive existing literature, our categorization of events and components relied on conventional definitions and time-locking conventions, for example response-locked measurement for the ERN. In several cases, this categorization diverged from the authors' original interpretations and may not fully capture the range of neural signatures the authors intended to highlight. As argued in Section 4.2, this taxonomy is itself of limited applicability to continuously controlled paradigms. The organization of our findings is therefore best understood as a framework for relating game-elicited activity to canonical paradigms and effects. Finally, most of the patterns reported here rest on few and unevenly distributed studies, often on a single one, and should be read as descriptive observations rather than as tested claims. 4.5. Potential future research The current state of event-related EEG research with video games suggests several potential directions for further investigation. Building on the methodological challenges and recommendations discussed in Section 4.3, future work could examine how gameplay affects the measurement and interpretation of neural responses, consolidate findings that currently rest on few studies, and explore neurocognitive questions that have received limited attention in the video game EEG literature to date. We consider these possibilities in turn.

Methodological research

One potential direction concerns the role of temporal structure in relating game-elicited responses to established laboratory effects. Several studies reported close correspondences in games organized into bounded, trial-like epochs (Verbaarschot et al., 2019; Yokota and Naruse, 2021; Eguinoa et al., 2026). Others embedded discrete events within continuous gameplay using controlled stimulus intervals or shot-timing rules (Sanuki et al., 2022; Qiao and Iramina, 2026; Maclin et al., 2011; Anderson et al., 2020; Arake et al., 2022). These findings leave open how much temporal separation is needed to identify the responses of interest and how imposing such separation changes gameplay. Comparisons of different temporal arrangements could help clarify when event spacing is beneficial and when analytical approaches to overlapping responses might offer a suitable alternative (Ehinger and Dimigen, 2019).

Another direction concerns the criteria used to identify and interpret game-elicited responses.

Where responses differ from their laboratory counterparts, it remains unclear when they should retain an established component label and when a new label might be warranted, as proposed for the P300-CE (Pei et al., 2025). Further work could examine how morphological similarities are supported by direct comparisons with matched control or laboratory tasks (Verbaarschot et al., 2019; Cavanagh and Castellanos, 2016; Pei et al., 2025), relationships with model-derived quantities (Eguinoa et al., 2026), behavioral performance (Arake et al., 2022), or performance on independently administered tasks (Mathewson et al., 2012). For continuous gameplay, it may also be useful to examine whether conventional distinctions between stimuli, responses, errors, and feedback adequately describe the unfolding activity, drawing on distinctions developed in motor-control research (Maurer et al., 2021).

Consolidating current findings

Many findings in the reviewed literature rest on individual studies, leaving their reproducibility and generalizability across games uncertain. Replication within the same paradigm and comparisons across games could help establish which response properties persist across implementations. For P3-like positivities, this includes both the reported sensitivities to event probability, magnitude, and player relevance (Anderson et al., 2020 (Anderson et al., , 2024;; Fincham et al., 2026) and the similarities and differences observed in comparisons with laboratory responses (Maclin et al., 2011; Cavanagh and Castellanos, 2016; Pei et al., 2025). Further matched comparisons could likewise establish how broadly the preservation of movement-preparation signatures observed in Free Wally extends to other games and action demands (Verbaarschot et al., 2019).

Targeted studies could also clarify findings that were infrequent or identified only tentatively.

These include FRN-and RewP-like responses to game outcomes (Yokota and Naruse, 2021; Anderson et al., 2024; Cavanagh and Castellanos, 2016) and error-related activity during responses, perturbations, and punishing outcomes (Arake et al., 2022; Mariman et al., 2023; Spüler and Niethammer, 2015; Wimmer et al., 2024; Yokota et al., 2019). Examining these responses under comparable task conditions could help distinguish inconsistent effects from differences in event definition, gameplay demands, or the studies' original objectives. The same consideration applies to spectral findings, for which similarities in frequency band do not necessarily imply similarities in function. Replicating reported relationships with performance, learning, or prediction errors could provide firmer evidence about which functional associations generalize across game paradigms (Sanuki et al., 2022; Qiao and Iramina, 2026; Eguinoa et al., 2026).

Areas for further exploration

Continuous gameplay may provide opportunities to examine how performance monitoring unfolds across an action rather than only at a discrete response or outcome. Negativities preceding punishing outcomes suggest that relevant information can become available before the game registers failure (Yokota et al., 2019; Martínez-Tejada et al., 2021; Spüler and Niethammer, 2015).

Building on motor-control research (Maurer et al., 2021 (Maurer et al., , 2022)), future studies could examine how predictions develop as a movement unfolds, how they change with practice, and how neural responses to the eventual outcome depend on the information already available. This could help explain how anticipatory and outcome-related processes contribute jointly to the monitoring of continuous goal-directed behavior.

A further opportunity concerns the significance that events acquire through a player's goals and role. Existing studies illustrate this through destination-dependent target relevance in a taxi-driving game (Weidemann et al., 2009) and different responses to events on a shared display in two-player games (Yokota et al., 2019; Fincham et al., 2026). More systematic manipulations could examine how neural responses change when a physically identical event becomes relevant to one's own action, a teammate's performance, or a shared goal. Such work could clarify how event-related activity reflects the relation between an event and the ongoing task, beyond its sensory properties. Together, these directions could help establish where video game paradigms provide a useful connection between the experimental control of traditional laboratory tasks and the continuous, consequential, and socially situated behavior studied outside the laboratory.

Conclusion

Video games offer new opportunities to study the neural correlates of cognition during continuous, goal-directed behavior. The current state of the literature suggests that game-elicited EEG responses show selective convergence with established laboratory effects, particularly for P3-like positivities sensitive to event probability, outcome magnitude, and player relevance, as well as for movement-related activity. Clearly, the interpretation of these responses requires attention to methodological challenges such as precise event marking, overlapping neural activity, and context-dependent event properties. These challenges notwithstanding, we believe that, compared with traditional cognitive event-related EEG research using highly simplified and abstract paradigms, combining video game play with event-related EEG research holds strong potential for elucidating the neural correlates of cognition in more complex and interactive settings with ever-increasing ecological validity.

The N400 is a negative deflection beginning approximately 200-250 ms and peaking around 400 ms post-stimulus, with a broad centroparietal maximum and, for written words, a slight right-hemisphere bias (Kutas and Hillyard, 1980; Kutas and Federmeier, 2011). It was first reported for semantically incongruent sentence-final words (e.g., He spread the warm bread with socks) and is typically quantified as the incongruent-minus-congruent difference, termed the N400 effect (Kutas and Hillyard, 1980; Kutas and Federmeier, 2011). Semantic violations only represent the extreme of a graded continuum: every word elicits some N400 activity, and its amplitude decreases as the word becomes more expected in its context, whereas its latency remains largely stable (Kutas and Federmeier, 2011). Although first characterized for words, N400 effects generalize to meaningful nonlinguistic stimuli, including line drawings, pictures, and visual scenes (Nigam et al., 1992; Ganis et al., 1996; Ganis and Kutas, 2003; Võ and Wolfe, 2013), visual narratives (Cohn and Kutas, 2015), and actions, gestures, and arithmetic problems (Kutas and Federmeier, 2011). The N400 is therefore thought to reflect the ease of accessing meaning from semantic memory in a given context, rather than language processing or violation detection per se (Kutas and Federmeier, 2011). This broader sensitivity to meaningful stimuli provides context for interpretations involving violations of environmental expectations, but does not establish that every expectancy-related negativity is an N400.

Alpha and beta ERD/ERS In the time-frequency domain, stimulus-induced responses are commonly quantified as event-related desynchronization (ERD) and event-related synchronization (ERS), describing phasic changes in oscillatory power associated with internal or external events, conventionally considered in terms of non-phase-locked activity (Pfurtscheller and Lopes da Silva, 1999). More generally, event-related power estimates may include both phase-locked and non-phase-locked contributions, depending on the analysis. Spectral measures should therefore not be assumed to isolate activity independent of the ERP. ERD denotes a power decrease relative to a pre-event baseline, also referred to as blocking of a given rhythm, while ERS denotes a power increase. Both phenomena are frequency-band-specific and characterized by a localized scalp topography. Alpha ERD (8-13 Hz) is elicited across a broad range of task types, is topographically widespread, and is broadly interpreted as an electrophysiological correlate of activated cortical areas engaged in sensory or cognitive processing, with magnitude scaling with task demand (Pfurtscheller and Lopes da Silva, 1999, 2017). In oddball paradigms, alpha ERD to target stimuli co-occurs with the P3 over similar scalp regions and exhibits comparable task sensitivity, suggesting that the two measures reflect associated or overlapping processes (Yordanova et al., 2001; Polich, 2007). Anticipatory alpha ERD may further be modality-specific, with occipitally maximal ERD preceding visual stimuli and temporally maximal ERD preceding auditory stimuli (Pfurtscheller and Lopes da Silva, 2017). Beta-band oscillations (13-30 Hz) have traditionally been studied in the context of sensorimotor tasks, where contralateral beta ERD over the motor cortex precedes voluntary movement and is followed by a post-movement beta ERS (see movement-related ERD/ERS discussed below; Pfurtscheller and Lopes da Silva, 1999). Beyond their sensorimotor role, beta oscillations have increasingly been implicated in a range of cognitive functions, including working memory, top-down processing, and decision making (Spitzer and Haegens, 2017).

Response-locked EEG components

RP and LRP Two of the most commonly studied response-locked ERPs are the readiness potential (RP) and the lateralized readiness potential (LRP) observed preceding voluntary movements. The RP is characterized by a slowly increasing negative potential starting approximately 2 s before the onset of voluntary movement, recorded over central scalp sites and originating from central motor regions including pre-SMA, SMA proper, premotor cortex, and primary motor cortex (M1) (Libet et al., 1983; Shibasaki and Hallett, 2006). The RP can be divided into two distinct phases: an early RP component beginning approximately 2 s pre-movement with bilateral distribution, and a late RP starting approximately 400 ms pre-movement that becomes maximal over the contralateral M1 opposite the moving hand (Shibasaki and Hallett, 2006).

The LRP is derived from differences between C3 and C4 activity calculated separately for leftand right-hand responses. Its sign depends on the subtraction convention. It typically emerges approximately 200 ms before movement onset (Trevena and Miller, 2002; Smulders and Miller, 2012).

Movement-related ERD/ERS Voluntary movement is associated with ERD in the upper alpha band (10-12 Hz) and lower beta band (13-20 Hz), most prominently over sensorimotor areas, with ERD beginning before movement onset (Pfurtscheller and Aranibar, 1979; Pfurtscheller and Berghold, 1989; Pfurtscheller and Lopes da Silva, 2017). Pre-movement alpha ERD begins with contralateral dominance and becomes bilateral shortly before movement execution, remaining almost symmetric during movement before recovering slowly over several seconds post-movement. This pattern is assumed to reflect general readiness or presetting of neurons in motor and sensory areas required for movement execution (Pfurtscheller and Berghold, 1989; Pfurtscheller and Neuper, 2003). Beta ERD during execution is followed by rapid recovery and a characteristic short rebound (post-movement beta ERS ) within one second, with strict somatotopic organization and area-specific frequencies (e.g., hand area around 16-20 Hz and midcentral foot area around 20-24 Hz), suggested to reflect the shift of motor neurons from an activated to a deactivated state after movement termination or somatosensory processing (Pfurtscheller et al., 1996 (Pfurtscheller et al., , 1998;; Pfurtscheller and Neuper, 2003; Pfurtscheller and Lopes da Silva, 2017; Cassim et al., 2001).

ERN, CRN and Pe

The error-related negativity (ERN; also termed error negativity, Ne) is a frontocentral negative deflection that emerges around the onset of an erroneous response and peaks approximately 50-100 ms post-response in speeded choice tasks such as flanker and choice reaction time tasks, with a maximum over midline frontocentral electrodes (Falkenstein et al., 1991; Gehring et al., 1993 Gehring et al., , 2012)). It is quantified in response-locked averages, either as the errortrial waveform relative to correct trials or as an error-minus-correct difference wave, and has been observed across sensory modalities (visual, auditory, and tactile) and response effectors (manual, foot, oculomotor, and vocal) (Gehring et al., 2012). In conventional discrete-response tasks, the ERN emerges before external feedback about response accuracy and is thought to reflect error detection based on internal information about the executed response (Gehring et al., 1993; Holroyd and Coles, 2002). The distinction between internal and external information is less clear during continuous motor control, as discussed in the motor-domain subsection below. A smaller negativity with similar latency and scalp distribution, the correct-response negativity (CRN), is sometimes also observed after correct responses, although whether the ERN and CRN reflect the same underlying process remains debated (Vidal et al., 2000; Gehring et al., 2018). The ERN is typically followed by the error positivity (Pe), a broad positive deflection beginning approximately 150-200 ms after the error and extending into the 200-500 ms range, usually with a centroparietal maximum, although its scalp distribution varies considerably across studies (Falkenstein et al., 2000; Overbeek et al., 2005). The Pe, particularly its later subcomponent (400-600 ms), is associated with conscious error awareness, whereas the ERN occurs irrespective of whether errors are consciously perceived (Nieuwenhuis et al., 2001; Endrass et al., 2007). Together, the ERN and Pe are thought to reflect performance-monitoring processes that support behavioral adaptation (Ullsperger et al., 2014).

Feedback-locked EEG

FRN, RewP and Feedback-related P3 Feedback-related electrophysiological responses are commonly characterized by an early frontocentral component peaking approximately 200-300 ms after feedback onset. Historically, this component has been termed the feedback-related negativity (FRN; Miltner et al., 1997; Gehring and Willoughby, 2002), a negative deflection that is typically larger for unfavorable (punishing or unrewarding) than for favorable outcomes in gambling, time-estimation, and probabilistic learning tasks. More recent work suggests that this apparent negativity may instead reflect a common N2-like response on which a reward-related positivity, the reward positivity (RewP; Holroyd et al., 2008; Foti et al., 2011; Proudfit, 2015), is superimposed. The RewP is elicited by rewarding outcomes and is reduced or absent following non-rewards, giving rise to the apparent FRN difference between outcome types. Whether the FRN and RewP reflect distinct components or alternative descriptions of the same process remains debated, and studies differ in how they quantify and label them. One approach refers to the negative waveform following unfavorable outcomes as the FRN and to the positive waveform following favorable outcomes as the RewP. Another computes difference waves between outcome types, in which subtracting rewards from non-rewards yields a negative deflection (FRN) and the reverse contrast yields a positive deflection (RewP) (Proudfit, 2015; Krigolson, 2018). FRN/RewP amplitude is sensitive to outcome probability and magnitude, with larger responses reported for unexpected or lower-probability outcomes and for outcomes of higher magnitude (Sambrook and Goslin, 2015; Paul et al., 2025). These effects should be interpreted in relation to the task and the way the component is quantified, rather than as invariant properties.

The FRN/RewP is typically followed by a feedback-related P3, more specifically the P3b, which peaks approximately 300-600 ms after feedback onset with a centroparietal scalp distribution (Polich, 2007; Walentowska et al., 2016; Paul et al., 2025). Its amplitude has been linked to outcome evaluation and is often larger for rewards than for non-rewards, for unexpected or lowprobability outcomes, and for outcomes of higher magnitude (Hajcak et al., 2005; Glazer et al., 2018; Stewardson and Sambrook, 2020; Paul et al., 2025).

Relationship between ERN and FRN

The ERN and FRN share a frontocentral scalp distribution, and source-localization and neuroimaging evidence points to a common generator in the medial frontal cortex, most notably the anterior cingulate cortex (Miltner et al., 1997; Holroyd and Coles, 2002; Holroyd et al., 2004; Gehring et al., 2012; Walsh and Anderson, 2012) but they differ in latency and time-locking convention. According to the reinforcement learning theory of Holroyd and Coles (2002), both components reflect a reward prediction error signal, conveyed by the midbrain dopamine system to the anterior cingulate cortex, indicating that an outcome is worse than expected (see also Nieuwenhuis et al., 2004; Walsh and Anderson, 2012).

The two components are thought to differ primarily in the information on which this evaluation is based and, consequently, in the event to which they are time-locked. The ERN (time-locked to action or response) emerges after movement and prior to external feedback and reflects an evaluation based on internal information about the executed response, whereas the FRN (timelocked to external outcome feedback) reflects an evaluation based on external feedback (Holroyd and Coles, 2002; Walsh and Anderson, 2012; Krigolson, 2018). Some studies indicate that their relative contribution depends on how much information about the outcome is available before feedback. The ERN is larger when the response determines the outcome, and the FRN when it does not (Holroyd and Coles, 2002; Walsh and Anderson, 2012). As stimulus-response mappings are learned, the error signal can accordingly shift from the feedback to the response. Frontal midline theta activity, which accompanies both components, has been proposed as a common mid-frontal mechanism signaling the need for control across these and related events (Cavanagh et al., 2012).

Feedback-related time-frequency components Feedback processing is also accompanied by modulations of oscillatory activity (for reviews, see Luft, 2014; Glazer et al., 2018). One commonly reported modulation is an increase in frontal midline theta power (4-8 Hz) approximately 200-500 ms after unfavorable outcomes or negative performance feedback, maximal over Fz and FCz (Cohen et al., 2007; Marco-Pallares et al., 2008; Cavanagh et al., 2010; Van De Vijver et al., 2011). Frontal midline theta is thought to originate in or near the anterior cingulate cortex and to contribute to the FRN. It has been linked to performance monitoring and reinforcement learning, as it scales with reward prediction errors and predicts behavioral adjustment and individual differences in learning (Cavanagh et al., 2010; Van De Vijver et al., 2011; Luft et al., 2013). Its sensitivity, however, is not restricted to negative outcomes. Because frontal midline theta has been found to be also modulated by novelty, conflict, and errors alike, it has been proposed to reflect a domain-general mechanism that signals the need for cognitive control (Cavanagh et al., 2012; Cavanagh and Frank, 2014). Beta activity (13-30 Hz) has been reported to increase after positive feedback and desynchronize after negative feedback over frontal and central sites, within approximately 200-600 ms after feedback onset (Cohen et al., 2007; Marco-Pallares et al., 2008; Van De Vijver et al., 2011; Luft et al., 2013). Two forms of feedback-related beta have been distinguished (Luft, 2014). One is a frontal increase in the beta and low-gamma range after rewards, particularly unexpected ones (Marco-Pallares et al., 2008; HajiHosseini et al., 2012). The other is a sensorimotor beta response to performance feedback in motor tasks, which increases after correct feedback and decreases after errors (Van De Vijver et al., 2011; Luft et al., 2013). Feedback-related beta has accordingly been interpreted both as a reward or learning signal (Marco-Pallarés et al., 2015; Luft, 2014) and as a signal for maintaining or adjusting the current motor state (Engel and Fries, 2010). Owing to its sensorimotor topography, the second form also overlaps with the movement-related beta ERD/ERS described above.

Feedback-locked delta power (below approximately 4 Hz), maximal over parietal sites approximately 100-500 ms after feedback onset, has been reported to be larger after gains than after losses (Bernat et al., 2015; Cavanagh, 2015). Time-frequency decompositions indicate that it contributes predominantly to the feedback-related P3, and in part to the RewP (Bernat et al., 2015).

Event-locked alpha modulations have received comparatively little attention in feedback paradigms. Where reported, alpha decreases are commonly interpreted, as in other contexts, as reflecting cortical activation and attentional engagement (see Alpha and beta ERD/ERS above, Glazer et al., 2018).

Error and feedback processing in the motor domain

The ERN and FRN were originally characterized in tasks in which responses are discrete button presses and errors result from selecting an incorrect response. A smaller but growing number of studies investigates error and feedback processing in more complex motor tasks, such as tracking, aiming, or throwing (e.g., Krigolson and Holroyd, 2006, 2007a,b; Krigolson et al., 2008; Anguera et al., 2009; Vocat et al., 2011; Torrecillos et al., 2014; Maurer et al., 2022). In such tasks, success depends not only on selecting the correct action, but also on how a movement is executed, so that errors can arise during both response selection and response execution (Maurer et al., 2021; Faßbender et al., 2023). Moreover, information about performance accrues continuously during the movement from efferent, proprioceptive, and visual sources, and the terminal outcome may occur only after the movement has ended, as when a thrown ball reaches its target. Internal and external error information can therefore overlap in time, which renders the classical distinction between the ERN and FRN by information source ambiguous (Maurer et al., 2021). Krigolson and Holroyd (2006, 2007a) proposed a hierarchical account of error processing in motor control. This account distinguishes low-level errors, or movement errors, that is, discrepancies between intended and actual movement parameters that can still be corrected during the movement, from high-level errors, that is, failures to achieve the task goal. In a continuous tracking task, highlevel errors elicited a frontocentral ERN-like negativity (Krigolson and Holroyd, 2006), and cues signaling that a tracking error had become unavoidable elicited this negativity before the error itself occurred (Krigolson and Holroyd, 2007b). In contrast, target displacements during aiming movements elicited posterior N1 and P3 components but no ERN, unless corrective movements were blocked so that the target could no longer be reached (Krigolson and Holroyd, 2007a).

Building on this account, Maurer et al. (2021) proposed to distinguish elicited components by the timing of error evaluation relative to terminal outcome feedback. These outcome errors concern failure to achieve the action goal and correspond to the action-effect errors discussed in the main text, as distinct from deviations in movement parameters. In this framework, the ERN reflects the prediction of an outcome error based on any efferent or sensory information available before the outcome is known, whereas the FRN reflects the postdiction of the outcome error once terminal outcome feedback has been presented. Because sensory information about the movement requires additional processing time, ERN-like negativities in such tasks tend to peak later than in speeded choice tasks, for instance, approximately 200-350 ms after ball release in a semi-virtual throwing task (Maurer et al., 2022). In the same task, the ERN increased and the FRN decreased over practice, suggesting that error processing shifts from postdictive to predictive evaluation as an internal model of the task is acquired (Maurer et al., 2022). Meta-analytic evidence further indicates that the FRN is larger in amplitude, shorter in latency, and more variable in latency in motor than in cognitive tasks (Faßbender et al., 2023). The larger amplitude may reflect the higher complexity and feedback ambiguity of motor tasks. The shorter latency may reflect the use of sensory information, such as the visible trajectory of a thrown object, as an early predictor of the final outcome (Faßbender et al., 2023; Maurer et al., 2022). Finally, outcomes in motor tasks are often graded rather than binary, and both ERN and feedback-locked components scale with error or performance magnitude (Anguera et al., 2009; Frömer et al., 2016). In the throwing task, this scaling was present for the FRN from the beginning of practice but emerged for the ERN only after practice (Maurer et al., 2022).

S2. Narrative short summaries

Studies are presented in the order of discussion in the Results section and Tables 1-3 of the main text. Studies that reported EEG activity time-locked to more than one event type are described in each corresponding section, together with the findings relevant to that event type.

Stimulus-related events

(1) Stimulus events conveying goal-relevant information Weidemann et al. (2009) employed a taxi-driving game to investigate event-related spectral changes during object recognition in spatial search. Participants navigated through 3D virtual towns with 6 × 6 grid layouts from a first-person perspective. First, participants had to search for passengers and subsequently deliver them to specific stores. The same stores served either as targets during store search, non-targets (irrelevant stores during store search), or neutral stores during passenger search. The stores' functional categorization was thus based solely on the task context rather than on their sensory features. Because stores emerged continuously as participants moved toward them, precluding precise temporal demarcation of their initial appearance, the authors operationalized stimulus onset as the moment at which stores subtended 0.35% of the display area. At this threshold, target stores were being directly approached in 95% of trials. All store stimuli elicited widespread power decreases in lower frequency bands relative to baseline (delta: 2-4 Hz, theta: 4-8 Hz, and alpha: 8-16 Hz). Target stores were accompanied by a greater frontal theta decrease compared to both neutral and non-target stores, with smaller differences between target and non-target conditions. Notably, all stimulus-condition differences were confined to frontal regions. However, it should be noted that the operational definition of the store stimulus onset likely placed the onset events after the perceptual detection and categorization of the store stimuli by the participants, and perhaps even after the participants' motor response initiation. This potentially complicates the attribution of the observed effects to specific cognitive processes. Chang et al. (2022) focused on alpha-band ERD/ERS (8-13 Hz) in participants interacting with virtual agents in a VR decision-making task, time-locked to when these agents started speaking. The authors compared EEG spectral changes across three forms of multimodal agent communication: embodied (speech and gaze), gestural (speech, gaze, and pointing), and voiceonly (baseline). Participants were seated in a virtual house with bare walls, facing two transparent planes, with the task of ranking 15 desert survival items by dragging them from the left transparent plane to numbered placeholders on the right transparent plane. The participants could make up to 10 help requests, asking the virtual agent for assistance by saying "help". For the embodied and gestural conditions, the virtual agent was visible behind the transparent planes, while in the voice-only condition, the agent was invisible. Alpha ERD/ERS, calculated as the change in spectral power within the 1-second interval after the virtual agent started speaking relative to the 1-second interval before speech onset, varied across the virtual agents' communication forms. Specifically, embodied agents produced alpha power increases in both parietal and occipital regions. Gestural agents produced alpha power decreases in both parietal and occipital regions, while voice-only agents induced alpha power decreases in parietal regions and alpha power increases in occipital regions. The authors related these differential responses to varying speech onset predictability and associated attentional demands across agent communication forms, with embodied agents presenting higher predictability due to the visible virtual body, while voice-only agents presented higher unexpectedness due to the lack of visual cues. Sudden gesturing in the gestural condition required additional attentional resources.

(2) Stimulus events prompting response selection Petras et al. (2016) examined ERPs during a shooting-themed go/no-go task presented in a desert environment with 180° active view control. Participants were instructed to shoot or withhold shooting at human-like avatars based on their appearance as soldiers (go targets) or civilians (no-go targets) under time pressure within a 1-second response window. Each avatar appearance constituted a single decision epoch with immediate visual and auditory feedback following go responses. The study identified several ERP components following avatar onset. An N1 component peaked at approximately 150 ms at Oz, a P2 component at around 200 ms at Fz, an N2 component at around 270 ms at Fz, and a P3 component at approximately 380 ms at Cz. The N2 component showed greater amplitudes for no-go targets compared to go targets, replicating the classical no-go N2 effect (Folstein and Van Petten, 2008), while the P2 and P3 components both exhibited larger amplitudes for go targets. Interestingly, the player's viewing perspective, which varied between an ego and a bird's-eye perspective, modulated several of these components. The first-person perspective produced larger posterior N1 amplitudes in the shooting condition only and larger anterior N2 amplitudes compared to the bird's-eye view. Additionally, the go/no-go amplitude difference in the anterior P2 was more pronounced in the first-person perspective than in the bird's-eye perspective. Sanuki et al. (2022) investigated ERPs and spectral power changes in response to go or no-go stimuli in a custom-built VR game set in a cartoon-like environment. Participants viewed their character from behind on a path bordered by trees, with stones serving as obstacles and circles serving as targets within the path. In addition, monsters with jewels appeared in front of the video game character. With the video game character continuously moving forward, participants had to navigate laterally to collect targets and avoid obstacles while simultaneously responding to color changes of the monster's jewel and the character's clothing. Responses were required when colors differed (go), and response inhibition was required when colors matched (no-go). The study further differentiated between successful and failed responses. ERP analyses time-locked to the color changes revealed that both successful and failed no-go trials, as well as successful go trials, elicited N2 and P3 components at Cz. In contrast, failed go trials were characterized by an attenuated N2 and a complete absence of the P3 component. In addition, frontal theta power preceding stimulus onset was higher in successful than in failed go trials. Qiao and Iramina (2026) extended the paradigm used by Sanuki et al. (2022) into a 10session training study, using the same custom game platform. EEG was recorded during the preand post-test multitask blocks, with analyses time-locked to the color-change onset of the colorchange go/no-go task. Participants were assigned to one of three gaze-strategy groups based on fixation-pattern clustering across the 10 training days: target-focused, distributed, and color-cuefocused. Post-versus pre-training changes differed by gaze-strategy group. The target-focused and color-cue-focused groups both showed a larger no-go N2 (200-400 ms, Cz) after training, though the increase was markedly larger in the target-focused group than in the color-cue-focused group. The target-focused group additionally showed a larger no-go P3 (400-600 ms, Cz and Fz), while the color-cue-focused group instead showed a smaller go P3 (400-600 ms, Fz). Both groups also showed increased midline theta power (200-600 ms, Fz), with the color-cue-focused group additionally showing increased alpha power (Fz, Cz, Pz) and increased frontoparietal theta connectivity. The distributed group showed little change in any measure. Across participants, larger post-training increases in parietal no-go P3 (400-600 ms, Pz) and frontal theta power (200-600 ms, Fz) were associated with greater gains in performance on the color-discrimination task (CDT) during multitasking. Maclin et al. (2011) analyzed EEG time-courses following video game events in Space Fortress, a space-themed game custom-built for research that integrates multiple cognitive tasks (Donchin, 1989). Participants view a space battle in a hexagonal arena with plain graphics in a 2D display. Their task is to navigate a spaceship by applying thrusts in a frictionless space, destroying a hostile fortress in the center, avoiding damage, and identifying appearing mines as friends or foes. Mine appearances elicited a P3 component peaking around 500 ms at frontal and parietal sites, which tended to be larger at frontal sites. Mine onsets were additionally followed by a transient alpha (10-15 Hz) power increase between 300 and 700 ms, which was stronger after training. Unlike the P3 to fortress hits in the same study (see Feedback-related events below), the mine-onset P3 did not decrease with training and tended to increase. The authors attributed this to the changing letter mappings across blocks, which prevented automatization of mine identification. Participants additionally performed an auditory oddball task, both alone and concurrently with gameplay (see Feedback-related events below).

(3) Stimulus events signaling changes in the conditions of ongoing play Spüler and Niethammer (2015) employed a custom-built arcade-style game with simple abstract graphics in which participants controlled the cursor movement angle with a gamepad thumbstick to avoid collisions with blocks falling at a constant speed from the top of the screen. The cursor was controlled by the participants continuously throughout the task. The researchers examined neural responses to abrupt, system-initiated perturbations in cursor control, during which the cursor's movement direction was offset from the participant's thumbstick input by 45°, 90°, or 180° to the left or right for a duration of 2 s, thereby transiently modifying the response-outcome mapping. These perturbations occurred at randomized intervals of 5 to 8 seconds. EEG analysis revealed neural responses time-locked to perturbation onset that were maximal at frontocentral electrodes (FCz and Cz), with a positive peak at 229 ms, a negative peak at 287 ms, a positive peak at 367 ms, and a small negative peak at 461 ms. Wimmer et al. (2024) investigated neural responses to system perturbations in a VR gamelike flight simulation with continuous control, in which participants controlled a virtual glider moving at a constant speed by steering left, right, up, or down through ring-shaped targets. Perturbations were introduced in the form of either sudden target jumps, for which the target was relocated to the left or right, or glider rotations, for which the glider was rotated horizontally, thereby disrupting the participant's intended flight path. EEG analysis focused on low-frequency activity (1-10 Hz). Neural responses at frontocentral sites showed a similar sequence following both perturbation types, with slightly delayed latencies for glider rotations. A positive peak at 188 ms was followed by negative deflections (234 ms for target jumps, 250-266 ms for glider rotations), positive deflections (313 ms, 344-391 ms), and negative deflections (469 ms, 484-500 ms). Center et al. (2026) combined an immersive VR task with externally imposed, systemgenerated interruptions of gameplay. Participants played a VR slingshot arcade game, launching a ball at color-coded targets viewed in first-person via a head-mounted display. The main objective was to investigate neural signatures of presence, which was manipulated at the block level through embodiment (tracked first-person avatar vs. a stationary avatar controlled via handheld thumbsticks). Because the embodied condition additionally introduced natural hand-tracking and controller vibration feedback -factors we treat as exclusion criteria elsewhere in this review -we focus here on the non-embodied (standard-controller) condition. The main events of interest were breaks in presence (BIPs): on 20% of trials, the display froze for 1000 ms immediately after release, temporarily disrupting the game's sensorimotor flow. ERPs were time-locked to the release of the ball from the sling, which by design coincided with BIP onset. Because the BIP manipulation was both an externally imposed disruption and time-locked to participants' own self-initiated response (the release of the ball from the sling), we report the later components here and the readiness-potential findings in the response-related section. BIP trials elicited a more negative central N400 (380-580 ms) than normal trials, and a central P3b (300-380 ms) that was reduced for BIP relative to normal trials. The N2 did not differ between freeze and normal trials. Bernal et al. (2024) employed the game Tetris in a virtual reality (VR) environment to investigate spectral modulations associated with what the authors define as a "helper-piece" event, namely the appearance and descent of a special ball-shaped piece that, once placed, clears four rows and consequently reduces the overall difficulty of the game. The helper-piece was triggered once the Tetris stack attained 60% of the playing field height, while the participants only knew that the helper-piece would appear at an unspecified time during gameplay. The occurrence frequency of the helper-piece was not reported. Spectral analysis revealed broadband power increases before the clearing, with frontal alpha (8-13 Hz) power rising approximately 4 s and frontal and parietal beta (15-30 Hz) power rising about 3 s before the event. Following the helper-piece event, parietal alpha power decreased from 0 to 1 s, while parietal beta power increased between 1 and 3 s. A frontal theta power decrease was also observed 2 to 4 s following the event. The authors associated the pre-event spectral changes with increasing task difficulty before the helper-piece event, and the post-event spectral changes with game state reassessment following the game's difficulty reduction.

Response-related events

(1) Preparation of self-paced actions Verbaarschot et al. (2019) examined the neural correlates of button presses in the custombuilt strategic decision-making game Free Wally, comparing them to those elicited by a control task that employed visually similar but abstract stimuli devoid of narrative context. In the Free Wally game, participants were required to make strategic decisions aimed at rescuing a whale by directing water jets either to the left or right in order to displace humanoid agents from a hill while simultaneously managing limited resources and game-level temporal constraints. Players chose the timing of individual button presses within these broader game-level constraints. Each decision epoch corresponded to one virtual day and concluded with an outcome presentation, thereby imposing a discrete trial structure onto the overarching game narrative. The control condition featured spontaneous button presses without meaningful goals or consequences. Although the principal objective of the study was to compare neural preparatory activity underlying deliberate versus spontaneous actions, the present review primarily focuses on how the incorporation of narrative context and a game-like framework influenced response-related EEG signatures. EEG analyses time-locked to self-paced button presses revealed typical movement preparation patterns for both tasks: RP as a slow buildup of negative potential maximal at Cz beginning 2000 ms before responses (Libet et al., 1983; Shibasaki and Hallett, 2006), an LRP emerging approximately 200-400 ms before the button press at C3/C4 (Trevena and Miller, 2002; Smulders and Miller, 2012), and central alpha/beta (8-30 Hz) ERD from 2 s before action onset, followed by ERS at 500 ms post-action (Pfurtscheller and Berghold, 1989; Pfurtscheller and Lopes da Silva, 2017). Crucially, the comparison of RP, LRP, and ERD between the two task conditions revealed no significant differences. The comparison therefore did not detect differences in the measured movement-preparation signatures attributable to narrative context and meaning. Center et al. (2026) examined EEG activity time-locked to participants' self-paced release of a ball from a slingshot in an immersive VR game, in which release could trigger an externally induced display freeze. Beyond the stimulus-related P3b and N400 effects reported in the stimulus-related section, the authors also examined response-locked activity preceding this release. Although not a planned component of interest, the authors reported a slow negative buildup beginning approximately 400 ms before ball release and maximal over central electrode sites, consistent with an RP.

(2) Action selection and execution during ongoing visuomotor control Mariman et al. (2023) used a custom-built 2D visuomotor game with abstract graphics that required participants to navigate a circular cursor through obstacles while simultaneously shooting at targets. Gamepad controls were perturbed with a 90° rotation for movement and elastic resistance for shooting, introducing unintuitive controls to which participants had to adapt. Participants trained the two control components either together (whole practice) or separately (part practice). Performance improved with training as participants adapted to the modified motor requirements. Response onsets were defined as the displacement of the left or right gamepad thumbstick for navigation or shooting responses, respectively. In their analyses, the authors included only events without temporal overlap with other events occurring within 500 ms before and 300 ms after event onset. Spectral analyses revealed beta-band ERD (12-30 Hz) around the onset of navigation responses and ERS following shooting responses, most clearly in the part-practice group. However, because the study primarily investigated training-induced modifications, the exact temporal characteristics and spatial distribution of these fundamental patterns were not delineated. Practice-related ERD/ERS changes may also partly reflect changes in thumbstickdisplacement duration, as navigation movements were shorter on the first day than in the final session. Anderson et al. (2020) analyzed ERPs time-locked to participants' responses in Autoturn, a simplified variant of the Space Fortress game (see Maclin et al., 2011 above). In this variant, the spaceship automatically targets the fortress in the center. As in the standard variant of Space Fortress, participants were required to navigate the spaceship between two hexagons by applying thrust, destroying the fortress, and avoiding the spaceship's destruction from fortress shells or hexagon collisions. Although players decided when to press, thrust responses were largely shaped by the spatial demands of navigation, such as avoiding the hexagon borders, and fire responses carried an additional pacing demand: Fortress destruction required participants to build fortress vulnerability through 11 shots spaced at a minimum of 250 ms intervals, followed by one further shot within 250 ms. Importantly, participants used the left hand for pressing the thrust button and the right hand for pressing the fire button. Consistent with the contralateral localization of the LRP (Trevena and Miller, 2002; Smulders and Miller, 2012), the study found greater rightlateralized central negativity preceding thrust button presses and slightly greater left-lateralized frontocentral negativity preceding fire button presses. Cavanagh and Castellanos (2016) examined response-locked ERPs in a custom arcade-style video game called Escape from Asteroid Axon. In this game, players navigate a starfighter through a continuous environment, collect stars and ammunition boxes while avoiding collisions with enemies or walls, and fire missiles at enemies. Of the six distinct video game events examined by the authors, one was response-related (missile launches), while the remaining five were feedback-related and will be discussed below. Missile launch button presses elicited a frontocentral negativity in the delta band, with an exact range not specified, that peaked around the button press. Reset events resulted in a strong central negativity at 300 ms post-event, followed by a stronger central positivity at 500 ms. The authors classified the post-event positive deflections associated with kill, death, and reset events as instances of the P3 response. The amplitude of these P3 responses systematically varied as a function of event probability, such that the most frequent events (kills) elicited the smallest P3 amplitudes, whereas the least frequent events (deaths) elicited the largest amplitudes. P3 latency also differed between events, peaking earliest after kills (approximately 300 ms) and latest after resets (approximately 500 ms). S1. Canonical event-related EEG components

This section provides a brief overview of ERPs and event-related EEG time-frequency modulations commonly observed in laboratory paradigms, with a selective focus on those relevant to the present review. Consistent with the organization of the Results section of the main text, the components are categorized according to whether they are typically time-locked to stimulusrelated, response-related, or feedback-related events. These categories describe conventional time-locking rather than exclusive component-event relationships. A final subsection considers error and feedback processing in the motor domain. Latency, polarity, and scalp distribution provide descriptive characteristics, while functional interpretation also depends on the task, experimental contrasts, and observed sensitivities.

Stimulus-locked EEG components

P3 The P3 is a broad, positive deflection occurring approximately 250-600 ms post-stimulus, observed across a wide range of laboratory paradigms, including oddball tasks, visual search arrays, flanker tasks, and cued attention paradigms (Polich, 2007). Two subcomponents are typically distinguished: the earlier P3a (approximately 250-280 ms, frontocentral maximum), elicited by novel or unexpected stimuli regardless of task relevance, and thought to reflect stimulus-driven attentional orienting, and the later P3b (approximately 300-500 ms, centroparietal maximum), evoked by task-relevant stimuli (Ranganath and Rainer, 2003; Polich, 2007 Polich, , 2012)). In oddball paradigms, in which infrequent targets are embedded among frequent standards, P3b amplitude varies inversely with target probability. The cognitive process underlying P3b remains debated, with proposed accounts including cognitive processing, memory storage, context updating, decision processes, and stimulus-response link reactivation (Verleger, 2020). In dual-task designs, P3 amplitude is associated with attention allocation: when participants divide attention between concurrent tasks, the amplitude for a given stimulus decreases relative to single-task conditions, reflecting reduced attentional resources available for its processing (e.g., Isreal et al., 1980; Kramer et al., 1985; Luck and Kappenman, 2011; Polich, 2007). Reducing primary-task demands may therefore leave more resources available for a concurrent task, motivating a prediction of reciprocal P3 changes rather than guaranteeing that pattern.

N2

The N2 is a negative deflection peaking approximately 200-350 ms post-stimulus, with frontocentral and posterior scalp distributions depending on the paradigm and modality. Two broad classes of anterior N2 have been distinguished (Folstein and Van Petten, 2008). One class is associated with cognitive control and is elicited in trials involving competing or withheld motor responses, as in go/no-go and flanker tasks (Donkers and van Boxtel, 2004; Nieuwenhuis et al., 2003). The other class is associated with mismatch detection and is elicited when a stimulus deviates from an established perceptual template, such as a novel or deviant stimulus embedded among a sequence of repeated standards (Folstein and Van Petten, 2008). In both contexts, the N2 frequently co-occurs with the P3, particularly in oddball paradigms (Folstein and Van Petten, 2008; Polich, 2007).
