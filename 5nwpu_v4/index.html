How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

The human face affords a remarkable array of complex social cues, yet our understanding of how it is spontaneously perceived relies almost exclusively on controlled, posed stimuli that suppress the natural variation which makes faces so informative. Here, using a data-driven similarity task with 75 naturalistic face images, we mapped 182,500 perceptual judgments across 365 adults aged 20 to 76. The results indicated that naturalistic face space was hierarchically organized by expressive and demographic cues: smiling faces formed a coherent category that transcended gender and age, whereas non-smiling adult faces stratified along apparent gender. Representational similarity analysis showed that affective dimensions (valence, intensity, energy), emotion category, demographic cues (age and gender), and image-based color each uniquely predicted similarity judgments, while specific personality traits and facial action unit intensity did not. These results demonstrate that highly variable naturalistic faces carry potent combinations of both categorical and dimensional information, advancing a more ecologically valid framework for understanding real-world social perception.

Multidimensional Representations of Naturalistic Faces

Making sense of the faces we see is an extraordinary feat. From a deceptively simple configuration of facial attributes, even the faces of strangers convey vast quantities of information, such as age, gender, identity, physiological state, personality traits, emotional appeals, and conversational intent (Buckland & Taubert, 2025; Cowen & Keltner, 2020; Holler, 2025; Leung et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2022; Scarantino et al., 2022; Sutherland & Young, 2022; White & Burton, 2022). To extract this multidimensional information, the brain is thought to compare incoming facial attributes against stored mental representations (Valentine et al., 2016). To isolate specific mechanisms, previous research has traditionally relied on tightly controlled face stimuli, varying attributes along isolated dimensions, such as identity, expression, gaze or face shape, while holding all other facial attributes constant. While such studies have provided foundational insights into how faces are categorized (Bruce et al., 1987; Bruce & Young, 1986; Porcheron et al., 2013; Rossion, 2002; Taubert et al., 2016), recognized (Taubert & Alais, 2011; Towler et al., 2023; White & Burton, 2022), elicit social inferences (Barrett et al., 2019; Fujimura et al., 2012; Russell, 1980), and guide predictions (Chanes et al., 2018), they necessarily restrict sampling to a curated subset of representational space. Consequently, these paradigms may not fully account for the variable and flexible feature integration characteristic of facial information 'in the wild'.

Further, traditional methods for eliciting behavioral judgments typically assess performance using categorization or recognition tasks, which make it difficult to elicit behavioral judgments without biasing responses toward predefined dimensions. For example, facial emotion recognition paradigms often rely on prototypical emotion categories (happiness, anger, fear, disgust, sadness, and surprise) represented by distinct configurations of facial action units (AUs) (Ekman & Friesen, 1978). Stimuli are typically controlled for these activations and paired with corresponding labels to assess behavior in laboratory settings. While highly valuable for establishing baseline mechanisms, this approach necessarily leaves aside non-prototypical AU configurations and constrains the diversity of descriptive MULTIDIMENSIONAL REPRESENTATIONS OF NATURALISTIC FACES labels available to participants. Restricting response options in this way has been shown to influence performance, by potentially inflating consensus (Adams & Weisbuch, 2026; Nelson & Russell, 2013), limiting observations to predefined categories (Betz et al., 2019; Lindquist & Gendron, 2013), and/or privileging singular dimensions over latent, complex, or contradictory social cues (Matsumoto & Wilson, 2026). Because natural faces reflect multiple interacting dimensions, constrained approaches risk inadvertently reinforcing the theoretical commitments they intend to test (Peluso et al., 2025).

Consequently, less is known about how mental representations of naturalistic faces are organized when facial signals retain information traditionally treated as noise.

More recently, data-driven methods have emerged for assessing perceptual face similarity in an unbiased way, making it possible to use larger and more complex stimulus sets which seek to explain face perception under more ecologically valid conditions. Here, our goal was to use one such method to characterize the multidimensional representations underlying a set of "uncontrolled" faces without imposing a facial attribute of interest. To this end, we quantified perceptual similarity using a triplet odd-one-out task previously used to understand latent dimensions in objects (Grootswagers et al., 2024; Hebart et al., 2020) with the Wild Faces Database (Peluso, Long et al., 2023), a diverse collection of ambient images displaying a range of heterogeneous facial gestures. The triplet odd-one-out task presents three stimuli simultaneously, with the participant choosing the one that is most different. No further instructions are provided to avoid biasing attention to any singular dimension, allowing the task to reveal similarities which might otherwise remain latent without presupposing or labeling the attributes of interest in advance.

In order to characterize the multidimensional space revealed by this paradigm, we separately collected behavioral ratings for 75 Wild Face images, consisting of 70 human faces (35 male, 31 female, four ambiguous) and five illusory faces (pareidolia), which we retained given evidence that perceiving face-ness in objects influences the perception of social cues such as valence and gender (Saurels et al., MULTIDIMENSIONAL REPRESENTATIONS OF NATURALISTIC FACES 2024; Taubert et al., 2023; Wardle et al., 2022). These 75 faces were independently rated using traditional emotion category labels (happiness, sadness, anger, fear, disgust, surprise, neutral), and along twelve other dimensions previously identified as important for social cognition (Adolphs et al., 2016; Baudouin et al., 2025; Bruce et al., 1987; Lin et al., 2025; Peluso, Long et al., 2023; Norton & Pettegrew, 1979; Porcheron et al., 2013; Todorov et al., 2011). We included categories perceived from relatively stable facial attributes (chronological age, gender, gaze direction), and dimensions derived from more variable facial features such as affective inferences (valence, intensity, energy), and social traits (attentiveness, attractiveness, familiarity, genuineness, likeability, and trustworthiness).

We then used Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA), Multidimensional Scaling (MDS), and hierarchical clustering to map this representational space. These allowed us to directly compare participants' similarity judgments against our socio-evaluative ratings, low-level image properties using Gabor-Jets, VGG Face, CIE L*a*b* color and luminance (Grootswagers et al., 2019; Parkhi et al., 2015; Yue et al., 2012), and computationally derived descriptions of facial activity (OpenFace 2.0; Baltrusaitis et al., 2018)). Crucially, we identify a remarkably robust geometry underlying naturalistic face similarity judgements, scaffolded by interpretable and flexible combinations of affective dimensions and social categories. The results provide an important and necessary step toward an ecologically informed model for describing how humans make sense of faces in the real world.

Materials and Methods

Participants

We recruited 365 healthy participants between 20 and 76 years of age (182 females, 1 nonbinary; M = 41.95, SD = 13.64) via Prolific (http://www.prolific.com/) to participate in an online experiment. To ensure we had enough power to examine whether representations would vary as a function of age, recruitment was streamed by nine age groups (20-24, 25-29, 30-34, 35-39 etc.) to ensure each group sampled a minimum of 40 participants containing an equitable distribution of gender. This number was MULTIDIMENSIONAL REPRESENTATIONS OF NATURALISTIC FACES derived through pilot testing to ensure there were at least 20 observations for each pairwise similarity comparison (see Supplementary Text 1). Participants were excluded if they were previously diagnosed with neurological conditions. Given the risk of non-human agents participating in online experiments, we manually screened participants for implausibly fast task completion (< 20 minutes), and none were excluded. Participants were primarily based in the UK, Canada, and the United States and were paid approximately $21 AUS (£11 GBP) for their participation, for which they provided informed consent. The study was approved by The University of Queensland's Human Research Ethics Committee (2024/HE000198).

Stimuli

Stimuli comprised 75 images (hereafter referred to as the WFD-75) selected from the Wild Faces Database using a computational Variational Interpretable Concept Embeddings (VICE) model (Muttenthaler et al., 2022). This model previously identified 15 dimensions underlying triplet odd-oneout judgments across 900 WFD images, excluding animal faces (Taubert et al., 2024). To ensure broad coverage of facial attribute space, the top five exemplars from each dimension were selected comprising 70 human faces (35 male, 31 female, four ambiguous) and five objects with face-like qualities (pareidolia). Images were 400 x 400 pixels and varied widely in head orientation, gaze direction, color profile, and image quality.

Procedure

Triplet Odd-One-Out Task

Visual similarity judgments were acquired remotely using a triplet odd-one-out task previously used to examine representational geometry in natural objects and human actions (Bockes et al., 2025; Grootswagers et al., 2024; Hebart et al., 2020). The task was programmed in jsPsych (de Leeuw, 2015) and hosted on Pavlovia (pavlovia.org). Each trial displayed a random combination of three faces arranged equidistant from each other from the center of the screen (Fig. 1). Participants were asked to MULTIDIMENSIONAL REPRESENTATIONS OF NATURALISTIC FACES select one image as the odd-one-out, as being the image they judged to be most different to the other two. No additional instructions were provided to avoid biasing to particular features, and each triplet remained visible until a choice was made by responding with a mouse click on the chosen image, initiating a 500 ms interval before the next trial. Breaks were provided every 201 trials, with participants completing 500 trials yielding a total of 182,500 similarity judgments across the entire experiment.

Socio-Evaluative Ratings Task

To characterize visual information in the WFD-75, independent ratings were collected from a separate sample of 75 undergraduates (age 17-25; 47 females, 27 males, 1 undisclosed) recruited via

The University of Queensland research participation scheme, who volunteered in exchange for course credit. Participants were asked to rate each image along dimensions of attentiveness, attractiveness, energy, familiarity, likeability, and trustworthiness using a continuous Likert sliding scale from 1 on the left to 9 on the right (for example, "How likeable/attractive/trustworthy is this face?" ;1 = Not at all,9 = Extremely), before advancing to the next image. Gaze ratings were obtained with the question "Which direction is the face gazing? (Report the direction from your perspective. If the eyes are closed, please guess)" with1 = To the left,5 = direct,9 = To the right. Age ratings were obtained using a continuous slider from 0 -100. Between 25 and 42 judgments were collected for each rating and the study was approved by The University of Queensland's Human Research Ethics Committee (2024/HE000198).

Additional ratings for gender, valence, emotion category, intensity, and genuineness were obtained from previous studies described elsewhere (Peluso, Long et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2026, see Supplementary Text 2).

Analysis

Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA)

Behavioral data were analyzed in MATLAB 2025b using the Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA) framework, a multivariate analysis used in previous tasks to characterize patterns of responses to MULTIDIMENSIONAL REPRESENTATIONS OF NATURALISTIC FACES visual stimuli (Kriegeskorte et al., 2008). Odd-one-out judgments were used to calculate dissimilarity between each pair of experimental stimuli (Fig. 1). For each pair of faces, similarity was computed as the proportion of trials where the pair appeared together and neither image was selected as the odd one out, divided by the total number of trials that included that pair. These similarity scores were then subtracted from 1 for conversion into dissimilarity scores, such that higher values indicated pairs were perceived as more different. Dissimilarity scores were calculated for all pairs of 75 stimuli, resulting in a 75 x 75 Representational Dissimilarity Matrix (RDM) comprising 2,775 unique pairs. We also used RSA to compute the split-half consensus of similarity judgments across participants by randomly dividing participants into two groups and correlating their RDMs across 1,000 bootstrap iterations. Using the same bootstrap method, we also assessed between-trial reliability by dividing trials into odd-and even-indexed halves, retaining the full N in each half. Correlations were corrected using the Spearman-Brown formula.

Model Representational Dissimilarity Matrices.

We built multiple model RDMs for perceived age, attentiveness, attractiveness, energy, familiarity, gaze, gender, genuineness, intensity, likeability, trustworthiness and valence by computing pairwise dissimilarity as the absolute difference between the two faces' mean ratings (using city-block distance, which for a single rating dimension is identical to Euclidean distance). The emotion category RDM was constructed using dissimilarity in the rated emotional profile of each face. First, for all 75 faces we constructed a 9 x 1 vector based on the proportion of raters who selected each emotion category label (anger, fear, disgust, sadness, neutral, happiness, surprise, other or unknown) for each face in a previous labeling task (Peluso, Long et al., 2023). Dissimilarity between each pair of faces was 1 -Pearson's r between their vectors.

Computational image-derived model RDMs were built using numerical vectors that described image luminance and color distribution (both using CIE L*a*b* color space), other image features extracted from Gabor-Jets (Lades et al., 1993) and VGG Face (Parkhi et al., 2015), and facial action unit MULTIDIMENSIONAL REPRESENTATIONS OF NATURALISTIC FACES intensity detection using OpenFace 2.0 (Baltrusaitis et al., 2018). These models allowed assessment of the low-level and facial movement features previously found to correlate with visual similarity. For exact model specifications see Supplementary Text 2.

Statistical Inference Using Multiple Regression on Distance Matrices

We used multiple regression on RDM distance matrices to estimate each candidate model's unique contribution to behavioral similarity with all other models held constant. The behavioral RDM for all participants was vectorized and regressed simultaneously onto the corresponding vectors from each of our candidate models as predictors, yielding standardized regression coefficients (β) reflecting each model's unique contribution after partialling out shared variance. One challenge with inference using RSA is that observations within an RDM are not independent. We therefore followed a stimulus-label randomisation approach (Kriegeskorte et al., 2008; Nili et al., 2014) and combined this exchangeability method with the Freedman-Lane procedure (Winkler et al., 2014), under which each predictor is tested against a null that retains the effects of the remaining predictors. Permutation p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate. Analysis was conducted using RStudio 2026.01.1+403.

Multidimensional Scaling (MDS)

We used Multidimensional Scaling (MDS) to map the underlying structure of the behavioral RDM, such that faces perceived as more similar are positioned closer to each other in space than faces perceived as more different (Kruskal & Wish, 1978). To assess the optimal number of dimensions, we computed MDS solutions from 2 -8 dimensions using Kruskal's Stress 1 formula to measure fit quality, achieving stress 0.077 by seven dimensions but with no clear elbow (see Supplementary Fig. 1). Given improvement dropped to ~1.2% between seven and eight dimensions, we retained the sevendimensional (7D) solution as an optimal balance between preserving meaningful structure while retaining interpretability.

Hierarchical Clustering Analysis

We applied hierarchical clustering (average linkage) using the pairwise Euclidean distances derived from the 7D MDS embedding using MATLAB 2025b. Average linkage defines the similarity between two clusters as the average distance between all possible pairs of observations across the two groups. Given naturalistic face stimuli are more perceptually graded, this algorithm was chosen for its robustness to outliers and to preserve the geometric structure of the underlying pairwise distances. We evaluated the optimal number of clusters by computing mean silhouette width for solutions from k = 2 to 10, using Euclidean distances in the same seven-dimensional space in which the clustering was performed. Silhouette width was maximal for the trivial two-cluster solution (s̄ = .35), which separated the five pareidolia stimuli from the remaining faces, while in the range of human faces silhouette width rose from a minimum at k = 3 (s̄ = .20) to a maximum at k = 7 (s̄ = .29), with k = 8 closely comparable (s̄ = .28) before plateauing to k = 10 (s̄ = .28). Given the graded nature of the silhouette values, we also examined the merge heights of the dendrogram itself, where two face images high in similarity are located in close proximity and connected by vertical branches which are low in height. Excluding the human/pareidolia cluster split (gap = .185), the largest increases occurred at k = 4 (gap = .055) and k = 8 (gap = .044); no other solution exceeded .025. We therefore converged upon an eight-cluster solution which explained 66.59% of the total variance in the 7D MDS space, representing a robust clustering solution with strong overall separation. Clusters were profiled using the independent ratings data and OpenFace 2.0 using RStudio 2026.01.1+403.

Results

Naturalistic faces elicit robust consensus in similarity judgments across the adult lifespan

We quantified consensus among participants using split-half reliability and found similarity judgments were remarkably consistent both across participants (r = .968) and across trials (r = .969), Spearman-Brown corrected. Given the equitable distribution of female and male participants within the MULTIDIMENSIONAL REPRESENTATIONS OF NATURALISTIC FACES full sample (n = 182 each), we also quantified consensus within and between each gender group which we found to be similarly high (females: across-participants, r = .938 and across-trials, r = .946; males: across-participants, r = .936 and across-trials, r = .939). Similarly, the behavioral RDMs that were generated by male and female participants were highly correlated with each other (Spearman's rho r = .92, p < .001).

To investigate any potential age differences in similarity judgments, we also verified consensus by grouping data by participant age (20-24, 25-29, 30-34, 35-39, 40-44, 45-49, 50-54, 55-59, 60-64+) to construct nine separate behavioral RDMs. Each age RDM contained 40-43 participants, each covering 100% of 2,775 pairwise comparisons with a median count of 21-23 observations. Despite the relative sparsity in these RDMs compared to the full sample RDM (which contained a median count of 149-197 observations per pair), consensus remained strong with all age groups significantly correlated with each other, ranging between rs = .697 and .764, p < .001, FDR corrected.

Independent predictors of naturalistic face similarity

After establishing impressive agreement between participants as to which faces were more similar than others, we next sought to identify which of our socio-evaluative ratings and stimulusderived computational models might independently predict these judgments using multiple regression.

Given our task included illusory faces (i.e., pareidolia) which do not feature facial action units and may contain contrasting visual statistics to human faces (such as luminance and color), we conducted three separate analyses: the first included all 75 stimuli (70 human faces and 5 pareidolia) with 17 predictors, excluding OpenFace. The second introduced OpenFace and excluded six stimuli OpenFace could not process (five pareidolia stimuli and one black-and-white human face), yielding a 69-face dataset with the full 18-predictor set. Given Analyses One and Two differed in both stimulus set and predictor set, Analysis Three was fitted on the 69-face dataset with the same predictors as Analysis One to be able to compare the effect of stimulus removal separately to the inclusion of OpenFace. Overall model fit was MULTIDIMENSIONAL REPRESENTATIONS OF NATURALISTIC FACES significant in all three analyses (permutation p < .001; R² = .351, .371, and .369, respectively), corresponding to approximately 36-39% of the explainable variance given the noise ceiling (R² = .969 and .957 for 75 and 69 stimuli).

In Analysis One (75 stimuli, all predictors except OpenFace), eight predictors contributed independently to behavioral similarity after FDR correction: genuineness (β = .268), emotion category (β = .204), age (β = .158), valence (β = .156), color (β = .154), gender (β = .143), luminance (β = .100), and energy (β = .072; Fig. 2, orange lines). In Analysis Two (69 human faces, including OpenFace), the significant predictors were valence (β = .254), emotion category (β = .238), gender (β = .197), age (β = .163), intensity (β = .134), luminance (β = .120), energy (β = .066), and color (β = .064), whereas OpenFace was not significant (β = .052; Fig. 2, green lines). Analysis Three (69 human faces, all predictors except OpenFace) yielded the same eight significant predictors with comparable coefficients (Fig. 2, blue lines), and OpenFace's unique contribution was negligible (ΔR² = .002). The differences between Analysis One and the 69-face analyses therefore reflect the removal of pareidolia rather than the inclusion of OpenFace: genuineness no longer contributed (β = -.026), color was attenuated (β = .064), and intensity emerged (β = .135). This suggests that the genuineness and color effects in Analysis One may partly reflect the perceptual distinction between human and illusory faces. Together, these results indicate that naturalistic face similarity is predicted by a combination of expressive (valence, emotion category, intensity, energy), demographic (age, gender), and image-level (luminance, color) dimensions. By contrast, in the 69-face analyses, none of the remaining socio-evaluative ratings (trustworthiness, likeability, attractiveness, attentiveness, familiarity, gaze, genuineness) or computational models (OpenFace, Gabor-Jet, VGG) explained unique variance after FDR correction (see Supplementary Tables 2 3 4).

We then asked whether this emotion category heterogeneity extended to facial action unit (AU) detection profiles by calculating the coefficient of variation (CV = SD/M) from the 69-stimuli OpenFace AU intensity detections across cluster members (Fig. 9) as well as the proportion of cluster members with active AUs and the mean intensity for each AU by cluster (Supplementary Fig. 5 6). Discussion

In this study we combined an unbiased measure of perceived similarity, data-driven multivariate analyses, and independent perceptual and socio-evaluative explanatory models, to map the similarity across 75 naturalistic face images, allowing the stimuli to freely vary without specifying in advance which dimensions should matter. Three major findings emerged. First, we found impressive agreement in how observers spontaneously responded to highly variable naturalistic facial attributes across the adult lifespan, even without providing explicit instructions. Second, we found that models evaluating the expressive (valence, emotion category, intensity, energy), demographic (facial age, gender), and imagelevel (luminance, color) dimensions of naturalistic faces partially captured this representational geometry. Third, we found these dimensions were flexibly combined to structure the underlying multidimensionality of naturalistic face space; cues consistent with facial age and gender provided pronounced categorical structure, yet smiles were elevated to cut across these stable demographic MULTIDIMENSIONAL REPRESENTATIONS OF NATURALISTIC FACES cues. Together, these findings reveal a hierarchically organized yet context-sensitive geometry for incorporating multiple behaviorally relevant social cues in naturalistic faces, with important consequences for understanding the foundations underlying human face perception.

That observers spontaneously extracted multiplexed social categories and dimensions from uncontrolled naturalistic faces is consistent with a contextually sensitive process for integrating social affordances from stable and expressive facial attributes using more controlled stimuli (Baudouin et al., 2025; Fujimura et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2022; Mehu and Scherer 2015). Here, we extend this work to reveal that categories such as gender, age, and emotion were not uniformly defined across naturalistic face space but rather interacted differently depending on the presence or absence of salient expressive cues. Further, dimensions of intensity and energy were not orthogonal to bipolar valence but instead appeared dependent, consistent with a V-shaped relationship between valence and arousal-relevant information (Kuppens et al., 2013). Together, these findings challenge the assumption that increasing variability in facial appearance necessarily compromises the separability or reliability of categorical and dimensional information (Dawel et al., 2022; Peluso et al., 2025; Ritchie & Burton, 2017). Instead, we demonstrate that even when observers are given no instructions about which facial attributes to evaluate, naturalistic faces retain a multiplexed, yet highly consistent and interpretable perceptual structure (Sutherland et al., 2013). It follows that difficulties in interpreting naturalistic expressions may arise not simply from variability in the stimuli themselves, but from experimental approaches that require observers to reduce complex, multidimensional facial information to discrete, predefined categories (Barrett et al., 2019; Hoemann & Feldman Barrett, 2019; McVeigh et al., 2026).

Importantly, we also found that this multiplexing was not associated with similar coherence in facial AU activations, consistent with evidence that the same facial AUs facilitate multiple dimensional and categorical inferences (Mehu and Scherer, 2015; Liu et al., 2022). Indeed, we found the activations themselves were considerably heterogeneous, which may reflect measurement noise. For example, we MULTIDIMENSIONAL REPRESENTATIONS OF NATURALISTIC FACES found confident detections of mouth-specific AUs in faces with no visible mouth (e.g., hand-over-face stimuli), and noticeably lower detections for faces shown in profile, highlighting potential challenges for computational AU detection in generalising across occlusions and other sources of variation in ambient pictures of faces (Cross et al., 2023). Alternatively, this heterogeneity may reflect genuine differences in facial appearance that are captured by OpenFace but are less relevant to human judgments of perceptual similarity. This possibility raises an important distinction between the physical features detected by computational models and the information prioritised by human observers. Indeed, AU activations varied considerably even among faces judged to be highly similar (Supplementary Fig. X).

One exception was smiling faces, which exhibited relatively consistent activation of AU06 (cheek raiser) and AU12 (lip corner puller), suggesting that OpenFace can recover coherent facial configurations despite naturalistic variability. Moreover, the emergence of smiling faces as a distinct perceptual cluster, transcending differences in age and gender, suggests that smiles provide a particularly salient and consistent cue that observers spontaneously prioritise amid competing facial information (Peluso et al., 2025), rather than an artifact of experimentally constrained stimulus categories. In sum, these findings suggest that the relationship between physical facial configurations and perceptual similarity is not uniform with some expressions, such as smiles, exhibiting coherence at both levels, whereas other perceptually similar faces may be supported by more heterogeneous combinations of facial features.

Our approach is not without limitations; we make no claim that our stimulus set represents the full spectrum of naturalistic facial attributes, and a different set of ambient faces could produce a distinct representational topography. However, we intentionally chose a compact, highly diverse stimulus set in contrast to data-driven approaches operating at much larger, sparser scales (Bockes et al., 2025; Hebart et al., 2020). This design facilitates future fMRI and E/MEG investigation, enabling dense repeated sampling of the same images across different populations to isolate contextual effects and individual differences that remain understudied (Hehman et al., 2017; White & Burton, 2022).

Accordingly, future work should prioritize testing non-Western cohorts and scaling this paradigm to larger stimulus sets to determine how broadly this representational geometry generalizes.

While traditional approaches to face perception have often sought to minimise stimulus variability, our findings demonstrate that naturalistic faces can elicit remarkably consistent and reliable perceptual judgments, even across observers aged 20 to 76 years of age. These findings suggest that the challenge for understanding face perception is not simply to control variability, but to identify the perceptual regularities that emerge despite it. This distinction has important implications for both computational models and applied measures of social cognition. For example, incorporating the multidimensional structure of naturalistic face representations into computational models may help develop more robust and generalisable facial analysis tools, with potential implications for reducing bias in automated decision-making. Similarly, conventional clinical assessments of social cognition often rely on posed facial expressions and forced-choice categorisation tasks, which may not adequately capture how individuals interpret complex or ambiguous facial information in everyday life. Establishing perceptual benchmarks grounded in naturalistic face similarity judgments may therefore provide a more ecologically valid framework for evaluating individual differences in social cognition (Henry et al., 2023).

Taken together, we demonstrate that humans spontaneously and flexibly combine broad categories and fine-grained dimensions to evaluate naturalistic faces. By advancing a new methodological framework that accommodates variation in both facial information and observer behavior, this work moves beyond traditional stimulus rewilding (Dawel et al., 2025; Peluso, Long et al., 2023; Peluso et al., 2025) toward data-driven paradigms that allow the complex, flexible structure of face representations to guide more veridical models for describing how faces invite real-world meaning.
