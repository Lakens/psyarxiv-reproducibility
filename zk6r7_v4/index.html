Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p = .196 Trust in perception increased by 2.4 points, with a confidence interval spanning zero [-1.0, 5.9] and Holm-adjusted p = .196.
p = .13 The image-level rank association was positive but imprecise with only six stimuli (reported ρ = .70, p = .13).

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Introduction

A visitor may leave an exhibition feeling calmer, puzzled by an illusion, or newly attentive to the way a first impression forms. These responses matter to cultural leisure, but they are not interchangeable. Feeling relaxed does not demonstrate recovered attentional capacity; reporting a new idea does not establish enduring learning; and participating alongside others does not necessarily produce belonging. Leisure research offers resources for understanding this complexity, from accounts of experiences unfolding over time to analyses of meaning, embodiment, and the social conditions of participation (Stewart, 1998; Iwasaki, 2008; Packer & Ballantyne, 2016). The challenge for an exhibition evaluation is therefore not simply to establish that visitors enjoyed themselves. It is to specify which aspects of experience were supported, what evidence bears on those aspects, and what another provider could reasonably do with the findings.

Research already connects leisure engagement with subjective well-being and identifies multiple possible pathways between participation and health (Kuykendall et al., 2015; Fancourt et al., 2021). Educational leisure scholarship also demonstrates that enjoyment and discovery need not be competing purposes (Packer, 2006), while museum research identifies possibilities for restoration in cultural as well as natural environments (Packer & Bond, 2010). A further demonstration that a short cultural visit can be enjoyable or beneficial would, by itself, add little to these established arguments. A more useful contribution is to examine how particular arrangements of cultural leisure bring bodily experience, explanation, and reflection into relation, while distinguishing the experience a setting invites from the outcome an evaluation actually measures.

Art of Perception provides a case through which to examine that distinction. The free exhibition, presented within Creative Lab Festival 2026 in Pune, India, combined seven zones addressing breathing and heartbeat, movement, aesthetic interpretation, visual illusions, social judgment, neurodivergent artistic expression, and the surrounding botanical garden. Several installations made visitors' own sensing and judging part of the content: movement generated imagery, breathing was accompanied by physiological feedback, and individual judgments contributed to a visible distribution of votes. Rather than only communicating information about perception, the exhibition invited visitors to notice themselves perceiving.

We use embodied reflexivity to describe this design invitation: attending to how one senses, interprets, or judges while participating in an activity. The term identifies a relationship between experience and reflection, not a measured psychological trait or a claim that visitors necessarily became more self-aware. It brings the case into conversation with leisure scholarship in which bodily activity is a way of knowing, rather than merely the delivery mechanism for a cognitive message (Borovica, 2020). It also makes the evaluation question more specific: what happens when cultural leisure invites people both to undergo an experience and to consider how that experience is being made? This paper offers three connected contributions. Empirically, it describes a differentiated profile of immediate self-reported responses in an arts-science exhibition outside the predominantly Anglophone institutional settings represented by much of the literature discussed here. Conceptually, it connects embodied learning with restorative and reflective possibilities without treating them as identical outcomes. Methodologically and practically, it develops a designexperience-evidence matrix: an explicit separation of opportunities provided, responses documented, and explanations or longer-term benefits still to be tested. This is an interpretive tool derived from the case, not a validated causal model.

The analysis addresses three questions. What immediate changes were recorded in visitors' selfreported bodily state, beliefs about perception and first impressions, and connection to nature? What additional aspects of participation appear in written responses, installation votes, and workshop drawings, and where do these materials fail to corroborate the survey? Finally, how can those findings inform cultural leisure design and evaluation without assuming that free admission ensures inclusion or that participation establishes a health benefit? The argument concerns the organization and interpretation of situated leisure experiences, rather than a contrast between serious and casual leisure.

Conceptual background

Leisure experience: More than an activity category

Experience-centered leisure research directs attention away from an activity label alone and toward what happens before, during, and after participation. Stewart's (1998) multiphase account challenges the treatment of leisure as a single, stable event. Packer and Ballantyne's (2016) multifaceted model likewise recognizes that visitor experience encompasses several dimensions rather than a uniform response to a venue. These approaches make room for different experiences within the same visit and for differences between the experience anticipated, experienced, and subsequently remembered. Duerden et al. (2018) distinguish memorable, meaningful, and transformational experiences through their relationships with emotion, discovery, and change. The importance of this distinction here is evidential: an immediately salient encounter need not reorganize a visitor's life or retain its significance after departure. An exit survey can capture a present appraisal, but claims about transformation require evidence beyond that appraisal. The relevant question is therefore not whether an exhibition qualifies as an extraordinary experience for everyone, but which dimensions of an encounter are documented and on what timescale. This perspective also limits what design can claim. We use affordance in the modest sense of an opportunity for action or experience that becomes available in a particular setting. An opportunity to sit quietly is not the same as feeling free to do so; a prompt for reflection may be welcomed, ignored, or experienced as instruction. Embodied and situated routes to well-being

The DRAMMA framework identifies five proposed psychological domains linking leisure and subjective well-being: detachment-recovery, autonomy, mastery, meaning, and affiliation (Newman et al., 2014). It is useful for generating questions about experiences that might matter, but a setting designed with those possibilities in mind has not thereby demonstrated them. In the present study, bodily relaxation is relevant to recovery, yet does not measure detachment from demands; reporting an idea about perception is not equivalent to mastery; and access to a selfpaced route does not establish experienced autonomy. We use the framework as an interpretive reference, not as a measurement model tested by the six survey items.

A complementary multilevel account locates potential leisure-health pathways in psychological, biological, social, and behavioral processes operating beyond the individual alone (Fancourt et al., 2021). This matters because explanations centered exclusively on the visitor's brain can obscure the relationships and conditions through which participation becomes possible. The exhibition's scientific content is part of its design, not a reason to reduce leisure experience to a neural or physiological event.

Leisure scholarship on embodiment offers a more expansive starting point. Borovica's (2020) creative inquiry uses dance to explore bodily knowing in relation to objects and the meanings of womanhood. Fullagar (2008), examining women's experiences of recovery from depression, attends to emotion-work and emotion-play rather than construing leisure as a standardized corrective to an individual deficit. More recently, Borovica et al. (2026) show how creative and leisure practices can support self-discovery while remaining entangled with the difficulties and politics of living with mental distress. These studies involve different populations and methods from the present evaluation. Their relevance is conceptual: embodied experience is situated, relational, and not uniformly positive.

An arts-science encounter may consequently invite reflection without necessarily improving well-being, and may be valuable without resembling treatment. Fancourt et al. (2026) discuss common processes across arts-based leisure and psychological therapies in a conceptual Personal View. That comparison does not erase differences in intention, professional context, or therapeutic relationships. It provides a reason to investigate mechanisms carefully, not to label a public exhibition therapeutic on the basis of immediate self-report. Iwasaki's (2008) work on meaning-making in global contexts connects leisure-like pursuits with identity, cultural and social relationships, learning, and ways of responding to life's difficulties. This approach discourages treating meaning as an individual score detached from everyday circumstances. In a garden-based exhibition, connection to nature may likewise involve more than temporary relaxation. Vuong and Nguyen's (2024) conceptual discussion of the kingfisher considers how artistic, scientific, and remembered encounters can mediate human-nature relationships. Such accounts broaden the questions worth asking; they do not demonstrate ecological commitment or behavioral change in the present visitors.

Meaning, inclusion, and the conditions of participation

Access also extends beyond an admission price. The leisure-constraints literature distinguishes intrapersonal, interpersonal, and structural constraints on participation (Crawford et al., 1991). Removing a fee addresses one potential barrier, while questions remain about transport, time, language, care responsibilities, physical access, and whether the setting feels welcoming. We therefore treat free admission as a documented feature of provision, not a demonstrated equity outcome. Attendance data describe those recorded at the venue, not those who could not attend or chose not to do so.

Inclusion must also concern whose creativity and expertise are recognized. Darcy et al. (2022) foreground artistic impact and career development in disability arts, extending attention beyond benefits experienced by audiences. Their argument is pertinent to an exhibition displaying work by neurodivergent artists: those artists should not be reduced to instruments for producing visitors' empathy or well-being. In another context, MacRitchie et al.'s (2023) review of technology-supported arts activities with people living with cognitive impairment or dementia highlights the relevance of choice, personalization, and co-design. These are useful considerations for future provision here, not evidence that this exhibition was accessible to those populations or co-designed with them.

Participatory evidence and its limits

Creative methods can reveal aspects of leisure that standardized response options fail to express. Parry and Johnson (2007) argue for creative analytic practices capable of representing complexity in lived leisure experience. Schultz and Legg (2020) introduce a/r/tography as an arts-based methodology in which artistic, research, and teaching practices are interconnected. Both challenge the assumption that an artwork is useful to research only once its features have been converted into categories. Their relevance to this evaluation is also cautionary: soliciting and counting drawings does not, by itself, constitute a/r/tographic inquiry or an interpretive account of participants' lives.

There is a further distinction between participation in an activity and participation in governing research. Contributing a token or a written response need not entail involvement in deciding the questions, interpreting the material, or determining its future use. Rose et al.'s (2023) discussion of leisure and surveillance makes the politics of observation relevant to proposals for collecting data within everyday leisure. An exhibition that invites participation should therefore not assume that measurement is unobtrusive, ethically self-evident, or free of influence on the experience. These literatures inform a common analytical discipline: separate what the setting makes possible, what people report or produce, and what the researcher infers. The present evaluation uses several materials to describe complementary dimensions of participation. It does not assume that their coexistence establishes a common mechanism, or that combining them resolves their individual limitations.

Method

Design, setting, and scope

The study was a multi-method, single-arm observational evaluation of Art of Perception and selected surrounding festival activities. Quantitative surveys and participatory materials were collected during Creative Lab Festival 2026 and considered together at interpretation. The exhibition ran from March 30 to May 17, 2026, at the Rupa Rahul Bajaj Centre for Environment and Art within Empress Botanical Garden, Pune. Exhibition admission was free, with attendance estimated at approximately 3,800. The wider festival included an academic Unconference on March 27-29, experiential workshops, art weekends, a nature weekend, and a children's sensory summer camp.

The primary analytic focus is the exhibition survey. Workshop drawings provide complementary evidence from another part of the festival, not repeated observations of the same exhibition participants. Feedback from the academic Unconference is treated only as separate program context in the supplementary material. This distinction prevents experiences in differently organized activities from being combined into a single estimate of exhibition benefit. Ethics approval was reported under ARISA Foundation IRB protocol 061123-AF-R²-A2. Survey participation was voluntary and consented. The evaluation arose within the organizing institution, and most authors are affiliated with that institution. This organizational proximity offers knowledge of the setting but also makes favorable interpretation and socially desirable responses relevant concerns. The findings are therefore presented as an internally situated evaluation rather than an independent impact assessment.

The exhibition as a set of opportunities

The seven zones offered different modes of engagement (Table 1). Zone 1 displayed visitors' heart rate variability through ambient light and sound while inviting paced, exhale-lengthened breathing. This installation drew on the rationale for breathing-linked biofeedback, but no physiological outcomes are analyzed here (Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014). Zone 2 coupled movement to projected imagery. Zone 3 invited interpretation of one artwork through sensory features, affective responses, and personal or cultural meaning, using the labels "The Eye," "The Feeling," and "The Storyteller." These lenses drew on accounts of interacting systems in aesthetic experience (Chatterjee & Vartanian, 2014).

Zone 4 presented visual illusions. Zone 5 displayed six unfamiliar faces and invited a token beneath the one that appeared friendliest; accumulated votes were visible. Zone 6 presented work by neurodivergent artists in a quiet, unhurried space without a prescribed route or pace. Zone 7 directed attention outdoors to the botanical garden. Figure 1 illustrates selected installations. The evaluation did not link individual outcomes to time spent in particular zones, so the analyses concern the visit as a whole rather than the effect of any component.

Data sources and participants

The attendance register contained 3,673 analysable sign-in entries. These entries describe recorded attendance rather than a verified count of unique visitors. The survey contained 200 matched participant identifiers: 100 using an early questionnaire and 100 using a revised version introduced during the exhibition. Of these, 166 had complete responses on all six outcomes at both time points. Valid paired observations for individual outcomes ranged from 174 to 178.

Additional materials comprised 148 responses to "one thing I know about the brain" and 146 to "one thing I learnt about the brain"; 1,339 tokens in the face-judgment installation; a separate pilot in which 21 raters judged the beauty of the same six face images; and 65 drawings from seven workshops. The units are consequently different: survey participants, written responses, tokens, pilot raters, and drawings. They are not pooled into a total sample, and no person-level linkage across these sources is assumed.

Survey measures and analysis

Six single-item self-reports addressed bodily relaxation, understanding perception as a brainbody process, trust in perception, skepticism of first impressions, experiencing the world differently, and connection to nature. The items were written in accessible language for completion in the exhibition. The full pre-and post-test instruments are reproduced in the supplementary material (Section S5). Their wording was aligned with exhibition content, rather than drawn from a validated multidimensional measure of leisure experience or well-being. Consequently, the analysis concerns these reported responses, not a global well-being construct.

Scores were transformed to a 0-100 range, with higher values indicating more of the named outcome. For scales beginning at 1, the transformation subtracted the minimum possible score, divided by the possible range, and multiplied by 100. Three outcomes changed from three-point to five-point response scales in the revised questionnaire. These were rescaled within questionnaire version before pooling. This transformation provides a common numerical range; it does not establish equivalence between response formats or remove potential differences between the two recruitment periods. No values were imputed, and each outcome used its available valid pairs. For each outcome, the analysis estimated pre-and post-visit means, paired mean change, and a bootstrap 95% confidence interval using 20,000 resamples. Standardized paired mean changes (d) are also reported. Two-sided sign-flip permutation tests used 100,000 Monte Carlo resamples, with Holm adjustment across six outcomes. The validity of this inference depends on the relevant exchangeability or symmetry assumptions; bounded, ordinal-origin data do not automatically guarantee them. Paired t-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were included as sensitivity analyses.

Positive individual score changes are reported descriptively in the supplement. A positive change is not necessarily clinically or practically important, and increasing trust in perception is not intrinsically a desirable outcome. Accordingly, the six items are not interpreted as a validated benefit index, and an average across them is not the paper's substantive endpoint.

Written and visual materials

The written responses were classified using an exploratory, deductive dictionary covering eight content areas: neuroplasticity/change; brain-body-emotion links; perception/illusion/prediction; brain-art-creativity; nature connection; neurodiversity/inclusion; specific neuroanatomy; and metacognition/humility. Responses could contain more than one code. This was a rapid computational content analysis, not an inductive thematic analysis or an assessment of conceptual accuracy. We report frequencies for each prompt descriptively. The two prompt datasets differ in response totals and are not treated here as a verified matched sample. Moreover, the prompts elicit different kinds of statements, so their contrast is not interpreted as a paired learning-gain test.

The workshop feedback procedure supplied associations between colors and affective descriptors, and between shapes and experiential domains. Blue indicated calm/grounded, yellow energized/interested, red intensity/overwhelm, and grey disconnection/uncertainty. A square represented awareness/clarity, a triangle curiosity, a hexagon emotion, and a circle connection/community. Drawings were coded using this workshop-specific key. Counts therefore represent markings interpreted within a prompted response format, not universal meanings of colors or shapes. The materials lacked independent dual coding, and inter-rater reliability could not be estimated. As the drawings were collected only after workshops, they describe post-workshop representations rather than measured change.

Installation votes and integration

The separate pilot rated each face's beauty on a five-point scale. The exhibition vote totals and their percentages were compared descriptively with those ratings. The analysis also calculated a chi-square test against equal vote shares and a Spearman correlation across the six images; these are retained in the supplement with their limitations. The 1,339 tokens are not 1,339 independent observations of the beauty-friendliness relationship. That comparison has six image-level units, while the public vote display, fixed positions, and unknown repeat participation complicate interpretation of the token distribution.

Integration proceeded by identifying what each source could address and where its evidential reach ended. Table 3 distinguishes design opportunities, observations, and unsupported extensions. Shared subject matter across sources was considered complementarity rather than independent confirmation when populations, timing, or constructs differed. This approach retains useful contextual material without allowing agreement in broad theme to substitute for measurement of a common process.

Findings

Recorded attendance and survey coverage

The sign-in register recorded 62.1% of entries as female, 37.3% as male, and 0.6% as unknown or other. The largest age bands were 21-30 years (29.9%), 11-20 (23.1%), 31-40 (14.7%), and 41-50 (10.5%). These figures characterize the attendance records, not the demographic composition of the matched survey sample. They indicate recorded participation across age groups but do not establish representativeness, inclusion of particular underserved communities, or equal opportunity to attend.

There were 200 matched survey identifiers, but no individual outcome had complete data for all 200. The outcome-specific sample sizes in Table 2 are therefore the relevant denominators for the reported changes. The 166 complete cases are used only for the supplementary exploratory summaries across items.

Immediate self-reported responses

Five outcomes increased after Holm correction (Table 2; Figure 2). Bodily relaxation changed from 59.4 to 82.3 on the harmonized 0-100 scale, with a paired mean change of 22.9 points, 95% CI [18.1, 27.8], and reported d = 0.69. The remaining statistically significant increases were smaller: understanding perception as a brain-body process, 11.0 points [4.2, 18.0]; skepticism of first impressions, 5.1 [1.6, 8.6]; experiencing the world differently, 5.7 [2.0, 9.3]; and connection to nature, 6.5 [3.3, 9.8].

Trust in perception increased by 2.4 points, with a confidence interval spanning zero [-1.0, 5.9] and Holm-adjusted p = .196. The results therefore do not support an increase on every item. However, the difference between one significant result and one nonsignificant result does not establish that their changes differ statistically, and this pattern does not rule out demand characteristics.

Average change also should not be confused with the number of individuals showing a positive score difference. Bodily relaxation increased for 103 of 178 respondents (57.9%), whereas understanding perception as a brain-body process increased for 32 of 178 (18.0%). Several baseline means were already high. The response formats, initial scores, and distribution of changes therefore matter when interpreting these percentages. The data do not establish a threshold for a meaningful improvement, nor whether visitors retained any change after leaving.

Content of written responses

Specific neuroanatomy was the most frequent code in prior-knowledge responses, appearing in 33 of 148 (22.3%). Learning responses most often contained brain-art-creativity (44/146, 30.1%), perception/illusion/prediction (38/146, 26.0%), metacognition/humility (37/146, 25.3%), and neuroplasticity/change (36/146, 24.7%). Nature connection appeared in 13 learning responses (8.9%), and neurodiversity/inclusion in six (4.1%). Full frequencies are supplied in Supplementary Table S2.

These distributions identify content visitors chose to mention under the two prompts. They are consistent with the exhibition's emphasis on perception, artistic experience, and connections across domains. They do not show that an individual replaced an anatomical understanding with a more sophisticated one, that the statements were scientifically accurate, or that visitors acquired transferable metacognitive skills. In particular, a dictionary category labeled metacognition/humility is not a direct measure of either capacity.

Participation in the face installation

The six receptacles accumulated between 189 and 261 tokens. Image 1 received the largest share (19.5%) and Image 3 the smallest (14.1%); those images also occupied the highest and lowest positions in the separate beauty-rating pilot. The middle ordering did not correspond consistently with beauty ratings. The image-level rank association was positive but imprecise with only six stimuli (reported ρ = .70, p = .13).

The installation made a collective pattern of judgments visible, which was part of the participatory design. Its distribution does not establish why visitors chose particular faces. Although attractiveness-based trait judgments are a relevant background construct (Dion et al., 1972), alternative influences include facial expression, image placement, visible previous choices, and repeat voting. Nor can the tokens be linked to the survey's increase in skepticism of first impressions. The installation provides a context for reflection on judgment, not evidence that it reduced bias.

Post-workshop drawings and cross-source interpretation

Connection/community markings appeared in 55 of 65 drawings (84.6%), curiosity in 46 (70.8%), and awareness/clarity in 45 (69.2%). Calm/grounded and energized/interested markings co-occurred in 45 drawings (69.2%). Emotion markings appeared in 29 (44.6%), intensity/overwhelm in 18 (27.7%), and disconnection/uncertainty in seven (10.8%). Supplementary Table S4 provides the complete descriptive coding results and interval estimates. These representations included different and sometimes coexisting affective descriptors. Cooccurrence of calm and energy is not itself evidence of emotional regulation, and red or grey markings cannot be assigned a positive or negative personal meaning beyond the response key without participants' explanations. The drawings nevertheless indicate that a single overall satisfaction score would not exhaust what this particular feedback format recorded.

Across sources, the clearest evidence of temporal difference is the paired survey. The texts identify reported learning content, the votes describe aggregate participation, and the drawings characterize prompted post-workshop expression. Together they broaden the description of the festival, but they do not independently verify a single outcome or demonstrate the complete operation of a well-being framework. Discussion

The contribution: Relating experience design to evidence

The findings support a bounded but useful account of cultural leisure. Respondents reported immediate differences in bodily relaxation, selected beliefs and appraisals, and connection to nature after a free arts-science visit. Those results are compatible with restorative and reflective possibilities occurring within the same setting. They do not demonstrate that all visitors experienced both, that one produced the other, or that exhibition design caused either. The contribution is therefore not a general discovery that brief leisure can matter. It is a case-based way of connecting distinct opportunities for experience with the kinds of evidence needed to evaluate them.

The design-experience-evidence matrix addresses a practical interpretive problem. A provider may install biofeedback to support relaxation, provide open routes to support autonomy, or invite collective activity to support affiliation. These are reasonable intentions, but each requires a different evaluation. In this study, relaxation was directly queried, whereas experienced autonomy and affiliation were not measured in the exhibition survey. Keeping those distinctions visible enables readers to design a more informative evaluation rather than infer success from the presence of an installation or from a favorable overall response.

This approach complements experience-centered leisure scholarship by treating the visit as heterogeneous and open to interpretation, rather than as a uniform dose of an activity (Stewart, 1998; Packer & Ballantyne, 2016). It also brings experience design into a more cautious relationship with well-being frameworks. Mechanism theories identify questions worth investigating; they do not convert content-aligned single items into measurements of those mechanisms (Newman et al., 2014; Fancourt et al., 2021). The transferable outcome is an analytical discipline: specify the opportunity, measure the relevant experience, and reserve claims about mechanism or durability for designs that can support them.

Embodied reflexivity as an invitation, not an achieved outcome

The distinctive feature of this exhibition was the possibility of moving between undergoing an experience and noticing its construction. A visual illusion can be encountered as a puzzle and as a prompt to question immediate certainty. A movement-responsive projection can make bodily action noticeable without requiring a prior scientific vocabulary. Such arrangements connect the pleasurable discovery described in educational leisure with bodily ways of knowing explored in leisure scholarship (Packer, 2006; Bultitude & Sardo, 2012; Borovica, 2020).

The survey and written responses provide limited support for examining this relationship further: respondents' self-reported understanding of perception increased, and perception-related content was prominent among learning statements. They do not show the moment-to-moment process by which an experience became a reflection. Embodied reflexivity is consequently a useful account of what the installations invited and a proposition for subsequent research, not a new capacity demonstrated in participants. Observations of interaction, visitors' explanations in their own terms, and later accounts of remembered encounters would address that process more directly.

The case also illustrates why cognitive, restorative, and evaluative outcomes should remain distinguishable. Greater acknowledgment of perceptual construction need not require global distrust of perception. Equally, feeling relaxed does not entail becoming more skeptical of social impressions. The present pattern justifies retaining multiple outcomes; it does not establish a statistical dissociation between them. This distinction protects the study's substantive interest without overstating what its measurements resolve.

Implications for provision: Choice between stimulation, reflection, and pause

For exhibition and leisure providers, the immediate implication is to consider a repertoire of modes of engagement rather than a single prescribed emotional journey. Art of Perception combined stimulating displays, opportunities to examine an interpretation, a quiet gallery, and an outdoor setting. Its findings make relaxation and reflection reasonable priorities for future design tests, while the workshop drawings remind evaluators to allow for intensity and uncertainty. The case does not identify an optimal sequence or prove that quiet zones caused relaxation.

One testable design proposition is that optional transitions between stimulation and pause may support a wider range of visitor preferences than a uniformly immersive route. A second is that invitations to reflect after an embodied activity may make scientific content approachable without converting the visit into a lesson. Neither proposition requires every visitor to complete every activity or produce a learning outcome. Choice to observe, skip, stop, or simply enjoy an artwork should remain part of the provision rather than be interpreted automatically as failed engagement. This recommendation follows the distinction between an available opportunity and an experienced benefit; it is not a finding that autonomy increased. Table 4 translates the case into decisions, evidence boundaries, and next evaluations. For example, a provider considering a breathing installation should first distinguish the value of a place to pause from the added value of biofeedback technology. This evaluation cannot make that comparison, and therefore does not support a claim that the technology is necessary, inexpensive, or cost-effective. Similarly, the garden is part of the context in which connection to nature increased, not an isolated causal ingredient. Comparison with a garden-only visit would make the practical question more informative.

The proposed use of the results is thus neither to replicate seven installations as an evidencebased package nor to advertise improved mental health. It is to use the observed profile to choose a small number of explicit design questions, preserve visitors' freedom to engage differently, and collect measures capable of informing the next decision. Measures developed for museum wellbeing evaluation offer one starting point for improving outcome coverage, with further adaptation and testing needed for the local languages, ages, and setting (Thomson & Chatterjee, 2015).

Inclusion is an outcome to investigate, not an attribute to presume

Free exhibition admission reduced a specified financial requirement. Nothing in the evaluation establishes which other barriers were removed, who remained absent, or whether all visitors could use the installations on equal terms. A constraints perspective therefore changes the practical question from whether provision is free to which conditions enable different people to participate (Crawford et al., 1991). Future evaluation should examine the reach of recruitment, reasons for nonattendance, access to the site, and experiences of sensory or social discomfort alongside the responses of those who completed a survey.

The neurodivergent artists' gallery also raises questions of representation and authority. The present audience-focused data cannot establish the artists' experience of inclusion, their control over interpretation, or the professional value of participation. Disability-arts scholarship makes those questions relevant rather than peripheral (Darcy et al., 2022). Future design should involve artists and intended visitor communities in deciding how work is presented and how accessibility is assessed, rather than treating representation on a wall as sufficient evidence of inclusion. The relevance of co-design and adaptable interaction identified by MacRitchie et al. (2023) supports asking these questions without transferring findings from a clinical population to this public audience.

The Indian setting adds contextual specificity, not a representative account of Indian leisure. We did not collect sufficiently detailed accounts of linguistic, class, caste, disability, or neighborhood differences to explain how the experience was culturally situated. Iwasaki's (2008) emphasis on meaning in global contexts makes this an agenda for deeper inquiry rather than permission to interpret the observed responses through assumed cultural characteristics. Contextual extension requires attending to local meanings as well as adding a new geographic location to an existing evidence base.

From a public laboratory to accountable participatory evaluation

The exhibition demonstrates how participatory activities can also produce research material. It does not establish that embedding measurement reduces reactivity or that additional materials automatically produce stronger causal evidence. Visible votes may influence later votes. Writing a learning statement may prompt reflection rather than merely record it. A supplied drawing key structures what can be expressed. These are features of the research encounter, not inconveniences eliminated by calling the venue a public laboratory.

Arts-based leisure scholarship offers a more demanding methodological direction than collecting more visual feedback alone. Creative analytic practice and a/r/tography treat representation and inquiry as substantive processes, not decoration around a numerical result (Parry & Johnson, 2007; Schultz & Legg, 2020). In a future study, participants could interpret their drawings, identify meanings absent from the coding key, or help decide which questions the evaluation should ask. These would be changes to the research relationship, not procedures claimed to have occurred here.

Accountability also requires distinguishing enjoyment of an installation from consent to research use. The concerns raised by Rose et al. (2023) suggest explicit attention to visibility, data retention, and the ability to decline measurement without losing access to an activity. The present study does not test a privacy or governance model. Its methodological contribution is the recognition that experience design and evidence production must be examined together, including the possibility that the latter alters the former. Under those conditions, participatory evaluation can be useful without being described as naturally nonintrusive or inherently emancipatory.

Limitations and a focused research agenda

The single-arm pre-post design cannot isolate exhibition effects from expectations, repeated questioning, time, companions, the garden, or other features of a visit. Self-selection into attendance and survey completion limits generalization. The absence of a comparison group and delayed follow-up prevents causal and durability claims. Heterogeneous item changes do not eliminate demand characteristics. Without participant-level exposure information, the relative contribution of the seven zones remains unknown.

Measurement is a second limitation. The six accessible items offer restricted construct coverage, and several had high baseline scores. The mid-run change in response formats complicates pooling even after rescaling. Stable wording, common response options, version-specific estimates, and sensitivity checks for the assumptions underlying the reported tests are needed before stronger comparative claims. The standardized effect-size denominator should be specified in the reproducible analysis, and none of the reported d values should be read as a treatment effect. There is no validated clinical or practical threshold for these item changes.

The additional sources have different limits. Dictionary coding does not establish learning quality; unverified person-level matching and different prompts preclude a paired learning-gain claim. The face installation has only six stimuli and uncontrolled social and positional influences. Drawings were post-only, prompted, and not independently dual-coded; they were not linked to exhibition survey outcomes. These strands therefore broaden description but cannot repair the causal or psychometric weaknesses of the survey. Organizational proximity further warrants transparency about who recruited, facilitated, coded, and interpreted the material.

A useful next study would prioritize a few comparisons rather than add more loosely related outcomes. It could compare an exhibition visit with a garden-only or other cultural visit, using consistent measures and recording exposure and immediate experience. Within the exhibition, ethically designed comparisons could examine a quiet pause with and without technological feedback, or alternative orders of activity and explanation. A delayed assessment could then ask what was remembered, reused, or discussed with others, distinguishing immediate appraisal from sustained significance. Finally, participant-led interpretation and targeted access research could address whose experiences and meanings are missing. These proposals follow from the present uncertainties; they are not evidence that any specific design feature has already been validated.

Conclusion

Art of Perception linked bodily activity, scientific explanation, artistic encounter, and opportunities for pause within a free cultural leisure setting. Its evaluation recorded immediate self-reported changes, most prominently in bodily relaxation, alongside varied written and visual responses. The study's value lies less in a general claim that leisure is beneficial than in showing how an exhibition's different opportunities can be connected to specific, limited forms of evidence.

The design-experience-evidence distinction gives leisure researchers and providers a practical way to use the case: identify the experience a feature is intended to support, examine what visitors actually report or do, and specify the comparison needed before claiming a mechanism or enduring benefit. This approach accommodates reflection and relaxation without making either compulsory, values participation without equating it with inclusion, and retains the possibility of meaningful cultural leisure without treating every favorable response as proof of transformation.
