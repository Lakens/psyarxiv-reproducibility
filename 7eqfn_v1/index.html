Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Although perceived person-environment (P-E) fit has been extensively studied and linked to important workplace outcomes, it has not been examined with the same level of attribute specificity as objective fit. This study addressed that gap by introducing and empirically examining perceived personality-work (mis)fit. Employing a multi-method, multi-informant design with 734 employees and 521 supervisors, we aimed to ( a)operationalize perceived personality-work (mis)fit using the too little/too much scale, (b) explore the workplace experiences that shape these perceptions, and (c) examine how they relate to supervisor-rated job performance. We found a high prevalence of perceived misfit, with both deficiency (30%) and excess (42%) commonly reported, while perceived fit was observed across a range of personality trait levels. Thematic analysis revealed that negative experiences associated with perceived misfit extended beyond task performance, encompassing well-being concerns and interpersonal difficulties, with distinct patterns across traits. Finally, perceived personality-work (mis)fit showed modest associations with supervisor-rated job performance. Theoretically, these findings provide insight into how employees experience the fit of their personality at work and highlight the value of examining perceived fit with respect to specific personal attributes. Practically, they inform efforts to better support employees' fit experiences in the workplace.

Employees' attitudes and behaviours are shaped not only by the realities of their work environment but also by how they perceive their fit within it. This subjective perception is captured by the concept of perceived person-environment (P-E) fit, which refers to individuals' own judgments about how well they fit a particular job or organization at a given point in time (De Cooman & Vleugels, 2022; Su et al., 2015). Unlike objective fit, which is inferred from explicit comparisons between personal and environmental attributes, perceived fit is grounded in employees' own interpretations of compatibility.

Meta-analytic evidence has consistently shown that perceived fit is associated with a wide range of work-related outcomes, including job satisfaction, organizational commitment, and job performance (Arthur et al., 2006; Kristof-Brown et al., 2005). Moreover, perceived fit often demonstrates stronger relationships with some of these outcomes than objective fit, leading scholars to argue that it is a more proximal determinant of employees' attitudes and decisions ( Kristof-Brown & Billsberry, 2013). However, research on perceived fit remains limited by its lack of attribute-level specificity. Objective fit research has extensively examined how particular employee characteristics-such as needs, goals, abilities, personality traits, and values-align with features of the work environment (De Cooman & Vleugels, 2022). In contrast, perceived fit is typically conceptualized and measured as a holistic psychological experience reflecting an overall sense of compatibility with one's job or organization (Englert et al., 2023; Su et al., 2015; van Vianen, 2018). Consequently, little is known about how employees perceive the fit of specific personal attributes.

Personality constitutes a particularly relevant attribute given its established links to important work outcomes (Wilmot et al., 2019; Wilmot & Ones, 2019). Studies of objective personality-environment fit typically compare employees' self-reported traits with externally derived estimates of occupational or job trait requirements (e.g., Ghetta et al., 2020; Juchem et al., 2026). Although this approach has generated valuable insights into personality fit and outcomes such as job satisfaction and career development, it reveals little about employees' own perceptions of personality-work fit. The few studies that do address personality within perceived fit research tend to assess it at a broad, undifferentiated level. For example, respondents may be asked to evaluate the overall match between their personality and the personality characteristics required by the job (Chuang et al., 2016) or to indicate their agreement with statements regarding the fit of their personality within a group (e.g., "I bring unique personality traits to this group"; Li et al., 2019). As a result, little is known about how employees perceive the fit of specific personality traits or whether they experience misfit as a trait deficiency or excess.

To address this gap, we introduce the concept of "perceived personality-work (mis)fit", defined as the extent to which employees perceive that each of their personality traits aligns with the demands of their work, taking into account the direction (i.e., excess or deficiency) of any misfit. The present study pursues three objectives. First, we operationalize perceived personality-work (mis)fit using the Too Little/Too Much (TLTM) rating scale (Kaiser & Kaplan, 2005), a response format originally developed for leadership assessment.

This approach enables employees to indicate whether each personality trait is perceived as "too little," "too much," or "just right" for their work. Second, we explore the factors underlying these perceptions using qualitative methods, responding to long-standing calls for greater insight into how fit perceptions are formed (Edwards et al., 2006; Su et al., 2015; van Vianen, 2018; Vleugels et al., 2018). Third, building on the central premise of P-E fit theory that better fit is associated with more favourable outcomes (e.g., De Cooman & Vleugels, 2022), we examine the relationship between perceived personality-work (mis)fit and supervisor-rated job performance.

Mapping perceived personality-work (mis)fit

We propose two core criteria for effectively operationalizing perceived personalitywork (mis)fit: assessments should be trait-specific and should capture the direction of perceived misfit. Specifically, employees may perceive fit with some traits while simultaneously experiencing misfit with others, and they may perceive such misfit as reflecting either too little or too much of a given trait relative to work demands. The latter distinction parallels van Vianen's (2018) conceptualization of deficiency and excess as two forms of misfit. In their review of broad P-E fit theory, these concepts are primarily defined from a needs-supplies perspective, whereby deficiency reflects a lack of valued attributes in the environment and excess reflects the presence of undesirable attributes (e.g., Kristof-Brown et al., 2005; van Vianen, 2018). Because our focus is on personality traits and their alignment with job demands, we interpret these concepts from a demands-abilities perspective: deficiency refers to a perceived lack of a trait relative to work demands, whereas excess describes the perception of possessing more of a trait than is functional or desirable.

Given that adaptive traits such as conscientiousness and emotional stability are wellestablished predictors of positive work outcomes (e.g., Judge et al., 2002; Wilmot & Ones, 2019), it is reasonable to expect that employees may perceive deficiencies in these traits.

However, research on the "Too-much-of-a-good-thing" effect (TMGT effect; Pierce & Aguinis, 2013) shows that even traits typically viewed as beneficial can have negative consequences when taken too far. Empirical support for this effect is growing, with curvilinear relationships observed for traits such as conscientiousness, emotional stability, and extraversion (e.g., Grant, 2013; Le et al., 2011). These findings suggest that perceptions of excess, while less commonly considered, are also plausible.

To translate these two core considerations-trait specificity and misfit directionalityinto measurement, the present study introduces the "Too Little/Too Much" (TLTM) scale (Kaiser & Kaplan, 2005) as a way to operationalize perceived personality-work (mis)fit. This scale ranges from -4 (too little) to 0 (the right amount) to +4 (too much) and was originally developed for leadership assessments to distinguish between appropriate, insufficient, or excessive behavioural expressions in a specific context (e.g., Vergauwe et al., 2017) 1 . While this approach is novel within the domain of perceived personality-work (mis)fit, it aligns with Edwards and colleagues' (2006) molecular approach, whereby fit is captured by directly assessing the perceived discrepancy between person and environment. For instance, Travaglianti et al. (2016) asked employees to rate, on a scale from -3 (would like much less than now) to +3 (would like much more than now), whether their job meets, exceeds, or falls short of their ideal in areas such as work-family balance or supervisor recognition.

While their research focused on broader P-E fit and its fluctuations over time, the findings highlight the potential role of work experiences as meaningful cues.

One category of work experiences that may give rise to perceived personality-work misfit is performance-related challenges. Because conscientiousness, extraversion, and emotional stability are positively associated with job performance (Wilmot & Ones, 2019; Zell & Lesick, 2022), employees who fail to meet performance expectations may perceive a deficiency in a trait they consider relevant to their role. Conversely, consistent with the TMGT effect (Pierce & Aguinis, 2013), employees may perceive misfit because they believe that an excess of a trait hinders their performance. For example, Grant (2013) found that ambiverts outperform highly extraverted salespeople, as they combine assertiveness with receptiveness to customer needs. A salesperson who struggles to listen effectively may thus conclude they possess "too much" extraversion for the role.

Perceived personality-work misfit may also stem from experiences beyond performance, including impaired well-being, job dissatisfaction, and interpersonal difficulties.

For instance, a large-scale review found that extraversion is associated not only with better performance but also with interpersonal, motivational, and emotional benefits (Wilmot et al., 2019). Accordingly, employees who perceive themselves as insufficiently extraverted for their job may be responding to various challenges, such as difficulties forming social connections at work or discomfort during work events (Wanberg et al., 2025). Similarly, perceived personality-work misfit may arise when negative experiences are attributed to having "too much" of a trait. For example, the perception of excess conscientiousness may originate from experiences of elevated stress and reduced well-being associated with higher levels of this trait (Carter et al., 2016).

Together, these examples suggest that a range of negative work experiences may contribute to perceived personality-work misfit. The current study therefore seeks to identify and categorize these experiences:

Research Question 3 (RQ3): What types of negative work experiences do employees identify as origins of their perceived personality-work misfit?

To address this question, we use open-ended qualitative methods, which are wellsuited to uncovering how individuals make sense of their experiences (Willig, 2019). By allowing employees to describe work experiences in their own words, this approach reveals the experiences they find most salient in shaping perceptions of misfit, while reducing the constraints and potential demand effects associated with structured inventories (Reja et al., 2003). Importantly, employees may recognize that a single personality trait can support success in some areas while creating difficulties in others. An open, exploratory approach thus enables a deeper understanding of which work experiences employees consider most important in shaping their perceived personality-work misfit (Vleugels et al., 2018).

Perceived personality-work (mis)fit and supervisor-rated job performance

Beyond understanding the origins of perceived personality-work (mis)fit, an important question is whether these perceptions relate to observable work outcomes-specifically, job performance as evaluated by supervisors. Although perceived fit is generally associated with better performance (e.g., Kristof-Brown et al., 2005), it remains unclear whether this also applies to perceived personality-work (mis)fit, a more focused operationalization of fit relating to a single personal attribute. Moreover, the effects of misfit may differ across its two forms: deficiency and excess. Van Vianen's (2018) review on various forms of P-E fit suggests that both deficiency and excess can lead to negative outcomes, though deficiency-defined in that context as the environment falling short of personal preferences or valuesoften had stronger adverse effects.

Whether deficiency and excess have similar consequences can be examined by considering the shape of the relationship between perceived personality-work (mis)fit and job performance. A curvilinear, inverted U-shaped relationship would suggest that both perceived deficiency and excess are associated with lower performance. For example, among sales employees, perceiving either too little or too much extraversion for the role may be associated with performance decrements observable to supervisors (Grant, 2013). Alternatively, the relationship may be asymmetrical, with only deficiency predicting lower performancesuggesting a positive, linear relationship. For instance, perceiving too little emotional stability may impair employees' ability to cope with stress (Judge & Bono, 2001), whereas perceived excess may result in subtler interpersonal difficulties that are less likely to be reflected in performance evaluations.

Accordingly, the final aim of this study is to examine whether-and in what form (i.e., linear or curvilinear)-perceived personality-work (mis)fit across the Big Five traits is related to informant-rated job performance. To capture a more nuanced understanding of job performance, we differentiate between three domains commonly identified in the literature: task, adaptive, and contextual performance (Pulakos et al., 2000; Renn & Fedor, 2001; Van Scotter and Motowidlo, 1996). This leads to the following research question: Research Question 4 (RQ4): How is perceived personality-work (mis)fit across each of the Big Five traits related to supervisor-rated job performance (task, adaptive, and contextual), and do these relationships follow linear or curvilinear patterns? Method Participants and procedure A cross-sectional, multi-informant study design was used, combining quantitative and qualitative data collection. For this purpose, undergraduate Belgian psychology students at Ghent University were asked to recruit one or more working adults to participate in this study as part of their course assignments or theses, yielding a sample of 734 participants who reported on the variables of interest. Participating employees (62% women) had an average age of 43.89 years (SD = 11.62 years), with 75% holding a bachelor's degree or higher. The sample represented various industries, with the largest proportions of participants employed in healthcare and welfare (19%), education (15%), manufacturing (9%), IT & communications (7%), and retail and wholesale (7%).

After completing the survey, participants were asked to invite their respective supervisors to provide observer ratings of their job performance. Both employees and supervisors provided digital informed consent prior to participation and were explicitly assured that their responses would not be disclosed to the other party. In total, 521 supervisors provided informant ratings. Supervisors generally reported having frequent interactions with their employees, with only 10% indicating verbal interactions occurring less than once a week. Moreover, 93% stated that they had worked with their employee for at least six months.

Materials

Perceived personality-work (mis)fit.

Employees' perceived personality-work (mis)fit was assessed using a Dutch adaptation of the validated Organizational Frame-of-reference Big-Five Personality Measure (ORG-B5; Pathki et al., 2022), which consists of 20 items (four items per Big Five dimension). This personality inventory was selected due to its work frame of reference and brevity, which made it suitable for dual use: to evaluate perceived (mis)fit using the TLTM rating format, and to assess personality trait levels via the traditional Likert rating format (see below). Following a back-translation of the original ORG-B5 into Dutch, each item was evaluated against two criteria to ensure its suitability for inclusion in the TLTM scale (see Dupré et al., 2022; Vergauwe et al., 2017). These criteria involved (a) ensuring positive phrasing (i.e., avoiding reverse-scored items), and (b) removing evaluative elements in the items, given the scale's inherently evaluative nature. As a result, nine out of twenty items required adaptation. Some changes were relatively minor, such as rephrasing "I talk a lot at work" (extraversion) to "I am talkative at work". Other items required more substantial revision because they contained words with strong evaluative connotations. For example, "I am not easily bothered by things at work" (emotional stability), was changed into "I quickly move past difficult workplace situations" to avoid using the word "bothered". Example items for the remaining domains are "I am prepared at work" (conscientiousness), "I care about others at work" (agreeableness), and "At work, I enjoy thinking about things" (openness). A side-by-side overview of all items adapted for use with the TLTM scale is provided in the Supplementary Material. Participants rated these items using a 5-point TLTM scale ranging from -2 (much too little) to 0 (the right amount) and +2 (much too much).

As shown in Table S1 in the Supplementary Material, Cronbach's alpha was acceptable for TLTM ratings of agreeableness (α = .76), extraversion (α = .76), conscientiousness (α = .78), and emotional stability (α = .60), but comparatively lower for openness (α = .41). To examine whether the Big Five structure was retained when applying this new scale format, factor analysis was conducted using Mplus version 8. 10 (Muthén & Muthén, 2017). We chose Exploratory Structural Equation Modeling (ESEM; Asparouhov & Muthén, 2009) because it allows for cross-loadings, rendering it more suitable for personalityrelated items (Marsh et al., 2010). Results indicated acceptable model fit (RMSEA = .045; CFI = .945; TLI = .896). Moreover, the pattern of factor loadings (see Table S2, Supplementary Material) demonstrated robust associations between all items and their intended factors for agreeableness, extraversion, and emotional stability. For conscientiousness, results showed that this factor was predominantly defined by the two items specifically emphasizing order and tidiness. Finally, the openness factor was defined by a blend of openness items, particularly "At work, I enjoy thinking about things", and the remaining conscientiousness items. Despite these deviations from the optimal factor structure, most notably for openness, we proceeded with the current item set.

Personality traits.

To assess personality trait levels, employees additionally evaluated the same set of items using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (totally disagree) to 5 (totally agree). To mitigate potential order effects, the sequence in which participants completed both scales was counterbalanced across participants 2 . Despite the extensive prior validation of the ORG-B5 with a traditional Likert response format (see Pathki et al., 2022), we re-evaluated the internal consistency and factor structure of the Likert ratings collected using the slightly modified version of this instrument. As shown in Table S1 in the Supplementary Material, Cronbach's alpha was acceptable for Likert ratings of agreeableness (α = .81), extraversion (α = .79), and conscientiousness (α = .74), but lower for openness (α = .54) and emotional stability (α = .55). In line with our procedure for personality-work (mis)fit, ESEM model fit indices again suggested an overall good fit, specifically for RMSEA (i.e., .053) and CFI (i.e., .936), although TLI suggested a somewhat lower fit (i.e., .877). The factor loadings in Table S4 (Supplementary Material) indicate that the data generally aligned with the expected Big Five structure, with only two items exhibiting a primary loading on a different factor.

Conceptual coding of negative work experiences

Open-ended responses explaining why participants perceived a trait as "too little" or "too much" were coded using a four-step procedure.

Step 1: Inclusion coding

The first author evaluated each response using eight categories to determine whether it was eligible for analysis (see Supplementary Material Table S6 for sample responses).

Overall, 70% of responses were deemed usable, of which 65% referenced negative work experiences and 5% included both negative and positive work experiences. The remaining 30% were excluded because they did not describe negative work experiences. Specifically, 8% paraphrased the perceived (mis)fit item or offered further clarification, and another 8% provided circumstantial justifications (i.e., situational reasons for behaviour rather than underlying perceived trait deficiencies or excesses). In addition, 5% referred only to positive work experiences, 5% stated the absence of negative work experiences or simply wrote "/", and 4% were coded as "undecided" due to vagueness or inconsistency with the participant's selected perceived (mis)fit rating.

Step 2: Category development

Next, we employed a hybrid thematic analysis (Proudfoot, 2023), combining predefined categories based on a theoretical framework (deductive coding), with themes generated from the data (inductive coding). A preliminary set of eight categories was established based on an investigation of the literature on personality traits and their impact on work-related outcomes. For example, we synthesized outcomes such as task performance (e.g., Hurtz & Donovan, 2000), innovative work behaviour (e.g., Woods et al., 2018), and leadership effectiveness (e.g., Derue et al., 2011) into a single category titled "Task performance and time management". The primary author reviewed the first 20 responses for each Big Five domain using these predefined categories and subsequently assessed whether any modifications to the coding scheme were needed, including creating, merging, or renaming categories. This systematic approach resulted in a final set of six distinct categories.

Responses were coded as Task performance and time management when they described declined task performance, a diminished ability to achieve results, and/or difficulties in managing time. Adaptive performance was used for responses that described challenges in effectively adjusting to changing circumstances. The Career and development opportunities category included responses that mentioned missed opportunities in terms of personal and/or career development. Reputation at work encompassed responses that indicated leaving a negative impression on others at work. Employees perceived that their coworkers held an unfavourable opinion about them, while not necessarily experiencing negative work experiences on a personal level. Responses coded as Interpersonal difficulties at work described challenges in interacting with colleagues, clients, or superiors, which may encompass interactions both within and outside the work environment. These challenges can range from difficulties in building friendships and conflicts with others to feelings of mistreatment and irritations with others. The Well-being category compiled responses that included both mental difficulties (e.g., stress, decreased job satisfaction) and physical problems (e.g., sleep disturbances, headaches) within or outside the work environment. It also encompasses issues in the personal sphere (e.g., limited work-life balance, interpersonal problems with friends). An additional category, More information needed, was reserved for responses that lacked sufficient detail for categorization (e.g., "problems remain unsolved").

Examples of an open-ended response for each category are shown in Table 1.

Step 3: Independent response coding

All responses were independently coded by the first author and a trained second rater (i.e., one of three master's students). Raters were instructed to select two (or three) categories when respondents unambiguously mentioned multiple categories. When considering only the first coded category, inter-rater agreement was substantial (86%; κ = .80). The second coded category demonstrated a lower agreement percentage (51%), with 92% of disagreements arising because one rater identified a second category whereas the other did not.

Step 4: Coding consensus

All coding disagreements were resolved through a discussion between the first and second rater. These discussions resulted in a final consensus for the coding of each response.

Results

Employees' perceived personality-work (mis)fit (RQ1)

To provide insight into the prevalence of employees' perceived personality-work (mis)fit, TLTM ratings were classified as "fit" (the right amount) when domain-level fit ratings fell within a range of ±3 standard errors of measurement around 0, as outlined in Ghiselli et al. (1981) (see also Dupré et al., 2022; Vergauwe et al., 2017). Fit ratings that exceeded this range were labelled "excess" (too much), while those that fell below this range were considered "deficiency" (too little). The distribution of responses in each category is presented in Table 2. Note that domain-level results for openness, and to a lesser extent conscientiousness, should be interpreted cautiously, as item-level ratings may not consistently exhibit the necessary covariation for seamless aggregation to the domain level.

Across the Big Five traits, 30.4% of ratings reflected perceived fit, 41.9% reflected excess, and 27.6% reflected deficiency. When examining individual Big Five traits, both deficiency and excess were endorsed for each of the traits. Notably, the response pattern for emotional stability differed from that of the other Big Five traits, with a majority of employees (61.3%) perceiving their emotional stability at work as deficient. In contrast, perceived deficiency for the other traits ranged from 11.2% to 28.6%.

Associations between personality trait levels and perceived personality-work (mis)fit (RQ2)

Origins of perceived personality-work misfit (RQ3)

The origins of employees' perceived personality-work misfit were examined in three ways. First, we assessed the prevalence of negative work experience categories across domains, regardless of misfit direction. Second, we examined these trends within each Big Five domain to identify potential differences in the categories most frequently mentioned across domains. Third, we differentiated between open responses that preceded a perceived personality-work misfit rating of "too little" (deficiency) versus "too much" (excess).

Negative work experiences across personality domains

Employees' descriptions of negative work experiences predominantly related to wellbeing (39%), task performance and time management (24%), interpersonal difficulties at work (22%), and reputation at work (6%). Notably, only 1% of responses fell within the adaptive performance category, while responses related to career and development opportunities were extremely rare (only 4 mentions). The remaining 9% of responses required more information for classification.

Domain-specific negative work experiences

As presented in Table 3, negative work experiences in the domain of agreeableness primarily fell into the well-being category (44%). Conscientiousness-related negative work experiences were mainly characterized by hindered task performance or time management (32%). Additionally, the majority of responses concerning extraversion highlighted interpersonal difficulties at work (46%). For openness, the largest percentage of responses fell into the task performance/time management category (38%), along with the well-being category (35%). Lastly, 62% of respondents mentioned negative work experiences related to well-being when evaluating their perceived misfit for emotional stability.

Although person-environment (P-E) fit is a foundational concept in organizational

Much of the existing literature treats perceived fit as a broad psychological experience, often overlooking how employees evaluate the alignment of specific personal attributes-such as personality traits-with their work (Su et al., 2015). The present study addressed this gap by introducing and empirically testing the concept of perceived personality-work (mis)fit, offering a more granular understanding of how trait-work misalignments are experienced, what shapes these perceptions, and how they relate to supervisor-rated job performance.

The TLTM scale as a tool for mapping fit, deficiency, and excess Our findings provide initial empirical support for the utility of the TLTM scale (Kaiser & Kaplan, 2005) in capturing nuanced perceived personality-work (mis)fit. Across the Big Five domains, nearly three-quarters of employees' ratings reflected perceived misfit, with 27.6% indicating deficiency and 41.9% excess. Importantly, this pattern reveals that misfit was not skewed toward one direction. In fact, perceived excess was more common than deficiency for most domains, except for emotional stability, where the reverse was true.

Notably, even for openness, a domain for which the potential costs of higher scores have not yet been documented in previous work (e.g., on the TMGT effect), 42.6% of participants reported having "too much" of the trait.

Overlap and differences between personality trait levels and perceived personality-work (mis)fit

This study further demonstrates that perceived personality-work (mis)fit is meaningfully associated with (Likert-rated) personality trait levels, yet offers distinct interpretive value (Research question 2). While robust positive correlations were evident at the domain level, a more granular examination of item-level responses revealed that perceived fit ("the right amount") occurred across the personality trait spectrum, predominantly clustered around Likert scores of 3, 4, and 5. Moreover, in instances where participants rated their personality trait as a 5 on the Likert scale, a substantial percentage (46% on average) perceived this trait level as excessive ("too much"), while 52% regarded it as appropriate.

These results suggest that perceived personality-work (mis)fit reflects more than trait magnitude-it captures perceived appropriateness in context, offering a complementary perspective to traditional trait assessments.

Perceived personality-work misfit is shaped by a variety of workplace experiences

Our third objective was to use qualitative methods to uncover the origins of perceived personality-work (mis)fit, addressing a notable gap in broader perceived fit theory (e.g., Jansen & Shipp, 2019; Vleugels et al., 2018). Thematic analysis revealed that negative work experiences associated with perceived personality-work (mis)fit extend well beyond in-role performance. In fact, the most frequently mentioned category of work experiences was wellbeing (39%), followed by task performance and time management (24%), and interpersonal difficulties at work (22%). We also identified discernible patterns across Big Five domains, as well as meaningful differences within each domain. These within-domain variations highlight that the perceived costs associated with deficiency in a domain do not necessarily mirror those of excess. For example, perceived deficiencies in conscientiousness were primarily linked to task performance and time management issues, whereas an excess of this trait was mainly associated with interpersonal difficulties and well-being concerns.

Modest links between perceived personality-work (mis)fit and supervisor-rated performance

Finally, our findings suggest that perceived misfit can correspond to observable performance outcomes, echoing results from broader perceived fit research (e.g., Kristof-Brown et al., 2005), though the observed associations were modest and trait-specific. One potential reason for these modest results may be the mismatch in specificity (i.e., bandwidth) between trait-specific misfit ratings and broader performance domains (Su et al., 2015).

Nevertheless, the fact that supervisors detected differences associated with employees' fit perceptions is noteworthy, particularly given that perceived fit is a subjective construct that only partly overlaps with objective fit (Edwards et al., 2006) and because supervisor-rated performance is substantially influenced by rater effects (Foster et al., 2024).

The observed relationships were largely linear rather than curvilinear, highlighting the importance of misfit direction (van Vianen, 2018). For example, perceived personality-work (mis)fit for conscientiousness was positively associated with task performance, indicating that perceived deficiency hindered performance, while perceived excess did not. Conversely, for contextual performance, we observed a negative association with personality-work (mis)fit for this trait, suggesting that excess may impair performance even when deficiency does not. These patterns align with our qualitative findings, in which participants more frequently linked conscientiousness deficiencies to task-performance problems than excesses in conscientiousness.

Theoretical and practical implications

By examining perceived fit at the level of specific personality traits, this study extends the literature that has traditionally conceptualized perceived fit as a broad, undifferentiated experience (e.g., Su et al., 2015). Our findings highlight the importance of distinguishing between deficiencies and excesses, as both were common and showed asymmetric relationships with performance outcomes, providing support for van Vianen's (2018) broader contention that misfit direction matters. In addition, perceived personality-work (mis)fit showed only partial overlap with personality trait levels, suggesting that these perceptions represent subjective evaluations of trait-environment alignment rather than mere reflections of personality trait levels alone. Qualitative findings further showed that these evaluations are informed by diverse workplace experiences, including well-being, task performance, and interpersonal functioning, consistent with recent calls to give greater attention to well-being outcomes in work psychology (Tay et al., 2023).

Methodologically, the study introduces a novel operationalization of perceived personality-work (mis)fit using the TLTM scale (Kaiser & Kaplan, 2005), which may serve as a template for assessing perceived fit in relation to other personal attributes (e.g., skills or values). Although psychometric results were generally acceptable, weaker reliability and structural validity emerged for openness and, to a lesser extent, conscientiousness. This may reflect the evaluative nature of perceived fit judgments, whereby items within the same trait domain can vary in their relevance to specific work-related demands and outcomes (e.g., Stewart et al., 2021). Practically, our findings offer actionable insights for organizations and career counsellors seeking to improve personality-work fit. Misfit experiences may serve as selfregulatory signals that motivate individuals to restore fit by changing either their work environment (i.e., job crafting) or their own personality traits (i.e., volitional personality change) (Dupré & Wille, 2025; Kandler et al., 2024). By identifying which traits are perceived as misaligned, in what direction, and why, the present approach can help guide more targeted interventions.

Limitations and future directions

A number of study limitations need to be recognized. First, while our choice of the ORG-B5 instrument was guided by its recognition as one of the few validated and concise contextualized personality measures, it did not include other relevant domains such as honesty-humility (Johnson et al., 2011). Moreover, the instrument's brevity has implications for domain coverage. For instance, within conscientiousness, two of the four ORG-B5 items focus on orderliness, while other potentially meaningful facets-such as perfectionismremain unrepresented (Ashton & Lee, 2007).

Another limitation is that our survey-based qualitative approach yielded a substantial proportion of irrelevant responses: 30% did not mention any negative work experiences.

While an interview-based methodology could offer greater depth and allow for follow-up questions, it would inevitably yield far less data. Although only 21% of open-ended questions received a response, our large sample size enabled a substantial volume of open responses (N = 1225), representing 83% of participants. This broad coverage is crucial, as it helps ensure diverse representation and addresses concerns from prior research (Miller & Lambert, 2014) about potential bias arising from group differences in non-responders.

Lastly, while our measurement approach allowed differentiation between the two directions of personality-work misfit (i.e., excess versus deficiency), it did not distinguish between different types of fit. Specifically, it did not capture whether fit arises when both personality and work demands are high, or both are low-configurations that have been shown to yield distinct outcomes in broader fit research (van Vianen, 2018).

We discuss three future research questions that warrant further exploration. First, the recent call for more organizational research using well-being as a criterion (Tay et al., 2023), along with insights from our qualitative data, provide a compelling case for extending our investigation into the predictive validity of perceived personality-work (mis)fit to other work outcomes. Second, while our approach examined personality domains in isolation-a strength in terms of conceptual clarity-it may have overlooked meaningful interactions between traits. It is plausible that perceived personality-work (mis)fit for one domain depends on the level of another. For instance, participants who reported an excess of conscientiousness may have done so in light of a combination of high conscientiousness and high neuroticism, which could contribute to a perfectionistic tendency linked to maladaptive outcomes (Smith et al., 2019). Finally, questions of causality remain open: do negative work experiences give rise to perceived misfit, or does perceived misfit itself contribute to adverse experiences? It is likely that these processes unfold through dynamic feedback loops over time. Longitudinal research is needed to capture the temporal dynamics through which perceived personality-work misfit is construed in interaction with the work environment (see, e.g., Vleugels et al., 2018).

Conclusion

This article introduced perceived personality-work (mis)fit as a more attribute-specific approach to understanding perceived fit. To this end, a new measurement approach was presented in which existing personality trait items are rated using a TLTM format, which allows the mapping of both perceived excess and perceived deficiency. Through the analysis of extensive qualitative data, we also provided initial insights into the negative work experiences associated with perceptions of personality-work misfit. Finally, our multiinformant design showed that some of these (self-) perceptions were also reflected in supervisor ratings of job performance.
