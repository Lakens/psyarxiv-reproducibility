Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

As Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) becomes more sophisticated and widespread, it matters how its perceived humanness affects trust. Previous work shows that anthropomorphism increases an agent's perceived trustworthiness as an advisor. We asked whether an author's presentation also affects trust in the information it produces in a non-advisory role. In two experiments, participants read a science communication blog post attributed to an author with varying anthropomorphic cues. They rated the trustworthiness of the author (author trust) and the content (content trust). In Experiment 1 (N = 270), the post was attributed to a GenAI author described with low or high anthropomorphic cues, or to a human expert. The human author, who also differed in implied professional expertise, received higher author trust than either GenAI author. This effect was reliably larger than the effect on content trust. The low-and high-cue GenAI authors did not reliably differ, including in anthropomorphism ratings. In Experiment 2 (N = 148, preregistered), participants chatted with a GenAI chatbot, presented as the author, that used fewer (low condition) or more (high condition) anthropomorphic cues. The highcue chatbot was rated more anthropomorphic and received modestly higher author trust. No reliable content trust effect was detected, and evidence that the two effects differed was inconclusive. Author and content trust were strongly correlated in both experiments. These results suggest that cues to an author's humanness may shape trust in the author more than in what it communicates, although the clearest evidence came from comparing human and GenAI authors.

1. Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has been around for decades, but with the release of Generative AI (GenAI) systems such as OpenAI's ChatGPT, human-level content creation technology is broadly accessible for the first time (Dale, 2024; Hu, 2023). With the ease of a conversational message, people can enlist the help of GenAI to perform a wide range of tasks, such as content writing, image generation, or summarizing complex topics. GenAI is able to create well-formed and believable content that can misinform, either intentionally or due to so-called AI hallucinations (Ji et al., 2023). As this technology continues to evolve rapidly, it is vital to understand how humans determine the credibility of AI-generated informational content.

In addition to the increasing scope of tasks that AI is taking on, GenAI is becoming more human-like. There is evidence that anthropomorphism, the attribution of human-like characteristics to non-human agents, increases trust in purpose-built AI agents, such as autonomous vehicles, and trust resiliency when using AI for decision making (De Visser et al., 2016; Waytz et al., 2014). However, there is little work directly looking at the link between anthropomorphism and trust in communicated knowledge in a non-advisory situation, known as epistemic trust (Sprecher et al., 2022). Most of what is known about anthropomorphism and trust comes from static cues, such as a human-like face, name, or description of an agent (Gong, 2008; Waytz et al., 2014). Conversational GenAI conveys humanness differently: through how it behaves during an interaction, for example by responding with warmth or empathy (Araujo, 2018; Liu & Sundar, 2018). Meanwhile, studies of AI-authored content usually measure the credibility of the content alone (Longoni et al., 2022; Waddell, 2018), so it is not known whether human-like cues affect trust in a GenAI agent and trust in what it says in the same way.

We address these gaps in two experiments that hold a science communication blog post constant and vary only how its author is presented. Experiment 1 tests static cues: participants read a short description of a GenAI author with low or high anthropomorphic cues, or of a human expert. Experiment 2 tests behavioral cues: participants converse with a GenAI chatbot, presented as the author, that responds in either a mechanical or an empathetic, emotionally expressive way. In both, we measure trust in the author and trust in the content separately, and test directly whether author cues affect the two to the same degree. To contextualize these questions, we first review the technological and psychological background underlying GenAI, anthropomorphism, and epistemic trust.

2. Background

>Background

2.1. Overview of GenAI

GenAI models come in many forms. Some of the most common forms at the time of writing are those that generate text, such as Large Language Models (LLMs). An LLM is an artificial neural network that has been trained on a large body of textual input, usually via unsupervised learning. This means that the text is not annotated or marked with groundtruth information to aid in the training process. Much like a human would read a book and encode the knowledge contained within it in their mind, the LLM transforms the words, synthesizes, and encodes the knowledge into its model according to a particular algorithmic strategy. For example, OpenAI models, such as ChatGPT, are generative pretrained transformer (GPT) models, which are based on the transformer algorithm (Vaswani et al., 2017). The transformer learns patterns in its training data, and models based on it generate output by predicting the next token-such as a word, letter, or phrase-in a sequence, based on the user input and the data used for training. As a result, they can produce nonsensical or non-factual output, colloquially known as hallucinations (Ji et al., 2023; Ooi et al., 2025). In addition, models are often fine-tuned after their initial training to make their output more truthful and less harmful, for example through reinforcement learning from human feedback (Ouyang et al., 2022).

There has been a rapid advancement in quality and sophistication of the output of GenAI models to the point that the content is now as good as that of human authors for some tasks. In fact, recent research has found that the quality of AI-generated content was rated more highly than that of human authors (Zhang & Gosline, 2023). With ongoing quality improvements, GenAI has the potential to help us be more efficient and productive in many aspects of society (Ooi et al., 2025). However, its potential to support humans in everyday tasks is conditional on humans' willingness to trust its content (i.e., their epistemic trust).

2.2. Epistemic Trust

Epistemic trust is one's willingness to accept and integrate communicated knowledge (Knapen et al., 2022). It is vital for social learning (Csibra & Gergely, 2009). This trust is balanced with epistemic vigilance in deciding what information to believe (Sperber et al., 2010). One's epistemic credulity is high when an individual trusts too widely without enough vigilance; in this case, they may be susceptible to misinformation and exploitation (Campbell et al., 2021). On the other hand, epistemic mistrust is high when one is overly vigilant and untrusting of others (Campbell et al., 2021). Individuals with high epistemic mistrust may lack the ability to learn effectively from social experiences. Without social learning, each person would need to experience everything directly (Csibra & Gergely, 2009; Mascaro & Sperber, 2009) and would likely suffer greatly as a result. Imagine the child who does not trust the warnings of a parent to not touch a hot stove because it will hurt, or the parent whose child dies from a preventable childhood illness because they were hesitant to vaccinate according to expert advice. Similarly, without enough vigilance, one can assimilate false information, possibly leading to false beliefs and taking dangerous actions leading to harm; for example, acting on claims that various diseases such as COVID-19 or cancer can be cured by ingesting chlorine dioxide (Burke et al., 2014).

While epistemic trust typically involves judgments about human communicators, emerging technologies introduce non-human agents as potential sources of information. Understanding how these socially based epistemic judgments may be used when considering content produced by GenAI requires consideration of anthropomorphism (i.e., the perception of human-like qualities in non-human entities).

2.3. Anthropomorphism

A powerful social marker that may play a key role in deciding the trustworthiness of an artificial agent is anthropomorphism (Kulms & Kopp, 2019). Anthropomorphism is the attribution of human characteristics and mental states (e.g., goals, intentions, emotions) to non-human things, whether they are living or not (Epley et al., 2007). The presence of a human-like face has been found to increase anthropomorphism and mind perception in non-human agents (Broadbent et al., 2013; Epley et al., 2007; Looser & Wheatley, 2010). Beyond the presence of human-like physical features, perceiving a mind in an agent can also evoke anthropomorphism (Epley, 2018). This is particularly relevant with increasing use and availability of conversational GenAI agents, which demonstrate language and reasoning capabilities previously only seen within humans. Past research has found that mind perception, as well as individual tendencies toward anthropomorphizing, predicts self-reported attitudes toward important everyday concerns: the extent of ethical consideration and respect directed toward an agent; the amount of responsibility and accountability conferred on an agent; the amount of social influence an agent has; and critically, the perceived trustworthiness of an agent (Gray et al., 2011; Gray et al., 2012; Gray & Wegner, 2012; Waytz et al., 2010).

2.4. Relationship Between Anthropomorphism and Trust

Epistemic trust and anthropomorphism in GenAI have yet to be explored in depth. In one of very few existing studies, the use of a spoken voice increased both ratings of anthropomorphism and the perceived accuracy of advice, showing a correlation between anthropomorphism and trust in advice given by the GenAI agents (Cohn et al., 2024).

Additionally, mind perception in GenAI, which is associated with anthropomorphism, has been shown to correlate with trust (self-reported) and with rates of advice taking; while trust was positively correlated with mind perception in general, when mental-state traits were associated with experience (e.g., happiness) rather than intelligence (e.g., planning), attribution was weakly negatively correlated with advice acceptance from an LLM agent (Colombatto et al., 2025).

Beyond GenAI agents, various studies have examined the relationship between trust and anthropomorphism in other computer-based agents. The general finding is that as anthropomorphism increases, the trust in the agents increases (Gong, 2008; Nowak, 2004; Waytz et al., 2010; Waytz et al., 2014). When participants interacted with a computer agent represented by a static image avatar of varying levels of human-likeness, higher levels of anthropomorphism resulted in the agent being more highly rated for competence and trustworthiness (Gong, 2008). Similarly, when autonomous vehicles in a driving simulation study were given anthropomorphic features (i.e., name, gender, and voice), drivers were more trusting of the vehicle compared with drivers in the non-anthropomorphized condition (Waytz et al., 2014).

Most of these studies conveyed anthropomorphism through static cues: a face or avatar (Gong, 2008; Nowak, 2004), a name, gender, or voice (Cohn et al., 2024; Waytz et al., 2014), or a description (Castelo et al., 2019). Conversational agents can also convey humanness through their behavior during an interaction, such as informal, responsive language (Araujo, 2018; Go & Sundar, 2019), while expressions of sympathy and empathy can lead people to respond to a chatbot as a social actor (Liu & Sundar, 2018).

2.4.1. Biases in Trust Toward AI and AI-Authored Content

Although higher levels of anthropomorphism often promote trust, prior research suggests that human-machine trust is shaped by additional cognitive biases. Phenomena such as automation bias and complacency (Goddard et al., 2012; Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010), algorithm aversion (Dietvorst et al., 2015), and human favoritism (Zhang & Gosline, 2023) highlight that trust in artificial agents depends not only on perceived humanness but also on task context and pre-existing expectations about technology. Humans demonstrate automation bias when they show an increased willingness to rely on automation without verifying the likely accuracy of its output. In line with automation bias, De Visser et al. (2016) found that participants initially deferred to a non-anthropomorphized computer agent more readily than to more human-like agents when doing a judgment task. However, trust in the human-like agents was more resilient as the agents' advice became less reliable. Algorithm aversion shows the opposite pattern: Castelo et al. (2019)found that people trusted algorithms less than humans for tasks that are perceived as subjective, such as writing content. When the algorithms were portrayed as having affective humanlikeness, their perceived usefulness for more subjective tasks increased (Castelo et al., 2019).

It remains unclear how users may perceive newer systems given the significant development of AI systems in recent years. Previous research was primarily about purpose-built automation, algorithms, and AI systems. General-purpose, conversational GenAI agents may invoke different cognitive biases and mechanisms. For instance, Zhang & Gosline (2023) compared persuasive content generated by human experts, by GenAI, and by the two in collaboration. When participants did not know the source, they rated AIgenerated content as higher quality than human-generated content. When they were told the source, however, content attributed to a human expert was rated more highly than when its source was unknown. Learning that AI was involved in the writing process did not lower ratings of the content. These findings suggest that human favoritism, rather than algorithm aversion, may bias judgments of generated content.

Other studies of AI authorship point in different directions. Readers rated news attributed to a robot reporter as less credible than identical news attributed to a human journalist (Waddell, 2018). Headlines labeled as written by AI were rated as less accurate than the same headlines labeled as written by humans, for both true and false headlines (Longoni et al., 2022; see also Altay & Gilardi, 2024). In contrast, the machine heuristic proposes that people may assume machine sources are objective and free of bias, which could protect machine-authored content from some of the skepticism directed at human sources (Sundar, 2008). However, when Waddell (2018) tested this directly, the machine heuristic did not explain how readers judged machine-written news; instead, the lower credibility was partly explained by the machine author seeming less human. These studies typically measure the credibility of the content alone and rarely ask whether author trust and content trust move together when the presentation of the author changes.

Taken together, these findings highlight a complex relationship between anthropomorphism and trust; while human-like cues can foster trust in automation, biases such as automation bias and human favoritism can complicate the relationship. Prior work has also largely examined either trust in artificial agents or the credibility of their content, but not how the two respond to the same cues. The current experiments addressed this by manipulating the anthropomorphic cues of an author and testing directly whether they affect trust in the author and trust in the content to the same degree.

3. The present research

In two experiments, we evaluated how manipulating the anthropomorphic cues of an author affected trust in that author and trust in the content attributed to them. In all cases, the content was a science communication blog post about a fictional medical breakthrough. Because the post was the same in every condition, we could test whether author cues affected judgments of the author and the content to the same degree. Experiment 1 presented one of three authors-a GenAI author with low anthropomorphic cues, a GenAI author with high anthropomorphic cues, or a human expert-using a name, image, and a short description. Experiment 2 varied whether a GenAI chatbot, presented as the author of the same blog post, exhibited more or fewer anthropomorphic cues during a conversation. Measuring author and content trust in response to the same manipulations, rather than either one alone, is what allows the two to be compared. Details of each manipulation are provided within the Methods section of each experiment.

We hypothesized the following: • H1: An increase in anthropomorphic cues would increase the ratings of trustworthiness of the author.

• H2: An increase in anthropomorphic cues would increase the ratings of trustworthiness of the content.

Our findings were consistent with H1 for the comparison between the human and GenAI authors in Experiment 1 and for the behavioral manipulation in Experiment 2, but not for the comparison between the low-and high-anthropomorphic-cue GenAI authors in Experiment 1. The findings did not support H2. We did not detect a reliable effect of author presentation on content trust in the primary analyses of either experiment. In Experiment 1, the effect of author presentation on author trust was reliably larger than its effect on content trust; in Experiment 2, the evidence on this comparison was inconclusive.

Anonymized data, study materials, and analysis scripts for all experiments are available online on the Open Science Framework (OSF) website (https://osf.io/6u74p/).

4. Experiment 1

In Experiment 1, participants read the blog post and a short description of the agent they were told was the author of the post. They were then asked to report how trustworthy they found the author and the content. They were also asked to report their perceptions of other characteristics of the author (i.e., anthropomorphism, likeability, and perceived intelligence).

4.1. Methods

This experiment was approved by a Research Ethics Board of the University of Waterloo (REB #47011) and was not preregistered.

4.1.1. Participants

We recruited 315 US-based participants through CloudResearch. This sample was determined in accordance with a power analysis performed using the pwr package (version 1.3-0; Champely, 2020) in R (version 4.6.1; R Core Team, 2026). The power analysis indicated that we would need a minimum of 53 participants in each of the three condition levels, for a total of 159, to detect a moderate size effect (Cohen's f = 0.25). To detect a small effect (Cohen's f = 0.10), we would need to recruit 323 participants per level for a total of 969. Due to the limited number of existing studies that looked at the effect of anthropomorphic cues in GenAI on epistemic trust, we took a conservative approach and aimed to target the detection of a small to moderate effect while balancing the overall cost of the study. We aimed to recruit a minimum of 300 participants, 100 in each condition, with the assumption of at most 20% exclusions due to poor data quality and attrition (n = 80, k = 3, Cohen's f = 0.20, α= 0.05, 1 -β≈0.80). With the final sample, the design had 80% power to detect an omnibus effect of Cohen's f = 0.19 and a pairwise difference of Cohen's d = 0.42 (Cohen's d = 0.49 with Bonferroni correction).

Participants were compensated with US$2.00 for completing this 15-minute online survey. Participants were from the United States of America, were recruited from the CloudResearch Approved Participants List, and were required to have an approval rating greater than or equal to 95% and to have completed more than 100 HITs.

After data exclusion for quality control (further described in the analysis section), the final sample was 270 participants (ages 21 to 79, Mage = 45.10, SDage = 12.27; 107 females; 268 fluent in English; 256 English as a first language; 232 with at least some post-secondary education). They did differ in how often participants consumed media similar to the blog post (p = .025): participants in the low condition were more likely to consume such media at least weekly (54%, compared with 34% and 38% in the high and human conditions respectively).

4.1.2. Materials

Participants were presented with a brief description and an accompanying image of an author. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three author description conditions. Two of the conditions used GenAI agents, and a third provided a human baseline for comparison. The description and image presented for the author had varying anthropomorphic cues per condition: (1) low anthropomorphic cues: the author, named "Science Communication AI," was described as a GenAI agent in functional, mechanical language and represented by an image of a computer; (2) high anthropomorphic cues: the author, named "Simone the Science Synthesizer," was described as a GenAI agent with human-like attributes (e.g., having emotions) and represented by a human-like cartoon image; and (3) human expert: the author, named "Dr. Simone Tran," was described as a human science communication expert and represented with a photograph of a person aligned with the description. We used ChatGPT (GPT-4o) (OpenAI, 2024) to generate the author descriptions for each of the three conditions. The photographs for the low and human conditions were free-to-use stock images obtained from Pexels (G., 2021; Nikhomkhai, 2016). The cartoon image used for the high condition was generated by ChatGPT based on the image for the human condition (OpenAI, 2024). See Figure 1 for the author descriptions and images by condition. Because a GenAI author cannot plausibly hold professional credentials, the human condition differed from the GenAI conditions in implied expertise as well as in anthropomorphic cues. We therefore interpret differences between the human and the two GenAI conditions as differences between kinds of author and treat the comparison between the low and high conditions as the test of anthropomorphic cues within GenAI.

The high and human conditions shared the name Simone and a human-like image, so the difference between them lay mainly in the type of author and the title that signaled expertise.

All participants then read the same fictional science communication blog post, which was presented as written by the author described in their assigned vignette. The Internet is increasingly a source of scientific information for many. In the United States, the National Science Board (NSB) reported an increase in Americans saying they use the Internet as their primary source of science and technology news, up from 9% in 2001 to 57% in 2018, with 70% saying they would go online to learn more about a science and technology issue (National Science Board, 2020). Similarly, Americans view new medical discoveries as the most interesting topic within science and technology news, with 56% of respondents being very interested in them (National Science Board, 2020). However, determining whether the content on the Internet is reliable and trustworthy is not necessarily straightforward. Therefore, to support external validity, we chose to use a science communication blog post as the authored content stimulus. Furthermore, given that Sperber et al. (2010) noted that people are more epistemically vigilant when information is relevant to them, we decided to use a fictional medical breakthrough related to inflammatory diseases which afflict many people worldwide (Slavich, 2015). We used ChatGPT (GPT-4o) (OpenAI, 2024) to generate the blog post; all prompts used to generate stimuli are available on OSF. The post reads as a summary of a placebo-controlled clinical trial conducted by other researchers, and its tone is optimistic. The full text of the post is available in Figure 2.

4.1.3. Measures

Anthropomorphism was measured using a subset of items from the Godspeed Instrument, which consists of a series of questionnaires developed to measure anthropomorphism, animacy, likeability, perceived intelligence, and perceived safety (Bartneck et al., 2009). Each trait is measured using a semantic differential scale with relevant anchor descriptives for each item (left descriptive = 0 and right descriptive = 100). We combined and randomized the items from the anthropomorphism, likeability, and perceived intelligence subsets (e.g., Fake-Natural, Unfriendly-Friendly, Incompetent-Competent).

We removed "Moving rigidly-Moving elegantly" from the anthropomorphism questionnaire because the participants were not interacting with a moving robot.

We used the Muenster Epistemic Trustworthiness Inventory (METI; Hendriks et al., 2015) to assess epistemic trustworthiness of the author of the provided communication content. The METI was designed to measure epistemic trustworthiness of experts when laypeople are presented with limited information about the expert, such as a short text description (Hendriks et al., 2015). It consists of 14 items in three subscales: expertise (6 items), integrity (4 items), and benevolence (4 items). Each item was measured with a semantic differential scale with a slider anchored between pairs of adjectives (e.g., Honest-Dishonest, Fair-Unfair, Considerate-Inconsiderate; left descriptive = 0 and right descriptive = 100). Given the similar structure to the Godspeed items, we presented these measures to participants on the same screen, intermixing and randomizing the order of the items.

In addition, we asked participants to rate the credibility of the provided communication content using the message credibility scale developed by Appelman & Sundar (2016). The message credibility scale consists of three items (i.e., accurate, authentic, believable).

Participants were asked to rate how well each of these three adjectives described the content on a 5-point scale. Three additional adjectives were added to the measure to make it less obvious what we were measuring and to gather perceptions of writing quality (i.e., well-written, boring, engaging). These additions were used for post hoc analysis and did not contribute to the content trust measurement. All items were presented on the same page, in a random order, and participants responded on a scale from 1 to 5 with scale labels of "Describes very poorly" to "Describes very well".

As an additional indicator of trust in the content and author, and consistent with our focus on trust judgments in realistic contexts, we included items related to behavioral intentions. We asked participants to rate a series of statements related to the influence of the content they read (i.e., "If I were to suffer from a relevant medical condition, I am likely to use Velocerin." [the medication announced in the blog post content stimuli]; "I would share the information in the article with a close friend or family member who may benefit from the medication described." ; "I believe the claims made about Velocerin in this article." ; "I do not have confidence in Velocerin based on what I have read." ; "The blog post is not trustworthy enough for me to share on social media, if I were a person who shares links on social media." ; "After reading this blog post I have enough information to make an informed decision about Velocerin." ; "If I were suffering from a relevant medical condition, I would look into Velocerin more after reading this blog post.") and the author (i.e., "I would ask the author of this blog post to summarize scientific content for me in the future." ; "I would read articles by the author of this blog post in the future if they were on a topic that was of interest to me."). A 7-point Likert scale was used for each scale item, with endpoints labeled "Strongly disagree" and "Strongly agree". This novel measure was specific to the content of this study and had not previously been validated in the literature.

We refer to the combination of the METI and the novel author items as author trust, but it reflects perceived expertise, integrity, and benevolence of the source and intentions to rely on it, not acceptance of the source's claims. Likewise, we refer to the combination of the message credibility scale and the novel content items as content trust, but it combines perceived credibility with belief, intended action toward the medication, and judgments of whether the post gave enough information. For this reason, we also report every main analysis using only the validated scales (METI for author trust and message credibility for content trust).

Internal consistency was high and comparable across the item sets used to assess anthropomorphism, author trust, and content trust (see Table 2).

4.1.4. Procedure

Participants enrolled in the study on the CloudResearch platform and were redirected to a survey hosted on the Qualtrics platform. Upon starting the study, participants were presented with an information letter and asked to provide their consent to participate in the study. To reduce bias during data collection, we used partial disclosure. We indicated that the study was examining perceptions of communicated information and withheld the intention to investigate the relationship between trust, anthropomorphism, and GenAI until participants completed the study.

Upon consent, participants were randomly assigned to one of three author conditions: low (n = 87), high (n = 92), and human (n = 91).

Participants were first presented with an instruction screen that indicated that they should carefully read the blog post and the author's description on the next screen and that they would later be asked to answer questions about the text. On the next page, they saw a screen containing an image of the authoring agent, a description of that agent, and the blog post; the author description and image varied according to the assigned author condition, whereas the blog post was the same for all conditions. Participants were asked to answer basic comprehension check questions about the content. If they did not answer them correctly, they saw a screen asking them to read the description and the blog post carefully again before proceeding. They were presented with the same stimuli content screen and comprehension check questions once again. Following this, they proceeded regardless of the correctness of their responses.

On the next screens, participants were shown the following measures in a randomized order: items from the Godspeed Instrument (14 items; Bartneck et al., 2009) and the METI (14 items; Hendriks et al., 2015), which were intermixed in a randomized order; the message credibility scale (3 items; Appelman & Sundar, 2016) combined with the writing quality items, intermixed in a randomized order; and our novel author and content trust scale developed for this experiment. These were used to measure perceptions of the author, author trust, and content trust respectively.

Participants were then asked about their familiarity with and use of artificial intelligence technology, how often they consumed media similar to the blog post, and demographic information, including age, sex, gender, language fluency, and educational level.

Participants were asked open-ended questions related to anything they found confusing, the purpose of the study, and whether they experienced technical difficulties.

Finally, participants received a debriefing letter that revealed the full purpose of the study and the condition to which they were assigned and stated that the blog post was fictional.

4.2. Analysis and Results

All data analysis was completed using R (version 4.6.1; R Core Team, 2026). We used the traditional median imputation technique (Alwateer et al., 2024) to replace missing values for continuous variables with the median value of the corresponding author condition subgroup; the mode was used for any categorical variables. We used the median instead of the mean for continuous variables because the data tended to be skewed and not normally distributed across many of the variables. Only three item responses were missing, each from a different participant (two in the anthropomorphism and trust measures and one in a writing-quality item). The items combined into each composite (i.e., Godspeed, METI, Appelman and Sundar, and the novel measure) used different response formats (0-100 sliders, a 5-point scale, and 7-point scales). As such, we standardized each item as a z-score across all participants in the experiment before averaging items into composite scores. Because each composite is the mean of z-scored items, its standard deviation is below 1 (author trust SD = 0.84, content trust SD = 0.80), so regression coefficients on the composites are in standardized-item units rather than Cohen's d.

In R, we used the stats package (version 4.6.1; R Core Team, 2026) to conduct all one-way analyses of variance (ANOVAs), Welch's t-tests, and Pearson correlations, and the psych package (version 2.6.5; Revelle, 2026) to calculate Cronbach's α; Cohen's d (with the pooled standard deviation) and ω 2 were calculated with functions written for this study. For all pairwise and post-hoc t-tests, p values were adjusted using Bonferroni correction.

Throughout the paper, condition comparisons are computed as high minus low, human minus low, and human minus high, so positive values of t, d, and B indicate higher scores in the high or human condition. We fit linear mixed-effects models with the lme4 (version 2.0-1; Bates et al., 2015) and lmerTest (version 3.2-1; Kuznetsova et al., 2017) packages. Confidence intervals for Cohen's d were computed with the large-sample normal approximation. Figures were made with the ggplot2 (version 4.0.3; Wickham, 2016) and ggpubr (version 0.6.3; Kassambara, 2026) packages.

We checked the main results in five ways: using only the validated scales, standardizing each composite before comparing author and content trust, adjusting for pre-existing participant characteristics, analyzing complete cases only, and excluding participants who needed to reread the stimuli. The adjusted models controlled for age, sex (female vs. all other responses), post-secondary education, frequency of GenAI use, and frequency of consuming media similar to the blog post (the latter two as three-level factors: at least weekly, occasionally, or rarely or never), with HC3 robust standard errors (sandwich, version 3.1-1, Zeileis et al., 2020; lmtest, version 0.9-40, Zeileis & Hothorn, 2002; and car, version 3.1-5, Fox & Weisberg, 2019). We did not adjust for likeability or perceived intelligence, because they were measured after the manipulation and can be changed by it (Montgomery et al., 2018). Where a content trust difference was not significant, we used two one-sided tests (TOST; Lakens et al., 2018) as a sensitivity analysis to describe which effect sizes the data could rule out, reporting bounds from ±0.20 to ±0.50 (Appendix B).

Equivalence tests, within-condition correlations, and the comparison of dependent correlations (Williams's test; Steiger, 1980) were computed in R with functions written for this study, which are available on OSF. We did not correct for multiplicity across outcomes or across these checks.

4.2.1. Participant Exclusion Criteria

From the initial 315 participants, those who did not progress through the complete survey were excluded from the analysis (n = 22). If a participant skipped individual questions or scale items, their responses were included in the analysis with the missing values imputed as described in the analysis section.

To gauge whether the participant read and understood the stimuli content, the study included three comprehension check questions based on the blog post and author descriptions of the stimuli (e.g., "What was the author?" with response options "a generative AI agent" or "a human expert"). Twenty-one participants incorrectly answered these questions and were asked to reread the stimuli before answering the questions a second time. We excluded data from participants who incorrectly answered any of these questions after the second attempt (n = 5). Participants who correctly answered the questions after the second attempt were included in the final analysis. An exploratory analysis that excluded these participants confirmed that their inclusion did not alter the pattern of findings reported below (see Table 3).

To ensure that participants read the questions and prompts attentively throughout the study, we included attention check items within the scales. These questions consisted of a prompt to select a particular answer (e.g., set the slider to the far right). Data from participants with incorrect values for any of these questions were excluded from the analysis (n = 16).

4.2.2. Manipulation of Anthropomorphism

There was a significant main effect of the anthropomorphic cue manipulation on anthropomorphism as measured by the Godspeed instrument (F(2, 267) = 85.55, MSE = 0.54, p < .001, ω2 = .39). Upon further analysis with pairwise Bonferroni-corrected Welch's t-tests, there was a significant difference between the anthropomorphism scores in the low (n = 87, M = -0.40, SD = 0.80) and human (n = 91, M = 0.82, SD = 0.54) conditions (t(149.09) = 11.87, p < .001, d = 1.79, 95% confidence interval (CI) [1.45, 2.14]) and the high (n = 92, M = -0.43, SD = 0.83) and human conditions (t(155.65) = 12.14, p < .001, d = 1.79, 95% CI [1.45, 2.13]); however, the effect of the anthropomorphic cue manipulation did not reach significance for anthropomorphism when comparing the high and low conditions (t(176.93) = -0.29, p = 1.000, d = -0.04, 95% CI [-0.34, 0.25]). See Figure 3 for a plot of the means and distributions of anthropomorphism scores by condition. The same pattern appeared for likeability, F(2, 267) = 15.67, p < .001, ω2 = .10, and perceived intelligence, F(2, 267) = 14.76, p < .001, ω2 = .09. The human author was rated higher than both GenAI authors on each (ds from 0.65 to 0.82, all ps < .001), and the two GenAI authors did not differ (|d|s ≤0.11, ps > .99). The human author therefore differed from the GenAI authors on every perception we measured.

We calculated a composite content trust score using the mean value of the standardized values of the three items on the Appelman message credibility scale and the novel measure of content trust. An ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of the anthropomorphic cue manipulation on author trust (F(2, 267) = 18.60, MSE = 0.62, p < .001, ω2 = .12). Pairwise Bonferroni-corrected Welch's t-tests showed a significant difference in author trust score means between the low (n = 87, M = -0.18, SD = 0.79) and human (n = 91, M = 0.41, SD = 0.70) conditions (t(171.64) = 5.27, p < .001, d = 0.79, 95% CI [0.49, 1.10]) and the high (n = 92, M = -0.23, SD = 0.86) and human conditions (t(174.87) = 5.52, p < .001, d = 0.82, 95% CI [0.51, 1.12]); however, the effect of the anthropomorphic cue manipulation on author trust between the high and low conditions was not statistically significant (t(176.85) = -0.40, p = 1.000, d = -0.06, 95% CI [-0.35, 0.23]). See the right panel of Figure 4 for a plot of the author trust score distributions by author condition.

Because a significant effect on one form of trust and a nonsignificant effect on the other would not show that the two effects differ (Gelman & Stern, 2006), we tested directly whether the manipulation affected author trust differently than content trust. We fit a linear mixed-effects model predicting trust ratings from author condition, trust type (author or content), and their interaction with random intercepts for participants. This analysis revealed a significant interaction between condition and trust type, indicating the difference between author trust and content trust varied between author conditions. Specifically, the difference in author and content trust was significantly greater in the human condition compared to the low condition (t(267) = 5.29, p < .001). A significant difference was not observed between the high and low conditions (t(267) = -0.77, p = .444).

In standardized-item units, the human condition raised author trust by 0.59 relative to the low condition and content trust by 0.19 (Table 3). Because the two composites contain different numbers and kinds of items, we repeated this comparison after standardizing each composite (SD = 1); the difference remained, B = 0.46, 95% CI [0.27, 0.65], p < .001 (Appendix B). Taken together, these results show that replacing the GenAI author with a human expert influenced trust judgments about the author more than trust judgments about the content itself.

The same pattern held with the validated scales alone. Condition affected METI scores, F(2, 267) = 19.61, p < .001, ω2 = .12 (human vs. low: d = 0.80, 95% CI [0.50, 1.11]), but not message credibility, F(2, 267) = 1.61, p = .20 (d = 0.26, 95% CI [-0.04, 0.55]), and the interaction remained significant, t(267) = 4.32, p < .001. The author trust effect appeared in all three METI subscales (human vs. low, in standardized-item units: expertise B = 0.65, integrity B = 0.55, benevolence B = 0.64, all ps < .001; Appendix B). Perceived writing quality did not differ by condition, F(2, 267) = 1.20, p = .30.

The absence of a significant content trust effect does not mean that the effect was zero. The high and low conditions produced statistically equivalent content trust at a bound of ±0.30 SD (d = 0.01, 90% CI [-0.24, 0.26], TOST p = .027), but not at ±0.20 (Appendix B); because these two conditions also did not differ in anthropomorphism ratings, this does not show that a successful anthropomorphism manipulation would leave content trust unchanged. For the human condition, the 90% CI for the content trust difference ([0.00, 0.50]) included effects larger than ±0.30, so a small positive effect cannot be ruled out.

4.2.4. Robustness

Table 3 compares the human and low conditions under each alternative specification. The author trust effect and the difference between the author and content trust effects were significant in every specification. The content trust effect was not significant in four of the five specifications but was significant after covariate adjustment (B = 0.30, p = .008). Most of this change came from adjusting for how often participants consumed media similar to the blog post, which was imbalanced across conditions (Section 4.1.1); adjusting for that variable alone gave B = 0.29, p = .012, whereas adjusting for all other covariates without it gave B = 0.21, p = .064. The human author may therefore have had a small positive effect on content trust as well, but the effect on author trust was more than twice as large, and the difference between the two remained significant (B = 0.36, p < .001). Across all specifications, the low and high conditions never differed on author trust, content trust, or their difference (all ps > .27).

4.2.5. Correlations

Because the manipulation of anthropomorphic cues did not effectively dissociate the low from high conditions (see Section 4.2.2), we followed up our main analysis with a correlational examination of the relationships among anthropomorphism, author trust, and content trust. All correlations are Pearson's r.

Anthropomorphism was positively correlated with both content trust (r(268) = .49, p < .001, 95% CI [.39, .57]) and author trust (r(268) = .70, p < .001, 95% CI [.64, .76]). Author trust and content trust were also strongly, positively correlated (r(268) = .78, p < .001, 95% CI [.73, .83]).

Because these pooled correlations include the differences between conditions produced by the manipulation, we also computed them within conditions (centering each variable on its condition mean, with degrees of freedom adjusted for the condition means removed). The pattern was similar: anthropomorphism was positively correlated with author trust (r(266) = .66, p < .001, 95% CI [.59, .72]) and with content trust (r(266) = .54, p < .001, 95% CI [.45, .62]), and author trust and content trust were strongly, positively correlated (r(266) = .80, p < .001, 95% CI [.75, .84]). Anthropomorphism was more strongly correlated with author trust than with content trust (Williams's t(265) = 4.29, p < .001). These correlations show that people who saw the author as more human-like also tended to trust both the author and the content more. They do not show which judgment influenced which (see Section 6.1.1).

5. Experiment 2

To extend the findings of Experiment 1, we ran a follow-up experiment with modifications to the procedure to improve the efficacy of the anthropomorphic cue manipulation. Our manipulation of anthropomorphic cues in Experiment 1 did not have the intended effect on the perception of anthropomorphism. We intended that through the manipulation of the descriptions and images of the GenAI agents, participants would have perceptions of anthropomorphism that aligned with their assigned condition (i.e., the mean anthropomorphism score would be lower for the low condition than the high condition). While there was a significant effect of the anthropomorphic cue manipulation on anthropomorphism, it was only evident between the human condition and each of the GenAI agent conditions. We did not find a significant difference in the ratings between the two GenAI agent conditions (see Section 4.2.2). We suspected that reading a description of an agent was not enough to override past experience with similar agents or deep-seated beliefs about non-human agent capabilities. Changing impressions, especially implicit impressions, can be challenging and require significant and believable counter-evidence for individuals to update their existing impressions of an agent (Ferguson et al., 2019; Mann & Ferguson, 2015). As such, we refined the design in Experiment 2 to see whether a more interactive experience with the author would yield the intended anthropomorphic effects. The manipulation was more effective with this paradigm as shown in Section 5.2.2.

This experiment used an interactive GenAI chatbot to represent the author agent to provide participants with a more externally valid experience. The chatbot was instructed to respond with varying levels of anthropomorphic cues during conversation to manipulate the author condition, rather than having participants read descriptive vignettes with avatars. Additionally, we simplified the anthropomorphic cue manipulation to include only two levels, eliminating the human condition, so that the author was always a GenAI agent.

5.1. Methods

This experiment was approved by a Research Ethics Board of the University of Waterloo (REB #47304) and was preregistered at https://aspredicted.org/n9sg-nwjx.pdf (AsPredicted #215144). The preregistration specified the hypothesis that increasing anthropomorphism would increase trust in the GenAI author and in its content, the composite measures of author and content trust, the two conditions, a test of the main effect of condition on each trust measure, the exclusion rules, and the sample size. Section 5.2.1 describes where our analyses departed from it.

5.1.1. Participants

A sample of 202 participants was recruited through SONA at the University of Waterloo in accordance with a power analysis carried out using the pwr package (version 1.3-0; Champely, 2020) in R (version 4.6.1; R Core Team, 2026). Similar to Experiment 1, we targeted a sample large enough to detect a small to moderate effect. The power analysis indicated we would need between n = 64 (Cohen's f = 0.25) and n = 394 (Cohen's f = 0.10) participants in each of the two condition levels. The final recruitment sample size was chosen to balance our desired detection ability and the availability of participants within the sample pool; as preregistered, we planned to recruit 200 participants or to stop on March 21, 2025, whichever came first. Remuneration was 0.5 credits for completing the 30-minute online survey. After data exclusion for quality control, the final sample was 148 participants (ages 18 to 59, Mage = 20.39, SDage = 4.40; 115 females). With 75 and 73 participants in the low and high conditions, the design had 80% power to detect a difference of d = 0.47 between conditions. Most participants used GenAI chatbots at least weekly (74%), and conditions did not differ on any measured characteristic (all ps > .09; Table 1). Because nearly all participants were undergraduate students in a narrow age range, this sample differs from the Experiment 1 sample in age, education, and GenAI experience.

5.1.2. Materials

An interactive chatbot was included instead of the descriptive author vignette and image. Participants saw a text box where they could enter their message and a larger box where the conversation history was displayed, including the chatbot's responses to their messages (see Figure 5). All participants saw the same interface and were required to complete three conversation turns before proceeding. The chatbot's GenAI functionality was created using Claude Haiku 3.5 model (Anthropic, 2024). Differences in the low and high author conditions were controlled through system messages to the API.

To generate responses, the chatbot used the Anthropic API to access the Claude Haiku 3.5 model (claude-3-5-haiku-20241022, temperature 0.5; Anthropic, 2024). There were two versions of the chatbot, one for each of the two anthropomorphic cue manipulation levels. Both versions received the same core system prompt, which described the chatbot as an AI science communicator, included the full text of the blog post, stated that the chatbot had written it, and instructed it to claim authorship if asked. Both versions were limited to responses of two sentences. Anthropomorphic cue level was manipulated by changing the system prompt to instruct the chatbot to respond in either a more human-like or a more machine-like manner. Drawing on findings from prior work (Sections 2.3 and 2.4), we instructed the chatbot to express different levels of empathy and emotional understanding. These expressions were intended to elicit the perception that the chatbot has a mind, making it seem more human-like. The low author condition chatbot was named "AI science communication"foot_1 and was instructed to respond in a more machine-like manner: to use non-anthropomorphic language, to avoid filler language, and not to express empathy. The high author condition chatbot was named "Simone the Science Synthesizer" and was instructed to respond in a more human-like manner, i.e., to pretend it had emotions and understood the emotions of others, to respond empathetically, and such. See Appendix A for the system prompts used to define the chatbots. We describe what the conversations contained in Section 5.2.5.

The dependent measures were identical to Experiment 1, except one duplicated scale item from the combination of the Godspeed and METI scales was removed in Experiment 2. Two additional author trust items were added to the novel trust measure: "I would pay for content written by this author if the topic was of interest to me." and "I trust the author to provide quality information." The language demographics question was removed because it was of limited use in Experiment 1.

Internal consistency was acceptable to high across the item sets used to assess anthropomorphism, author trust, and content trust (see Table 4).

5.1.3. Procedure

Participants were randomly assigned to one of two conditions: low (n = 75) or high (n = 73) anthropomorphic cues. After participants read the blog post, they were given the opportunity to chat with the GenAI bot, which was programmed to respond according to the participant's assigned condition; they were told they were going to chat with the GenAI author of the blog post and were instructed to have a conversation on the topic of their choice with the bot. They were required to complete a minimum of three conversational turns before proceeding with the remainder of the experiment. The participants then completed the same questionnaires as in Experiment 1, with the modifications noted in the Materials section.

5.2. Analysis and Results

All score calculation and analysis followed the same procedures as Experiment 1, except that the main analysis was performed with Welch's t-tests instead of ANOVA since there were only two levels of the anthropomorphic cue manipulation. Because each outcome involved a single comparison, no within-outcome correction was applied. The composite standard deviations were 0.58 for author trust and 0.63 for content trust.

To describe the conversations, we used automated keyword counts. For each chatbot response, we counted words, exclamation marks, emotion words (e.g., excited, glad, sorry, feel, wonderful), and first-and second-person pronouns, and compared conditions with response-level regressions using standard errors clustered by participant. For each conversation, we flagged whether the participant or the chatbot referred to the blog post, Velocerin, or the underlying study; whether the participant asked about the chatbot's identity or authorship; and whether the chatbot used its fallback reply for missing information, comparing conditions with Fisher's exact tests. These keyword rules were not validated against human coding.

5.2.1. Participant Exclusion Criteria

We followed the same exclusion methods as defined in Experiment 1: failure to complete the survey (n = 12), survey duration less than one-third of the median time to complete (n = 1), failure to pass a content comprehension check (n = 11), and survey attention checks (n = 29). No participants were excluded as a result of the bot detection check. An additional participant was removed because they indicated they had not read the blog post before proceeding (n = 1). This exclusion was decided after reviewing the open-ended responses; retaining this participant did not change the results (Table 5). Before these exclusions, we removed two people who did not consent. In addition, five participants took the survey more than once (six additional records). For each, we kept their first complete attempt, or their first attempt if none was complete; four of them had abandoned an earlier attempt before answering any study questions. Exclusions were balanced across conditions (27 per condition; Appendix B). Eighty-three item responses were missing across 15 participants and were imputed as in Experiment 1; analyses of the 133 participants with complete data gave the same pattern (Table 5).

These exclusions departed from the preregistration, which specified incomplete surveys, failed attention checks, reCAPTCHA scores below 0, and random characters in openended responses. We also applied the completion time and comprehension check exclusions from Experiment 1, used the reCAPTCHA threshold of 0.5 recommended by Qualtrics (which excluded no one), and excluded the participant described above. The preregistration did not address repeat participation, which we handled as described above.

Applying only the preregistered rules, with the same handling of repeat participation, leaves 154 participants (78 low, 76 high) and gives the same pattern: author trust d = 0.38, 95% CI [0.06, 0.70], p = .019; content trust d = 0.14, 95% CI [-0.18, 0.45], p = .40 (Appendix B). We analyzed the main effects with Welch's t-tests rather than the preregistered ANOVA.

With two groups, the ANOVA is equivalent to Student's t-test, and because the groups were nearly equal in size, Student's and Welch's tests gave practically identical results (largest difference in p: < .001). The test of whether condition affected author trust more than content trust, the robustness models, the equivalence tests, the within-condition correlations and comparison of dependent correlations, and the conversation analyses were not preregistered and should be considered exploratory. The preregistered correlations between the main measures and sample characteristics are reported in Appendix B.

5.2.2. Manipulation of Anthropomorphism

A t-test comparing the anthropomorphism score means across the high (n = 73, M = 0.26, SD = 0.72) and low (n = 75, M = -0.25, SD = 0.92) conditions showed a moderately sized significant effect of the manipulation on anthropomorphism (t(139.72) = 3.73, p < .001, d = 0.61, 95% CI [0.28, 0.94]). Results are illustrated in Figure 6. The high condition chatbot was also rated as more likeable, t(139.88) = 5.10, p < .001, d = 0.84, 95% CI [0.50, 1.17]; no reliable difference in perceived intelligence was detected, t(144.83) = 1.91, p = .058, d = 0.31, 95% CI [-0.01, 0.64].

5.2.3. Effects of Anthropomorphic Cues on Author and Content Trust

As shown in the left panel of Figure 7, there was no significant effect of the anthropomorphic cue manipulation on content trust (t(146.00) = 1.19, p = .234, d = 0.20, 95% CI [-0.13, 0.52]). In contrast, there was a significant effect of the anthropomorphic cue manipulation, with higher trust for the agent programmed to show high (n = 73, M = 0.12, SD = 0.53) vs. low (n = 75, M = -0.12, SD = 0.60) anthropomorphic cues (t(144.90) = 2.61, p = .010, d = 0.43, 95% CI [0.10, 0.75]) as shown in Figure 7.

Similar to Experiment 1, we fit a linear mixed-effects model to test for differing effects of the anthropomorphic cue manipulation on author and content trust. This analysis did not reveal a significant interaction between condition and trust type. Specifically, the difference between author and content trust in the high author condition did not differ significantly from that observed in the low author condition (t(146) = 1.30, p = .196). This difference corresponds to about 0.2 standard deviations of the author-minus-content difference, and the design had only about 25% power to detect a difference of that size; the nonsignificant interaction therefore does not show that the manipulation affected author and content trust to a similar extent.

With the validated scales alone, the high condition chatbot again received higher METI scores, d = 0.45, 95% CI [0.12, 0.77], p = .007, with no reliable difference in message credibility, d = 0.06, 95% CI [-0.26, 0.38], p = .72; the difference between these effects was not significant, t(146) = 1.76, p = .080. In exploratory analyses of the METI subscales, the author trust effects were d = 0.40, 95% CI [0.07, 0.72], for expertise, d = 0.44 [0.11, 0.76] for integrity, and d = 0.30 [-0.02, 0.63] for benevolence (Appendix B); we did not test whether the subscale effects differ. Perceived writing quality did not differ, d = 0.25, p = .13.

The absence of a significant content trust effect again does not mean the effect was zero. Its 95% confidence interval extended to about d = 0.5, and equivalence tests established that the content trust effect was negligible only at the widest bound we examined (±0.50; message credibility at ±0.40 or wider; Appendix B).

5.2.5. Conversations

Participants sent 531 messages in total, with a median of three per participant in both conditions (low: M = 3.79, high: M = 3.38, p = .39). Nineteen messages from 10 participants received no recorded reply. Across 512 chatbot responses, high condition responses contained more exclamation marks (0.56 vs. 0.00 per response), more emotion words (0.70 vs. 0.01 per response), and more first-person (2.8 vs. 1.4 per 100 words) and secondperson (0.82 vs. 0.02 per 100 words) pronouns (all ps < .001). They were also longer, 50.1 compared with 38.2 words on average, B = 12.0, t(147) = 7.54, p < .001.

Most conversations engaged with the post. Participants asked about the post, Velocerin, or the underlying study in 98 of 148 conversations (66%), and the chatbot mentioned Velocerin in 114 (77%), with no difference between conditions (ps > .55). Participants asked about the chatbot's identity or whether it had written the post in 9 conversations (6%), and the chatbot used its fallback reply in 25 (17%), again with no difference between conditions.

5.2.6. Correlations

Similar to Experiment 1, we followed up our main analysis by looking at the correlation between anthropomorphism and our other variables of interest. We calculated Pearson correlation coefficients for all correlational analyses.

Anthropomorphism was positively correlated with both content trust (r(146) = .42, p < .001, 95% CI [.28, .55]) and author trust (r(146) = .44, p < .001, 95% CI [.30, .56]). Author trust was positively correlated with content trust (r(146) = .58, p < .001, 95% CI [.46, .68]). Within conditions (centering each variable on its condition mean, as in Experiment 1), the correlations were nearly identical: anthropomorphism was positively correlated with content trust (r(145) = .41, p < .001, 95% CI [.27, .54]) and with author trust (r(145) = .41, p < .001, 95% CI [.26, .53]), and author trust was positively correlated with content trust (r(145) = .57, p < .001, 95% CI [.45, .67]). Unlike in Experiment 1, anthropomorphism was no more strongly correlated with author trust than with content trust (Williams's t(144) = -0.08, p = .94).

Overall, these results were consistent in direction with Experiment 1: the more human-like chatbot received higher author trust, and no reliable content trust effect was detected. However, the difference between the two effects was not significant, so Experiment 2 is inconclusive on its own about whether anthropomorphic cues within GenAI affect author and content trust differently.

6. General Discussion, Limitations, and Future Directions

6.1. Relationships Between Anthropomorphism, Author Trust, and Content Trust

In general, and aligning with past research, we found a positive correlational relationship between anthropomorphism and trust for the author and the content it produces. In Experiment 1, replacing a GenAI author with a human expert, a comparison between kinds of author rather than of anthropomorphism alone, shifted evaluations of the author more than evaluations of the post attributed to it. Experiment 2 was consistent in direction but inconclusive about whether behavioral cues affected the two forms of trust differently. Contrary to H2, then, we found no reliable evidence that anthropomorphic cues increase content trust.

6.1.1. Why Might Author and Content Trust Diverge?

Past research has highlighted that confidence in information partially depends on judgments of source trustworthiness (e.g., Li et al., 2023). Our data are consistent with this in one sense: author and content trust were strongly correlated in both experiments. Because the two were measured at the same time, these correlations cannot show that author trust transmits the effect of author cues to content trust. Moreover, in Experiment 1, author type (GenAI vs. human) affected author trust much more than content trust. Several accounts could explain this divergence. Our design cannot distinguish them, but each makes a prediction that future studies could test.

First, the message carried its own epistemic cues. Theories of epistemic vigilance hold that people weigh what is said as well as who says it, and that social cues about the source matter most when epistemic cues are scarce (Li et al., 2023; Sperber et al., 2010). Our post named a mechanism, a trial size, a placebo comparison, and specific outcomes, and every participant read the same content, so the cues that varied by condition were mostly informative about the author rather than about the claims. This account predicts larger transfer from author to content when messages are short, vague, or hard to evaluate, unlike the detailed post used here.

Second, the author relayed rather than produced the evidence. The post presented itself as a summary of a trial conducted by other researchers, so the credibility of its claims could rest on that trial rather than on whoever summarized it. People may be comfortable with AI summarizing information they would not trust AI to produce. This account predicts that author cues would matter more for content trust if the GenAI were presented as the source of the evidence rather than as a summarizer of it.

Third, the largest effect in our data, the difference in author trust between the human expert and the GenAI authors, may reflect credit for the human author, a penalty for the GenAI authors, or both; without a condition with no attributed author, we cannot tell which. The corresponding content trust effect (d = 0.25) is in the same direction as the content penalties reported for AI-labeled news and headlines and is similarly modest (Longoni et al., 2022; Waddell, 2018). Human favoritism (Zhang & Gosline, 2023) and a machine heuristic that treats machine sources as objective (Sundar, 2008) are both compatible with this pattern.

Finally, some of the divergence may reflect measurement. Anthropomorphism and author trust were rated on the same page in the same slider format, whereas content trust was rated on separate pages with different response formats. This would inflate correlations among the author measures, although it less clearly explains the condition effects, because the dissociation held with the validated scales alone.

The pattern of correlations also differed between experiments: in Experiment 1, anthropomorphism was more strongly related to author trust than to content trust, whereas in Experiment 2 it was related to both to a similar degree, although the experiments also differed in population and cues. One possibility is that the differences in experimental methods, passively reading a description of the author (Experiment 1) versus interacting with a chatbot (Experiment 2), engaged differing cognitive mechanisms underlying trustworthiness judgments for agents we directly interact with (vs. simply learn about).

In Experiment 2, participants interacted directly with a GenAI agent they were told was the author, likely increasing the salience of the authoring agent. Social presence theory proposes that interpersonal cues play a stronger role in judgments when a source feels more present (Cui et al., 2013; Short, 1976). Consistent with social presence theory (Short, 1976) and attribution theory, the increased salience of author characteristics could have led participants to rely more directly on interpersonal trust cues when judging the content (Fiske et al., 1982; Nass & Moon, 2000). In contrast, in Experiment 1, where the author was less salient, attribution may have relied more on heuristic-based trust judgments, such as algorithm aversion or human favoritism, which are known to shape trust and credibility judgments (Dietvorst et al., 2015; Morewedge, 2022). Therefore, the salience of the author could account for the different relationship between content trust and anthropomorphism between experiments.

Beyond social presence, differences in cognitive engagement may also have contributed to these divergent effects in the correlational relationships across experiments. Experiment 1 involved passive reading, potentially leading to shallow processing of the content, while the interaction with the chatbot in Experiment 2 may have prompted deeper engagement.

In the Elaboration Likelihood Model, Petty & Cacioppo (1986) postulate that when one is more motivated and able to consider communications deeply, they are likely to consider the content of the message rather than relying on peripheral cues, and conversely that shallow processing results in dependency on these external cues. Thus, the deeper processing may have triggered different cognitive mechanisms to be used in the trust judgments.

Another possible explanation lies not in the manipulation itself but in participants' beliefs about the plausibility of the GenAI author. Some participants noted that they did not believe that the GenAI agent could have authored the blog post. Other participants may have held similar beliefs without sharing this information with us. This is especially probable in Experiment 1, where participants read a description of an agent that may not have matched with their prior experience or beliefs. The potential expectancy violation and disbelief that participants may have experienced could also have suppressed potential effects of anthropomorphism on content trust. And when participants had the chance to interact with the GenAI chatbot, particularly the one in the high condition, they could see evidence that the writing ability was aligned to that necessary to write the blog post. In Experiment 2, only one participant spontaneously reported doubting that the AI had written the post, although we did not ask about this directly. This difference in believability could explain the difference in correlations between anthropomorphism and content trust across experiments.

6.2. Static and Behavioral Human-Likeness

A strength of this research is that it tested the same question with two designs: a written description of the author (Experiment 1) and a live conversation with it (Experiment 2). In both, the author's presentation affected author trust more than content trust, reliably so only in Experiment 1, and the two forms of trust were strongly correlated, so these patterns do not depend on a single paradigm. The two operationalizations produced different results. A human-like description of a GenAI author produced no detectable difference in perceived humanness, likeability, or trust, whereas human-like behavior during a conversation made the chatbot seem more human and likeable. Because the experiments also differed in population and cues, this contrast does not show that behavioral cues are more effective than descriptive ones.

The behavioral manipulation also changed some perceptions more clearly than others. The empathetic chatbot was rated as clearly more likeable, whereas the difference in Godspeed perceived intelligence was smaller and not significant; yet the METI expertise subscale, which asks about the author's competence as a source of information, showed a modest effect, as did integrity (Section 5.2.3). These results therefore do not show that the manipulation affected warmth rather than competence. Because attributions of intelligence, rather than of emotion or experience, predict advice taking from large language models (Colombatto et al., 2025), manipulations that vary warmth and perceived competence separately would help clarify which, if either, carries over to message credibility.

We also saw little sign of algorithm aversion toward the GenAI authors' content: ratings of the post differed only modestly between human and GenAI authors. Algorithm aversion is strongest for tasks people see as subjective (Castelo et al., 2019) and after an algorithm makes a visible error (Dietvorst et al., 2015). Summarizing a research study may be seen as an objective task, and in Experiment 1 participants had no opportunity to observe an error. Similar credibility ratings for human and GenAI authors also do not by themselves indicate automation bias or inappropriate reliance (Goddard et al., 2012; Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010); that question requires measures of reliance on information whose accuracy is known.

6.3. Practical Implications

The clearest result for designers is that replacing a GenAI author with a human expert shifted evaluations of the source more than evaluations of the message it delivered. Whether the same holds for changes in how a GenAI system itself is presented needs confirmation, because our evidence within GenAI was weak. If it does, favorable impressions of a system may still shape whether people keep using it or rely on it across many interactions, so human-like style should not be treated as a proxy for reliability.

Interfaces that support calibrated trust, such as provenance information, citations, and expressions of uncertainty, address message credibility more directly than social cues do. For researchers, the results argue for measuring trust in a system and trust in its content separately: a study that measured only one of them would reach a different conclusion about the effect of author presentation than a study that measured only the other.

6.4. Limitations and Future Directions

While these findings help clarify the role of anthropomorphism in shaping epistemic trust, some methodological and contextual limitations warrant consideration. First, Experiment 1 did not isolate anthropomorphism. The human condition differed from the GenAI conditions in the type of author and in implied expertise as well as in anthropomorphic cues, and the two GenAI conditions did not differ in anthropomorphism ratings. Because a GenAI author cannot plausibly hold professional credentials, separating humanness from expertise would require holding expertise constant in another way, for example by describing every author with the same role and experience and no title, and including a condition with no attributed author.

Although the interactive chatbot was a more externally valid method of interacting with GenAI in general, it was less realistic for general consumption of science content from the Internet. We think this was an appropriate trade-off to make in order to test the hypothesis that the direct interaction with the agent would more effectively trigger anthropomorphism. The manipulation, however, changed more than humanness: the high condition chatbot was more likeable, gave longer responses, and had a different name, and participants received different additional information during their conversations. We did not assess whether the chatbots added unsupported details or made factual errors. Future studies may want to delve into exploring the emergence of anthropomorphism and the mechanisms of mind perception in non-embodied agents.

As demonstrated by the different results of Experiment 1 and Experiment 2 in relation to the anthropomorphic cue manipulation achieving the expected change in anthropomorphism ratings, not every anthropomorphic cue manipulation will be effective for GenAI agents. Further exploration of what cues people are susceptible to when forming anthropomorphic impressions of agents is critical to inform our understanding of potential risks of GenAI agent design.

The two experiments also differed in population (online adults versus undergraduate students), and Experiment 2 was underpowered for the comparison between author and content trust; roughly 300 participants per condition would be needed to detect a difference of the size we observed with 80% power. Experiment 1 was not preregistered, several analyses in both experiments were exploratory, and the equivalence bounds were chosen after data collection.

Additionally, there are individual differences in the tendency to anthropomorphize agents (Waytz et al., 2010). We used a between-subjects design to avoid potential carryover effects between conditions, but in doing so also introduced variability due to individual differences between participants. Ideally, we would have collected data from participants to be able to test the impact of these individual differences but, balancing the risk of survey fatigue, chose not to include an additional survey such as the Individual Differences in Anthropomorphism Questionnaire (IDAQ; Waytz et al., 2010). We also measured prior GenAI use but not AI literacy or technical self-efficacy, which may moderate how people respond to human-like AI. Future research should explore using measures such as the IDAQ or a within-subjects design with suitable precautions taken to prevent carryover effects between conditions.

All outcomes were self-reported ratings collected in a single session. The author trust composite combines perceived expertise, integrity, and benevolence with intentions to rely on the author, and the content trust composite combines perceived credibility with belief and intended action; neither is a direct measure of accepting communicated knowledge. The novel items have not been independently validated, although our conclusions held with the validated scales alone.

Additionally, we chose one particular type of content (i.e., science content about medical research aimed at lay people) to focus our research on for simplicity and consistency. Holding the message constant across conditions is what allowed us to compare author and content trust, but using a single, optimistic message limits generalization. It is entirely plausible that the relationship between anthropomorphism and content trust may vary across contexts; in particular, the results may not generalize to subjective content, such as creative writing or opinion, for which people are more averse to algorithmic sources (Castelo et al., 2019). And as highlighted by Sperber et al. (2010), subject matter that is less relevant to a participant reduces the need for epistemic trust. Exploration of different types of content, which may have different relevance to participants, is necessary before general conclusions can be drawn about the relationship between anthropomorphism and content trust.

Furthermore, these studies included only a plausibly credible, fictional blog post as the content stimuli. Future studies may consider using an article that provides misinformation, disinformation, or a conspiracy theory to see whether the epistemic trustworthiness of such content is affected similarly based on anthropomorphism of the authoring agent.

While these experiments focused on anthropomorphism, future studies could explore other factors that may influence epistemic trust in GenAI content. Some potential factors that could be interesting to explore are interactivity, organizational credibility, transparency and explainability of content, content domain, social cues from peers or experts (e.g., reviews, endorsements), and communication style (e.g., tone, style, empathy).

7. Conclusion

As GenAI becomes increasingly embedded in the information ecosystem, understanding how to foster appropriate levels of epistemic trust is essential. Across two experiments, how human-like an author appeared to be affected how much people trusted the author more than how much they trusted what it wrote. This was clearest when the author was a human expert rather than a GenAI agent. Participants trusted the human author more, while there was not a significant difference in how much they trusted the same blog post. Among GenAI authors, describing the AI as more human-like had no detectable effect. However, a chatbot that behaved in an empathetic, emotionally expressive way was seen as more human-like and was trusted modestly more as an author. Experiment 2 was underpowered to test whether this behavior affected trust in the author and trust in the content differently.

This work underscores the need to distinguish between trust in agents and trust in information, especially as GenAI becomes more integrated into communication and education settings. Understanding how people come to trust-not only GenAI agents, but also the content they produce-is critical for combating misplaced trust in potentially unreliable content and over-reliance on GenAI.
